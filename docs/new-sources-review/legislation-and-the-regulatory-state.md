**C-1. The unitary executive and the "checks and balances executive" (Shane's framing)**
- Kind: Concept. Map: Appointments and executive structure, core concepts; cross-link `lrs-s-removal`, `lrs-o-ministerial-duties`, `lrs-o-fragmented-execution`, `lrs-s-slaughter`, `lrs-s-morrison`, `lrs-s-myers`.
- Why first: it is the organising debate for Classes 9 to 12, it is the professor's own position, and the atlas has only one sentence on it (inside `lrs-o-fragmented-execution`).
- Draft: The question is whether the President may direct other executive officials in the substance of how they exercise discretion that Congress vested in them and not in the President; possible sources of such a power are a statute, the Vesting Clause, or the faithful execution clause (C9 s17). Unitary executive theory starts from the premise that Article II guarantees the President control over the discretionary functions of the executive branch. It comes in "different flavors": plenary removal power over principal officers; plenary removal power over all officers who assist in exercising executive power; power to command directly how discretion is exercised; and power to execute the laws personally (C9 s18). Its claimed sources are the Vesting Clause, the Take Care Clause, "or the alchemy of both" (C9 s19).
  The professor summarises a "Roberts narrative", which he labels "supposedly rooted in history", in three parts: the one-person branch (Trump v. Mazars USA, LLP, 591 U.S. 848, 868 (2020)); the unbreakable chain of command (Free Enterprise Fund, 561 U.S. at 498); and the uncheckable power over subjects within the President's "conclusive and preclusive" authority (Trump v. United States (2024)), which the slide says includes the removal power and control of investigative and prosecutorial decisionmaking (C9 s20).
  His own "likeliest coherent reading of the text": the President heads the branch; the President's powers relate to execution of the law; Congress determines how law is best executed, creates the executive departments and assigns their duties; the President supervises to ensure faithful execution and may demand reports from department heads; inferior officers may be appointed, at Congress's choice, by the President alone, department heads or courts of law (C9 s21). Side by side: under the unitary model the President may command how all executive discretion is exercised and disobedience is grounds for at-will dismissal; under the checks-and-balances model the officer Congress designated must exercise the discretion, the President may "cajole, persuade, nudge", and may fire a displeasing officer only "if Congress permits" (C9 s22). The directive question has never been directly tested in court; the usual assumption ties it to appointment and removal, but the professor asks whether at-will removal necessarily carries power to dictate outcomes (C9 s23).
  The same debate returns in Morrison: the professor labels Scalia's dissent the "One-Person Branch Theory" (C11 s14) and answers it from the text: Article I vests powers "herein granted", the Opinions Clause would be odd if the President already had total control, and the Necessary and Proper Clause lets Congress legislate for powers vested in "any department or officer" (C11 s15). Myers supplies the "unitary and uniform execution of the laws" language (C10 s23).
- Cautions: C9 s20 is the professor's characterisation of the Chief Justice's opinions, not a neutral summary. Trump v. Mazars and Trump v. United States have no atlas entries; the slide gives a full citation only for Mazars.

**C-2. Discretion and the nondelegation problem (Shane's framing)**
- Kind: Concept, with a built-in hypothetical. Map: Delegation and statutory authority, core concepts (beside `lrs-s-intelligible-principle`, `lrs-s-accountability`, `lrs-s-enabling-statute`).

- Draft: The professor calls discretion "the foundational problem of administrative law" (C3 s9). **C-3. Why majorities do not get their way in Congress: vetogates, House rules, the filibuster and collective action**
- Kind: Concept. Map: Legislative process and statutory interpretation (beside `lrs-k-lawmaking`, `lrs-k-congress`).
- Draft: The class opens with "a democratic puzzle": why does Congress not routinely enact measures that track sometimes overwhelming majority opinion (C2 s3; C1 s2 agenda)? In the Senate, practice for most bills permits unlimited debate; a vote to end debate is cloture; impeding the end of debate is a filibuster; cloture takes 60 votes, with only a majority needed for reconciliation bills and votes to confirm executive appointees and judges (C2 s4). The standing rules set no limit on debate; the workarounds are cloture votes and unanimous consent agreements; filibusters may frustrate the majority "but work to the benefit of moderates", letting Senators vote for things they know will not pass and letting majority dissenters vote no when it does not matter; the Senate has abolished filibusters for all presidential nominations (C2 s10). In the House, each bill reaches the floor under its own rule; the Rules Committee, dominated by loyalists of the majority leadership, can keep a bill from the floor even if a House majority would support it; under Republican control in recent Congresses no bill reached the floor without the support of a majority of Republican members, the "Hastert Rule" (C2 s5). Party discipline holds because moderates support the leadership's rule preferences instead of forming cross-party coalitions: there is a strong norm of supporting special rules, the cost of going along is less than the cost of dissent, and leadership rewards loyalty (C2 s9).
  The "vetogates" at which a bill can die: the committee chair, who "holds the most power" and can refuse to assign a bill to a subcommittee, refuse a hearing, or send it to a hostile subcommittee; floor scheduling; floor consideration, including the Senate filibuster; refusal of either House to adopt the conference version; and the presidential veto (C2 s8). The obstacles are described as designed to encourage deliberation and allow input from multiple constituencies; norms (customary practices not enforceable by rule) matter; legislators respond to policy preferences, leadership and reelection prospects, party-loyalty pressure and available bargains (C2 s6). Legislation also reflects voters, executive personnel, committee staff, lobbyists, legislative counsel and the press, "who also may or may not reflect popular majorities" (C2 s13).
  The "logic of collective action": legislation tracks the influence of well-organized groups, because it is harder to organize large groups with diffuse interests than small groups with intense common interests. The professor immediately asks how, then, to explain environmental, civil rights and safety laws; the slide leaves that open (C2 s7). The idea returns as a limit of tort law (C2 s25).
  Budget process: the slide distinguishes appropriations from authorizations and describes the concurrent budget resolution, which cannot be filibustered and is not sent to the President; it directs committees to change spending, change revenues, or raise or lower the debt limit by specified amounts (C2 s11).
- Cautions: C2 s12 ("Reconciliation Process") and C3 s2 ("Another Look at Reconciliation") are pictures; do not write the reconciliation steps from memory. C2 s9's proposals "C, M, and L" are not defined on the slide. The Parrillo-Shane supplement on file covers the motion to proceed, filibuster, cloture, the Rules Committee and the Hastert Rule and should be read before this entry is expanded beyond the slides. Background, not from the source: "The Logic of Collective Action" is the title of a 1965 book by Mancur Olson; the slide does not name him.

**C-4. How a bill becomes law: the constitutional framework, the vocabulary, and the "simple model"**
- Kind: Concept. Map: Legislative process and statutory interpretation; link to C-3 and `lrs-k-legislative-history`.
- Draft: Constitutional framework: Congress has two Houses; each makes its own procedural rules; a statute requires approval in both Houses of an identical text; and either the President signs or Congress overrides the veto (C1 s13). Vocabulary: a "Congress" sits for two years following the most recent election (for example the 119th) and each year is usually one "Session"; each House is divided into subject-matter committees led by a Chairman, with the most senior minority-party member as Ranking Member (C1 s14). The simple model: (1) a legislator proposes a bill; (2) it is referred to committee; (3) the committee holds hearings, "marks up" the bill and refers it to the floor with a report; (4) the chamber debates and votes; (5) if the chambers pass different versions, a "conference committee" reconciles them (C1 s17). Each step leaves a document that later counts as legislative history (C1 s18). Katzmann's five reasons legislation is often vague or ambiguous: unforeseeability of problems; inherent imprecision of language; decisions to delegate broadly and let agencies resolve specifics later; coalition politics; congressional organisation, including the decentralised committee system (C1 s19).
- Cautions: the deck calls the model "simple" and does not describe the departures from it (see C-3). C1 s15-16 list the House and Senate committees of the 119th Congress; if used at all, attach them as a collapsed reference and label them a snapshot of one Congress. **C-5. Three approaches to interpretation, interpretation versus construction, and the persistent puzzles**
- Kind: Concept. Map: Legislative process and statutory interpretation, interpretive methods (beside `lrs-k-purposivism`, `lrs-k-textualism`). The atlas has no entry and no text on intentionalism or on the interpretation/construction distinction.
- Draft: The professor separates two tasks. Interpretation asks what the words mean; construction asks how to apply them. Even if an "ambulance" is a "vehicle", it is a further question whether a ban on "vehicles" applies to ambulances (C3 s18). Two sources of difficulty: first, properties of language, ambiguity and vagueness, arising from unforeseen applications, intentional delegation to the executive branch, coalitions that "fudge" disagreement, and the inevitable limits of language (C3 s18, citing "RAK at 15"); second, institutional roles: what it means to be "faithful to the work of the people's representatives" (text or purposes?), whether to defer to an agency that has already interpreted the statute, and whether the process of enactment should affect interpretation (C3 s19, citing Katzmann at 105).
  He names three approaches: intentionalism, purposivism and textualism. They use "largely same tools, but different emphasis", and all "start from premise of legislative supremacy" (C3 s21; C4 s1). Contrasting premises: the purposivist assumes Congress "acts rationally to achieve its purposes" and reads laws in light of purpose "because legislatures may act in haste or inattentively"; the textualist says "every statute is a compromise", misalignments between purpose and detail are "likely to reflect bargains", and fidelity to Congress is "fidelity to the bargain", especially because purpose is "too slippery a guide" (C4 s2). Later recap: both camps claim to be Congress's "faithful servants"; the difference is the range of tools (C8 s1).
  Persistent puzzles (C4 s24): should judges apply statutes according to "letter" or "spirit"? What is "spirit": the legislators' subjective or imputed intentions, the "objective" purpose of the statute, or public policy identified by judges? Two "big puzzles": is there ever evidence of intention or purpose more compelling than the text, and if not, how does one explain that Congress "historically overrules more textualist interpretations than purposive interpretations"? And what does it mean to "hew to the text" when, "in case after case, judges who purport to agree on method disagree on meaning"? Later theme: it is hard to ignore the possibility that unstated context is "shaping choice and application of method" (C7 s1).
- Cautions: the slides do not define the three approaches; any definitions added must be marked background. "RAK" as Robert A. Katzmann is an inference. **C-6. Structuring your analysis of a statutory interpretation case (six questions)**
- Kind: Note (study method). Map: Legislative process and statutory interpretation; worth surfacing on every LRS interpretation case card.

**C-8. The path to administration, and why agencies exist**

## E. Conflicts to settle

No slide contradicts an atlas holding. The first five are real differences on a checkable fact. In each, my recommendation is to leave the atlas as it is until someone opens the opinion.

**E-1. Collins v. Yellen: how Justice Kagan voted.**
- Slide (C11 s41): Sotomayor with Breyer is quoted, then "(Kagan also dissents from extension of Seila Law.)"
- Atlas (`LRS-COMP-SLAUGHTER-COLLINS`): "Kagan separately agreed that the removal restriction was unconstitutional under precedent, producing a 7-2 result on that question", and she "concurred in part and in the judgment".

**E-2. United States v. Gayle: reporter volume.**

- Atlas (`lrs-k-gayle`): "342 F.3d 89 (2d Cir. 2003), decided August 27, 2003, amended January 7, 2004".

**E-3. Raila v. United States: year, and who wrote it.**
