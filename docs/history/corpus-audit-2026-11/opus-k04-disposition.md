## What the court decided under the actual agreement

The deposit receipt made the purchaser's duty to buy conditional on "Coldwell Banker & Co. obtaining leases
satisfactory to the purchaser." Three things in that agreement need to be kept apart.

- **The condition** is an event that must occur before Mattei's duty to buy becomes due: leases satisfactory
  to him.
- **The purchaser's remaining obligation**, while the condition is open, is to judge the leases in good
  faith. The court put lease satisfaction in the category of "fancy, taste, or judgment", where the
  promisor's good-faith dissatisfaction is a defense (51 Cal. 2d at 123–24; C&M 104). It did not apply a
  reasonable-person standard, because the factors were too numerous to apply one (C&M 104). The court
  quoted the First Restatement: the purchaser "must be dissatisfied with the performance, as a performance of
  the contract, and his dissatisfaction must be genuine" (§ 265, comment a; 51 Cal. 2d at 124–25).
- **The consideration** is that good-faith duty. "The promisor's duty to exercise his judgment in good faith is
  an adequate consideration to support the contract", so the contract was "neither illusory nor lacking in
  mutuality of obligation" (51 Cal. 2d at 124–25). The court reversed. It did not decide whether any
  particular rejection would have been in good faith; Hopper had repudiated first.

**An arbitrary rejection.** The court applied a good-faith standard, not a reasonableness standard, so an
honest but unreasonable dissatisfaction with the leases would still excuse the purchaser: the condition fails.
A rejection whose real reason is that the purchaser no longer wants the deal is not dissatisfaction "with the
performance, as a performance of the contract." Under the standard the court adopted, it would not excuse him.
That second point is an inference from the stated standard and the § 265 comment, not a holding. Note 1
(C&M 106) asks exactly this question.

## What the sources establish about the slide 6 clauses

Slide 5 asks "Is it possible to contract around this interpretation?" Slide 6 shows two drafts: "acting at his
sole discretion", and a definition of "sole discretion" as "without regard to any standard of reasonableness or
other standard by which the determination … might be challenged." Neither the opinion nor the assigned pages
say what those words would do.

- The first draft adds words but no standard. Mattei already treated the clause as one of judgment, and
  judgment clauses are already measured by good faith. A court could read "sole discretion" as Mattei read
  "satisfactory to the purchaser".
- The definition excludes reasonableness, which Mattei did not impose for this kind of clause. Whether
  "or other standard" also excludes good faith is unresolved by these sources.
- If it did exclude good faith, it would remove the very obligation the court held to be the consideration.
  The purchaser would then not have contracted around the Mattei reading. He would have reopened the
  illusory-promise problem Mattei resolved. That is the question Note 1 poses: "Would Hopper have been bound
  if Mattei had inserted a clause permitting him to do this?"

**Counterexample.** After signing a deposit receipt with the slide 6 definition, the purchaser rejects leases
that would satisfy anyone, because land prices have fallen. On the lecture note's wording, the drafting "does"
contract around Mattei, so he walks away free. On the sources, either good faith still applies, so he is in
breach because his rejection is not dissatisfaction with the leases, or the definition removes it, so the
seller may argue there was no consideration and no contract. Neither result is the one the note implies.

## Proposed wording (lecture notes, class 4, "Mattei v. Hopper and satisfaction clauses")

Replace "Slide 6 shows drafting that does: “acting at his sole discretion”, with a definition excluding any
standard of reasonableness." with:

> Slide 6 shows drafting that attempts it: “acting at his sole discretion”, with a definition excluding any
> standard of reasonableness. Mattei itself measured this kind of judgment by good faith, not reasonableness, and
> held that the good-faith duty was the consideration (51 Cal. 2d at 124–25), so the sources do not establish
> what the slide 6 wording changes. A clause that also removed good faith would raise the illusory-promise question
> of Note 1 (C&M 106).
