# OP-CT-13 learn list: Contracts Class 13, the parol evidence rule

Derived only from the assigned sources. Pinpoints: C&M printed page; Supp. = supplement page (the "n of 138" footer); R2 = Restatement (Second) of Contracts; RLCC = Restatement of the Law, Consumer Contracts; S = slide number. C&M 531–534 (most of Mitchill v. Lath) are not assigned; they are cited below only where an assigned note relies on them.

Source notes. (1) The no-oral-modification (NOM) note is in C&M 546–547, not in the supplement; Supp. 37–45 holds only Gianni (37–39) and TeeVee Toons (40–45). (2) R2 comments in the selection exist only for § 210 (cmts a–c); §§ 209, 213–216 appear as black letter only. (3) RLCC wording and numbering differ across sources (see Unresolved item 7).

## L1. What the rule is and what it is not (C&M 527–530)
- The rule addresses terms allegedly agreed before or contemporaneously with a writing but omitted from it; ambiguity is handled by interpretation rules in later sections (C&M 528).
- It may preclude a finding that a prior or contemporaneous agreement not reflected in the writing is part of the contract (C&M 529).
- The name misleads: it bars prior writings (letters, emails) as well as oral agreements, and it is a rule of substantive law, not of evidence, that precludes any proof that the terms are other than as written (C&M 529).
- Factors courts weigh: whether the term is within the writing's scope or "collateral," whether it contradicts the writing, whether the writing is the parties' final expression, and whether they intended it to embody all terms (C&M 529).
- Controversy: the English Law Commission (Working Paper No. 70, 1976) called it a technical rule that at worst prevents courts from getting at the truth and provisionally recommended abolition, which was not carried out; Posner, J., defended it in Olympia Hotels, 908 F.2d at 1373 (C&M 529–530).

## L2. Consequences of "substantive" (C&M 530 Note)
- Failure to object does not waive the rule; the party may assert it later (Gajewski v. Bratcher, N.D. 1974).
- Under Erie a federal court hearing a state-law claim applies the state's parol evidence rule (Betz Labs. v. Hines, 3d Cir. 1981).

## L3. Rationales and policy (C&M 535 Note 2; 538 Masterson; S12)
- Discharge rationale: under R2 § 213 a later integrated agreement discharges prior agreements, so the rule affirms the primacy of the later agreement (C&M 535 Note 2).
- McCormick's jury-control rationale: the party urging the spoken word is often the economic underdog, and the rule lets the court control sympathetic juries (C&M 535 Note 2; 538). The note asks whether this explains barring prior writings and not barring subsequent oral agreements.
- Masterson's accounting: the accuracy-of-writings policy is served by excluding evidence that directly contradicts the writing; the fear-of-fraud policy means collateral oral agreements should be excluded only when the fact finder is likely to be misled, so the rule rests on credibility (C&M 538).
- Slide 12: benefits are cautionary effect, reduced risk of interpretive error, ease of interpreting writings, biased juries, reduced reading costs and reduced litigation costs; competing costs are increased drafting costs and frustration of unsophisticated parties' intentions.
- Fact finding versus preclusion: Mitchill held the agreement "may not be proved," a different thing from a finding that it was not made (C&M 535 Note 1).

## L4. Restatement two-step structure; the court decides (C&M 536 Note 3; R2 §§ 209, 210, 213, 215, 216)
- Integrated agreement: a writing that is a final expression of one or more terms (§ 209(1)). A writing that, in view of its completeness and specificity, reasonably appears complete is taken to be integrated unless other evidence shows it was not a final expression (§ 209(3)).
- Completely integrated: adopted as a complete and exclusive statement of the terms (§ 210(1)); partially integrated: any other integrated agreement (§ 210(2)).
- Both questions are for the court, as questions preliminary to interpretation or to application of the rule (§§ 209(2), 210(3); C&M 536).
- Effect: a binding integrated agreement discharges prior agreements to the extent inconsistent (§ 213(1)); a completely integrated one discharges prior agreements within its scope (§ 213(2)); an agreement not binding, or voidable and avoided, discharges nothing (§ 213(3)).
- Evidence rule: for any integrated agreement, partial or complete, prior or contemporaneous agreements are inadmissible to contradict a term (§ 215). Consistent additional terms are admissible unless the court finds complete integration (§ 216(1)); when the agreement is complete, they are barred only within its scope (C&M 536).
- § 216(2): an agreement is not completely integrated if the writing omits a consistent additional agreed term that is (a) agreed to for separate consideration or (b) such that in the circumstances it might naturally be omitted from the writing.
- § 210 cmt a: rejects the assumption that a writing final on some matters includes all matters agreed. Cmt b: complete integration may be proved by any relevant evidence; an apparently complete signed writing may be decisive absent credible contrary evidence; "a writing cannot of itself prove its own completeness, and wide latitude must be allowed for inquiry into circumstances bearing on the intention of the parties." Cmt c: incompleteness may appear on the face of the writing or be shown by other writings; any relevant evidence may show that an apparently complete writing never became fully effective or was modified after adoption.

## L5. What evidence of prior agreements is still admissible (R2 § 214; S7)
- § 214: prior or contemporaneous agreements and negotiations are admissible to establish (a) whether the writing is integrated; (b) whether it is completely or partially integrated; (c) its meaning, whether or not integrated; (d) illegality, fraud, duress, mistake, lack of consideration, or other invalidating cause; (e) grounds for rescission, reformation, specific performance or another remedy.
- Slide 7 lists what extrinsic evidence cannot do (vary or contradict an integrated writing; add to a completely integrated writing within its scope; explain an unambiguous writing) and can do (invalidate; reform; show grounds for a remedy; establish a collateral agreement beyond the writing's scope or that might naturally be omitted; establish the meaning of ambiguous writings; explain, supplement or qualify through commercial context; establish that performance is subject to a condition).
- The rule does not speak to subsequent agreements (C&M 546; see L15). Conditions to performance: S7 lists them; R2 § 217 is in the selection but not assigned.

## L6. Gianni v. R. Russel & Co. (Pa. 1924) (Supp. 37–39; S16)
- Facts in the excerpt: a tenant who sold tobacco, fruit, candy and soft drinks signed a three-year lease; he claimed that, in consideration of his promises not to sell tobacco and to pay increased rent, the landlord orally promised him the exclusive right to sell soft drinks in the building; no such term is in the lease; the landlord then leased the adjoining room to a drug company that sold soft drinks. The tenant won below on the oral contract (Supp. 37).
- Test: the writing must be the entire contract for parol evidence to be excluded; the court looks at the writing, and if it appears to be a complete contract it is conclusively presumed that the whole engagement was reduced to writing (Supp. 38). Whether the oral agreement falls within the field of the written one is answered by comparing the two and asking whether parties situated as these were "would naturally and normally include the one in the other if it were made"; if they relate to the same subject matter and are so interrelated that both would be executed at the same time and in the same contract, the writing covers it. "This question must be determined by the court" (Supp. 38).
- Application: the lease covered the use of the room and what the tenant could and could not sell; since his promise not to sell tobacco was in the writing, "it would be the natural thing to have included the promise of exclusive rights"; where a claim rests on an oral understanding about a subject the writing deals with, the writing is presumed to set out the whole agreement on that subject (Wigmore's index: whether the element is dealt with at all in the writing) (Supp. 38).
- Exceptions: fraud, accident or mistake, which the plaintiff expressly disclaimed; the evidence must be such as would cause a chancellor to reform the instrument (Supp. 39).
- Holding: reversed; judgment entered for the defendant; the court will "stand for the integrity of written contracts" (Supp. 39).
- Not in the excerpt: the lease's use clause verbatim (OCR gaps on Supp. 37, 38); any merger clause. The excerpt notes the plaintiff produced no witness to corroborate what was said at signing (Supp. 37).

## L7. Mitchill v. Lath as the older "four corners" approach (C&M 530, 535 assigned; 531–534 unassigned)
- Assigned: the Court of Appeals of New York (1928) treated the rule as substantive; the oral promise to remove an icehouse "may not be proved" (C&M 530, 535 Note 1). Masterson Note 2 calls Mitchill an example of the older view that integration is determined from the face of the instrument (C&M 541).
- Unassigned but relied on: the three conditions (collateral in form; not contradicting the writing; not a term parties would ordinarily be expected to embody in the writing), and the holding that the agreement was so closely related to the subject of the writing that it could not be proved (C&M 531–532); Lehman's dissent (C&M 532–535).

## L8. Masterson v. Sine (Cal. 1968) (C&M 536–541; S17)
- Facts: Dallas and Rebecca Masterson conveyed their ranch to Medora (Dallas's sister) and Lu Sine by grant deed reserving an option to repurchase by Feb. 25, 1968 at the "same consideration as being paid heretofore" plus depreciation value of improvements; Dallas went bankrupt; his trustee and Rebecca sued for declaratory relief to enforce the option (C&M 536).
- Trial court (no jury) admitted extrinsic evidence of what the price terms meant ($50,000; tax-depreciation formula) and excluded the Sines' evidence that the option was personal to the grantors to keep the property in the family; judgment for plaintiffs (C&M 536–537).
- Holding: reversed. Admitting evidence to explain the price language was proper; excluding the evidence that the option was personal and nonassignable was error (C&M 537, 539).
- Rules: a complete integration bars parol evidence to add to or vary terms; in a partial integration the same rule applies to the integrated part, but parol evidence may prove elements not reduced to writing (C&M 537). The crucial issue is whether the parties intended the writing as the exclusive embodiment of their agreement; the instrument may help (e.g., a clause stating there are no other agreements), but the collateral agreement itself must be examined, and circumstances at the time of writing may aid (C&M 537). Rejects the strict California formulation that integration is decided solely from the face of the instrument (C&M 537–538; Wigmore: a self-determinative writing is "an impossible" conception).
- Test adopted: Restatement (First) § 240(1)(b) admits a collateral agreement that "might naturally be made as a separate agreement by parties situated as were the parties to the written contract"; UCC § 2-202 cmt 3 would exclude in still fewer cases ("would certainly have been included") (C&M 538–539). Footnote 1: Corbin would admit the evidence even when an agreement would be unnatural if the court is convinced it was made (C&M 539 n.1).
- Application: the deed does not say it contains the complete agreement and is silent on assignability; a deed's formalized structure makes inclusion of collateral terms less likely; the option may have been placed in the recorded deed only to protect against later purchasers; nothing shows these family parties had warning of the risk of leaving terms out; so the agreement "might naturally be made as a separate agreement," and a fortiori it is not one that "would certainly" have been included (C&M 539). A writing does not necessarily preclude parol evidence rebutting a term the law would otherwise presume, here assignability (C&M 539).
- Dissent (Burke, J., McComb, J., concurring): undermines the rule as applied in California since 1872; the option was absolute in form, so a nonassignability limit contradicts the writing; California statutes make the option assignable; nothing prevented adding "this option is nonassignable"; the ruling lets a bankrupt defeat creditors with family testimony (C&M 539–541).

## L9. Who decides and what the court may look at: four corners versus context (C&M 541 Note 2; Supp. 38; R2 § 210 cmt b; UCC § 2-202 cmt 3; S10)
- Four corners (Gianni, Mitchill): integration judged from the writing; if it appears complete, the parties are conclusively presumed to have put the whole engagement in it.
- Restatement: completeness cannot be proved by the writing itself; wide latitude for circumstances (§ 210 cmt b); still decided by the court (§§ 209(2), 210(3)).
- Three formulations of the "natural" test, which students must not merge: Gianni ("would naturally and normally include the one in the other," Supp. 38); Restatement (First) § 240(1)(b) as quoted in Masterson ("might naturally be made as a separate agreement," C&M 538); R2 § 216(2)(b) ("might naturally be omitted from the writing"); UCC § 2-202 cmt 3 ("would certainly have been included in the document in the view of the court").
- Slide 10 lists as contested whether extrinsic evidence may be used to decide integration and ambiguity, and whether the rule should apply to consumer contracts.

## L10. Merger (integration, entire-agreement) clauses (C&M 541–542 Notes 3–4; S9, S14)
- Integration turns on intent; a merger clause is strong evidence of it (C&M 542). Courts split on whether it is conclusive: Howard v. Perry (Idaho 2005) and Phelps-Dickson (N.C. App. 2005) treat it as conclusive or barring; Jenkins v. Eckerd (Fla. App. 2005), Behrens (N.H. 2006) and Bellman (Ohio 2007) treat it as not dispositive (C&M 542).
- Weight varies with circumstances: great weight between sophisticated, lawyered parties (Telecom Int'l Am. v. AT&T, 2d Cir. 2001); little weight when inconspicuous in a form given to an unsophisticated buyer (Seibel v. Layne & Bowler, Or. Ct. App. 1982) (C&M 542). International: UNIDROIT art. 2.1.17. Unconscionability: Franklin v. White (Ind. 1986) (C&M 542).
- Slide 9: a merger clause is relevant to the extent of integration but not dispositive; parol evidence can challenge the validity even of a completely integrated agreement.
- Collateral agreements: integration of one agreement has no effect on an entirely separate agreement; the note asks how Traynor's "collateral agreement" differs from partial integration (C&M 542 Note 5).

## L11. Consumer contracts: RLCC § 8 and § 9 (C&M 542–543 Note 6; RLCC §§ 8, 9; S11)
- § 8: a standard contract term that contradicts, unreasonably limits, or fails to give the reasonably intended [C&M: "expected"] effect to a prior affirmation of fact or promise by the business is not a final expression on that subject and does not discharge the obligation arising from the affirmation or promise.
- Comments: the rule negates the finality prerequisite for integration (cmt 1); it applies even when the standard term is unambiguous, so the four corners test does not apply in consumer contracts (cmt 2); merger clauses do not control the integration question because consumers are unlikely to notice or understand them (cmt 3); § 8 supplements R2 § 213 and UCC § 2-202 (cmt 4). Illustrations: snow removal by 10 a.m.; car delivery within 10 weeks despite a merger clause; gym contract not effective on signing (an effectiveness assurance).
- C&M frames § 8 as responding to McCormick's underdog concern and asks about its cost to businesses that use forms for uniformity (C&M 542–543).
- Numbering: the selection prints the 2019 Tentative Draft, where the parol evidence section is § 8, § 7 is "Affirmations of Fact and Promises That Are Part of the Consumer Contract," and § 9 is "Effects of Derogation from Mandatory Rules." Slide 11 (and the 2024 Official Text the Atlas cites) numbers the parol evidence section § 9. If the syllabus's "§§ 8 and 9" follows the Official Text, the assigned sections are the affirmations rule (selection § 7) and the parol evidence rule (selection § 8). Both readings are recorded here.
- Affirmations rule (selection § 7): a business's affirmation of fact or promise that creates a reasonable expectation in a reasonable consumer in its intended audience that the subject matter will have the described attribute becomes part of the contract; a third party's affirmation does so under the conditions in (b); standard terms purporting to negate or limit such affirmations or promises are not enforceable (c).
- Derogation rule (selection § 9): if a term excludes, limits or violates a mandatory rule, the court should refuse to enforce the contract, enforce it without the term, or limit the term; if it strikes the term it may substitute a reasonable term, a minimal correcting term, or, for a term placed in bad faith, a term giving the business an incentive not to use such terms. It does not itself address the parol evidence rule.

## L12. Bollinger v. Central Pa. Quarry Stripping & Constr. Co. (Pa. 1967): reformation for mutual mistake (C&M 543–544; S18)
- Facts: a written agreement let the contractor deposit turnpike construction waste on the Bollingers' land; the Bollingers alleged a mutual understanding that the contractor would remove the topsoil, deposit the waste, and restore the topsoil over it; they signed without reading, assuming it was included; the contractor did this at first, then stopped, saying the writing did not require it (C&M 543–544).
- Rule: equity may reform the written evidence of a contract to match the parties' understanding, but the mistake must be mutual; one party's denial does not prevent a finding of mutual mistake; a signer cannot escape merely by saying he did not understand, but equity will "break a hole through the wall" when proper evidence shows a real mistake (C&M 544).
- Proof: the plaintiffs carried the "heavy burden"; their account was corroborated by undisputed evidence that the contractor had in fact stripped and replaced topsoil on part of the area and had done the same for a neighbor (Beltzner) (C&M 544).
- Holding: decree granting reformation affirmed (C&M 544).
- Note 1 asks how the Bollingers differ from Mrs. Mitchill and what she would have needed to show (C&M 544). Problem: Ballard v. Chavez (N.M. 1994), double deduction in a change order; does timing of discovery matter (C&M 545). Left open by the source.

## L13. Validity and fraud (C&M 544–545 Notes 2–3; R2 § 214(d); S9, S13–15)
- Because the rule assumes a valid written agreement, it does not bar evidence that the writing is not valid: a sham not intended to be enforced, or a false recital of consideration (§ 214(d)) (C&M 544 Note 2).
- Fraud in the inducement may be shown (Riverisland, 2013) (C&M 544). Distinguish a claim that a party made a fraudulent representation during negotiations (not barred) from a claim that it made an express warranty during negotiations (barred) (C&M 545).
- Promissory fraud may be shown (Lovejoy Electronics, 7th Cir. 1989); contra Alling v. Universal Mfg. (Cal. App. 1992) (fraud exception applies only if the false promise is independent of or consistent with the writing) (C&M 545).
- Merger clause and fraud: the note poses the question and cites § 214 and Keller v. A.O. Smith Harvestore (Colo. 1991) (a general integration clause does not waive a negligent-misrepresentation claim not specifically prohibited) (C&M 545).
- Slides 13–15 (Psenicska v. Twentieth Century Fox, not in the assigned excerpts) contrast a merger clause, a non-reliance statement, and a waiver of fraud claims, and ask whether parties should be able to contract out of fraud liability (fair and efficient for the maker to bear known inaccuracies; autonomy; litigation and agency costs).

## L14. Sale of goods: UCC § 2-202 (C&M 545; § 2-202 and cmts 1–3)
- Terms in a writing intended as a final expression of the terms it includes may not be contradicted by evidence of any prior agreement or of a contemporaneous oral agreement, but may be explained or supplemented (a) by course of performance, course of dealing or usage of trade, and (b) by consistent additional terms unless the court finds the writing was intended also as a complete and exclusive statement.
- Cmt 1 rejects (a) the assumption that a writing final on some matters includes all matters agreed; (b) the premise that language carries the meaning given by rules of construction rather than its commercial context; (c) any requirement that the court first find ambiguity before admitting course of dealing, usage or performance. Cmt 2: such evidence is admissible to explain or supplement any writing, and unless carefully negated becomes part of the words' meaning; course of performance is the best indication of meaning. Cmt 3: consistent additional terms may be proved unless the court finds the writing intended by both parties as complete and exclusive; if the terms, if agreed, "would certainly have been included in the document in the view of the court," the evidence must be kept from the trier of fact.
- C&M asks which parts of § 2-202 are parol evidence rules, whether it matches the Restatement, and notes § 2-202 is silent on fraud, pointing to § 1-103(b) for supplementary common law (C&M 545).
- Dentco problem: oral statement that the chair reclines a full 180 degrees; a signed writing stating it is "a complete and exclusive statement of all of the terms"; does Dr. Miller have a claim? (C&M 545–546). The source poses but does not answer it; the tools are § 2-202(b) with the merger clause (L10), the warranty-versus-fraud distinction (C&M 545), and the fact that the buyer is a professional buyer, not a consumer.

## L15. Subsequent agreements and no-oral-modification clauses (C&M 546–547)
- The rule does not bar proof of a later oral modification (C&M 546).
- Common-law answer: a NOM clause is traditionally not effective, because the clause itself can be modified by a later agreement (Wagner v. Graziano Constr., Pa. 1957, Musmanno: "acetylene torch"; Beatty v. Guggenheim Exploration, N.Y. 1919, Cardozo: "Those who make a contract may unmake it") (C&M 546–547).
- N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 15-301(1): a written agreement containing a no-oral-change provision cannot be changed by an executory agreement unless the change is in writing and signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought or its agent; courts construe it narrowly (Rose v. Spa Realty, 1977) (C&M 547).
- Even where NOM clauses are honored, reliance on the oral modification can defeat the clause (Universal Builders v. Moon Motor Lodge, Pa. 1968: an owner who requests extra work, promises to pay, and watches it performed cannot rely on the lack of a written change order) (C&M 547).
- UCC § 2-209(2): a signed agreement excluding modification or rescission except by a signed writing cannot otherwise be modified or rescinded; § 2-209(4): a failed modification may operate as a waiver; § 2-209(5): a material change of position in reliance on the waiver may prevent its retraction; Wisconsin Knife Works (7th Cir. 1986) shows Posner and Easterbrook disagreeing over (5) (C&M 547).
- CISG art. 29(2) and UNIDROIT art. 2.18 enforce writing-only modification clauses, with a reliance exception (C&M 547 Note).

## L16. CISG and TeeVee Toons v. Gerhard Schubert GmbH (S.D.N.Y. 2006) (Supp. 40–45; CISG art. 8; S19–20)
- CISG applied under art. 1(1)(a): a sale of goods between parties with places of business in different contracting states (U.S. and Germany) (Supp. 41).
- TVT sued under CISG art. 35 (fitness) and art. 36 (guarantee); Schubert relied on attached "Terms and Conditions" disclaiming or limiting warranties and capping damages, which also contained a merger clause; TVT said Schubert's agent orally told it, between drafting (Feb. 3) and acceptance (Feb. 13, 1995), not to worry about the boilerplate and that it was meaningless for this project (Supp. 41–42).
- Law: the CISG has no statute of frauds (art. 11) and no parol evidence rule; art. 8(1) interprets a party's statements by its intent where the other party knew or could not have been unaware of it; art. 8(2) otherwise applies the reasonable-person standard; art. 8(3) requires due consideration of all relevant circumstances, including negotiations, practices, usages and subsequent conduct (Supp. 42; CISG art. 8). MCC-Marble (11th Cir. 1998): art. 8(3) is a clear instruction to consider parol evidence of negotiations to the extent it reveals subjective intent (Supp. 42).
- Merger clause: whether a prior oral agreement to disregard boilerplate that itself contains a merger clause trumps that clause was treated as a question of first impression; following CISG Advisory Council Opinion No. 3, extrinsic evidence is not excluded unless the parties actually intended the merger clause to have that effect, and the court read this to mean the clause operates only if both parties shared the intent to be bound by it (Supp. 43; n.2 at Supp. 44: writings are not presumed to be integrations under arts. 8 and 11).
- Holding: summary judgment denied on the art. 35(2)(a)–(b) claim because of a genuine issue whether the parties shared the intent to be bound by the Terms and Conditions and merger clause; the fact finder must determine the subjective intent of Gottlieb and Neuber at acceptance; summary judgment granted as to certain damages categories (Supp. 44). If the shared intent existed, art. 6 lets the terms override art. 35 (Supp. 44).
- Slide 19: CISG art. 8(3) contains no parol evidence rule; the U.S. has ratified the CISG; English law treats the rule as more like a rebuttable presumption that the document is complete. Slide 20 asks how the case would come out under New York law and how parties could have precluded parol evidence.

## Unresolved or inconsistent in the sources
1. Whether a merger clause is conclusive: the courts split (C&M 542); the slides say not dispositive (S9).
2. Whether extrinsic evidence may inform the integration question: Gianni and Mitchill say no (four corners), Masterson and R2 § 210 cmt b say yes; S10 lists it as contested.
3. Promissory fraud under a contradicting writing: Lovejoy versus Alling (C&M 545). The excerpt does not say whether Riverisland changed the California rule stated in Alling.
4. Whether a merger clause bars fraud evidence: posed, not answered (C&M 545).
5. The Dentco and Ballard problems and Slide 20's New York-law question are posed without answers.
6. UCC § 2-209(5) has conflicting interpretations (C&M 547); the content of the Posner and Easterbrook positions is not in the excerpt.
7. RLCC text and numbering differ: the selection (2019 Tentative Draft) has § 8, "reasonably intended effect"; C&M 543 quotes Tent. Draft No. 2 (2022) as § 8, "reasonably expected effect"; Slide 11 labels it "§ 9" with "the effect reasonably expected by the consumer," matching the 2024 Official Text numbering. Which two sections the syllabus means by "§§ 8 and 9" depends on which numbering it uses.
8. Slide 19 states a broader CISG applicability rule (art. 1(1)(b)) than TeeVee quotes (art. 1(1)(a)); the sources do not discuss the U.S. position on art. 1(1)(b).
