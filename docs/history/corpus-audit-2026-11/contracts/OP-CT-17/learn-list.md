## 1. Why standard forms exist and why they worry courts (C&M 653–655)

1. Advantages (Morris Cohen, C&M 653): lessons of experience; one judicial interpretation serves for all contracts; less uncertainty, time and trouble; simpler planning and administration; superior drafting skill spread more widely; calculable risks. Easterbrook in Carbajal (7th Cir. 2004): in competition, lower costs of doing business show up as lower prices.
2. Kessler (C&M 653): uniform terms let an enterprise calculate, exclude or control risk, including "juridical risk" that a court or jury will be swayed against a powerful defendant.
3. Three means of imposition (C&M 653–654), often combined: (a) disproportionate economic power lets the enterprise dictate terms; (b) no opportunity to bargain (take-it-or-leave-it); (c) drafter had time and expert advice while the adherent has no real opportunity to scrutinize and often no means to understand, compounded by fine print and obfuscating clauses.
4. The section's framing question (C&M 654): whether a party "can reasonably be held to have seen, understood, and assented to its unfavorable terms, and accordingly, to be bound by them." Follow-ups: does it matter whether the adherent is a firm or a consumer; how courts determine public policy; whether fairness rules raise contracting costs for those they protect.
5. Maine's "status to contract" and Kessler's reverse thesis (C&M 654–655): standard contracts may let "industrial and commercial overlords" impose a "new feudal order."
6. Origin of the term (C&M 655): Saleilles coined "contrat d'adhésion" (1901); Patterson first used "contract of adhesion" in the U.S. (33 Harv. L. Rev. 198, 222 (1919)).
7. Not every standard form is adhesive in the legally relevant sense (C&M 655): Oblix v. Winiecki (7th Cir. 2004, Easterbrook) upheld a take-it-or-leave-it employment agreement and its arbitration clause, reasoning that the salary was consideration for agreeing to non-judicial dispute resolution. The editors note that the fact of exchange "may not fully answer" whether terms were imposed rather than bargained for.

## 2. Llewellyn on covert tools and on assent (C&M 661, 665, 672)

8. Covert tools (C&M 661, closing the O'Callaghan notes): courts that police by "construing" clauses (strict construction, finding inconsistency, rejecting a clause as counter to the transaction's purpose) (i) invite the drafter to redraft, (ii) build no authority on "the minimum decencies" a court will insist on, and (iii) embarrass true construction. "Covert tools are never reliable tools."
9. Blanket assent (C&M 665, Common Law Tradition 370–71): there is no specific assent to boilerplate. What is assented to specifically is the few dickered terms and the broad type of transaction, plus "a blanket assent (not a specific assent) to any not unreasonable or indecent term the seller may have on his form, which do not alter or eviscerate the reasonable meaning of the dickered terms."
10. Abusive Drafting problem (C&M 661): an attorney asked to draft a tenant waiver the attorney **knows** is unenforceable. Alaska Ethics Op. 84-4: if the lawyer determines the contract is illegal, the lawyer must refuse to draft it; the Code does not bar drafting a contract that "might be held voidable or unenforceable," provided the lawyer advises the client of the particular risks. New York State Bar Op. 584 agrees. The distinction taught: illegal (must refuse) versus possibly unenforceable (may draft with risk advice).

## 3. Graham v. Scissor-Tail, Inc. (Cal. 1981) (C&M 662–664)

11. Facts (C&M 662): Bill Graham, an experienced concert promoter, signed four A.F. of M. Form B contracts for a Leon Russell tour; each sent disputes to the union's international executive board. The board ruled for Scissor-Tail; Graham appealed the judgment confirming the award, arguing the contract was adhesive and the arbitration clause unconscionable.
12. Definition (C&M 662, adopting Tobriner in Neal v. State Farm (1961)): a contract of adhesion is "a standardized contract, which, imposed and drafted by the party of superior bargaining strength, relegates to the subscribing party only the opportunity to adhere to the contract or reject it."
13. Adhesion found despite Graham's prominence (C&M 662–663): all significant artists belong to the A.F. of M., and its constitution and bylaws forbid members to sign any form other than the union's; Graham "was required by the realities of his business" to sign the form. An experienced, prominent party can be an adherent.
14. Adhesion is "the beginning and not the end of the analysis" (C&M 663): an adhesion contract "is fully enforceable according to its terms" unless other legal rules make it otherwise.
15. Two judicially imposed limits (C&M 663): (1) a term that does not fall within the reasonable expectations of the weaker or adhering party will not be enforced against him; (2) a term, even if consistent with reasonable expectations, will be denied enforcement if, considered in its context, it is unduly oppressive or "unconscionable." The second is "a principle of equity applicable to all contracts generally"; the first is specific to adhesion contracts.
16. Reasonable expectations applied (C&M 663): the arbitration provision was "wholly consistent with Graham's reasonable expectations" because he had signed literally thousands of A.F. of M. contracts with similar clauses, had promoted 15 or more concerts with Scissor-Tail on such contracts in the preceding three years, had been in prior A.F. of M. proceedings, and had himself threatened to file charges with the A.F. of M. The test looks at this adherent's actual knowledge and experience.
17. Unconscionability (C&M 663–664, editors' bracketed summary only): the court held the provision unconscionable "[b]ecause [it] designates an arbitrator who, by reason of its status and identity, is presumptively biased in favor of one party." The opinion's "minimum levels of integrity" language, its reasoning about the union as a non-neutral decisionmaker, and its remedy are **not in the assigned excerpt**. What the excerpt supports: the clause failed on the second limit (unconscionability), not the first (reasonable expectations), and the defect was the identity of the decisionmaker, not the fact of arbitration.
18. Notes (C&M 664): Kronman argues the attack on adhesion contracts rests on an unstated conception of distributive fairness (painting versus housing stock); the editors ask whether a cruise ticket can be a contract of adhesion. The "Agreeing to Boilerplate" note restates that the arbitration clause did not by itself make the contract unenforceable because Graham was familiar with both the fact and content of the clause.

## 4. Restatement (Second) § 211 (sel. 108–110; C&M 665, 667)

19. § 211(1): a party who signs or otherwise manifests assent to a writing and has reason to believe like writings are regularly used for agreements of the same type adopts the writing as an integrated agreement with respect to the terms included, except as stated in (3).
20. § 211(2): the writing is interpreted wherever reasonable as treating alike all those similarly situated, without regard to their knowledge or understanding of the standard terms. Comment e: courts seek to effectuate the reasonable expectations of the average member of the public who accepts it; this may give sophisticated customers the benefit of a restrictive reading.
21. § 211(3): "Where the other party has reason to believe that the party manifesting such assent would not do so if he knew that the writing contained a particular term, the term is not part of the agreement." The trigger is the **drafter's** reason to believe; the consequence is that **that term** drops out, while the rest of the agreement stands.
22. Comment f: customers are "not bound to unknown terms which are beyond the range of reasonable expectation" (C&M 665 quotes this). The drafter's reason to believe may be shown by prior negotiations or inferred from circumstances; it may be inferred from the fact that the term is bizarre or oppressive, that it eviscerates the non-standard terms explicitly agreed, or that it eliminates the dominant purpose of the transaction. The inference is reinforced if the adherent never had an opportunity to read the term or if it is illegible or hidden. The rule applies §§ 20 and 201 and is closely related to unconscionability (§ 208) and contra proferentem (§ 206). Blank-check example: authorizing a blank amount does not authorize an infinite figure.
23. Comment b: customers ordinarily do not read standard terms and trust the drafter's good faith and the tacit representation that others accept like terms, but "they understand that they are assenting to the terms not read or not understood, subject to such limitations as the law may impose."
24. Comment c: apart from regulation, standard terms imposed by one party are enforced, but they yield to separately negotiated or added terms (§ 203), are construed against the drafter (§ 206), are subject to good faith (§ 205) and unconscionability (§ 208), and may be against public policy.
25. Comment d: a party may assent to a document for other purposes without understanding that it embodies contract terms; baggage checks and parking-lot tickets may look like mere identification tokens, and a party without knowledge or reason to know that the token purports to be a contract is not bound by its printed terms. Insurance policies, steamship tickets, bills of lading and warehouse receipts are usually obviously contractual.
26. C&M's caveat (C&M 665): what is "reasonably expected" gets complicated when categories of terms (liability disclaimers, mandatory arbitration clauses) are so commonly inserted that they have become expected.

## 5. Klar v. H. & M. Parcel Room (N.Y. App. Div. 1946, aff'd 1947) (C&M 665–667)

27. A patron received but did not read a parcel-room claim check that limited liability to $25; the package was misdelivered; the trial court awarded nearly $1,000; affirmed. The coupon was "presumptively intended ... to serve the special purpose of affording a means of identifying the parcel"; in the bailor's mind it "did not arise to the dignity of a contract" limiting liability for the bailee's negligence. To limit liability for negligence, the bailee "must at least show that it has given adequate notice of the special contract and that it has received the assent thereto." Dissenting judges stressed the red-ink word "Contract," brevity and the ten-cent charge.
28. Note (C&M 667) asks how Klar fares under § 211(3) and whether the test is objective or subjective; compare § 211 cmt d (identification tokens). Courts policing by manipulating formation principles is the covert-tools problem again.

## 6. Insurance and the doctrine of reasonable expectations (C&M 667–668)

29. Darner Motor Sales v. Universal Underwriters (Ariz. 1984) applied § 211 to an umbrella policy for the insured; customers are not expected to read such policies and have no real power of negotiation.
30. The insurance "doctrine of reasonable expectations" (Keeton & Widiss): when insurance is sold in circumstances that discourage detailed inquiry, the buyer's reasonable expectations are honored "even though the policy terms do not support them," and "even though a careful examination of the policy provisions indicates that such expectations are contrary to the expressed intention of the insurer." This is stronger than § 211(3), which turns on the drafter's reason to believe.
31. Powers v. Detroit Auto. Inter-Insurance Exchange (Mich. 1986) was overruled by Wilkie v. Auto-Owners (Mich. 2003); Sparks v. St. Paul (N.J. 1985) speaks of courts' "special responsibility" against unrealistic coverage. Jurisdictions split.

## 7. The duty to read and disclosure regulation (C&M 668–672)

32. Starting rule (C&M 669): absent fraud, one who signs is bound whether or not he read or understood it, or can read (Cohen v. Santoianni, Mass. 1953). Washington Mutual Finance v. Bailey (5th Cir. 2004, applying Mississippi law): illiteracy is no defense; a person who cannot read must find someone to read it.
33. Exceptions for forms that do not fairly disclose their terms (C&M 669): DeLancey (N.H. 1873), typography as a triable "fraudulent plot"; McCarthy Well Co. v. St. Peter Creamery (Minn. 1987), unreadable exculpatory clause not enforced as a matter of law, expressly "not a case of unconscionability."
34. Statutes (C&M 669–670): N.Y. Pers. Prop. L. § 402 ("Do not sign this agreement before you read it"); UCC § 2-316(2) conspicuousness defined by UCC § 1-201(b)(10) ("a reasonable person against which it is to operate ought to have noticed"); N.Y. plain-language statute, Gen. Oblig. L. § 5-702 (residential leases and consumer agreements for personal, family or household purposes, excepting amounts over $50,000). Remedy (C&M 670): actual damages, and a violation **does not affect enforceability**. TILA requires early APR disclosure.
35. Sophistication (C&M 671): Avenell (Ohio App. 1974) found a disclaimer conspicuous partly because the buyer was a prominent, sophisticated entity. Fraass Surgical v. United States (Ct. Cl. 1978): a supplier's president who did not read a contract because earlier contracts had a different risk clause got no relief; reformation would have been available only if the government had known of his unawareness, which was not proved.
36. Online (C&M 671–672): FTC ".com Disclosures" guidance ("clear and conspicuous"); Caspi v. Microsoft (N.J. App. Div. 1999 per C&M's citation "732 A.2d 528 (N.J. 1999)"): a forum-selection clause in a scroll-through online membership agreement was enforceable; nothing about its size or placement showed it was proffered unfairly or with a design to conceal.

## 8. Courts, legislatures and agencies (C&M 672–674)

37. Judicial measures: Llewellyn's dickered/boilerplate distinction; Dawson's objection that courts lack the equipment to resolve conflicts over economic power; Schwartz's criticism of unequal bargaining power as smuggling in distributive justice.
38. Statutory measures fall into three loose types (C&M 673): (1) control of the terms of exchange; (2) disclosure to support informed shopping; (3) remedies, premised on consumers' systemic handicaps in enforcing rights. The New York dating-service law exemplifies all three.
39. Administrative measures (C&M 673–674): agencies regulate terms especially in insurance; the FTC acts quasi-judicially against unfair practices that "unreasonably create[] or take[] advantage of an obstacle to the free exercise of consumer decisionmaking" (FTC v. IFC Credit, N.D. Ill. 2008). FTC v. QT (N.D. Ill. 2006): Q-Ray bracelet sales enjoined, refunds and $22.5 million disgorgement.

## 9. EU Directive 93/13/EEC on unfair terms (C&M 674 only)

40. Purpose: protect consumers against abuse of seller or supplier power, in particular one-sided standard contracts and unfair exclusion of essential rights.
41. Art. 3(1) test: a term "which has not been individually negotiated shall be regarded as unfair if, contrary to the requirements of good faith, it causes a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations arising under the contract, to the detriment of the consumer." Elements: (a) not individually negotiated, (b) contrary to good faith, (c) significant imbalance in rights and obligations, (d) to the consumer's detriment. The test asks about the content of the term, not whether the consumer could have read or expected it.
42. The Annex is "a non-exclusive list of 17 types of terms that are deemed potentially unfair" (indicative, not automatically unfair). Examples quoted: inappropriately excluding or limiting legal rights on non-performance; irrevocably binding the consumer to terms he had no real opportunity to become acquainted with before conclusion; unilateral alteration without a valid reason specified in the contract; excluding or hindering legal action, particularly by requiring arbitration not covered by legal provisions.
43. Art. 5: written terms "must always be drafted in plain, intelligible language"; where meaning is in doubt, the interpretation most favourable to the consumer prevails.
44. Member states must ensure unfair terms do not bind consumers and that their continued use is prevented. The Directive is a floor, not a ceiling. National laws vary: some add "black" lists (always unfair) and "gray" lists (presumptively unfair); lists are usually revisable by agencies, making the EU a hybrid administrative/legislative system. In C&M's usage, "black list" and "gray list" describe national lists; the Directive's own Annex is described as a non-exclusive list of potentially unfair terms. The Directive's article on core terms, its consequences article and its full Annex are not in the assigned sources.

## 10. Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute (U.S. 1991) (supp. 76–93)

45. Posture and scope (supp. 76, 78): admiralty case; federal law governs enforceability of the forum clause (*590). The Court did **not** decide notice: respondents "essentially have conceded that they had notice of the forum-selection provision" (*590). Personal-jurisdiction question not reached because the forum clause was dispositive (*589).
46. Bremen frame (supp. 78–79): The Bremen (1972) held forum clauses in freely negotiated commercial agreements "prima facie valid" (sentence partly lost in extraction) and enforceable absent a strong showing of unreasonableness; Carnival "refine[s]" Bremen "to account for the realities of form passage contracts" (*593).
47. Holding and reasons (supp. 79–80): the Court rejected the Ninth Circuit's view that a nonnegotiated forum clause in a form ticket is "never enforceable simply because it is not the subject of bargaining." A reasonable forum clause in such a contract "well may be permissible" because (i) a cruise line has a special interest in limiting the fora where it can be sued, since passengers come from many locales; (ii) an ex ante forum clause dispels confusion and saves litigants and courts the cost of forum motions; (iii) passengers presumably benefit from reduced fares reflecting the savings (*593–94).
48. Inconvenience (supp. 80): the Ninth Circuit's finding that the Shutes were physically and financially incapable of litigating in Florida had no basis in the record (the District Court made no such finding); Florida is not a remote alien forum, and the dispute is not essentially local to Washington. The sentence stating the "heavy burden of proof" holding is lost in the extraction.
49. Limits (supp. 80, *595): forum clauses in form passage contracts "are subject to judicial scrutiny for fundamental fairness." No indication Carnival chose Florida "as a means of discouraging cruise passengers from pursuing legitimate claims"; bad-faith motive "belied" by Carnival's principal place of business in Florida and many cruises departing from and returning to Florida ports. (A sentence on fraud or overreaching is partly lost; only "or overreaching" survives.) Respondents conceded notice "and, therefore, presumably retained the option of rejecting the contract with impunity."
50. Statute (supp. 80–81): 46 U.S.C. App. § 183c (1936) voids ticket provisions that relieve the owner of liability for negligence-caused injury or that "lessen, weaken, or avoid the right of any claimant to a trial by court of competent jurisdiction." The forum clause does not violate it because it allows judicial resolution in a Florida court and does not limit liability. Judgment reversed.
51. Dissent (Stevens, joined by Marshall) (supp. 81–85): the clause is the 8th of 25 fine-print paragraphs; many passengers see it only after purchase, and the no-refund term makes rejection costly; adhesion terms get heightened scrutiny and reasonableness review (citing Williams v. Walker-Thomas and Steven v. Fidelity); the prevailing pre-Bremen rule refused enforcement of forum clauses not freely bargained for; § 183c's "lessen, weaken" language reaches the clause. Fn. 6: the majority does not say whether the result would hold for a clause requiring suit in Panama.
52. What Carnival teaches for this class: the Court enforced a nonnegotiated term in an adhesion contract on reasonableness grounds, conditioned on notice (conceded, not decided), fundamental fairness, absence of bad-faith motive, and absence of fraud or overreaching. It is a federal admiralty decision, not a general state contract-law rule.

## 11. Doe v. Great Expectations (N.Y. Civ. Ct. 2005) (C&M 675–679)

53. Facts (C&M 675 & n.1): two claimants paid $1,000 (six months) and $3,790 (36 months, extended) for a video-dating service; the form stated the company "will provide zero number of social referrals." Roe's contract had handwritten notations ("Marriage Program," "Platinum Shopper") and she was orally assured of twelve introductions. Small claims; consolidated; Judge Lebedeff.
54. Coverage (C&M 675): the Dating Services Law, N.Y. Gen. Bus. Law § 394-c, covers "social referral service[s]" for a fee matching members by computer or other means; following Great Expectations Creative Management v. Attorney-General (1994), it is enough that the service "made available the matching of members or supplied the means for matching."
55. Violations (C&M 676): (a) "massive overcharge": where the service does not assure a specified number of referrals per month it may charge no more than $25 (§ 394-c[3]); (b) the form violated every mandate except the three-day cooling-off notice, including subdivisions 3, 4, 5, 5-a, 6, 8, 8-a, and failed to provide the Dating Service Consumer Bill of Rights (§ 394-c[7][e]).
56. Damages (C&M 676–677): statute allows "actual damages or fifty dollars whichever is greater" (§ 394-c[9][b]). "Actual damages" includes the difference between each contract price and the $25 maximum. The final $25 is refunded because, on credibility findings, each claimant "would not have signed a contract containing terms violating applicable law, had she known of her rights." Result: full refund as "restitutionary damages," face amount with interest from the payment date.
57. Statutory history (C&M 677): the 1992 amendment added "actual damages" and Attorney General enforcement with civil penalties up to $1,000 per violation. As a result such contracts "may no longer simply be set aside as contrary to public policy," as Chassman v. People Resources (1991) did under the earlier statute's silence on penalties. Full refund here rests on the actual-damages remedy, not on voiding the contract for public policy.
58. Reporting (C&M 677–678): the court exercised discretion to report to the Attorney General's Consumer Fraud Unit and NYC Department of Consumer Affairs, weighing public interest, effect on the truth-determining function, and prior litigation against the same or a related defendant.
59. Notes (C&M 678–679): what result if the claimants had received the Bill of Rights; how a civil penalty differs from punitive damages; what result for claimants of average ability who were nonchalant (the last $25 turned on credibility findings about these "intelligent" careful negotiators); Civil Court jurisdiction up to $25,000 and pro se litigants; the statute appears to cover only opposite-sex matching.

## 12. Bridge to unconscionability (C&M 679)

60. UCC § 2-302 cmt 1: lets courts police explicitly rather than by adverse construction, manipulation of offer and acceptance, or public-policy findings; "the prevention of oppression and unfair surprise ... and not of disturbance of allocation of risks because of superior bargaining power." (Section 3 begins here; it is the next class's material.)

## 13. RLCC (2019 Tentative Draft) § 8 (sel. 512–513), with § 2 for comparison

61. § 8: a standard contract term that "contradicts, unreasonably limits, or fails to give the reasonably intended effect to a prior affirmation of fact or promise by the business" is not a final expression of agreement on that subject and does not discharge, under the parol evidence rule, obligations arising from the prior affirmation or promise.
62. Comments: the parol evidence rule's finality protects the business's interest in certainty, but standard terms are not a joint memorialization, so they get less preclusive force (cmt 1); the consumer may show contradiction even when the standard term is unambiguous, so the four-corners test does not apply (cmt 2); merger clauses do not control integration in consumer contracts (cmt 3); § 8 supplements Restatement (Second) § 213 and UCC § 2-202 (cmt 4). § 8 is a corollary of § 6 (deception) and § 7 (affirmations and promises become part of the contract; standard terms that negate them are unenforceable, § 7(c)).
63. Illustrations: snow removal by 10 a.m. versus written "by sunset"; dealer's oral 10-week delivery promise survives a merger clause; health-club agent's assurance that the contract would not take effect on signing.
64. § 2 (2019 draft) is the adoption rule: a standard term is adopted if the consumer manifests assent after (1) reasonable notice of the term and of the intent to include it and (2) a reasonable opportunity to review it; § 2(b) covers terms available only after assent (notice before assent, opportunity to review, and opportunity to terminate without unreasonable cost); § 2(c): a contract exists even if some terms are not adopted.
65. Link to Great Expectations: Roe's oral assurance of twelve introductions against the printed "zero number of social referrals" is the § 7/§ 8 pattern; the case was decided under the statute, not under the RLCC.

## 14. Distinctions this class teaches (exam-relevant)

- Adhesive is not the same as unenforceable (Graham, C&M 663; Oblix, C&M 655).
- Reasonable-expectations exclusion versus unconscionability (Graham's two limits; a term can pass the first and fail the second, as the arbitration clause did).
- § 211(3) turns on the drafter's reason to believe and excludes only the term; the insurance doctrine of reasonable expectations honors the insured's expectations even against clear policy language; Graham's test applied this adherent's own experience.
- Notice and formation (Klar, § 211 cmt d, Caspi) versus content review (EU Art. 3(1), unconscionability).
- Carnival: notice conceded, not decided; enforcement conditioned on fundamental fairness, no bad-faith motive, no fraud or overreaching; federal admiralty law.
- Great Expectations: statutory remedy (actual damages measured against the $25 cap plus the final $25 on credibility findings), not common-law voiding for public policy (Chassman, superseded).
- Plain-language statute violations give damages without affecting enforceability (C&M 670).
- Illegal versus possibly unenforceable terms for the drafting lawyer (C&M 661).
