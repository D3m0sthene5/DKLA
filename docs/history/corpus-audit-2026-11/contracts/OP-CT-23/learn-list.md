## A. Measuring expectation (C&M 954–958; § 347)

1. Expectation damages are a sum of money that, so far as money can, puts the promisee in the position he would have been in had the promise been performed; the promisee then receives "the benefit of the bargain" (C&M 954; § 344(a)).
2. Expectation is measured by the value of the performance to the particular promisee, "quite without regard to that of the hypothetical reasonable promisee." Markets are evidence of that value, either because market value approximates the promisee's interest or because the promisee can buy a substitute (C&M 955; § 347 cmt. b). This is the premise of the whole cost-versus-value debate. Market diminution measures what the property is worth to a typical buyer. The question in every case is what the performance was worth to this owner.
3. Two proxies stand in for the promisee's loss when performance is defective or incomplete. "Difference in value" (also called diminution in value or the diminished-value rule; standard terms) is the market value of what was promised minus the market value of what was delivered. "Cost of completion" (also called cost of performance or cost to remedy; standard terms) is what it would cost to finish or correct the work. Lord Mustill in Ruxley, quoted at C&M 955–956: these "are not two alternative measures of damage, at opposite poles, but only one: namely the loss truly suffered by the promisee."
4. § 347 formula, restated at C&M 957: damages = (a) loss in value + (b) other loss − (c) cost and loss avoided. "Incidental losses" (standard term) are costs of reasonable efforts to avoid loss, such as a broker's fee for arranging a substitute (§ 347 cmt. c). "Consequential losses" (standard term) include injury to person or property caused by the breach. When the seller is injured, the hard part is (c), the seller's costs avoided; when the seller breaches, the hard part is (a), the buyer's loss in value (C&M 957). Footnote d (C&M 956) distinguishes cost avoided (not making the second product) from loss avoided (reselling the first).
5. § 347 cmt. d, Illus. 6: builder repudiated against, price $100,000, $60,000 more needed to finish; damages are $40,000 less progress payments. This is the arithmetic for a contractor stopped by the owner.

## B. Cost to complete versus diminution in value (C&M 968–982; § 348(2))

6. Threshold (C&M 968–969). A builder who seeks the price must first clear substantial performance. Plante: "Something less than perfection is the test," and no mathematical rule of percentage of price or cost of completion decides it.
7. Plante v. Jacobs (Wis. 1960), holding (C&M 969–971). The house was substantially performed despite omissions and some twenty claimed defects, so the builder recovers "the contract price less the damages caused the defendant by the incomplete performance." The opinion states the general measure as the diminished-value rule: the difference between the value of the house as built and as it would have been if built to the plans (Venzke). "The cost of replacement or repair is not the measure of such damage, but is an element to take into consideration in arriving at value under some circumstances." Small separable defects that can be remedied without reconstructing a substantial part of the building or sacrificing much completed work may be valued at the reasonable cost of correction (Stern v. Schlafer). Where separating defects would cause confusion, diminished value may govern all of them (Mohs v. Quarton). Which rule applies to a defect "depends upon the nature and magnitude of the defect."
8. Plante's application. Repair cost stood for the plaster cracks, mud jacking and the patio floor, and the nonstructural patio wall, because those repairs "did not involve an unreasonable economic waste." The court added that it "has not allowed items of such magnitude under the cost-of-repair rule as the trial court did," but it could not say the trial court erred. The misplaced living-room wall "was clearly under the diminished-value rule." Moving it would substantially destroy the wall and damage other parts of the house, and the owners had not demanded relocation during construction. "The rule of diminished value contemplates the wall is not going to be moved." Both sides' experts agreed the misplacement had no effect on market price, so the owners suffered "no legal damage" even though their desire for a particular room size was not satisfied (C&M 971).
9. Notes after Plante (C&M 971–972). Note 1, burden: Advanced v. Wilks put on the builder the burden of proving diminution if it thought repair cost unreasonable; Tessmann let homeowners give opinion evidence of diminution. Note 2, value to whom: Cardozo's "nominal or nothing" for the Cohoes pipe in Jacob & Youngs; the red barrel tile roof replaced by purple tile in Cory Associated Industries v. Jupiter Roofing. Note 3, limits: market diminution fixes a lower limit, because property is usually worth at least its sale price to its owner; cost to remedy fixes an upper limit, because that sum lets the owner complete. Wilks: property held solely for investment may be limited to diminution as a matter of law, while property of special significance to the owner, where repair seems likely, may justify repair cost above diminution.
10. Problems (C&M 972–973). The Deep End (Ruxley): a pool 6'6" deep instead of 7'9", safe for diving, $180,000 to rebuild, no intent to rebuild, $10,000 jury award for "loss of pleasure and amenity"; what if Forsyth would rebuild, or Ruxley saved $50,000? The Thinned Blue Line: 14 guards supplied instead of 20, no protest occurred. For the Birds: defective feeders, repair cost $600, market price fell to $1,200; UCC § 2–714(1). The assigned pages pose these questions without answering them.
11. § 348(2) (Selections). When a breach results in defective or unfinished construction "and the loss in value to the injured party is not proved with sufficient certainty," he may recover based on (a) the diminution in market price or (b) "the reasonable cost of completing performance or of remedying the defects if that cost is not clearly disproportionate to the probable loss in value to him." Both are fallbacks. The primary measure remains § 347(a), loss in value to the injured party.
12. § 348 cmt. c. For unfinished work, the owner usually proves cost to complete more easily, and because that cost is usually less than his loss in value, avoidability (§ 350) limits him to it. For defective work, the owner can usually recover cost to remedy even if that gives "a small windfall," because over-compensation is better than under-compensation. If much of the cost goes to undoing what was improperly done, so that cost is "clearly disproportionate to the probable loss in value," the award is diminution in market price, "the least possible loss in value." The comment calls "economic waste" "a misleading expression" because an owner awarded excessive damages will not usually spend them on the repair. Illus. 3: $30,000 to make cracked foundations safe, adding $20,000 of market value, is recoverable. Illus. 4: Reading pipe replaced with equally good Cohoes pipe, $20,000 to replace, no effect on market price, no proof of special value: nominal damages only.
13. "Economic waste" (standard term, with two meanings in this reading). The first Restatement § 346 cmt. b, as quoted in Groves (C&M 977) and Peevyhouse (C&M 980), uses it for tearing down and rebuilding a completed structure. Peevyhouse also adopts McCormick's and Cardozo's broader formulation: cost "grossly and unfairly out of proportion to the good to be attained," which it calls "relative economic benefit." § 348 cmt. c replaces both with "clearly disproportionate to the probable loss in value." Note 2 (C&M 982) asks in what sense there is economic waste if Kent would never actually have replaced the pipe.

## C. Groves v. John Wunder Co. (Minn. 1939) (C&M 973–979)

14. Facts. Wunder paid $105,000 for a seven-year lease of 24 acres, Groves's screening plant and the right to remove sand and gravel, and promised to leave the land at a uniform grade, using the overburden to do so. It took "the richest and best of the gravel" and left the land "broken, rugged and uneven." Completing the grading on the construction adopted below meant moving 288,495 cubic yards at a cost above $60,000; the land properly graded would have been worth only $12,160. The trial court gave judgment for a little over $15,000 on a difference-in-value measure.
15. Majority (Stone, J.), first ground, willfulness. "Defendant's breach of contract was wilful. There was nothing of good faith about it." A contractor who "wilfully and fraudulently varies" from a construction contract cannot claim the equitable doctrine of substantial performance (Elliott v. Caldwell). Jacob & Youngs was a good-faith substantial-performance case, "distinctly the opposite" of this one; "the willful transgressor must accept the penalty of his transgression."
16. Majority, second ground, the object of a construction contract. The law aims to give the promisee "what he was promised." Minnesota cases award the cost of completing grading (Carli v. Seymour). "Even in case of substantial performance in good faith, the resulting defects being remediable," cost of remedying is the "proper" measure (Snider v. Peters Home Building). The value of the land "is no proper part of any measure of damages for wilful breach of a building contract." Tort damages to land measure lost market value; a contract to improve land promises a physical accomplishment, so "its only appropriate measure is the cost of performance." An owner may build "a monument to his caprice or folly" (Chamberlain v. Parker). No unconscionable enrichment results from giving a party only what the other promised, especially where the delinquent "has had full payment."
17. Majority, third ground, economic waste read narrowly. The waste the first Restatement guards against "is only that which would come from wrecking a physical structure, completed, or nearly so." None was involved, so § 346's rule applied: cost of remedying the defect is the award (C&M 977).
18. Disposition. Reversed for a new trial, the first question being what the grading promise actually required (whether overburden had to be removed from the premises). The court fixed no award (C&M 977–978). Peevyhouse later described Groves as decided by "a plurality rather than a majority of the members of the court" (C&M 980).
19. Dissent (Olson, J., joined by Holt, J.), as summarized by the editors (C&M 978): the diminished-value rule should apply absent evidence that the finished product was to satisfy the promisee's personal taste, and the wilfulness of the breach should not affect the measure of damages.
20. Notes (C&M 978–979). Note 1 asks why the burden was so large and the benefit so slight, and which explanation (underestimated cost, overestimated benefit, rising cost, falling land values in the Depression, failure to restore as work progressed, value to Groves above market) would justify the decision. Posner: enforcement gave Groves a windfall cushion against the Depression. The case settled for $55,000; part of the land was graded for $6,000 in 1951 and sold for $45,000 in 1953. Note 2: Droher & Sons v. Toushin (Minn. 1957) distinguished Groves as resting in part on wilful, bad-faith breach; Holmes in Globe Refining said the measure of damages is generally the same "whatever the cause of the breach"; is the excess over $47,000 a penalty for wilfulness? Problem, Advising Wunder: would a considered refusal to spend $60,000 for a benefit under $13,000 be "wilful"?

## D. Peevyhouse v. Garland Coal & Mining Co. (Okla. 1962/1963) (C&M 979–982; Selections 692–699)

21. Facts (C&M 979). Coal strip-mining lease with specific restorative and remedial covenants. The omitted work would cost about $29,000; done, it would have increased the farm's market value by only $300. The owners sued for $25,000; the jury gave $5,000; both appealed. The lease's restoration clauses (Selections 698) are 7b (fill pits on property lines so fences can be placed and access had), 7c (smooth the tops of spoil banks), 7d (leave the creek crossing usable), 7e (cattle guard if an access road is cut through the south fence) and 7f (no shale or dirt on the high wall). Clause 8 lets the lessee cancel on 30 days' notice when mining becomes unprofitable in its sole judgment.
22. Majority (Jackson, J.). Groves's analogy to building or grading contracts is not "strictly applicable": the primary purpose of the lease was "the economical recovery and marketing of coal," and the remedial provisions "were incidental to the main object involved." The court contrasts the first Restatement's economic-waste test (destruction of a substantially completed structure, which was not involved) with McCormick's and Jacob & Youngs's "relative economic benefit" test (C&M 980).
23. Holding, stated by the court (C&M 980–981). Under a coal-mining lease in which the lessee agrees to do restorative and remedial work at the end of the lease, when the contract is otherwise fully performed by both parties, the measure is "ordinarily the reasonable cost of performance of the work." But "where the contract provision breached was merely incidental to the main purpose in view, and where the economic benefit which would result to lessor by full performance of the work is grossly disproportionate to the cost of performance," damages are limited to the diminution in value. Both conditions are required. Judgment reduced to $300 and affirmed (the casebook's bracket says 4–3).
24. Dissent (Irwin, J.) (C&M 981). Garland admitted the owners insisted on the restoration provisions and would not have leased without them. The owners were entitled to specific performance, so the measure should be cost of performance; any other measure gives Garland the benefits without the obligation and makes a new contract for the parties.
25. Notes and problem (C&M 981–982). Are Groves and Peevyhouse distinguishable? Was the Peevyhouses' loss in value perhaps $5,000? Did Garland get a windfall? What result under § 348(2)? Schneberger v. Apache (Okla. 1994) adhered to Peevyhouse, noting the parties could have specified the measure and that nothing would require a plaintiff to spend a cost-of-remediation award on the land. Threatening Inefficient Performance: if a court would award $30,000 for work worth $8,000 to the owners, Garland can offer to split the $22,000 difference or threaten to perform; Ayres and Madison note that threatened performance saves litigation costs.

## E. The three interests and losing contracts (C&M 988–992; §§ 344, 349, 370, 371, 373)

26. § 344. Expectation interest: being put in as good a position as if the contract had been performed. Reliance interest: being reimbursed for loss caused by reliance, so as to be put in as good a position as if the contract had not been made. Restitution interest: having restored any benefit conferred on the other party.
27. A "losing contract" (casebook heading, C&M 988; descriptive term) is one on which the injured party would have lost money had both sides performed. On a losing contract expectation damages may be zero.
28. Reliance on a losing contract. L. Albert & Son v. Armstrong Rubber (2d Cir. 1949) (L. Hand, J.) (C&M 988): the promisee may recover his outlay in preparation for performance, "subject to the privilege of the promisor to reduce it by as much as he can show that the promisee would have lost, if the contract had been performed." The breaching party carries the burden because "by his wrong" it made the issue relevant. § 349 adopts the same rule: reliance expenditures "less any loss that the party in breach can prove with reasonable certainty the injured party would have suffered had the contract been performed." So reliance damages are capped by expectation, with the burden of proving the loss on the breaching party. The lottery example (C&M 988): a purchaser who never got his ticket gets his money back "whether or not he eventually would have won."
29. Restitution of a prepayment. UCC § 2–718(2) gives some restitution to a prepaying buyer; a pre-delivering seller is pointed to § 2–709 (C&M 989). Flour Markets problem (C&M 989): Buyer paid $500,000 on a $1,400,000 flour contract; Seller failed to deliver; the market fell to $1,100,000. Is Buyer entitled to restitution of $500,000 although performance would have cost it $300,000? (Bush v. Canfield cited, not excerpted.) Under § 373(1), restitution is for "any benefit" conferred by part performance, with no deduction for the injured party's expected loss.
30. United States v. Algernon Blair, Inc. (4th Cir. 1973) (C&M 989–991). Coastal, a steel-erection subcontractor, supplied labor and its own cranes; Blair refused to pay crane rental; after about 28 percent of the work Coastal stopped; the trial court found Blair's refusal a material breach justifying termination. About $37,000 was due under the contract less payments made, but Coastal would have lost more than $37,000 had it finished, so the district court denied recovery. Holding: "a subcontractor, who justifiably ceases work under a contract because of the prime contractor's breach," may "recover in quantum meruit the value of labor and equipment already furnished pursuant to the contract irrespective of whether he would have been entitled to recover in a suit on the contract." Quantum meruit (Latin, "as much as he deserved"; standard term) is recovery of the reasonable value of services. The measure is "the reasonable value of the performance," "undiminished by any loss which would have been incurred by complete performance." The contract price may be evidence of reasonable value but "does not measure the value of the performance or limit recovery." Value is "the amount for which such services could have been purchased from one in the plaintiff's position at the time and place the services were rendered." Payments already made are deducted; the case was remanded for valuation. The court quotes Fuller and Perdue: restitution, combining unjust impoverishment with unjust gain, "presents twice as strong a claim to judicial intervention as the reliance interest."
31. § 371. Restitution may be measured, as justice requires, by (a) the reasonable value to the other party of what he received, in terms of what it would have cost him to obtain it from a person in the claimant's position, or (b) the extent to which the other party's property was increased in value or his interests advanced. Algernon Blair uses (a). Note 1 (C&M 991) asks whether that is a proper measure of Blair's benefit, how it differs from reliance, and what if the Peevyhouses had sought restitution. Patterson called the idea that an owner is enriched "by each stroke of the hammer" "Pickwickian." A restitution claimant must account for benefits it received (Coastal's progress payments; EarthInfo's apportionment of profits).
32. Contract price as a ceiling, Note 2 (C&M 992). For "an injured party who has fully performed and then been refused payment," recovery cannot exceed the contract price. Whether a party who has only partly performed may recover reasonable value above the price is disputed: Johnson v. Bovee (ceiling); Southern Painting (no ceiling); Restatement § 373 (not a ceiling, per the casebook); Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment § 38(2)(b) (ceiling). John T. Brady & Co. v. City of Stamford: with work 99 percent complete, restitution was precluded by the rule that after full performance the measure of the benefit is the value the parties assigned in their contract.
33. § 373. (1) On a breach by nonperformance giving rise to a claim for damages for total breach, or on a repudiation, the injured party may have restitution for any benefit conferred by part performance or reliance. (2) "The injured party has no right to restitution if he has performed all of his duties under the contract and no performance by the other party remains due other than payment of a definite sum of money for that performance." A full performer owed only money sues for the price. If Coastal had finished all its work and Blair owed only the remaining price, Coastal could not escape the price by claiming a higher reasonable value. § 370 limits restitution to benefits conferred by part performance or reliance; § 374 gives a party in breach restitution in excess of the loss its breach caused.
34. Security Stove problem (C&M 992). Late delivery of the key part of a trade-show exhibit; Security Stove sues for shipping, travel, hotel, salaries and booth rental, which are reliance expenses, because its profits from exhibiting could not be proved. The excerpt asks the question without giving the decision.
