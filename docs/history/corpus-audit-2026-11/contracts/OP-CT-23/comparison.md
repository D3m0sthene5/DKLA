**E2. Flowchart K-31 node c, detail.** Verdict: CHANGES RESULT (Plante's wall) and MISSING CONDITION.
Current text: "Use reasonable correction cost where supported. Weigh waste, proportionality and whether the breached undertaking was central. Groves favored restoration cost. Peevyhouse used $300 diminution for restoration it treated as incidental. Plante selected repair costs by defect."
Counterexample: Plante's wall. The cost to move it ($4,000) is proved, so "use reasonable correction cost where supported" yields a $4,000 deduction. The court allowed nothing, because the wall fell "clearly under the diminished-value rule" and caused no market loss (C&M 971). "Plante selected repair costs by defect" omits the ruling that made the case famous. The node also gives no test for choosing a measure: it omits § 348(2)(b)'s condition that cost not be "clearly disproportionate to the probable loss in value," Peevyhouse's requirement that both conditions hold, and Groves's reliance on wilfulness and on the destruction-of-a-structure meaning of waste.
Pinpoint: C&M 970–971 (Plante), 975–977 (Groves), 980–981 (Peevyhouse); § 348(2) and cmt. c.
Minimal replacement: "Start from the loss in value to the owner. If that is not proved, § 348(2) allows the drop in market price or the reasonable cost to complete or repair, but cost only if not clearly disproportionate to the probable loss in value to the owner. Plante used repair cost for small defects fixable without tearing out finished work and diminution, here zero, for the misplaced wall. Groves (Minnesota) gave completion cost for a wilful failure to grade. Peevyhouse (Oklahoma) gave $300 because restoration was incidental and its benefit grossly disproportionate to its cost."
Optional structural fix, which would also repair the route tests in (e): split node c into two edges, "Correction would not destroy finished work and its cost is not clearly disproportionate to the owner's loss in value" → cost of repair or completion, and "Correction would destroy finished work or its cost is clearly disproportionate" → diminution, unless the owner proves greater value to him. Note Groves and Peevyhouse on each edge as jurisdiction-specific variations.

**E3. Plante, Takeaway (tools/r19/data/takeaways.json).** Verdict: OVERSTATED.
Current text: "A builder who substantially completed a house gets the price minus the cost of fixing defects."
Counterexample: the opinion states the general measure as the diminished-value rule. "The cost of replacement or repair is not the measure of such damage, but is an element to take into consideration in arriving at value under some circumstances" (C&M 970). Repair cost applied only to small, separable defects that could be remedied without substantial reconstruction, and the court said it "has not allowed items of such magnitude under the cost-of-repair rule as the trial court did" (C&M 971). A student using the first sentence would deduct repair cost for any large defect whose correction does not demolish finished work. The second sentence fixes only the wall.
Pinpoint: C&M 970–971.
Minimal replacement (51 words, two sentences): "A builder who substantially completed a house gets the price minus the owners' damages: repair cost for small defects fixable without tearing out finished work, and otherwise the lost market value. The misplaced wall cost nothing in market value and moving it would be economic waste, so it earned no deduction."

**E4. Groves, Effective brief CT-NEXT-CM-0147: rule (repeated verbatim in Chronology 'course-chronology-0' step 109).** Verdict: MISSING CONDITION.
Current text: "When a contractor deliberately fails to perform a construction obligation, the owner's damages are the reasonable cost of performing the promised work"
Counterexample: change Wunder's breach to a good-faith misreading of the grade. A student applying the rule as written concludes that Groves does not apply and reaches diminution. The majority's second and third grounds do not depend on wilfulness. It applied Snider: "Even in case of substantial performance in good faith, the resulting defects being remediable," cost of remedying is "the 'proper' measure." It also confined economic waste to "wrecking a physical structure, completed, or nearly so" (C&M 976–977). Later, Droher distinguished Groves as resting "in part" on wilful, bad-faith breach (C&M 978–979). So the innocent-breach result in Minnesota is contested, not settled for diminution. The brief's Reasoning ("Stone characterized the breach as willful ... and distinguished cases of good-faith substantial performance") does not cure this, because it omits Snider.
Pinpoint: C&M 975–977, 978–979.
Minimal replacement (48 words): "Minnesota measures damages for a contractor's wilful failure to improve land as promised by the reasonable cost of the work, even above the improved land's value; economic waste concerns only wrecking a completed structure. Under cited Minnesota precedent, cost also governs remediable defects even after good-faith substantial performance."

**E5. Groves, Cold Call plain-English sentences, at 2.0.** Verdict: OVERSTATED.
Current text: "Stone called the breach \"willful\" (deliberate) and in \"bad faith\" (dishonest)."
Counterexample: the court found deliberate breach and said "There was nothing of good faith about it" (C&M 975). It made no finding of deceit. The editors frame the question as what "wilful" means and whether a considered refusal to spend $60,000 for a benefit under $13,000 would be wilful (C&M 978–979, Advising Wunder). Glossing bad faith as "dishonest" suggests that Groves requires deception, so a student would treat an open, calculated refusal to perform as outside Groves.
Minimal replacement: "Stone called the breach \"willful\" (deliberate) and said there was \"nothing of good faith about it,\" because Wunder knowingly left the promised work undone."

**E6. Algernon Blair, Map entry: sections.Material Facts.** Verdict: MISSTATES HOLDING (a misstated fact that the holding turns on).

## (c) Teaching gaps

**G1. § 348(2) and comment c.** Home: completion-damages concept, notes. Also link rules entry restatement-348 (see G17). Draft: "Restatement § 348(2) applies when the owner cannot prove his own loss in value with sufficient certainty. He may then recover the drop in market price or the reasonable cost to complete or repair, but cost only if it is not clearly disproportionate to the probable loss in value to him. Comment c calls 'economic waste' misleading, because an owner overpaid will rarely rebuild. Illustration 3 allows $30,000 to fix cracked foundations that add $20,000 of market value; Illustration 4 allows only nominal damages for equally good Cohoes pipe."

**G2. Groves and Peevyhouse under § 348(2) (C&M 981).** Home: reading-notes-23, notes (Peevyhouse paragraph). Draft, as an application rather than a holding: "The casebook asks what § 348(2) would do here. A cost above $60,000 for land worth $12,160 when graded, or $29,000 for a $300 increase, looks clearly disproportionate unless the owner proves the work was worth more to him than its market effect. The Groves dissent said no such personal purpose was shown, and the editors ask whether the Peevyhouses' loss might have been the jury's $5,000."

**G3. Whose value: floor, ceiling and the particular owner (C&M 955, 971–972; § 347 cmt. b).** Home: completion-damages concept, notes. Draft: "Expectation measures the performance's value to this owner, not to a typical buyer. Market diminution sets a floor, because property is worth at least its sale price to its owner, and the cost to remedy sets a ceiling, because that sum buys the promised result. Courts may limit investment property to diminution and allow repair cost where the property has special significance and repair is likely (Advanced v. Wilks)."

**G4. Wilfulness (C&M 975, 978–979).** Home: Groves map entry, notes. Draft: "Whether wilfulness should change the measure is the case's open question. A later Minnesota decision, Droher, distinguished Groves as resting partly on wilful, bad-faith breach. Holmes said the measure is generally the same whatever the cause of breach, and the editors ask whether the excess over $47,000 is a penalty and whether a considered refusal to spend $60,000 for under $13,000 of benefit is wilful."

**G5. Groves explanation note (C&M 978).** Home: Groves map entry, notes (append to the settlement sentence). **G6. Plante's reservation about magnitude (C&M 971).** Home: Plante, Effective brief CT-NEXT-CM-0146: Holding, appended. Draft: "The court noted that it had not itself allowed repair-cost items of such magnitude, but it could not say the trial court erred."

**G8. Schneberger's reasons (C&M 981–982).** Home: Peevyhouse map entry, notes (for the redesign team, since Peevyhouse was primary-reviewed). **G9. Reliance on a losing contract (C&M 988, 992; § 349).** Home: reliance-interest concept, notes; link rules entry restatement-349. **G10. Restitution of a prepayment (C&M 988–989; § 373(1); UCC § 2–718(2)).** Home: restitution-interest concept, notes. Draft: "A buyer who prepaid $500,000 of a $1,400,000 price and received no flour asks for its money back although the market fell to $1,100,000 (Flour Markets). The excerpt's lottery example makes the same point: the buyer of an undelivered ticket gets his money back whether or not he would have won."

**G11. § 373(2) and the contract-price ceiling (C&M 992).** Home: Algernon Blair map entry, notes (replace "separately discuss the limitation after full performance" with the rule), and K-31 node e (E10). Bovee and Restatement (Third) § 38(2)(b) say yes, Southern Painting and, per the casebook, § 373 say no, and Brady treated work 99 percent complete as full performance."

**G12. § 371 and the critique of reasonable-value restitution (C&M 991).** Home: Algernon Blair map entry, notes; link rules entry restatement-371 to algernon-blair. Note 1 asks whether that measures Blair's benefit at all, since Patterson called the idea that an owner is enriched by each hammer stroke 'Pickwickian', and a claimant must credit what it received, such as Coastal's progress payments."

**G13. § 347's formula (C&M 956–957).** Home: expectation-interest concept, notes; link rules entry restatement-347. Draft: "Section 347 adds the loss in value of the other side's performance to any other loss, incidental or consequential, and subtracts the cost and loss the injured party avoided. A builder repudiated against before finishing recovers the unpaid price less the cost it saved by stopping (§ 347 Illus. 6)."

**G14. Substitute or lost volume (C&M 968).** Home: Flowchart K-31 node d2, detail, or reading-notes-23.
