954 (tail of specific-performance notes)
1. Civil-law preference for specific performance is mostly tone; Posner (Bodum) attributes it to canon-law vs. law-merchant origins; differences in outcome small (Lando & Rose).
2. CISG art. 46(1) lets a buyer require performance, but art. 28 lets a court refuse unless it would grant SP under its own law for non-CISG sales (Magellan: in U.S., look to UCC 2-716(1)); forum-dependence problem.
3. UNIDROIT 7.2.2: performance of a non-money obligation may be required unless the obligee can reasonably obtain performance from another source; question of what Van Wagner result would be.

954-958 Measuring expectation
4. Expectation = money that puts promisee where performance would have; contrasted with restitution (benefit conferred) and reliance (amount expended); counterfactual difficulty.
5. Easy cases: substitute transaction at extra cost (§ 347 cmt a); profit-driven exchanges in thick markets.
6. Defective vs. incomplete performance; cover/resale fix damages retrospectively by the injured party's own act.
7. Market-value difference approximates expectation, but expectation is that of the particular promisee (§ 347 cmt b), not a hypothetical reasonable one; markets only help identify it.
8. Difference-in-value vs. cost-of-completion are proxies for one measure, "the loss truly suffered" (Lord Mustill, Ruxley).
9. § 347 formula: (a) loss in value + (b) other loss - (c) cost and loss avoided; definitions of each; serious breach gives the choice to stop and treat as terminated, which brings in (c).
10. Footnote d: cost avoided (not making the second product) vs. loss avoided (reselling the first).
11. Seller injured: hard part is (c); buyer injured: hard part is (a).
12. Incidental loss (reasonable mitigation costs, brokerage fee, § 347 cmt c; UCC 2-710 seller's incidental) vs. consequential (harm to person/property; more likely suffered by buyer).
13. Lost profits; fixed vs. variable costs; short run vs. long run; how fixed costs/overhead fit into (c) (Vitex intro and facts only on 958).

968 Alternative product rule and Star Paving problem
14. Overstreet v. Norden (6th Cir.): no expectation recovery if no alternative product would have achieved the purpose (bald-man analogy; causation); dissent called it the "alternative product" rule; contrast Chatlos v. NCR (3d Cir.): buyer recovers value of system as warranted even if far above price (Rolls-Royce/Ford argument rejected). Minority rule ("one court").
15. Star Paving v. Drennan problem: $15,000 price, $5,000 cost to complete, substitute contract with School District at $7,000. Net profit on substitute ($2,000) is subtracted: $8,000 (Drennan right; Reynolds v. Maples-Reeves; § 347 cmt d/e); no lost volume because same job; different terms (e.g., liability) may change whether the second contract is a true substitute or require adjustment.

968-971 Cost of completion vs. diminished value; Plante v. Jacobs
16. Substantial performance as threshold; "something less than perfection"; no mathematical percentage rule.
17. Two measures usually track; diverge; promisor argues lower.
18. Plante facts: $26,765 price, $20,000 paid, builder sues on lien; $1,601.95 conceded omissions allowed; wall misplaced > 1 ft; ~$4,000 to fix; no market effect; owners claim 25-30% cost to strictly comply; owners argue $10,000, builder $650.
19. Substantial performance -> contract price less damages for incomplete performance.
20. Venzke diminished-value rule; repair cost an element of value; Stern v. Schlafer (many small remediable items -> cost of correction); Mohs v. Quarton (if separating defects causes confusion, diminished value for all).
21. Defect-by-defect choice by nature and magnitude: repair cost allowed for plaster cracks, mud-jacking patio, nonstructural patio wall (no unreasonable economic waste); wall -> diminished value: no request during construction, tearing down = substantial destruction; zero market effect = no legal damage although personal desire unmet.
22. Note 1 burden: Advanced v. Wilks (builder must show diminution if it says repair unreasonable); Tessmann (owner bears burden of reasonable basis but homeowner can opine on value, may rely on repair cost; attack on cross).
23. Note 2 value to whom: J&Y "nominal or nothing"; Grossman purple-tile hypothetical (Cory v. Jupiter Roofing): market damages may not offset aesthetic offense.
24. Note 3: diminution is a floor (owner can sell), repair cost a ceiling (enables completion); Wilks: owner not necessarily limited to diminution; repair award may overcompensate (pocket and sell); investment property -> as a matter of law diminution; special significance and likely repair -> repair cost even if > diminution.
25. Problem Deep End (Ruxley): $100,000 pool, 7'9" vs 6'6", 5'6" safe; $180,000 rebuild; no intent to rebuild; $10,000 loss of amenity; variants: intent to rebuild; builder saved $50,000 (disgorgement/restitution question).
26. Problem Thinned Blue Line: 14 of 20 guards, $18,000 prepaid, no protest; loss in value may be zero/nominal, but partial failure of a paid-for performance raises restitution/price-reduction (6/20 of price) and cost-saved questions.
27. 973-978 Groves v. John Wunder
28. Facts: 24 acres; 7-year lease; $105,000 paid in advance; deliberate breach (took best gravel, left ground broken); 288,495 cu. yd.; cost > $60,000; value as graded $12,160; judgment for value.
29. Holding/reasoning: wilful variance bars substantial-performance doctrine (Elliott v. Caldwell; J&Y "willful transgressor"); law gives what was promised; even good-faith remediable defects -> cost of remedy (Snider); land value no part of measure for wilful breach of building contract; tort/trespass vs. contract distinction ("hypothetical peak of accomplishment"); Chamberlain v. Parker "monument to his caprice"; contractor-side analogy; future value of trackage property; genealogy of error (Seely tort -> Bigham contract); Morgan v. 30. Dissent (Olson): diminished value absent proof of personal taste; wilfulness irrelevant.
31. Note 1: candidate explanations (underestimate, overestimate, cost rise, value fall, progressive restoration, subjective value above market); Posner: equal and opposite windfall (Depression cushion); settlement $55,000; 1951 partial grading $6,000; 1953 sale $45,000.

### (a) Teaching points or Atlas errors the worker missed

Ordered by effect on an exam answer.

A1. Peevyhouse takeaway (tools/r19/data/takeaways.json) drops the second condition. ATLAS ERROR, changes result.
Current text: "Oklahoma's court awarded the small drop in the land's value rather than the much larger restoration cost, because restoration was incidental to the lease's main purpose."
Source: the holding requires both that the provision be "merely incidental" and that the benefit be "grossly disproportionate to the cost"; otherwise cost of performance is "ordinarily" the measure (C&M 980-981). A student given an incidental restoration term whose cost is close to its value would cut damages to diminution. The worker skipped Peevyhouse surfaces as "primary-reviewed," and its T5 checked only the map Holding and Cold Call; prior-corrections.json contains no change to this takeaway, so nothing in the record covers it. Replacement: "...because restoration was incidental to the lease's main purpose and its $29,000 cost was grossly disproportionate to the $300 it would add."

A2. Alternative-product rule (C&M 967-968, Note 4 continued on assigned 968). TEACHING GAP, absent from both learn-list and comparison.
Overstreet v. Norden (6th Cir.) denied expectation recovery where no effective vaccine existed anywhere (bald-man analogy, framed as causation); Chatlos v. NCR (3d Cir.) let the buyer recover the value of the system as warranted even though it far exceeded the price (Rolls-Royce/Ford argument rejected). The casebook calls Overstreet a one-court rule. Exam use: a seller of a warranted product that could never have worked will argue Overstreet; the buyer answers with Chatlos and § 347 cmt. b (value to the injured party).

A3. Restatement § 348(1) and § 348(3) (Selections). TEACHING GAP; the worker listed § 348 Illus. 1-5 as read but its learn-list states only subsection (2).
§ 348(1): delay in use of property, unprovable profits -> fair rental value or interest (Illus. 1, drive-in). § 348(3): breach of a promise conditioned on a fortuitous event -> value of the conditional right ("chance of winning"; Illus. 5, one-in-four horse, $25,000), limited to aleatory promises; a contractor who was repudiated must still prove it could and would have performed (cmt. d). Exam use: the casebook's lottery example (C&M 988) gives the buyer his money back in restitution; § 348(3) is the separate expectation route that values the lost chance. A student who conflates them misstates both.

A4. Paradox problem (C&M 988). TEACHING GAP. Reliance recovery on a contract nobody appears to have relied on is justified by an assumed forgone alternative contract that need not be proved, while lost-volume reasoning assumes the injured party would have made both contracts. This is the assigned bridge from the lost-volume material (C&M 982) to "Losing Contracts."

A5. Peevyhouse map entry, sections.Reasoning, last sentence. ATLAS IMPRECISION, does not change a result.
Current text: "...the narrower destructive-repair reasoning in Plante and Jacob & Youngs."
Source: Peevyhouse groups Jacob & Youngs with McCormick as the "relative economic benefit" (disproportion) test and contrasts both with the first Restatement's destruction-of-a-structure test (C&M 980). Jacob & Youngs's facts are used as a destruction example in Note 2 (C&M 982), but its stated rule, "grossly and unfairly out of proportion to the good to be attained," is the broader test the Peevyhouse majority adopted. Suggested fix: "...the destructive-repair reasoning in Plante and the first Restatement, and the disproportion test Peevyhouse took from Jacob & Youngs."

### (b) Worker findings that are wrong or overstated

B1. E4 (Groves brief rule "When a contractor deliberately fails...") is overstated as MISSING CONDITION. The court itself confined the land-value exclusion to "wilful breach of a building contract" (C&M 975-976), so the brief's condition tracks the opinion, and a conditional rule does not state its converse. The Snider sentence the worker relies on is a cited precedent, and Droher (C&M 978-979) shows Minnesota itself read Groves as resting partly on wilfulness. The proposed added sentence is accurate and useful, but the verdict should be "incomplete," not a misleading condition.

B2. E3 (Plante takeaway) is valid in citing "The cost of replacement or repair is not the measure" (C&M 970), but the worker rates the Plante brief rule and chronology step 158 "Covered" although they state the same default ("measured by cost of repair for defects that can reasonably be corrected"). Either extend E3 to those two texts or soften E3. The court's operative test in application was "unreasonable economic waste" (C&M 971), and it affirmed repair cost for every remaining defect, so the takeaway reports the disposition correctly; only its rule framing is too broad.

B3. G10 draft attributes the no-deduction point to § 373(1) ("is not reduced by the injured party's expected loss"). The § 373 black letter in the Selections says nothing about deduction, and the worker's own source note 3 says no § 373 comments are printed. G10 also says the lottery example "makes the same point." The casebook poses the lottery example as a question ("Does this mean...?") and offers L. Albert's reduce-by-proven-loss rule "as a possible solution" (C&M 988). Attribute the restitution result to Algernon Blair (C&M 991) and Bush v. Canfield (cited in the Flour Markets problem, C&M 989), and present the lottery line as the framing question.

B4. Learn-list § E heading and comparison sources list §§ 370, 371 and 374 among the Selections read; these are not in the class's assigned Selections (§§ 344, 347, 348, 349, 373). Using them is fine, but G12's home for § 371 should say it comes from the casebook's cross-reference (Note 1, C&M 991), not from this class's assigned Selections.

No worker finding is wrong on the sources. E1, E2, E6, E7, E10 and E11 check out against C&M 970-971, 975-981, 989-992 and § 373(2).
