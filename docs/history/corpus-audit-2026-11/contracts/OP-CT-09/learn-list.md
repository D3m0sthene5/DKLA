## 1. Section 2-207: the frame (C&M 284; UCC § 2-207 and Comments 1–7)

- Section 2-207(1) lets a "definite and seasonable expression of acceptance or a written confirmation" operate as an acceptance even though it states additional or different terms, unless acceptance is "expressly made conditional on assent to the additional or different terms". Under the common-law mirror-image rule (an acceptance must match the offer exactly) such a reply was a counteroffer, and the "last shot" (the final form sent before performance) governed if the other side performed (C&M 284).
- Once (1) forms a contract, the question is which terms it contains. Section 2-207(2), first sentence: additional terms are "proposals for addition to the contract". That sentence is the only one that applies if either party is not a merchant (C&M 284). How such a proposal is accepted is left to ordinary assent; Comment 3 says a materially altering term is not included "unless expressly agreed to by the other party".
- Second sentence, "between merchants" only (§ 2-104(3): both parties are "chargeable with the knowledge or skill of merchants"): additional terms become part of the contract unless (a) the offer expressly limits acceptance to its terms, (b) they materially alter it, or (c) notification of objection has been given or is given within a reasonable time after notice of them.
- Comment 4: a material alteration is a term that would "result in surprise or hardship if incorporated without express awareness by the other party" (examples: warranty disclaimers, 90% or 100% delivery guarantees where trade allows leeway, seller's power to cancel on any missed invoice, complaint periods materially shorter than customary). Comment 5 lists terms that are not material (reasonable force-majeure enlargements, reasonable complaint periods within customary limits, interest on overdue invoices within trade practice, reasonable remedy limits).
- Comment 6: where both parties' confirmations contain conflicting clauses, each party is assumed to object to the other's clause, so the conflicting terms drop out and the contract consists of terms originally expressly agreed, terms on which the confirmations agree, and Code-supplied terms.
- Section 2-207(3): if the writings do not form a contract (for example, an expressly conditional acceptance), conduct by both parties recognizing a contract establishes one; its terms are those on which the writings agree plus supplementary Code terms. Comment 7: when goods are shipped, accepted and paid for, there is no need to identify which document was the offer.

## 2. Material alteration and the burden: Bayway (C&M 285–289)

- Facts as edited: OMT (buyer) faxed an offer to buy 60,000 barrels of MTBE; Bayway's response "operated as an acceptance" and added a Tax Clause making the buyer pay excise and similar taxes the seller incurred. OMT did not object and took delivery; the sale to an unregistered buyer generated $464,035.12 of federal excise tax (26 U.S.C. § 4081(a)(1)(A)(iv)), which Bayway paid and billed to OMT (C&M 285, 288).
- It was undisputed that Bayway's response formed a contract under (1) because it was not expressly conditional; the parties were merchants (§ 2-104(1), (3)), so the Tax Clause was presumed to enter unless an exception applied. OMT invoked only (2)(b) (C&M 285).
- Holding on burden, an issue of first impression in the Second Circuit: the general rule of inclusion is "in the nature of a presumption", so "the party opposing the inclusion of additional terms shoulders the burden of proof" of material alteration; the court joined "almost every court to have considered this issue" (C&M 285–286).
- Per se materiality: some terms are material as a matter of law (an arbitration clause in New York, Marlene; Comment 4 examples). The Tax Clause was not, because it allocated tax on a specific sale, unlike the "open-ended" back-tax liability in Union Carbide (C&M 286).
- Surprise has a subjective element (what the party actually knew) and an objective element (what it should have known). To carry the burden of showing surprise a party "must establish that, under the circumstances, it cannot be presumed that a reasonable merchant would have consented to the additional term" (quoting Union Carbide). Objective-surprise evidence can include prior course of dealing, the number of confirmations exchanged, industry custom and the term's conspicuousness (Chateaugay) (C&M 286–287).
- OMT showed subjective surprise ("contract by ambush") but offered no evidence on objective surprise. "Ordinarily, our inquiry into surprise would end here." Bayway nonetheless had introduced evidence of custom (two unchallenged experts; five major oil companies' standard contracts that Bayway introduced and OMT cited: three mirror the clause, Chevron folds tax into price, Texaco is silent). Common sense supported the custom because the buyer controls whether the tax arises by registering (C&M 287–288).
- Hardship: Comment 4 "seemingly" treats hardship as an independent ground, but courts have doubted it (Union Carbide). The court did not decide the question, because OMT's evidence (thin margins, a loss it cannot afford) did not show hardship; courts that rely on hardship typically face open-ended, prolonged liability; this loss was "limited, routine and self-inflicted" (C&M 288).
- Note 1, Union Carbide (Posner, J.): "An alteration is material if consent to it cannot be presumed ... What is expectable, hence unsurprising, is okay"; "surprise or hardship" misreads Comment 4; "Hardship is a consequence, not a criterion." The court affirmed summary judgment that the back-tax indemnity was material. A sequence of four documents for one sale is "a prototypical situation for the application of UCC § 2-207" (C&M 289). Note 2: warranty limits, arbitration, forum and payment-time terms are common materiality battlegrounds (C&M 289). Air Products held an implied-warranty disclaimer always material (C&M 284).

## 3. Different terms, the knockout rule, and Posner's approach: Northrop (C&M 290–293)

- Section 2-207(1) speaks of terms "additional to or different from" the offer; (2) speaks only of "additional" terms (the "notorious distinction", C&M 290).
- Facts as edited: Litronic offered printed wire boards with a 90-day warranty in lieu of other warranties; Northrop's responding document (the editors call it a "return invoice") had a warranty unlimited in duration. Northrop tried to return boards after 90 days; Litronic refused. Litronic was the offeror and Northrop the offeree (C&M 290).
- Three views (C&M 290–291): (i) the majority view: discrepant terms in both forms "fall out and are replaced by a suitable UCC gap-filler" (Daitom); (ii) the "leading minority view": the discrepant terms in the acceptance are ignored and the offeror's terms control (Valtrol), which "would give the palm to Litronic"; (iii) Posner's preferred view, adopted only by California (Steiner): treat "different" as "additional" and ask whether the term is materially different; if it is, it operates as a proposal and the offeror's term prevails unless the offeror agrees; if not, it enters. Under (ii) and (iii) Litronic's 90-day warranty would govern, because Northrop's unlimited warranty was materially different.
- Holding: predicting Illinois law, the court presumed Illinois would adopt the majority view because Illinois tends to adopt majority UCC rules and the Code values uniformity (§ 1-102), and found the presumption unrebutted. The magistrate judge had applied the majority approach, used § 2-309's reasonable time (the excerpt also cites § 2-601(1)), and held six months reasonable given complex testing (C&M 291).
- Rationale for the majority view: different terms suggest the offeree did not really accede, yet both wanted a contract, so a neutral term should govern; the offeror's terms may themselves be thoughtless boilerplate; an offeror can protect itself by limiting acceptance to its terms under § 2-207(2)(a); the leading minority view tempts the offeror to "spring a surprise" (C&M 291–292).
- Note 1 calls this the "knockout rule" and describes it as applying "when the difference between the seller's terms and the buyer's are material"; asks why the court applied it despite preferring another rule. Keating: easy never to lose the battle, hard to win it (C&M 292). Note 2: fairness, Gardner Zemke; warranty gap-fillers generally favor buyers; sellers use forms to avoid them (C&M 292–293). Notes 3–4: drafting history, strategies, master agreements, trade-association codes, EDI (C&M 293).
- Problem, "Misplaced Strategies" (C&M 293): buyer's purchase order has arbitration; seller's invoice is silent on arbitration but says it is not an acceptance or confirmation and that acceptance is expressly conditional on buyer's assent to the seller's terms; goods ship and burn. Teaching point: an expressly conditional form prevents formation by the writings; performance then forms a § 2-207(3) contract containing only terms on which the writings agree plus Code supplements, so the arbitration clause that appears only in the buyer's form is not a term, and the seller cannot invoke it. (The scanned PDF carries a typed annotation reaching that answer; it is not casebook text.)

## 4. Money now, terms later: who is the offeror in Hill and in Klocek (C&M 294–304)

- Section (F) intro asks whether § 2-207 applies to terms delivered after a basic contract seems formed, and whether consumer and commercial transactions should be treated differently (C&M 294).
- Hill (7th Cir. 1997, Easterbrook, J.): the Hills ordered by phone and paid by card; the box contained the computer and a four-page list of terms, said to govern unless the customer returned the computer within 30 days, including arbitration. They kept it more than 30 days (C&M 294–295).
  - Offeror: Gateway. Following ProCD, "a vendor, as master of the offer, may invite acceptance by conduct". The contract is formed not on the phone with payment but after the customer can inspect the item and the terms; "By keeping the computer beyond 30 days, the Hills accepted Gateway's offer, including the arbitration clause" (C&M 295, 297).
  - ProCD is about contract law, not software; practical considerations (no oral recitation of four pages) favor enclosing terms; "Competent adults are bound by such documents, read or unread" (C&M 295).
  - The executory-contract objection confuses formation with performance; Gateway still owed warranty and support (C&M 295–296).
  - Section 2-207: ProCD held that when there is only one form "sec. 2-207 is irrelevant"; the question is how and when the contract was formed, and the answer is the same "for merchants and consumers alike"; Zeidenberg was not a merchant anyway (C&M 296–297).
  - No notice on the shipping carton: "functional, not legal". The case of a buyer first alerted on opening and deterred by return shipping cost was left open, because the Hills knew before ordering that important terms would come in the carton (ads mentioned limited warranties and lifetime support). Shoppers can ask for terms (Magnuson-Moss requires warranty terms on request), consult public sources, or inspect after delivery (C&M 297).
  - Note 1: "rolling contract formation"; Brower (N.Y. App. Div. 1998) follows it. Note 2: if the contract forms only after 30 days, could Gateway refuse to ship at the agreed price after charging the card without breaching? Does that square with § 2-207's policy favoring formation? (C&M 297–298).
- Klocek (D. Kan. 2000, Vratil, J.): Standard Terms in the box, with a boxed notice that keeping the computer beyond five days accepts them; paragraph 10 requires ICC arbitration in Chicago (C&M 298).
  - Gateway bore an initial summary-judgment-like burden to show an agreement to arbitrate; gaps in the record prevented a choice of law, so the court analyzed Kansas and Missouri UCC together (C&M 299).
  - Payment and receipt "clearly demonstrates a contract for the sale of a computer"; the issue is whether that contract includes the Standard Terms (C&M 299).
  - The court declined to follow Hill and ProCD: their one-form conclusion has no support in the statute, which applies to "an acceptance or written confirmation" and requires no second form; Comment 1 covers an oral agreement followed by one party's memorandum (C&M 301).
  - Offeror: "In typical consumer transactions, the purchaser is the offeror, and the vendor is the offeree"; a vendor can be the offeror, but Gateway offered no evidence of that, so the court assumed Klocek offered and Gateway accepted "either by completing the sales transaction in person or by agreeing to ship and/or shipping" (C&M 301–302).
  - The Standard Terms were an expression of acceptance or a written confirmation; they would be a counteroffer only if Gateway had expressly conditioned acceptance, which requires clearly communicating unwillingness to proceed without them; shipping goods with terms enclosed did not do so (C&M 302; n.2 surveys the Roto-Lith, Dorton and Construction Aggregates standards).
  - Because Klocek was not a merchant, additional terms did not enter "unless plaintiff expressly agreed to them" (Kansas Comment 2). Keeping the computer past five days did not show express agreement; express assent "cannot be presumed by silence or mere failure to object" (C&M 302–303). Footnote 3: same result if treated as a § 2-209 modification. Footnote 4: a vendor can clearly communicate at the time of sale the complete terms or "the fact that the vendor will propose additional terms as a condition of sale" (C&M 302–303).
  - Notes: (1) array the factors explaining the different outcomes, including when each contract was formed; (2) Howard v. Ferrellgas (10th Cir. 2014, Gorsuch, J.): rolling formation is controversial; "It's pretty clear ... that Kansas is among those states that reject the rolling theory of contract formation"; (3) do the practical considerations carry over to wholly online assent? (C&M 303–304).

## 5. Section 2-207 and a single form

- Hill/ProCD: one form, so § 2-207 is irrelevant; the vendor's form is the offer and retention the acceptance (C&M 296).
- Klocek: nothing in § 2-207 requires two forms; it reaches "an acceptance or written confirmation", and Comment 1 expressly covers one party's confirming memorandum (C&M 301).
- Union Carbide Note: the prototypical § 2-207 case is a sequence of documents for one sale (C&M 289).
- RLCC 2024 Comment 15 (outside the Selections; Official Text on file) reports that most courts reject reading § 2-207 to bar post-purchase terms and use § 2-204 plus common law instead.

## 6. RLCC § 2(b), (c) in each edition

- 2019 Tentative Draft (Selections, p. 477). (b): when a standard term is available for review only after the consumer manifests assent to the transaction, it is adopted if (1) before assenting the consumer receives reasonable notice that the term will be provided later and will be part of the contract, informing the consumer of the opportunity to review and terminate, and explaining that failure to terminate adopts the term; (2) after assenting, the consumer receives a reasonable opportunity to review the term; and (3) after the term is available, the consumer has a reasonable opportunity to terminate without unreasonable cost, loss of value or personal burden, and does not. (c): if the consumer manifests assent to the transaction, a contract exists even if some standard terms are not adopted; its terms are those adopted under (a) and (b), the unadopted standard terms if the consumer elects, and terms supplied by law.
- 2019 comments: Comment 2 ("Adoption of standard terms may be separate from manifestation of assent") contains "Manifesting assent to the transaction as a whole generally involves adoption of at least some standard contract terms—the core deal terms ... other standard contract terms may then be adopted separately". (The sentence sits at the top of the right-hand column of p. 478, which continues Comment 2 from the foot of the left-hand column; it is not part of Comment 1.) Comment 5 says the majority of courts do not require all the (b)(1) notices, and the Restatement adopts the stricter minority approach. Illustration 7: a clear statement above the signature line, plus terms arriving with the product and a 30-day paid-return right, satisfies (b).
- 2024 Official Text (CR 873–889): the same three (b) conditions in the past tense, but adoption occurs only "if the business demonstrates that" they were met, so the business bears the burden. (c) now says a contract "is not rendered unenforceable" if some terms are not adopted, and its terms include "any of" the unadopted terms the consumer elects, terms "to which both parties agreed", and terms "supplied or incorporated by law". 2024 Comment 3 adds "This Section does not address the formation of the contract or the assent to nonstandard terms"; 2024 Comment 6 carries the majority/minority statement.
