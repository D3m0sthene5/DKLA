## (b) Errors

Ordered by how likely each is to change an exam answer.

### E1. Kenford takeaway states New York's tacit-agreement rule as general law

- Surface: Takeaway (tools/r19/data/takeaways.json), kenford, field text.
- Current text: "Unusual losses, like the rise in value of land around a stadium that was never built, are recoverable only if the breaching party could be seen to have taken on that risk when it made the contract."
- Verdict: CHANGES RESULT.
- Counterexample: a seller of goods who had reason to know of the buyer's particular resale needs. Under § 2-715(2)(a) the seller is liable for loss from requirements "of which the seller at the time of contracting had reason to know"; Comment 2: "The 'tacit agreement' test for the recovery of consequential damages is rejected"; Comment 3: no "conscious acceptance of an insurer's liability" is needed. Restatement § 351 cmt. a: "the party in breach need not have made a 'tacit agreement' to be liable for the loss." A student who applies the takeaway denies recovery that the Code and Restatement allow. The casebook says of the test: "This test has not generally found favor."
- Pinpoint: C&M 1025–1027, 1028 Note 1; § 351 cmt. a; § 2-715 cmts. 2–3.
- Replacement (2 sentences, about 68 words): "In New York, unusual losses, like the rise in value of land around a stadium that was never built, are recoverable only if the breaching party could be seen to have taken on that risk when it contracted; foreseeing a loss is not agreeing to pay for it. The UCC and Restatement § 351 reject this tacit-agreement test and ask what the breaching party had reason to foresee."

### E2. Flowchart K-32 node d makes an assumption-of-liability test part of every foreseeability analysis

- Surface: Flowchart K-32 (Which losses survive the damages limits?) node d, field detail.
- Current text: "Special consequences depend on knowledge at contracting and the applicable assumption-of-liability test."
- Verdict: CHANGES RESULT (MISSING CONDITION).
- Facts entered: Kenford's facts, but the governing law follows Restatement § 351 (or the claim is a goods buyer's consequential loss under § 2-715(2)(a)). When contracting, the defendant knew the plaintiff had bought and intended to buy land whose value depended on the project; no clause addressed the risk.
- Path: a, "Test avoidability", to b; "No shown reasonable substitute", to d. The detail tells the student to apply "the applicable assumption-of-liability test" and cites Kenford's rule; the student takes "Item too remote" to f.
- Displayed result: "Exclude the remote item."
- Source-supported result: under § 351(2)(b) the loss passes the foreseeability limit if it followed from special circumstances "that the party in breach had reason to know" and was foreseeable as a probable result; no tacit agreement is required (§ 351 cmt. a; § 2-715 cmt. 2). Recovery then depends on § 351(3) and certainty, not on assumed liability. Kenford's test is New York's (C&M 1028 Note 1 asks what its use "show[s] about the attitude of the New York Court of Appeals").
- Pinpoint: C&M 1020 Note 1 (Achilleas as an English "additional" principle), 1028 Note 1; § 351(2)(b) and cmt. a; § 2-715(2)(a) and cmt. 2.
- Replacement (node d detail): "Ordinary losses can arise naturally. Special losses count if the party in breach knew or had reason to know the special circumstances when contracting; Restatement § 351 and UCC § 2-715 require no tacit agreement to bear the loss, but New York (Kenford) also asks whether liability was assumed. Hadley lacked adequate notice of the mill’s unusual shutdown consequences. Kenford found knowledge of likely appreciation insufficient under its rule."

### E3. Flowchart K-31 gives a contractor who kept building after repudiation the full price

- Surface: Flowchart K-31 (How much can a contractor recover after breach?) node f, field detail; node d, field detail. (tools/flowcharts/data/contracts.json; K-31 cites no class-24 case, but it is the chart a student opens for Rockingham.)
- Current text (f): "Subtract supported defect offsets or avoided costs as applicable." (d): "Expectation equals the $100 unpaid price minus $60 expenses saved, or $40."
- Verdict: CHANGES RESULT.
- Facts entered: Rockingham. The county tells the builder on February 21 not to proceed and refuses to recognize the contract; about $1,900 has been spent; the builder completes the bridge and claims the amount due for work through November 3.
- Path: a, "Other side stopped performance", to d; "Use contract measure", to f. The builder actually saved nothing because it finished, so "avoided costs" and "expenses saved" are zero.
- Displayed result: "Award net contractual recovery": the unpaid price for the completed work.
- Source-supported result: after notice of the repudiation the builder "had no right thus to pile up damages"; it recovers damages sustained, "including any profit which he would have realized upon performance, as well as any other losses," not the cost of post-notice construction. The deduction is for costs avoidable by stopping, not costs actually saved.
- Pinpoint: C&M 994–995; § 350(1).
- Replacement (node f detail): "Subtract supported defect offsets and costs avoided, including costs the contractor could have avoided by stopping after a clear repudiation (Rockingham). Where starting from the total price, also credit money already paid. Use the measure supported by the governing rule and facts." Append to node d detail: "Count as saved the costs the contractor would have avoided by stopping when it received the repudiation, even if it kept working."
- K-30: for Astra. Node b detail, "Add proved related loss and subtract costs and losses avoided.", has the same gap; suggested wording "subtract costs and losses avoided or reasonably avoidable after breach (Rockingham)".

### E4. Flowchart K-32 node h sends a failed lost-profits claim to nominal damages only

- Surface: Flowchart K-32 node h, field detail.
- Current text: "A proved breach may still support nominal damages where applicable."
- Verdict: CHANGES RESULT (MISSING CONDITION).
- Facts entered: Fera, changed: the tenants offer only their own budget, with no expert, comparable-store or licensing evidence, so the lost-profit projection is speculative. They paid a $1,000 deposit and had spent money preparing the shop.
- Path: a to b to d (profits of the leased business are foreseeable to the lessor) to e; "Speculation or insufficient proof", to h.
- Displayed result: speculative loss excluded; nominal damages.
- Source-supported result: when profits cannot be proved, the injured party "can recover his loss based on his reliance interest instead" (§ 352 cmt. a; Illus. 2, the drive-in theatre whose profits are too speculative: "his recovery will be limited to expenses incurred in reliance or, if none can be proved with reasonable certainty, to nominal damages"). For a lessor's failure to give possession, the measure Fera quotes from Jarrait is "the difference between the actual rental value and the rent reserved," and Justice Coleman notes the deposit is recoverable. Nominal damages are the last step, not the next one.
- Pinpoint: § 352 cmt. a and Illus. 2–3; C&M 1036, 1039.
- Replacement (node h detail): "A factfinder may estimate on a sound record, but speculative loss cannot be awarded. If lost profits fail, the claimant may still recover reliance expenditures proved with reasonable certainty (§ 349; § 352 cmt. a) or another proved measure, such as rental value less rent reserved for a lessor's failure to give possession; nominal damages remain if no loss is proved." Suggested edge: {"label": "Use reliance measure instead", "to": K-30 node c}.
- K-30: for Astra. K-30 node e ("Nominal damages can mark a proved breach without proved actual loss.") is consistent if K-32 h points to K-30 c first.

### E5. Kenford rule line and chronology note omit the jurisdiction

### E6. Kenford plain-English sentence drops "liability", the word the case turns on

- Surface: Cold Call plain-English sentences, kenford, at "3.0", field plain.
- Current text: "\"Extraordinary losses\" are recoverable only if, at or before the contract was formed, both sides contemplated them as a probable result of breach."
- Verdict: CHANGES RESULT (for a student reading the ≈ line).
- Counterexample: Kenford itself. The court found it "beyond dispute" that all parties expected the stadium to raise land values, and the County knew of Kenford's purchases. On the ≈ wording both sides contemplated the loss, so Kenford recovers; the court held that it did not, because the County had not assumed liability for it.
- Pinpoint: C&M 1026–1027.
- Replacement: "Designated Judge Mollen, a judge assigned to sit on the court, explained that ordinary \"damages\" pay for the natural and probable results of a breach. \"Extraordinary losses\" are recoverable only if, at or before the contract was formed, the parties contemplated that the breaching party would be liable for them as a probable result of breach."

### E7. Timeline guide merges Hadley's test into Kenford's

- Surface: Timeline 'damages-limits' summary fields, field guide (a prior correction, 2026-11-17-timeline-redesign.json; the corrected text is itself inaccurate).
- Current text: "Hadley and Kenford ask which risks the contract covered"
- Verdict: OVERSTATED.
- Counterexample: Hadley's rule asks what "may reasonably be supposed to have been in the contemplation of both parties ... as the probable result of the breach"; the casebook calls it foreseeability and contrasts the later "exclusive principle" of assumed responsibility (Achilleas) and the tacit-agreement test, which the UCC rejects. Reading Hadley as a risk-coverage test imports Kenford's New York rule into a Restatement or UCC answer.
- Pinpoint: C&M 1017, 1019–1020 Note 1, 1028 Note 1.
- Replacement: "Use three separate checks. Hadley asks which losses both parties could reasonably foresee at contracting as a probable result of breach, and New York's Kenford decisions also ask whether the county assumed liability for them; Rockingham and Parker concern losses that might have been avoided; Fera and the first Kenford decision concern proof of lost profits."

### E8. Fera plain-English sentence removes "necessarily"

- Surface: Cold Call plain-English sentences, fera, at "3.1", field plain.
- Current text: "Once it is clear the plaintiff was harmed, difficulty measuring exactly how much does not bar recovery, especially when the defendant's own wrong made it hard to measure."
- Verdict: OVERSTATED (the source sentence says "did not necessarily defeat recovery"; the underlying principle is not in the assigned excerpt).
- Counterexample: Hendricks v. Daewoo: injury and foreseeability were established, but with "no formula or method" for projecting profits the jury "was left entirely to guesswork" and recovery was cut to $45,000. Fera itself: "The jury cannot be asked to guess."
- Pinpoint: C&M 1034 Note 1, 1036–1037; § 352 cmt. a ("Doubts are generally resolved against the party in breach").
- Replacement: "Once it is clear the plaintiff was harmed, difficulty measuring exactly how much does not necessarily bar recovery, especially when the defendant's conduct contributed to the difficulty. A reasonable basis for the estimate is still required."

## (c) Teaching gaps

Each item is assigned material that no Atlas surface teaches.

G1. Reasonable mitigation costs. Home: K-32 node c, detail. Draft: "Add back reasonable costs of trying to avoid loss, even if the effort failed (§ 350(2)). Failing to mitigate creates no liability to the breaching party, but it bars recovery of the avoidable loss." C&M 993.

G2. § 351(3) disproportionate compensation. Home: K-32, new node between d and e (edge from d "Foreseeable under governing rule" to it, then to e). Draft label: "Would full recovery be disproportionate compensation?" Detail: "Even for foreseeable loss, a court may exclude lost profits or limit recovery to reliance if justice so requires to avoid disproportionate compensation (§ 351(3)). Look for an extreme disparity between the loss and the price, informal dealing, or a non-commercial setting; Sundance Cruises refused a $264 million claim against an $85,000 classification fee." § 351 cmt. f, Illus. 17–19; C&M 1020 Note 3.

G3. Mitigation in sales of goods and the windfall problems. Home: K-32 node b, detail (one sentence) and the mitigation concept, notes. Draft (K-32 b): "For goods, the market measures (§§ 2-708(1), 2-713) assume a substitute transaction was available; a buyer who covered may not claim a larger market differential (§ 2-713 cmt. 5), and a seller with unfinished goods may complete or stop manufacture in reasonable commercial judgment (§ 2-704(2))." Draft (concept notes): "The problems ask whether a buyer who delays cover, or a seller whose market differential exceeds its actual lost profit, gets a windfall; courts divide on using § 2-708(2) to cap § 2-708(1) (Nobs Chemical versus Trans World Metals)." C&M 995–997, 1009.

G4. Doubtful repudiation. Home: K-32 node b, detail (after the timing sentence in E9). Draft: "If the other party's intent to perform is only doubtful, the claimant may demand adequate assurance before deciding whether to stop (Restatement § 251; UCC § 2-609)." C&M 995 Note.

G5. Parker's notes and the history of constructive service. Home: mitigation concept, notes (for coordination with the Parker team). Draft: "The employer bears the burden of showing actual comparable vacancies (Delliponti). An offer from the breaching employer need not be accepted where further association would be offensive or degrading (Voorhees), and the casebook asks whether an offer conditioned on giving up rights under the old contract counts. Courts rejected 'constructive service' (Howard v. Daly) in favor of mitigation." C&M 1009–1010, 1014.

G6. Mitigation by dealing with the breacher's transferee or affiliate. Home: mitigation concept, notes. Draft: "The problems ask whether a buyer must mitigate by buying the goods back from the breacher's purchaser at a higher price (Simeone) and whether a seller must accept the defaulting buyer's affiliate as a substitute buyer (Schiavi); the casebook leaves both open." C&M 1015.

G7. Buyers' consequential versus sellers' incidental damages. Home: rules entry ucc-2-715 (study note) or K-32 node d, detail. Draft: "Under Article 2 buyers recover incidental and consequential damages (§§ 2-712(2), 2-713, 2-715), but sellers recover only incidental damages (§§ 2-708(1), 2-710); some courts treat a seller's added interest costs as incidental (St. Paul Structural Steel). A clause excluding consequential damages is enforced on its terms (Reynolds Metals)." C&M 1020–1021.

G8. Inability to cover. Home: K-32 node d, detail. Draft: "For goods, consequential loss is recoverable only if it could not reasonably be prevented by cover (§ 2-715(2)(a)); loss caused by the buyer's inability to cover is foreseeable only if the seller had reason to know of that inability (§ 351 cmt. d)." C&M 1021 Note 5.

G9. (Folded into E2 and E7.) Restatement § 351 cmt. a's rejection of a tacit-agreement requirement appears on no surface; the E2 and E7 replacements supply it.

G10. Objective foreseeability and later-acquired knowledge. Home: foreseeability concept, notes. Draft: "Foreseeability is objective: the question is what a reasonable person in the defendant's position knew or should have known when contracting (Redgrave jury instruction). The Cold Steel problem asks whether knowledge acquired when a later term, such as a delivery date, is agreed can count." C&M 1028–1030.

G11. Sentimental value and emotional distress. Home: K-32 node d, detail (one sentence) and link rules entry restatement-353 to K-32 d. Draft: "Emotional distress is excluded unless the breach caused bodily harm or the contract or breach made serious emotional disturbance a particularly likely result (§ 353); more courts use categories such as funeral contracts (Lamm), and most exclude it for employment contracts. A lost item's actual value to the owner is recoverable, but not unusual sentimental value (Mieske)." C&M 1030–1033.

G12. The certainty standard itself. Home: K-32 node e, detail. Draft: "The standard is reasonable, not mathematical, certainty (§ 352), but it is more demanding than proof by a preponderance; doubts are resolved against the party in breach once loss is shown (§ 352 cmt. a)." C&M 1034.

G13. Kenford I and Hendricks beside Fera. Home: K-32 node e, detail. Draft: "Contrast Kenford I, where twenty years of projected profits for a stadium operator were too speculative, and Hendricks, where lost future contracts were cut to $45,000 for want of any projection method." C&M 1025, 1033–1034.

G14. Value of a lost chance. Home: certainty concept, notes. Draft: "Courts divide on a lost chance to win a contest: Collatz denied recovery because winning could not be proved; Chaplin v. Hicks valued the chance (§ 348(3))." C&M 1034–1035.

G15. Lost reputation. Home: certainty concept, notes. Draft: "American courts generally deny damages for lost reputation as uncertain or unforeseeable; Redgrave recovered for specific lost engagements, and English cases treat billing as a valuable right." C&M 1035.

G16. Royalties and good will. Home: certainty concept, notes.
