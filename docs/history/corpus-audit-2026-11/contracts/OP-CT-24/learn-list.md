## 1. Three limits on expectation (C&M 992–993)

1. The section treats three general limits on the expectation award: mitigation (avoidability), foreseeability, and certainty. Foreseeability "will often, but not always, imply limits on the recovery of sentimental value and damages for emotional distress." Certainty "is grounded in ordinary rules of evidence as much as substantive contract law." C&M 992–993.

## 2. Avoidability (C&M 993–997, 1009–1015; § 350)

2. Rule. "An aggrieved promisee is not allowed to recover loss that it could reasonably have avoided." § 350(1): damages are not recoverable for loss the injured party "could have avoided without undue risk, burden or humiliation." C&M 993. Virtue v. Bird (1678): the carter who let his horses stand in the open air while the defendant delayed six hours could not recover for their deaths ("the plaintiffs folly"). C&M 993.
3. There is no "duty to mitigate" in the sense of liability: the injured party "incurs no liability to the party in breach for a failure to mitigate," but "the converse is not true. The promisor must compensate the injured party for costs associated with reasonable mitigation efforts and attempts, whether or not mitigation occurs." C&M 993; § 350(2) ("reasonable but unsuccessful efforts to avoid loss").
4. For goods with a market, the buyer's damages assume a substitute purchase was possible: UCC § 2-713 measures "the difference between the market price ... and the contract price." C&M 993.
5. Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge (4th Cir. 1929), C&M 993–995. County board contracted January 7, 1924; on February 21 it notified the builder not to proceed and refused (unjustifiably, as the court found) to recognize the contract; about $1,900 of labor and materials had been spent; the builder kept building and sued for the amount said to be due for work through November 3, 1924; the trial court directed a verdict for that sum. Holding (Parker, J., on the "third question," the measure of recovery): after notice, "while the contract was still executory, that it did not desire the bridge built and would not pay for it, plaintiff could [not] proceed to build it and recover the contract price." The county had no right to rescind and its notice was a breach, but "after plaintiff had received notice of the breach, it was its duty to do nothing to increase the damages flowing therefrom." The remedy is to treat the contract as broken on receipt of notice and sue for damages sustained, "including any profit which he would have realized upon performance, as well as any other losses which may have resulted to him." The bridge "in the midst of the forest" was of no value because the county abandoned the road. Frost v. Knight rejected as not the American rule; Williston quoted: the plaintiff "is interested only in the profit he will make out of the contract." Judgment reversed. (The excerpt prints only the damages question; the authority and pleading questions are omitted.)
6. Note "A Bridge Too Far": a firm uncertain whether to stop or continue should consider Restatement § 251 and UCC § 2-609 (adequate assurance). C&M 995.
7. Problem "Walking Across the Brooklyn Bridge": can B finish the walk after A tries to revoke and recover $100 plus a $10 taxi? § 45(2); what if the offer had been for a promise; is § 45(2) consistent with avoidability? C&M 995 (questions only).
8. Goods: the "second step" is that the injured party cannot recover loss avoidable by arranging a substitute transaction. Buyer covers (§ 2-712) or gets market differential (§ 2-713); seller resells (§ 2-706) or gets market differential (§ 2-708). Proof of market price §§ 2-723, 2-724. C&M 995–996.
9. Unfinished goods, § 2-704(2): the seller may "in the exercise of reasonable commercial judgment for the purposes of avoiding loss and of effective realization" complete or cease manufacture. Two-by-two grid: reasonable completion lets the seller resell (§ 2-706) or sue for price (§ 2-709(1)); reasonable cessation gives § 2-708(2) lost profit with allowance for costs and scrap proceeds; the question what happens when the seller's judgment was unreasonable is left open, with a reminder of Luten Bridge. C&M 996.
10. Note "Windfalls?": UCC § 1-305(a) aims at the position full performance would have produced. Comment 5 to § 2-713: the market remedy "is completely alternative to cover and applies only when and to the extent that the buyer has not covered," so a buyer who covered favorably cannot claim the larger market differential; the Code nowhere says the same of a seller who resold above market. C&M 996–997; § 2-713 cmt. 5.
11. Problem "Waiting for a Windfall?": contract $100,000, buyer's resale contract $125,000, market at delivery $110,000; buyer waits six months, market falls to $90,000 (variant: rises to $120,000). Does $10,000 give a windfall; is § 2-712 or § 2-713 applicable? C&M 997. § 2-713(1) fixes market price "at the time when the buyer learned of the breach."
12. Problem "Tongish Topsy-Turvy?": contract $100,000, seller's supply contract $90,000, market falls to $75,000, buyer repudiates; seller has neither received nor resold. Would $25,000 under § 2-708(1) be a windfall? Does § 2-708(2) apply if § 2-708(1) overcompensates? Nobs Chemical (5th Cir. 1980) is compared with Trans World Metals (2d Cir. 1985). C&M 1009. (The preceding Tongish notes on efficient breach and middlemen, C&M 1009, belong to the unassigned Tongish case.)
13. Services. Employment mitigation has been "particularly troublesome." Gandell v. Pontigny (1816) allowed "constructive service" for the whole quarter; Howard v. Daly (N.Y. 1875) rejected constructive service as at war with the rule that a discharged employee "must accept employment elsewhere if offered." C&M 1009–1010.
14. Parker v. Twentieth Century-Fox (Cal. 1970), C&M 1010–1014 (primary-reviewed by the redesign team; noted for coverage only). Rule: salary for the period "less the amount which the employer affirmatively proves the employee has earned or with reasonable effort might have earned from other employment"; before projected earnings from opportunities "not sought or accepted" count, the employer must show the other employment "was comparable, or substantially similar"; rejection of "employment of a different or inferior kind may not be resorted to in order to mitigate damages." Different in kind (song-and-dance production in Los Angeles versus straight dramatic western in Australia) and, independently, inferior (elimination or impairment of director and screenplay approvals). Affirmed; the court did not reach whether the guaranteed-pay terms excused mitigation. Dissent (Sullivan, Acting C.J.): the relevant question is whether differences are "substantial enough to constitute differences in the kind of employment" or render it inferior, a factual inquiry; "in the typical case" summary judgment must be withheld.
15. Parker notes. (1) Would it matter if the substitute offer were conditioned on Parker's surrender of rights under the old contract? Burden of proof: Delliponti (Pa. 1996) put it on the employer, which must show actual vacant positions. (2) "Honor and respect": a general counsel need not take a dishwasher's job (the casebook quotes this in A.A. Healy Co. v. Milwaukee Metropolitan Sewerage District, 7th Cir. 1995, Posner, C.J., which adds that a corporate sewer contractor loses no "honor and respect" by mitigating); offers from the breaching employer may be refused where further association would be "offensive or degrading" (Voorhees, W.Va. 1994). (3) Equal-opportunity exception: Shea-S&M Ball (D.C. Cir. 1979) held mitigation inapplicable where the breaching party had equal opportunity and knowledge; criticized in Cates (7th Cir. 1985, Posner) as "discordant with common law principles." C&M 1014–1015.
16. Problems. (1) Simeone: bank refused tender, sold cars to SMB, which resold for $1,000,000; Simeone recovered $2,600,000 on a $3,000,000 value; must he have mitigated by buying from SMB at the increased price? (2) Diasonics reprise: an affiliate of the defaulting buyer offers to buy the equipment so the buyer would not lose its deposit; what if the seller ignored it (Schiavi Mobile Homes, Me. 1983)? C&M 1015. Results are not given in the assigned text.
17. Lost-volume seller. The doctrine itself is on earlier, unassigned pages (Diasonics, R.E. Davis, p. 982); the assigned pages reach it only through § 2-708(2) (C&M 996) and the Diasonics reprise problem (C&M 1015).

## 3. Foreseeability (C&M 1015–1021, 1024–1033; § 351; UCC § 2-715)

18. History: the rules developed to curb jury discretion and "outrageous and excessive" verdicts, coinciding with the end of the Industrial Revolution. C&M 1015.
19. Hadley v. Baxendale (Ex. 1854), C&M 1016–1019. Facts: crank shaft broken; Joyce & Co. at Greenwich needed it as a pattern; the reported trial narrative says the servant told the clerk the mill was stopped and the shaft must be sent immediately; carriage paid £2 4s; delay of several days; £25 paid into court; jury gave £25 more. Rule (Alderson, B.): damages "such as may fairly and reasonably be considered either arising naturally, i.e., according to the usual course of things, from such breach of contract itself, or such as may reasonably be supposed to have been in the contemplation of both parties, at the time they made the contract, as the probable result of the breach of it." Communicated special circumstances make recoverable "the amount of injury which would ordinarily follow from a breach ... under these special circumstances so known and communicated"; if "wholly unknown," only loss arising "in the great multitude of cases." Rationale: had they been known, the parties "might have specially provided for the breach ... by special terms as to the damages." Application: the only circumstances communicated were that the article was a broken mill shaft and the plaintiffs the millers; a spare shaft or other defective machinery would leave profits unaffected; the loss of profits neither arose naturally nor was the result of communicated special circumstances. New trial.
20. Hadley notes. (1) Victoria Laundry: the headnote and statement of facts are misleading; the court must have rejected the evidence that the clerk was told the mill was stopped. Criticism (Adler, Eisenberg); Danzig history. The Achilleas (H.L. 2008): Hadley is an "inclusive principle"; Lord Hoffmann adds an "exclusive principle" that a party is not liable even for foreseeable loss for which it did not assume responsibility; Supershield extends it to unforeseeable risks within the scope of the duty. Contrast § 351, § 2-715(2) and Torts § 435. (2) Limitation of risk: Patterson's rationale (encourage the entrepreneur); the blacksmith and the lost heiress criticized in British Columbia Saw Mill v. Nettleship; Coppola v. Kraushaar (wedding gowns; never-to-occur wedding too remote). (3) Allocation of risk: § 351(3) lets a court limit damages for foreseeable loss "if it concludes that in the circumstances justice so requires in order to avoid disproportionate compensation"; Sundance Cruises (2d Cir. 1993): an $85,000 classification fee versus a $264,000,000 claim. (4) Consequential damages: buyers recover "incidental or consequential" (§§ 2-712(2), 2-713, 2-715), sellers only "incidental" (§§ 2-708(1), 2-710); courts read the Code to preclude sellers' consequential damages; St. Paul Structural Steel treated a seller's added interest as incidental; Reynolds Metals applied a consequential-damages exclusion clause. (5) Availability of cover: losses from inability to cover are foreseeable only if the seller knew facts making it foreseeable (Marcus v. K.L.G. Baking); does § 2-715(2)(a) dispense with that? C&M 1019–1021.
21. § 351 text: (1) not recoverable for loss the party in breach "did not have reason to foresee as a probable result of the breach when the contract was made"; (2) foreseeable because it follows (a) in the ordinary course or (b) from special circumstances "that the party in breach had reason to know"; (3) disproportionate-compensation limit. Comment a: probable, not necessary; "the party in breach need not have made a 'tacit agreement' to be liable"; the test is objective. Comment b: Illus. 1 is Hadley's facts with notice that the shaft must be sent at once but not that the mill is stopped: no liability. Comment d: unavailability of substitute. Comment f, Illus. 17–19: extreme disproportion between loss and price, informality of dealing, non-commercial setting.
22. § 2-715(2)(a): consequential damages include loss from requirements and needs "of which the seller at the time of contracting had reason to know and which could not reasonably be prevented by cover or otherwise." Comment 2: "The 'tacit agreement' test for the recovery of consequential damages is rejected." Comment 3: no "conscious acceptance of an insurer's liability" is needed; particular needs must generally be made known, general needs rarely. Comment 4: burden on buyer; no near-mathematical certainty. Comment 6: resale is a requirement a seller of wares to a reseller has reason to know.
23. Delchi (2d Cir. 1995) begins on C&M 1021 (facts only on the assigned page); its CISG art. 74 holding and the "possible" versus "probable" note are on unassigned pages 1022–1023.
24. Kenford Co. v. County of Erie (N.Y. 1989), C&M 1024–1028. County authorized a domed stadium and $50 million financing; Kenford donated 178 acres; County to begin construction within 12 months and negotiate a 40-year lease with DSI whose revenues included increased real estate taxes from "the peripheral lands"; else a 20-year management agreement. Cost rose to about $72 million and the County terminated. First trial: $18 million lost appreciation and over $6 million out-of-pocket for Kenford; $25.6 million lost profits for DSI. The Court of Appeals earlier rejected DSI's profits on "the twofold basis" that liability for them was not in the parties' contemplation and that they were too speculative. Retrial: $6.5 million appreciation, affirmed. Holding: reversed and the award stricken; general damages are the natural and probable consequence of breach; "unusual or extraordinary damages" must have been "brought within the contemplation of the parties as the probable result of a breach at the time of or prior to contracting," judged by "the nature, purpose and particular circumstances of the contract known by the parties" and "what liability the defendant fairly may be supposed to have assumed consciously, or to have warranted the plaintiff reasonably to suppose that it assumed" (Globe Refining, Holmes, J.). All parties expected an economic boom and higher land values, but that did not show the County assumed liability for lost appreciation; Kenford had no contractual obligation to acquire or keep peripheral land; the County's awareness of its purchases was "insufficient, as a matter of law"; Kenford assumed the risk; the principle limits liability "for unassumed risks."
25. Kenford notes. (1) Globe Refining's "tacit agreement" test; "[M]ere notice to a seller of some interest or probable action of the buyer is not enough." "This test has not generally found favor"; UCC § 2-715 cmt. 2 rejects it; what does Kenford's use show about New York's attitude? (2) Redgrave v. Boston Symphony: jury instruction defining harm "within the contemplation of the parties" objectively, including harm "of a kind foreseeable as sufficiently likely to result" and requiring that BSO knew or should have known of "a substantial likelihood" of lost professional work; the First Circuit allowed only about $12,000 of the $100,000 verdict. C&M 1028–1029.
26. Problem "Cold Steel": steel subcontract with delivery "to be mutually agreed upon"; in November the parties set a June delivery; Fort Pitt argued knowledge after formation cannot expand liability under Hadley; Torrington argued Fort Pitt is charged with what it knew when the June date was agreed. Spang Industries (2d Cir. 1975). C&M 1029–1030 (question only).
27. Sentimental value: Mieske v. Bartell Drug (Wash. 1979): compensation for "sentimental or fanciful values will not be allowed," but "actual or intrinsic value" to the owner of a lost wedding film is recoverable; the excluded sentiment is "indulging in feeling to an unwarranted extent." C&M 1030–1031.
28. Emotional distress: courts reluctant (§ 353: excluded unless the breach caused bodily harm or the contract or breach is of a kind making serious emotional disturbance "a particularly likely result"). Lamm v. Shingleton (N.C. 1949): funeral vault; recovery where the contract is personal and mental anguish should be known to result. B&M Homes (Ala. 1979) allowed anguish for a defective new house; Ostrowe contra. Notes: most courts exclude distress damages for breach of employment contracts (Saporoso, Gaglidari), some allow after harassment; Gupta (meat samosas) allowed spiritual injury under §§ 2-313 and 2-715; Levin v. Halston dismissed; Cummings v. Premier Rehab (2022) denied distress damages under Spending Clause statutes because they are not generally available for breach of contract; Note 5: the § 353 approach is followed in "a sizable minority"; "more common is a categorical approach" (funeral contracts high among them); a minority require the contract to contemplate them explicitly. C&M 1031–1033.

## 4. Certainty (C&M 1033–1040; § 352)

29. Kenford I is restated as the bridge: DSI's $25.6 million lost profits failed on both contemplation and certainty. Naval Institute's rejection of a "speculative" challenge is recalled. C&M 1033–1034.
30. Standard. Griffin v. Colver (N.Y. 1858) required "certainty"; modern formulations require "reasonable certainty": § 352 (no recovery "for loss beyond an amount that the evidence permits to be established with reasonable certainty"); UCC § 1-106 cmt. 1 (now § 1-305): not "mathematical accuracy," "with whatever definiteness and accuracy the facts permit, but no more"; UNIDROIT 7.4.3. Still, the burden is "more onerous" than a preponderance. C&M 1034. § 352 cmt. a: doubts "are generally resolved against the party in breach"; if profits cannot be proved, reliance can be recovered (§ 349, Illus. 2–3); good will may be recovered if estimable (Illus. 4). Cmt. b: new businesses harder but possible (Illus. 6); availability of specific performance may justify requiring greater certainty (cmt. c).
31. Notes. (1) Hendricks v. Daewoo: foreseeability satisfied, but lost prospective profits not proved beyond $45,000; remittitur. (2) Value of a chance: Collatz (no recovery for the arbitrarily ended quiz contest) versus Chaplin v. Hicks and Wachtel; § 348(3). (3) Right to work: could MacLaine recover more than $750,000 for lost reputation? Clayton & Waller (billing at the Hippodrome a valuable right); Malik; American courts generally deny lost-reputation recovery for uncertainty or unforeseeability; Redgrave claimed specific lost engagements, not general reputation. C&M 1034–1035.
32. Fera v. Village Plaza (Mich. 1976), C&M 1035–1039. Ten-year lease of a specific location for a "book and bottle" shop; $1,000 deposit; lease misplaced and the space rented to others; alternative space refused as unsuitable. Jury $200,000 lost profits; Court of Appeals reversed. Older Michigan authority (Jarrait, 1906) measured a lessor's failure to give possession by rental value less rent reserved and excluded profits of a new business; Isbell (1912) said profits are excluded only because often not provable. Holding: these cases do not state a rule "which prevents every new business from recovering anticipated lost profits"; the plaintiff "must lay a basis for a reasonable estimate"; "The jury cannot be asked to guess"; the new-business/interrupted-business distinction reflects proof. Testimony ranged from no profits (a Cunningham Drug expert said they would probably lose money) to $270,000; the issue was the most completely tried; credibility for the jury; "While we might have found plaintiffs' proofs lacking had we been members of the jury, that is not the standard of review"; verdict reinstated. Coleman, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part: no license and testimony that none could be obtained made liquor profits too speculative; would allow bookstore losses and affirm on remittitur above $60,000; plaintiffs entitled to the $1,000 deposit.
33. Notes after Fera. (1) Royalties from artistic creations: Contemporary Mission v. Famous Music: statistical and expert proof of the success of "Fear No Evil" should have been admitted; the "domino theory" of tours and benefits rejected as speculative. (2) Good will: Pennsylvania's former bar (Harry Rubin, hula hoops) overruled in AM/PM Franchise (1990), joining the "overwhelming majority." C&M 1040.
34. Causation: causation-in-fact is "a bare minimum"; substantial-factor formulations (Ind. Mich. Power); Nirdlinger (burglar alarm not the cause of the burglary). C&M 1040.

## 5. What the notes and problems distinguish

35. Stopping performance (Luten Bridge) versus arranging a substitute transaction (goods, employment). C&M 995.
36. Avoidable loss excluded versus reasonable mitigation costs added. C&M 993; § 350(2).
37. Market-differential measure as a hypothetical substitute versus actual cover or resale, and the windfall asymmetry between buyer (§ 2-713 cmt. 5) and seller. C&M 996–997.
38. Different or inferior work versus mere differences; burden on the employer; offers from the breacher; humiliation. C&M 1011–1015; § 350(1).
39. Hadley's two branches; communication (Hadley) versus reason to know (§ 351(2)(b), § 2-715(2)(a)); foreseeability versus tacit agreement or assumed responsibility (Kenford, Globe Refining, Achilleas) and the UCC's and Restatement's rejection of tacit agreement. C&M 1019, 1028; § 351 cmt. a; § 2-715 cmt. 2.
40. Foreseeable but limited anyway: § 351(3) disproportion (Sundance). C&M 1020.
41. Buyers' consequential versus sellers' incidental damages under Article 2. C&M 1021.
42. Contemplation (Kenford, 1989) versus certainty (Kenford I, 1986, and Fera): Kenford I failed on both; Fera's new-business profits went to the jury on extensive evidence. C&M 1025, 1033, 1037.
43. When profits fail for uncertainty, recovery falls back to reliance (§ 352 cmt. a, Illus. 2–3) or, for a lessor's failure to deliver possession, rental value less rent reserved (Jarrait as quoted in Fera), and the deposit (Coleman). C&M 1036, 1039.
