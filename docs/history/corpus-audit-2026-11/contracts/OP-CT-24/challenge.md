## A. Statements that change a legal result

### A1. Flowchart K-32 node b: avoidability has no trigger, no goods rule, and states Parker's test conjunctively

- **Surface/field:** Flowchart K-32 (Which losses survive the damages limits?) node b, `detail` (contracts.json; shown in ct24-surfaces.md under rockingham and parker).
- **Current text:** `Look for reasonable steps and comparable substitutes without undue risk or burden. Rockingham excludes avoidable construction after repudiation. Parker permitted rejection of a different and inferior acting role.`
- **Verdict:** MISSING CONDITION; it changes the result on three changed-fact runs.
- **Run 1 (Rockingham facts, decisive fact changed: no clear repudiation).** Facts entered: the contract is signed; there is public opposition and talk that the county will cancel, but no notice that the county will not accept or pay; the builder keeps building; months later the county refuses payment. Path: a → b. Node b asks only whether loss could have been avoided "after breach" and cites Rockingham for construction "after repudiation"; a student who treats the rumoured cancellation as the repudiation takes "Avoidable amount identified" → c "Deduct loss reasonably avoidable" → d → e → g. Displayed result: the builder loses its post-rumour construction costs. Source-supported result: Judge Parker's rule runs from the point when the county "had given notice, while the contract was still executory, that it did not desire the bridge built and would not pay for it"; Williston's rule applies "after an absolute repudiation or refusal to perform." Before that, the builder is bound to perform and its costs are not avoidable loss. The editors' note directs a builder unsure whether to stop to Restatement § 251 and UCC § 2-609 (adequate assurance). The lecture-notes record says the class hypothetical "separates suspected cancellation from a clear repudiation"; the chart does not.
- **Run 2 (decisive fact changed: goods instead of a bridge).** Facts entered: a buyer repudiates while the seller is part-way through manufacturing goods; the seller, judging that finished goods will resell for more than scrap, completes them and resells. Path: a → b, where Rockingham is the only stated rule → "Avoidable amount identified" (completion costs incurred after repudiation) → c → g. Displayed result: completion costs deducted. Source-supported result: UCC § 2-704(2) lets the aggrieved seller "in the exercise of reasonable commercial judgment for the purposes of avoiding loss and of effective realization" either complete manufacture or cease; if it reasonably completes, it may resell under § 2-706 or, on the stated conditions, recover the price under § 2-709(1) (C&M 996).
- **Run 3 (Parker facts, decisive fact changed: substitute different in kind but not inferior).** Facts entered: Fox offers the same pay and every original approval right for the lead in a dramatic western filmed in Los Angeles. Path: a → b. The detail says Parker permitted rejection of a "different and inferior" role; this role is different but not inferior, so a student takes "Avoidable amount identified" → c and deducts the $750,000 she could have earned. Source-supported result under the majority: the employee's rejection of "employment of a different or inferior kind may not be resorted to in order to mitigate damages"; the musical-versus-western contrast alone "demonstrates the difference in kind," and inferiority from the lost approvals was an additional ground ("Additionally"). The dissent would have sent comparability to trial, so the chart's conjunctive wording matches neither opinion. The chart also never says who carries the burden: the employer "must show that the other employment was comparable, or substantially similar."
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 994 (Parker, J.; Williston quotation), 995 Note "A Bridge Too Far"; C&M 996 (§ 2-704(2) discussion); C&M 1011–1012 (Parker majority); Restatement § 350(1) black letter ("undue risk, burden or humiliation"); UCC §§ 2-704(2), 2-712, 2-713, 2-708.
- **Replacement (node b detail):** `Avoidability runs from an unequivocal repudiation or other breach the claimant knows of; a party unsure whether the other has repudiated may demand adequate assurance (Restatement § 251; UCC § 2-609). Look for reasonable steps and substitutes available without undue risk, burden or humiliation (§ 350(1)). Rockingham excludes construction costs incurred after the county's notice that it would not accept or pay for the bridge. A goods seller may instead complete manufacture in the exercise of reasonable commercial judgment (UCC § 2-704(2)), and the market and cover measures assume a substitute transaction (§§ 2-708, 2-712, 2-713). In employment cases the employer must prove that rejected or unsought work was comparable or substantially similar; Parker could reject a role that was different in kind and, independently, inferior.`

### A2. Flowchart K-32 node h: failed lost-profit proof routed to nominal damages, with no reliance fallback

- **Surface/field:** Flowchart K-32 node h, `detail`.
- **Current text:** `A factfinder may estimate on a sound record, but speculative loss cannot be awarded. A proved breach may still support nominal damages where applicable.`
- **Verdict:** CHANGES RESULT.
- **Facts entered (Fera, decisive fact changed: no reasonable basis for profits):** the landlord refuses the Feras their space; they have no experience, no budget and no experts, only an owner's estimate of profits; they paid a $1,000 deposit and spent money preparing to open. Path: a → b ("No shown reasonable substitute", since the offered space was unsuitable) → d ("Foreseeable under governing rule") → e ("Speculation or insufficient proof") → h. Displayed result: no recovery beyond nominal damages.
- **Source-supported result:** § 352 comment a: when the amount of profit cannot be proved, the injured party "can recover his loss based on his reliance interest instead of on his expectation interest. See § 349"; Illustration 2 (a new drive-in theatre whose profits are too speculative): "his recovery will be limited to expenses incurred in reliance or, if none can be proved with reasonable certainty, to nominal damages." In Fera itself Justice Coleman added that "Plaintiffs are also entitled to the $1,000 deposit." Nominal damages come only after reliance (and restitution) fail.
- **Pinpoint:** Restatement § 352 comment a and Illustrations 1–3; § 349 black letter; C&M 1039 (Coleman, J.).
- **Replacement (node h detail):** `A factfinder may estimate on a sound record, but speculative loss cannot be awarded. If lost profits cannot be proved with reasonable certainty, the claimant may still recover reliance expenditures it can prove (§§ 349, 352 comment a) and restitution of payments such as a deposit; nominal damages remain only where no loss is proved.`

### A3. Kenford takeaway: New York's assumption-of-liability test stated as general law

- **Surface/field:** Kenford, Takeaway (tools/r19/data/takeaways.json).
- **Current text:** `Unusual losses, like the rise in value of land around a stadium that was never built, are recoverable only if the breaching party could be seen to have taken on that risk when it made the contract. Foreseeing a loss is not the same as agreeing to pay for it.`
- **Verdict:** OVERSTATED (a jurisdiction's rule stated as general law); it changes the result outside New York and under Article 2.
- **Counterexample:** Hadley with the decisive fact changed: when the contract is made the servant tells the clerk that the mill is stopped and has no spare shaft. Under the takeaway, the millers lose unless the carrier also agreed to bear the loss ("Foreseeing a loss is not the same as agreeing to pay for it"); Globe Refining's version of that test says "mere notice to a seller of some interest or probable action of the buyer is not enough." Under Hadley's own second branch the loss "which would ordinarily follow from a breach of contract under these special circumstances so known and communicated" is recoverable; Restatement § 351 comment a says the party in breach "need not have made a 'tacit agreement' to be liable for the loss"; UCC § 2-715 Comment 2 says the "tacit agreement" test "is rejected," and Comment 3 says it "is not necessary that there be a conscious acceptance of an insurer's liability." The editors: the test "has not generally found favor."
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 1017 (Hadley rule); C&M 1026 (Kenford, quoting Globe Refining); C&M 1028 Note 1; Restatement § 351 comment a; UCC § 2-715 Comments 2–3.
- **Replacement (takeaway, two sentences, 65 words):** `Under New York's Kenford rule, unusual losses, like the rise in value of land around a stadium that was never built, are recoverable only if the breaching party could be seen to have taken on that risk when it made the contract. That tacit-agreement test has not generally found favor; Restatement § 351 and UCC § 2-715 ask whether the loss was foreseeable at contracting.`

### A4. Parker map entry Reasoning and learning.topics: actual replacement earnings deducted only if the work was comparable

- **Surface/field:** Parker, Map entry: sections.Reasoning (first sentence) and Map entry: learning.topics "expectation-interest".
- **Current text:** `The employer bears the burden of establishing earnings actually obtained or reasonably available through comparable or substantially similar employment.` and `The $750,000 award is the full expectation measure for a wrongfully discharged employee, reduced only by earnings from comparable employment.`
- **Verdict:** MISSING CONDITION; it changes the result. This is the defect the 2026-11-17 register corrected in the brief rule line ("Distinguish actual earnings from hypothetical earnings from rejected work"); it survives in these two fields.
- **Counterexample (Parker, decisive fact changed: she accepts the substitute):** Parker accepts the Big Country lead and is paid $750,000 for it. Both Atlas sentences limit deductions to earnings from comparable employment; the western is different and inferior, so a student awards the full $750,000 on top of the $750,000 earned. The court's general rule deducts "the amount which the employer affirmatively proves the employee has earned or with reasonable effort might have earned from other employment"; the comparability requirement applies only to "projected earnings from other employment opportunities not sought or accepted."
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 1011.
- **Replacement (Reasoning, first sentence):** `The employer bears the burden of proving earnings the employee actually received from other employment and, for work she did not seek or accept, earnings reasonably available from comparable or substantially similar employment.`
- **Replacement (learning.topics "expectation-interest"):** `The $750,000 award is the full expectation measure for a wrongfully discharged employee, reduced by earnings actually received elsewhere and by earnings the employer proves she could with reasonable effort have obtained from comparable employment.`

### A5. Fera Cold Call plain sentence 3.1: "does not bar recovery" once harm is shown

- **Surface/field:** Fera, Cold Call plain-English sentences, at "3.1".
- **Current text:** `Once it is clear the plaintiff was harmed, difficulty measuring exactly how much does not bar recovery, especially when the defendant's own wrong made it hard to measure.`
- **Verdict:** OVERSTATED; the source sentence says the difficulty "did not necessarily defeat recovery," and the same Cold Call paragraph adds that the principle "did not remove the need for a reasonable evidentiary foundation." The plain sentence drops both qualifications.
- **Counterexample:** Hendricks v. Daewoo (C&M 1023, 1034): the jury could find that Daewoo's defective garments cost Hendricks its future Champion contracts, so harm was established, yet the court allowed no more than $45,000 of a $375,000 award because, with "no evidence of past profits, future profit margins, future business revenues or expenses ... the jury was left entirely to guesswork." The DSI profits in the first Kenford decision were likewise denied as "too speculative" although the breach caused some loss (C&M 1025, 1033). A student relying on the plain sentence would sustain the full award. Restatement § 352: damages are not recoverable "for loss beyond an amount that the evidence permits to be established with reasonable certainty." The principle itself (inability to measure precisely after injury is shown) is not in the assigned Fera excerpt.
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 1034 Note 1 (Hendricks); C&M 1033–1034 (certainty introduction); Restatement § 352 black letter and comment a.
- **Replacement (plain):** `Once it is clear the plaintiff was harmed, difficulty measuring exactly how much does not necessarily bar recovery, especially when the defendant's own wrong made it hard to measure. The plaintiff still needs evidence that supports a reasonable estimate of the amount.`

### A6. Kenford Cold Call plain sentence 3.0: "contemplated them" drops "liability for"

- **Surface/field:** Kenford, Cold Call plain-English sentences, at "3.0".
- **Current text:** `are recoverable only if, at or before the contract was formed, both sides contemplated them as a probable result of breach.`
- **Verdict:** MISSING CONDITION; it changes Kenford's own result.
- **Counterexample (Kenford's facts):** the court found it "beyond dispute" that all parties expected the stadium to bring "increased land values," and the County knew Kenford was buying peripheral land. On the plain sentence a student finds the appreciation contemplated and awards it. The court denied it because the parties did not contemplate "that the County would assume liability for Kenford's loss of anticipated appreciation," asking "what liability the defendant fairly may be supposed to have assumed consciously." The Cold Call paragraph the sentence comes from states that test; the plain rendering loses it, and with it the decisive distinction between foreseeing a gain and assuming liability for its loss.
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 1025–1027.
- **Replacement (plain, second sentence):** `"Extraordinary losses" are recoverable only if, at or before the contract was formed, the parties contemplated that the defendant would be liable for them as a probable result of breach, judged by what liability the defendant consciously assumed or led the plaintiff reasonably to believe it assumed.`

### A7. Flowchart K-32 node d: an "assumption-of-liability test" presented as part of every special-damages inquiry

- **Surface/field:** Flowchart K-32 node d, `detail`.
- **Current text:** `Special consequences depend on knowledge at contracting and the applicable assumption-of-liability test.`
- **Verdict:** MISSING CONDITION; on a changed-fact run it changes the result.
- **Facts entered (Hadley, decisive fact changed: special circumstances communicated):** when the carriage contract is made the servant tells the clerk that the mill is stopped, that it has no spare shaft and that the new shaft depends on the old one reaching Greenwich. Path: a → b ("No shown reasonable substitute") → d. The detail requires an assumption-of-liability test as well as knowledge; a carrier that merely received notice has not agreed to insure the mill's profits, so the student takes "Item too remote" → f. - **Source-supported result:** under Hadley's rule the injury that "would ordinarily follow from a breach of contract under these special circumstances so known and communicated" is recoverable; § 351(2)(b) asks only whether the party in breach "had reason to know"; § 351 comment a and UCC § 2-715 Comments 2–3 reject a tacit-agreement or conscious-acceptance requirement. ### A8. Hadley map entry Holding and Cold Call plain sentence 3.1: the time of contracting is dropped

- **Surface/field:** Hadley, Map entry: sections.Holding; and Cold Call plain-English sentences, at "3.1" (the sentence beginning "Telling the other side").
- **Current text:** `losses reasonably contemplated by both parties as a probable consequence because relevant special circumstances were known` and `Telling the other side about “special circumstances,” unusual facts about one’s situation, could make the ordinary consequences of those circumstances recoverable.`
- **Verdict:** MISSING CONDITION.
- **Counterexample:** the Cold Steel problem (C&M 1029–1030): Fort Pitt learned of Torrington's June need in November, after the September subcontract, and argues that "under Hadley v. Baxendale, whatever knowledge it received after the contract was made cannot expand its liability." Both Atlas sentences would charge Fort Pitt simply because the circumstances became known or were told. Hadley requires contemplation "at the time they made the contract" and treats as decisive the circumstances "communicated by the plaintiffs to the defendants at the time the contract was made"; § 351(1) asks what the party had reason to foresee "when the contract was made"; § 2-715(2)(a) speaks of needs of which the seller "at the time of contracting had reason to know." (Whether the November agreement on the delivery date counts as a time of contracting is the problem's open question; see Gap 7.) The brief rule line and the timeline step state the time element correctly; these two fields do not.
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 1017–1018; C&M 1029–1030 Problem; Restatement § 351(1); UCC § 2-715(2)(a).
- **Replacement (Holding):** `Recoverable damages include losses arising naturally in the ordinary course and losses both parties reasonably contemplated, when they made the contract, as a probable result of breach because special circumstances had then been communicated. The mill’s lost profits could not be included on the facts the court accepted; a new trial was required.`
- **Replacement (plain 3.1):** `Telling the other side about “special circumstances,” unusual facts about one’s situation, before the contract is made could make the ordinary consequences of those circumstances recoverable.`

### A9. Rockingham rule line, chronology step 95, timeline step 1 and plain sentence 3.2: recovery "limited to" pre-repudiation costs plus profit

- **Surface/field:** Rockingham, Effective brief CT-NEXT-CM-0151: rule; Chronology 'course-chronology-0' step 95 `note` (same text); Timeline 'damages-limits' step 1 `note`; Cold Call plain-English sentences at "3.2".
- **Current text:** `recovery is limited to labor, materials and expenses in partial performance before repudiation plus the profit the contract would have produced.` (rule and chronology); `after a clear repudiation the other party may not keep performing and charge the cost; recovery is costs to date plus profit.` (timeline step 1).
- **Verdict:** OVERSTATED in two respects. (1) The rule excludes losses the excerpt preserves: Judge Parker's remedy is "such damages as he may have sustained from the breach, including any profit which he would have realized upon performance, as well as any other losses which may have resulted to him," and the editors state that "the promisor must compensate the injured party for costs associated with reasonable mitigation efforts and attempts, whether or not mitigation occurs" (§ 350(2)). (2) The timeline step bars charging "the cost" of any continued performance, where the rule concerns avoidable cost; in goods cases § 2-704(2) allows reasonable completion (A1, Run 2).
- **Counterexample:** after the February 21 notice Luten reasonably spends money securing materials on site and trying to sell them, without success. Under "limited to ... before repudiation plus the profit," the sum is excluded; under Parker's "any other losses" and § 350(2), it is recoverable. The Atlas's own lecture-notes record says the class asks "how to handle reasonable but unsuccessful efforts to find substitute work." The precise "labor, materials and expenses ... plus profit" measure is not in the assigned excerpt (it comes from the omitted part of the opinion).
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 993 (avoidability introduction), 994 (Parker, J.); Restatement § 350(2); UCC § 2-704(2) (C&M 996).
- **Replacement (rule line and chronology step 95, 44 words):** `After an absolute repudiation of an executory contract, a contractor may not continue performing and charge the avoidable cost to the repudiating party. It recovers pre-repudiation expenditures in partial performance, expected profit, and other resulting losses, including reasonable costs of efforts to avoid loss.`
- **Replacement (timeline step 1 note):** `Avoidable loss: after a clear repudiation the other party may not keep performing and charge the avoidable cost; it recovers costs to date, expected profit and other unavoidable loss.`
- **Same fix, plain 3.2:** add after the last sentence: `It could also recover other losses the breach caused, including reasonable costs of trying to avoid loss.`

## C. Teaching gaps (assigned material no surface teaches)

1. **Sales mitigation through market and cover measures (C&M 993, 995–997; UCC §§ 2-704(2), 2-708, 2-712, 2-713 and Comment 5).** No surface explains that the market-price formulas assume a substitute transaction, that § 2-713 Comment 5 makes the market measure "completely alternative to cover" (so a buyer who covered cheaply cannot claim the larger market difference), or the "Waiting for a Windfall?" problem's choice between §§ 2-712 and 2-713. Home: K-32 node b detail (A1 replacement) and the mitigation concept node. Draft: `In sales, the buyer's market measure (§ 2-713) and the seller's (§ 2-708(1)) assume that a substitute transaction was available; a buyer who has covered recovers under § 2-712, because § 2-713 applies only to the extent the buyer has not covered.`
