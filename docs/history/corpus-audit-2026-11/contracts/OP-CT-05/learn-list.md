## 1. Reliance as a basis of enforcement, separate from consideration

- Section 4 opens by separating reliance as an interest a remedy protects from reliance as a reason for
  enforcing a promise at all; reliance means a change of position by the promisee (p. 120).
- The casebook frames the trade-off: reliance relieves injustice where consideration is missing (Feinberg,
  Kirksey's Sister Antillico), but dropping the consideration requirement risks unfair outcomes (p. 120).
- Holmes's objection: letting a promisee make a gratuitous promise binding by acting on it "would cut up the
  doctrine of consideration by the roots" (Commonwealth v. Scituate Savings Bank, quoted p. 124).
- Gilmore's thesis that § 90 "swallowed up" the bargain principle of First Restatement § 75, leaving the wholly
  executory exchange as the only place § 75 is needed; Atiyah's response that courts are reluctant to enforce
  promises neither paid for nor relied on (p. 130).

## 2. The § 90 elements and "injustice"

- First Restatement § 90 (p. 126): a promise the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or
  forbearance "of a definite and substantial character" on the part of the promisee, which does induce it, is
  binding "if injustice can be avoided only by enforcement of the promise."
- Second Restatement § 90(1) (pp. 128–129; Selections): drops "definite and substantial," adds reliance by "a
  third person," and adds "The remedy granted for breach may be limited as justice requires." The casebook asks
  whether the deletion and the new sentence are related (p. 128).
- Comment b: the principle is "flexible"; the promisor is affected only by reliance he does or should foresee;
  whether enforcement is needed to avoid injustice may depend on the reasonableness of the reliance, "its
  definite and substantial character in relation to the remedy sought," the formality of the promise, how far
  the commercial setting meets the functions of form, and policies such as enforcing bargains and preventing
  unjust enrichment. Reliance need not be substantial for charitable subscriptions but must be for firm offers
  and guaranties.
- Cohen on remand (Minnesota): (1) a clear and definite promise; (2) the promisor intended to induce reliance and
  the promisee relied to his detriment; (3) whether enforcement is required to prevent an injustice is a legal,
  policy question for the court. "[T]he test is not whether the promise should be enforced to do justice, but
  whether enforcement is required to prevent an injustice" (pp. 135–136).
- Courts often speak of "detriment"; the § 90 black letter does not use the word (Selections; the casebook's
  question on this is at p. 134, just outside the assignment). Vastoler treated the stress of a supervisory job
  as possible detrimental reliance even though the promotion paid more (p. 135).
- Feinberg: the induced action was the 1949 retirement from a well-paid job; her later cancer was not the
  "action or forbearance" induced, and injustice did not depend on when the disability arose (pp. 127–128).
- Hayes problem: the employee announced retirement before the assurance; the casebook asks whether Feinberg
  controls (p. 128). Inducement is a causal requirement.

## 3. Remedy "limited as justice requires"

- Williston on the ALI floor: either the promise is binding and "has to be enforced as it is made," or recovery
  restores the status quo; "you have to take one leg or the other" (p. 126).
- Second Restatement sentence: the remedy "may be limited as justice requires" (p. 128).
- Comment d: a promise binding under § 90 is a contract and "full-scale enforcement by normal remedies is often
  appropriate"; relief may be limited to restitution or to reliance damages; unless the promisor is unjustly
  enriched, damages should not exceed what performance would have given; for gift promises consequential
  damages heavier than performance would rarely be proper. Illustrations 8 and 10 award expenses and losses but
  not lost profits.
- Results in the cases: Ricketts enforced the $2,000 note (p. 123); Feinberg affirmed $5,100 in accrued pension
  installments with interest (pp. 69, 128); Cohen affirmed the $200,000 compensatory verdict (pp. 135–136);
  D&G Stout allowed only reliance injury, and on remand the district court awarded $394,050 (pp. 141–143).
- Fuller and Perdue's uncle and nephew: $1,000 promised, $2,000 lost in reliance (p. 129). The problem on p. 130
  asks which of Scothorn, Feinberg and Kirksey should be limited to reliance recovery.

## 4. Charitable subscriptions and marriage settlements, § 90(2)

- Second Restatement § 90(2): a charitable subscription or marriage settlement is binding under subsection (1)
  "without proof that the promise induced action or forbearance" (p. 129; Selections). Comment f: a probability
  of reliance is enough. Illustration 18 treats a marriage as reliance on a father's formal written promise.
- Earlier routes for charitable pledges: a bargain among subscribers, especially a "bellwether" pledge
  conditioned on others' pledges (Congregation B'nai Sholom v. Martin); an undertaking by the charity in exchange,
  as in Allegheny College, whose promissory-estoppel passage is dictum (pp. 125–126).
- The law "remains unsettled; most jurisdictions have not even addressed the matter." King v. Trustees of Boston
  University (Mass. 1995) still required consideration or reliance; Congregation Kadimah Toras-Moshe v. DeLeo
  found no injustice in refusing to enforce an oral $25,000 promise to a synagogue (p. 129).

## 5. The shift from equitable estoppel to promissory estoppel

- Ricketts: no consideration, because the grandfather requested nothing and the resignation was not done in
  fulfillment of any obligation (p. 122); the court reasons that charitable-note cases rest on estoppel and
  applies "equitable estoppel" (Pomeroy's definition) because he "intentionally influenced" her to change
  position for the worse; "whether he did or not" desire it, he contemplated such action as "a reasonable [and
  probable] consequence of his gift" (pp. 122–123; bracket marks words lost in the OCR).
- Note 2: equitable estoppel traditionally rests on a representation of fact; Scothorn relied on a promise, so
  the doctrine is now called promissory estoppel (p. 123). Comment a to § 90 says the same.
- Note 1 hypotheticals: no mention of quitting work; reliance by buying a horse to ride to work; could a court
  have found a bargain (compare Hamer v. Sidway); how might Ricketts have structured the promise (p. 123).
- The four historical categories: family promises; promises to convey land relied on by moving onto and
  improving it (Freeman v. Freeman); promises coupled with gratuitous bailments (Siegel v. Spear); charitable
  subscriptions (pp. 124–125).

## 6. Past consideration and moral obligation (contrast)

- Feinberg: 37 years of past service could not be consideration; continued employment was not either, because
  the resolution did not condition the pension on further work and she made no promise to stay; she testified
  she would have kept working whether or not the resolution passed (pp. 70, 72).
- Note 2 asks whether reciting "in consideration of her many years of long and faithful service" would have
  helped (p. 73). The Prentis problem uses a naming agreement made "in recognition of" past gifts (pp. 73–74).
- Mills v. Wyman begins at p. 74: a promise made after services were rendered unrequested to an adult son, on
  "moral obligation," is not enforceable. Restatement § 86 illustration 1 treats those facts as not binding.
  (Only the opening page is assigned; § 86 is not relied on in the assigned pages.)
- DeMentas v. Tallas problem: a notarized memorandum promising $50,000 for past companionship; consideration,
  moral obligation, writing and notarization each tested (p. 120).

## 7. Promises in at-will settings (D&G Stout)

- Bacardi, an at-will supplier, twice assured General it would keep its line; General rejected National's offer;
  Bacardi withdrew the same afternoon; the later sale was $550,000 lower (pp. 138–139).
- The relationship stayed at will and the promise was not enforceable for future performance, but General could
  reasonably rely on it (p. 140). Indiana denies lost future wages on an at-will job promise but allows reliance
  costs such as moving expenses and wages forgone while preparing to move (Eby, Pepsi-Cola) (pp. 140–141).
- The line Indiana draws is between expectation and reliance damages, not between out-of-pocket and forgone
  amounts (p. 141). The price drop was reliance injury: Bacardi's withdrawal destroyed General's option to
  continue and its negotiating leverage; National never expected to keep the Bacardi account (pp. 141–142).
- How long reliance on an at-will assurance stays reasonable is a trial question (pp. 142–143).
- Note 1: was "no intention of taking its line" a promise? Note 2: on remand the promise was found conditional,
  reliance still reasonable, award $394,050; the casebook suggests it looks like "lost opportunities" damages
  denied elsewhere (p. 143).

## 8. The First Amendment framing in Cohen

- Cohen I (Minn.): no contract because the parties were not thinking in terms of a legally binding commitment;
  promissory estoppel would violate the First Amendment (p. 135 headnote).
- U.S. Supreme Court: applying generally applicable promissory-estoppel law to confidentiality promises does not
  offend the First Amendment, because its effect on news gathering and reporting is only "incidental";
  remanded (p. 135). Slide 18 quotes Justice White.
- On remand: promissory estoppel satisfied; the papers' own testimony that sources' promises must be kept and the
  absence of any "compelling need" to break the promise made denial of recourse unjust (pp. 135–136).
- Note: if the commitment was only "moral and ethical," was reliance justified? Reliance shows the promisee took
  the promise seriously, not that the promisor did; might the understanding be a bargain (pp. 136–137)?

## 9. Unjust enrichment and restitution without a contract

- Restitution here is a theory of recovery, not only a measure of damages; liability arises from receipt of a
  benefit whose retention without payment would be unjust, and can arise "even when there has been no promise"
  (Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 1 cmt. a; Dawson) (p. 145).
- Examples: overpayment by mistake; services performed by mistake; a house built on the wrong lot (R3d § 10
  illustration) (p. 145).
- Limit: a person who confers a benefit without justifying circumstances (an "officious intermeddler") has no
  restitution claim for an unrequested benefit; Posner's serenading musicians: when bargaining is cheap, parties
  should set the price in advance (pp. 145–146).
- Vocabulary: quasi-contract, quantum meruit, quantum valebant; restitution is the broader term (p. 146).
- Cotnam v. Wisdom (emergency surgery on an unconscious victim) begins on p. 146; only its opening is assigned.
- Restatement § 371 (Selections): the restitution interest is measured by (a) what the benefit would have cost
  the recipient to obtain from someone in the claimant's position, or (b) the increase in the recipient's
  property or other interests. Slide 3 gives the same two measures.
