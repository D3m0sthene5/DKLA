## A. Statements that change a legal result

### A1. Pacific Gas: plain-English gloss of "indemnify" adopts the losing reading

- **Surface/field:** pacific-gas, Cold Call plain-English sentences, `"at": "0.0"` and the first `"at": "1.0"` item.
- **Current text:** `(repay it)` (in "It also promised to \"indemnify\" PG&E (repay it) for losses") and `the contract duty to repay its losses`.
- **Verdict:** CHANGES RESULT.
- **Counterexample:** The case turned on whether "indemnify" in this clause meant reimbursing PG&E for damage to its own property or only protecting PG&E against liability to third parties. Glossing the word as "repay it for losses" builds PG&E's first-party reading into the definition. A student who reads only the gloss will conclude that the clause plainly covers damage to PG&E's rotor and that the trial court was right to exclude Thomas's evidence, which is the ruling the California Supreme Court reversed. Footnote 6 rests the alternative holding on "the double sense in which the word 'indemnify' is used in statutes and defined in dictionaries," and the continuation of the footnote contrasts Civ. Code § 2772 ("engages to save another from a legal consequence of the conduct of one of the parties, or of some other person") with § 2778.
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 549 (Thomas's offer of proof that the parties "meant to cover injury to property of third parties only"); 551 n.6; 552 (n.6 continuation, OCR degraded).
- **Replacement (0.0 plain):** `It also promised to "indemnify" PG&E (protect it) against losses, "damages", expenses, and "liability" (legal responsibility) arising from injury to property connected with "performance" of the work. Whether that protection reached PG&E's own property or only claims by others was the disputed question.`
- **Replacement (1.0 plain, gloss only):** `contractual "indemnity" (the contract promise to protect PG&E against loss, which PG&E read to cover damage to its own property)`

### A2. Greenfield: New York described as reading the contract "from its text alone"

- **Surface/field:** greenfield, Cold Call plain-English sentences, `"at": "3.1"`.
- **Current text:** `from its text alone` (in "New York reads the \"recording\" contract by a \"four-corners\" approach, from its text alone.")
- **Verdict:** CHANGES RESULT.
- **Counterexample:** New York decides whether a writing is ambiguous from its four corners. If the writing is ambiguous, extrinsic evidence may then be considered. A student told that New York reads a contract "from its text alone" will exclude extrinsic evidence even after finding the writing ambiguous on its face. Under the two-stage rule the casebook describes, that evidence "will be admitted to inform the court's determination of the meaning." This is the error of treating the New York rule as a bar on extrinsic evidence once ambiguity is found.
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 548 (second stage of the plain meaning rule); 555 (W.W.W.: "reaching extrinsic evidence only when required to do so because of some identified ambiguity"); 558 (Greenfield: "Extrinsic evidence of the parties' intent may be considered only if the agreement is ambiguous").
- **Replacement (first sentence of the plain text):** `New York decides from the recording contract's text alone (its "four corners") whether it is ambiguous, and considers outside evidence only if it is.` The rest of the sentence can stay.

### A3. "Ambiguity" glossed as "unclear" in four plain-English sentences

- **Surface/field:** www-associates Cold Call plain `"at": "2.0"` (second item); greenfield Cold Call plain `"at": "2.0"` (first item); trident Cold Call plain `"at": "1.2"` (third item) and `"at": "2.1"` (second item).
- **Current text:** `whether contract words are unclear`; `(unclear wording)`; `no unclearness on the documents`; `\"ambiguity\" (unclearness)`.
- **Verdict:** OVERSTATED. The effect is to change the result.
- **Counterexample:** In Greenfield the contract was silent about synchronization licensing, which makes it "unclear" about that use in ordinary speech. A student who applies the gloss will find an ambiguity, admit the artists' extrinsic evidence, and reverse Greenfield's holding. The legal test is narrower. Under it, a writing is unambiguous if its language has "a definite and precise meaning ... concerning which there is no reasonable basis for a difference of opinion," and it is unambiguous if it is "reasonably susceptible of only one meaning." Silence about a use "does not" create an ambiguity. The casebook also separates ambiguity (more than one sense) from vagueness (borderline cases). "Unclear" covers both, as well as gaps.
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 558–559; 564 (vagueness distinguished from ambiguity); slide 5.
- **Replacement (each gloss):** `"ambiguity" (language reasonably open to more than one meaning)`. For Trident 1.2, use `no "ambiguity" on the documents' face, no language reasonably open to more than one meaning, that would supply ...`.

### A4. Trident: map notes say Trident could offer evidence "to vary" the documents

- **Surface/field:** trident, Map entry: notes.
- **Current text:** `to vary the unambiguous documents` (in "under Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. G.W. Thomas Drayage & Rigging Co. Trident could nevertheless offer extrinsic evidence to vary the unambiguous documents").
- **Verdict:** CHANGES RESULT.
- **Counterexample:** Pacific Gas keeps the rule that "extrinsic evidence is not admissible to add to, detract from, or vary the terms of a written contract." It also makes evidence offered to prove a meaning the language is not reasonably susceptible to inadmissible. A student relying on the note would conclude that a California party may prove an oral bargain contradicting a clear integrated term, for example a right to prepay notwithstanding "shall not have the right to prepay." The Ninth Circuit ordered something narrower: "an opportunity to present extrinsic evidence as to the intention of the parties in drafting the contract." The word "vary" echoes Trident's argument and Kozinski's polemical description (561–562), not the disposition. The same note's "the note's terms did not entitle Trident to prepay" also lacks the opinion's qualifier "as written." The Rule 11 holding is not in the assigned excerpt.
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 550–551 & n.4 (Pacific Gas); 561 (Trident's argument); 562 ("as written, is not reasonably susceptible"); 563 (disposition).
- **Replacement (the "held three things" clause):** `held three things: the note's text, read without extrinsic evidence, gave Trident no right to prepay; under Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. G.W. Thomas Drayage & Rigging Co. Trident must nevertheless be allowed to offer extrinsic evidence of the parties' intended meaning; and the district court's Rule 11 sanctions were reversed.`

### A5. Trident brief Holding: the "not reasonably susceptible" finding lacks its "as written" qualifier

- **Surface/field:** trident, Effective brief CT-CB-0560: Holding (not covered by prior-corrections.json, which corrected only the rule line).
- **Current text:** `held that the written documents were not reasonably susceptible`.
- **Verdict:** MISSING CONDITION.
- **Counterexample:** Under Pacific Gas, a court decides reasonable susceptibility after it has preliminarily considered the offered evidence. Once a court holds the language not reasonably susceptible, the evidence is inadmissible (n.4). Read without the qualifier, the Holding has the court finally deciding non-susceptibility and then requiring the evidence anyway, which is inconsistent. A student applying it would either dismiss the case, repeating the district court's error, or conclude that evidence comes in even after a final non-susceptibility ruling. Kozinski limited the point to the contract "as written."
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 551 n.4; 562; 563.
- **Replacement:** `held that the written documents, read without extrinsic evidence, were not reasonably susceptible to Trident's reading and gave the borrower no option to prepay.` The rest of the Holding can stay.

### A6. W.W.W.: rule line states New York's rule as universal and drops "on its face"

- **Surface/field:** www-associates, Effective brief CT-CB-0553: rule; the same text appears in Chronology 'course-chronology-0' step 370.
- **Current text:** `A complete, clear and unambiguous written contract is enforced` (the full line continues "according to its terms, and extrinsic evidence of what the parties intended may not be used to create an ambiguity in it or to add to or vary it").
- **Verdict:** MISSING CONDITION. The effect is to change the result outside New York.
- **Counterexample:** Under California law, a court must preliminarily consider credible extrinsic evidence, and that evidence may expose an ambiguity in a writing that looks clear (PG&E n.5). A student applying the line as stated to a California contract would exclude the evidence, which is the ruling reversed in Pacific Gas and in Trident. The line also drops two conditions. The first is "upon its face," because New York judges clarity from the writing itself. The second is the source's hedges "as a rule" and "generally inadmissible." In the chronology, where no case caption is shown, the missing jurisdiction matters most.
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 548 (California and New York as "exemplars of opposite approaches"); 551 & n.5; 555 ("as a rule"; "generally inadmissible"; "complete and clear and unambiguous upon its face"; ambiguity "a question of law").
- **Replacement (46 words):** `Under New York law, a complete written contract that is clear and unambiguous on its face is generally enforced by its terms. Ambiguity is a question of law decided from the writing, and extrinsic evidence cannot create an ambiguity or add to or vary the writing.`

Philles still owed the royalties the contract required.`
