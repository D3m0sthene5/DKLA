## 1. The traditional two-stage plain meaning rule (p. 548)

- Rule. Courts have traditionally used a two-stage process "often referred to as the 'plain meaning rule.'" In the first stage the judge decides whether the written language, "with respect to the dispute in question, admits of only one plausible meaning or, rather, is ambiguous." If it is not ambiguous, extrinsic evidence of its meaning is excluded and, in the second stage, the court fixes the meaning without extrinsic evidence. If it is ambiguous, extrinsic evidence is admitted "to inform the court's determination of the meaning." (p. 548)
- Condition. The process "is generally used only for completely integrated agreements," so it "is often regarded as a corollary of the parol evidence rule." (p. 548)
- The contested question is what evidence the court may consider at the first stage to decide whether there is ambiguity. California and New York "are often seen as exemplars of opposite approaches." (p. 548; slide 16 lists "Can extrinsic evidence be used to determine whether a writing is ambiguous?" as a contentious issue)
- Framing quotations: Thayer's denial of a "lawyers' Paradise" in which words have fixed meanings; Holmes in Towne v. Eisner: a word "is the skin of a living thought." (p. 548)

## 2. California: Pacific Gas & Electric v. G.W. Thomas Drayage & Rigging (Cal. 1968, Traynor, C.J.) (pp. 548–552)

- Facts. Defendant contracted to remove and replace the upper metal cover of plaintiff's steam turbine "at [its] own risk and expense" and to "indemnify" plaintiff "against all loss, damage, expense and liability resulting from ... injury to property, arising out of or in any way connected with the performance of this contract," and to procure at least $50,000 of property-damage insurance naming plaintiff as additional insured. The cover fell and damaged the turbine's exposed rotor; plaintiff sued for $25,144.51 in repair costs, dismissed its negligence count, and won judgment on the theory that the clause covered injury to all property regardless of ownership. (pp. 548–549)
- Proffered evidence. Defendant offered "admissions of plaintiff's agents," "defendant's conduct under similar contracts entered into with plaintiff," and "other proof" that the clause was meant to cover injury to third parties' property only. The trial court called the language "the classic language for a third party indemnity provision" but held that its "plain language" also covered plaintiff's property and refused all extrinsic evidence contradicting that reading. (p. 549)
- Rule (the "reasonably susceptible" test). "The test of admissibility of extrinsic evidence to explain the meaning of a written instrument is not whether it appears to the court to be plain and unambiguous on its face, but whether the offered evidence is relevant to prove a meaning to which the language of the instrument is reasonably susceptible." (pp. 549–550)
- Procedure. "[R]ational interpretation requires at least a preliminary consideration of all credible evidence offered to prove the intention of the parties," including the circumstances surrounding the making of the agreement, so the court can place itself in the parties' situation. If the court decides, after considering that evidence, that the language "in the light of all the circumstances" is "fairly susceptible of either one of the two interpretations contended for," evidence relevant to prove either meaning is admissible. (p. 551)
- Provisional (conditional) receipt. Footnote 4: when an objection is made to an item of evidence, the trial court may not yet know whether it will turn out to support a meaning the language can reasonably bear, so it "may admit the evidence conditionally by either reserving its ruling on the objection or by admitting the evidence subject to a motion to strike" (Evid. Code § 403). (p. 551 n.4)
- Footnote 5: admitting evidence "on the stated ground that the contract was ambiguous" is harmless "if it is kept in mind that the ambiguity may be exposed by extrinsic evidence that reveals more than one possible meaning." (p. 551 n.5)
- Footnote 6 (alternative ground): exclusion "would be error even under a rule that excluded such evidence when the instrument appeared to the court to be clear and unambiguous on its face," because the trial court's own recognition of third-party-indemnity language and the "double sense" of "indemnify" in statutes and dictionaries "demonstrate the existence of an ambiguity." (p. 551 n.6; statutes at p. 552 n.6 continuation)
- Limit retained. "[E]xtrinsic evidence is not admissible to add to, detract from, or vary the terms of a written contract," but the terms "must first be determined before it can be decided whether or not extrinsic evidence is being offered for a prohibited purpose." (pp. 550–551)
- Rationale. Intention of the parties as expressed in the contract is the source of contractual rights in California; words "do not have absolute and constant referents"; a four-corners rule would "either deny the relevance of the intention of the parties or presuppose a degree of verbal precision and stability our language has not attained"; excluding evidence that might contradict the judge's linguistic background reflects belief in "perfect verbal expression," "a remnant of a primitive faith in the inherent potency and inherent meaning of words." The court rejects the Hotchkiss/"magic words" view that obligations arise from the words regardless of intent. (pp. 549–550 & n.3)
- Holding and disposition. The trial court "erroneously refused to consider" the evidence; it was relevant and admissible to show the clause was reasonably susceptible of defendant's meaning and, because it was so susceptible, admissible to prove that meaning. "The judgment is reversed." Peters, Mosk, Burke, Sullivan and Peek concur; McComb dissents. (pp. 551–552) The court did not itself decide that the clause covered only third-party property; it held the evidence admissible.
- Notes. (1) Does California still have a plain meaning rule, and how different is the PG&E two-stage process from p. 548? (2) Why footnote 6? (3) Delta Dynamics v. Arioto (Cal. 1968, four months later): termination clause "reasonably susceptible" of being the exclusive remedy for missing the quota; nothing in the rest of the contract precludes that reading and the attorneys' fees clause keeps effect for other breaches; exclusion was prejudicial error. Masterson, PG&E and Delta Dynamics were all written by Traynor within a year, with declining support; Delta Dynamics was 4–3, and Justice Mosk, dissenting, "confessed" misgivings about the earlier decisions as "a course leading toward emasculation of the parol evidence rule" and urged "a halt." (pp. 552–553)
- Slide 18 asks: what meaning defendant gave the clause; what evidence it offered; how Traynor justified admission; and the countervailing policy concerns.

## 3. New York: W.W.W. Associates v. Giancontieri (N.Y. 1990, Kaye, J.) (pp. 553–555)

- Facts. Sellers contracted to sell real property to a developer, closing "on or about December 1, 1986." Paragraph 31 acknowledged a pending lawsuit concerning the property; if closing was delayed by it, closing would be adjourned until the litigation ended, "provided, in the event such litigation is not concluded, by or before 6-1-87 either party shall have the right to cancel this contract," with the down payment returned. The buyer wrote that it was ready to close; on June 2, 1987 the sellers canceled and returned the down payment; the buyer refused it and sued for specific performance. (p. 553)
- Extrinsic evidence. Buyer's vice-president's affidavit: paragraph 31 was added for the buyer's "sole benefit" because a lis pendens (a recorded notice of pending litigation affecting title) threatened its construction financing; the broker reported that a seller said they were doing nothing to defend the suit, awaiting June 2 to cancel, and hoped for a higher price. Sellers made no response to these factual assertions and relied only on the contract language. (p. 554)
- Procedural history. Trial court granted sellers summary judgment (agreement unambiguously gave both parties the right to cancel). Appellate Division reversed, adopted the buyer's facts, and directed specific performance, relying on the rule that a party for whose sole benefit a condition is included may waive it before the deadline. Court of Appeals reversed and dismissed the complaint. (p. 554)
- Holding. The extrinsic evidence "is not material." "[B]efore looking to evidence of what was in the parties' minds, a court must give due weight to what was in their contract." Extrinsic and parol evidence "is not admissible to create an ambiguity in a written agreement which is complete and clear and unambiguous upon its face" (quoting Intercontinental Planning v. Daystrom). "Whether or not a writing is ambiguous is a question of law to be resolved by the courts." (pp. 554–555)
- Rationale. When parties set down their agreement "in a clear, complete document," the writing should as a rule be enforced according to its terms; the rule imparts "stability to commercial transactions" by guarding against fraudulent claims, perjury, death of witnesses, infirmity of memory, and the fear that the jury will misjudge extrinsic evidence; these considerations are "all the more compelling" in real property transactions. An analysis that begins with extrinsic evidence instead of what the parties said "unnecessarily denigrates the contract and unsettles the law." (p. 555)
- Point to notice. The buyer's evidence was undisputed; it lost because the evidence was immaterial once the clause ("either party") was found unambiguous on its face.
- Slide 20 asks how the buyer sought to use the evidence and whether the result would differ under California law; the sources do not answer the second question.

## 4. Notes after W.W.W. (pp. 555–556)

- Posner, FDIC v. W.R. Grace (7th Cir. 1989): the rule excluding extrinsic evidence if the contract is clear on its face "is not ridiculous"; it cuts litigation partly by reducing the jury's role, since the jury interprets when extrinsic evidence is needed; parties may prefer ex ante to avoid jury expense and uncertainty "even at the cost of some inflexibility." (p. 555)
- UNIDROIT Principles art. 4.3: regard is had "to all the circumstances, including preliminary negotiations"; because it has no first admissibility stage it "is not a plain meaning rule"; the note asks whether its main use in international arbitration with sophisticated arbitrators and limited discovery matters. (pp. 555–556)
- Variation by contract (note 3): could California parties preferring W.W.W. contract out of PG&E's first-stage admission? Choice of New York law is left to conflict of laws. Garden State Plaza v. S.S. Kresge (N.J. Super. 1963) held "void as against public policy" a clause that no previous negotiations "shall be used to interpret or construe this lease," because it would require the court to construe the contract "wearing judicial blinders." The note asks whether "plain" language would make a difference. This is the casebook's only treatment in the assigned pages of a clause that tries to control interpretive evidence. (p. 556) Slide 19 asks "Can parties in California opt out of the PG&E rule?"; the sources pose but do not answer it.

## 5. New York: Greenfield v. Philles Records (N.Y. 2002, Graffeo, J.) (pp. 556–560)

- Facts. The Ronettes signed a 1963 five-year personal services recording contract with Phil Spector's Philles Records, without counsel, on a two-page form widely used by producers; they received a single collective advance of about $15,000 and no royalties after that. Defendants later licensed the masters for film and television ("synchronization," e.g., "Be My Baby" in "Dirty Dancing" in 1987), licensed third-party domestic redistribution, and sold compilations, paying no royalties. Trial court awarded about $3 million; Appellate Division affirmed, holding the contract did not specifically transfer synchronization and domestic licensing rights, allowed unjust enrichment, and applied the industry's standard 50% royalty rate. (pp. 556–557)
- Ownership clause: recordings and performances "shall be entirely [Philles'] property, free of any claims whatsoever"; "Without limitation of the foregoing," Philles may make reproductions "by any method now or hereafter known." Plaintiffs conceded the contract unambiguously gave unconditional ownership of the masters but argued silence about new media withheld those uses. (p. 558; slide 21)
- Rule. Agreements are construed according to the parties' intent; the best evidence of intent is the writing; a written agreement "complete, clear and unambiguous on its face must be enforced according to the plain meaning of its terms." Extrinsic evidence "may be considered only if the agreement is ambiguous, which is an issue of law for the courts to decide." A contract is unambiguous if its language has "a definite and precise meaning, unattended by danger of misconception ... and concerning which there is no reasonable basis for a difference of opinion." If the agreement on its face "is reasonably susceptible of only one meaning," a court may not alter it to reflect "its personal notions of fairness and equity." (pp. 558–559)
- What counts as ambiguity. The contract's "silence on synchronization and domestic licensing" does not "create an ambiguity which opens the door to the admissibility of extrinsic evidence." Absent "an explicit contractual reservation of rights by the artists," the transfer of full ownership carried the unconditional right to redistribute in any format, "subject to payment of applicable royalties due plaintiffs." (pp. 556, 559)
- Release issue under California law. Defendants argued Greenfield's 1974 divorce-settlement general release barred her share. The court applied California law (where the release was executed and the divorce finalized), contrasting New York's "four corners" rule with California's preliminary consideration of all credible evidence and quoting PG&E's test. Trial court's finding that her compensation under the 1963 contract "was not an intended subject of the release," affirmed below, was supported by the record; she shares in damages. (p. 559)
- Disposition. Order of the Appellate Division "modified ... and the case remitted to Supreme Court for further proceedings," and "as so modified, affirmed." The excerpt elides (by "....") what the modification did to the damages; the 50% unjust-enrichment royalty point is not resolved in the assigned text. (p. 559)
- Slide 23 (outside the casebook excerpt): Graffeo also rejected the argument that the royalty schedule limits ownership rights ("it does not inhibit defendants' ability to use the master recordings"), acknowledged that the result "effectively prevents plaintiffs from sharing in the profits ... from synchronization licensing," and stated: "Our guiding principle must be to neutrally apply the rules of contract interpretation because only in this way can we ensure stability in the law and provide guidance to parties."
- Notes. (1) Was the contract unambiguous, and would the answer change without "without limitation of the foregoing"? (2) The release ruling applied California law; did the court describe it fairly, and how would the first issue come out under California law? (p. 560) Slide 22 adds: what if it had been ambiguous; why was the claim not barred by the release; policy justifications; how the Ronettes could have protected themselves.

## 6. Trident Center v. Connecticut General Life Ins. (9th Cir. 1988, Kozinski, J.) (pp. 560–564)

- Facts. Plaintiff partnership (an insurer and two large Los Angeles law firms) borrowed $56.5 million from Connecticut General at 12 1/4% for 15 years, secured by a deed of trust. The note: maker "shall not have the right to prepay the principal amount hereof in whole or in part" for the first 12 years (before January 1996); prepayment allowed in years 13–15 with a sliding fee; on default in years 1–12 the lender may accelerate and add a 10% prepayment fee. When rates fell, Trident sued for a declaration that it could prepay now subject only to a 10% fee. The district court dismissed and sua sponte sanctioned Trident for a frivolous suit; Trident appealed both rulings. (pp. 560–561)
- Contract ruling. It is "difficult to imagine language that more clearly or unambiguously expresses the idea that Trident may not unilaterally prepay"; Trident's reading of the default-prepayment-fee clause as an option to prepay is rejected "out of hand." (p. 561) Under traditional principles, extrinsic evidence is inadmissible "to interpret, vary or add to the terms of an unambiguous integrated written instrument." (p. 562)
- Criticism of PG&E. PG&E "turned its back on the notion that a contract can ever have a plain meaning discernible by a court without resort to extrinsic evidence"; under it, no contract, however clearly written, integrated, negotiated, or squarely on point, can be "rendered impervious to attack by parol evidence"; if extrinsic evidence "raises the specter of ambiguity where there was none before," the language is displaced by self-serving testimony; it "casts a long shadow of uncertainty," generates litigation, and "chips away at the foundation of our legal system" by undermining the principle that language meaningfully constrains conduct (statutes, decrees, mandates to lower courts). (pp. 562–563)
- Result. Bound by California law under Erie, the court "must reverse and remand to the district court in order to give plaintiff an opportunity to present extrinsic evidence as to the intention of the parties in drafting the contract," and invites the California Supreme Court to revisit PG&E. (p. 563) The assigned excerpt does not report the court's disposition of the sanctions appeal or what happens on remand.
- Slide 19 questions: does PG&E dictate admission of all such evidence; is Trident likely to prevail on the merits; is the rule consistent with the parties' intentions; would it make more sense for foreign borrowers; can parties opt out. The excerpt answers none directly. Note that the Ninth Circuit itself found the contract not "reasonably susceptible" to Trident's reading (p. 562) yet read PG&E to require an opportunity to present evidence.
- Notes. (1) Which approach do you favor? (2) Under UCC Article 2, must a court find ambiguity before considering extrinsic evidence of meaning; does § 2-202 guide? (p. 564)

## 7. Methods of interpreting ambiguous contracts: vagueness versus ambiguity (pp. 564–567)

- Definitions. A word is vague "when its applicability in marginal situations is uncertain" ("green"; "all the dirt" includes subsoil sand, Highley v. Phillips). A word is ambiguous "when it has two entirely different connotations so that it may be at the same time both appropriate and inappropriate" ("light" applied to dark feathers). Because "ambiguous" is often used to include vagueness, some writers use "ambivalent" or "equivocal" for the narrow sense; example: long versus short "tons" (Chemung: short; Higgins: long). (p. 564) Slide 5: "ambiguous if it has more than one sense. It is vague when it has borderline cases."
- Drafting lesson. Vague terms ("a reasonable time," "good faith and fair dealing," "best efforts") can usefully delegate decisions to a later adjudicator; "Use of ambiguous terms is simply bad drafting." (p. 564)
- Notes. Inconsistent provisions as "a lawyer's version of syntactical ambiguity" (Robinhorne); interpretation at the offer-and-acceptance stage may demand greater precision (Braunstein); punctuation is not a reliable guide (Overhauser, Posner, J.). (pp. 564–565)
- Problems. Drafting exercises: Marion College (Lawrence v. Cain); lottery tickets as "gross sales" (Hartig); 45 miles straight-line but a 55-mile drive (Vantage Technology); pipe insulation "above finished ceilings" (Abbott). Tastings restrictive covenant (Servomation v. Englert) as structural/syntactic ambiguity; "minerals," "matriculated," "exploit"; Steuart v. McChesney right of first refusal at a price tied to "assessment rolls" in a completely integrated agreement, asking whether a court would admit the county's 50%-of-market valuation practice and testimony about negotiations. (pp. 565–567) Slides 5 and 8–11 use the 50-mile, Marion College and Tastings problems; slide 11's speaker notes say the chosen reading of the Tastings clause was supported because the alternative "would render clause c) redundant" and "might be so broad as to contravene public policy" by leaving the restriction on former clients unqualified.

## 8. Canons of construction (pp. 567–569)

- Maxims. Ejusdem generis ("of the same kind": a general term after a list of specifics covers only things similar to the specifics); expressio unius est exclusio alterius (expressing one thing excludes others); noscitur a sociis (a word is known from its associates); contra proferentem (ambiguities construed against the drafter). Footnote illustration: "sedans, SUVs, and minivans" excludes other vehicles (expressio unius); adding "and other motor vehicles" is read in light of the listed types (ejusdem generis; tractor-trailer excluded, motorcycle uncertain); "vehicles" in an otherwise passenger-vehicle contract means passenger vehicles (noscitur a sociis). (p. 567 & nn.b–d)
- Contra proferentem condition. Typically used "only as a matter of last resort after all aids to construction have been employed without a satisfactory result" (Rottkamp v. Eger). (p. 567) Slide 13: "[often described as a last resort]."
- Public interest. Restraints on trade and land use "are to be strictly construed" (North Gate v. National Food Stores); a doctor's covenant not to "establish" a practice within five miles did not bar house calls or treating patients from the area (State ex rel. Youngman v. Calhoun); Sun Oil v. Vickers rejected a reading that would have voided the contract under the antitrust laws. Public interest may affect interpretation as well as enforceability. (pp. 567–568)
- Statutory analogy; purpose interpretation (Heydon's Case's five steps); recitals ("whereas" clauses) and Lord Esher's three rules in Ex parte Dawes: clear recitals govern an ambiguous operative part; a clear operative part prevails over ambiguous recitals; if both are clear but inconsistent, the operative part is preferred. (pp. 568–569)
- Lawyer-drafters. Weiland Tool v. Whitney: ambiguous language resolved against its author, with added weight because he was an experienced lawyer who could have expressed his intent clearly; Gulf Oil: representation by lawyers suggests differences in wording between sections were not inadvertent. (p. 569) Slide 12 adds International Fidelity v. County of Rockland (S.D.N.Y. 2000): sophisticated lawyers are presumed to know how to use parallel and different wording and to know expressio unius.
- Slide 13's list also includes "Purpose" and "Contract must be read as a whole, consistently, giving meaning to every term" (cf. Restatement (Second) §§ 202(2), 203(a), context only, not cited in the assigned pages).
- Farm-animal problem (p. 569; slide 14): "sheep, cows, pigs and other animals" versus a tiger; "sheep, cows and pigs" versus a wild boar; "cats and dogs" versus a gerbil. The casebook gives no answers. On the footnote's taxonomy, list-plus-general-term points to ejusdem generis (tiger) and a closed list points to expressio unius (gerbil); the wild boar is arguable as expressio unius or as noscitur a sociis/narrow reading of "pigs" among domestic livestock. Slide 14 reframes as a landlord's drafting choice and adds a guinea pig.

## 10. Function of judge and jury; scope of review (pp. 574–575)

- The meaning of language "is, strictly speaking, a question of fact," yet interpretation of written agreements without dispute over the words used has often been treated as a question of "law" for the judge, because of distrust of unsophisticated or once-illiterate jurors and a desire for consistency (e.g., standard insurance policies). (p. 574)
- Traynor, Parsons v. Bristol Development (Cal. 1965): it is "solely a judicial function to interpret a written instrument unless the interpretation turns upon the credibility of extrinsic evidence." Friendly, Meyers v. Selznick (2d Cir. 1966): given Corbin's lesson that words are seldom plain, the exception "bids fair largely to swallow the supposed general rule"; reliance on the jury to resolve ambiguities in light of extrinsic evidence is appropriate "save where the form or subject-matter of a particular contract outruns a jury's competence." (p. 574) (Context only: Restatement (Second) § 212(2) assigns interpretation to the trier of fact when it depends on credibility of extrinsic evidence or a choice among reasonable inferences from it, otherwise to the court as a question of law.)
- Scope of review: Ram Construction (3d Cir. 1984): assignment to judge or jury does not itself fix the appellate standard; a trial court's interpretation of a factual matter is reviewed for clear error. Paragon Resources (5th Cir. 1983): ambiguity is a question of law reviewed first; if the contract is ambiguous, the interpretation in light of extrinsic evidence gets clearly-erroneous deference. (p. 575 & n.f) Also W.W.W. and Greenfield: ambiguity is a question of law for the court (pp. 555, 558).

## 11. Supplement: Hall and Elliot, "Can AI Fix Bad Rules?" (July 16, 2026) (Supp. 46–52)

- Claim: an AI-graded rubric applied to the text of prediction-market resolution rules predicts which contracts end up disputed "at a rate far better than chance." (Supp. 46)
- Method: about 10,000 resolution rules from Kalshi and Polymarket, with dispute data; main analyses on Polymarket using UMA's governance-oracle records, splitting "material disputes" (not dismissed as premature) and "confirmed disputes" (those receiving a second dispute request); a 10-dimension rubric developed with Claude, each scored 0 (clear) to 3 (severe ambiguity); grading run by GPT-4.1; sample weighted toward disputed contracts because disputes are rare. (Supp. 46–47)
- Findings: the three most predictive features are a vaguely defined core question, uncertainty about which entity counts, and an unidentified settlement source (Supp. 48); grading bins from A to CCC, with CCC contracts 3.4 times as likely to be disputed as A contracts (Supp. 49).
- Caveats the authors state: possible reverse causation (hard-to-call events may be inherently harder to write rules for); possible leakage because the model may know about a famous disputed case (the "Zelensky suit" market); perfect wording cannot anticipate major future shifts or fix imperfect enforcement. (Supp. 46, 47, 49)
- Link to class: the rubric's features map onto vagueness and ambiguity (p. 564) and support ex ante drafting review; the epigraph from Montaigne asks why ordinary language becomes obscure in contracts. The supplement does not address courts, the plain meaning rule, or admissibility.
