## A. The remedial baseline and the adequacy gate

1. The remedy for breach is presumptively money ("substitutional relief"); specific relief (an order to perform or to refrain) is the exception (C&M 929).
2. The editors say the principal aim of both forms of relief is compensation, and that specific relief "also seeks primarily to compensate promisees for breach rather than to compel promisors to perform" (C&M 929).
3. History: the adequacy test grew out of the jurisdictional struggle between law and equity. Equity stayed its hand if damages at law were "adequate", with the added gloss that damages were ordinarily adequate because a market economy lets the injured party arrange a substitute (C&M 929, quoting Farnsworth § 12.4).
4. Contracts for the sale of land have long been specifically enforced because English tradition treated land as so distinctive that damages were categorically inadequate; family heirlooms are another traditional category; output and requirements contracts are said to reflect a modern trend favoring specific relief (C&M 930).
5. Restatement § 357(1): specific performance "will be granted in the discretion of the court" against a party who has committed or is threatening to commit a breach, subject to §§ 359–69. Relief is discretionary and can be sought against a threatened breach.
6. Restatement § 359(1), the gate: specific performance or an injunction "will not be ordered if damages would be adequate to protect the expectation interest of the injured party." § 359(2): adequate damages for one part do not bar relief as to the whole. § 359(3): another remedy (other than damages) does not by itself bar relief but may be weighed.
7. Restatement ch. 16 Introductory Note, quoted at C&M 42: the traditional goal is compensation, not compulsion; specific performance "has not been granted where compensation in damages is an adequate substitute for the injured party"; willful breaches are not distinguished.

## B. The § 360 factors and uniqueness

8. Restatement § 360: in judging adequacy, three circumstances are "significant": (a) difficulty of proving damages with reasonable certainty; (b) difficulty of procuring a suitable substitute performance with money damages; (c) the likelihood that a damages award could not be collected.
9. Uniqueness is one way of showing factor (b). UCC § 2–716(1): for goods, "Specific performance may be decreed where the goods are unique or in other proper circumstances." § 2–716(2): the decree may include terms on price, damages or other relief.
10. UCC § 2–716 Official Comment 1: the section seeks "a more liberal attitude" than some courts had shown, without impairing the court's discretion. Comment 2: uniqueness is judged "in terms of the total situation which characterizes the contract"; output and requirements contracts with a particular or peculiarly available source are "the typical commercial specific performance situation"; "inability to cover is strong evidence of 'other proper circumstances'". Comment 3 and § 2–716(3): a buyer has replevin for identified goods when cover is unavailable after reasonable effort.
11. Klein v. PepsiCo (4th Cir. 1988), Virginia law:
    - Formation. Janas's March 31 telex said the $4.4 million offer was "subject to a factory inspection satisfactory to the purchaser, and a definitive contract" (supp. 109). After counteroffers, Janas accepted $4.6 million on April 3. The district court found a contract from the confirming telex and conduct: no objection to the telex, the requested $100,000 down payment received, counsel's statement that the sales agreement was "fine", execution of the bill of sale and its delivery to escrow, sending the plane for the inspection, and Rashid's admissions (supp. 110). Intent to memorialize in writing was not necessarily a condition of the contract (supp. 111). Review was for clear error; PepsiCo only disagreed with the characterization (supp. 111).
    - Inspection condition: satisfied because PepsiCo agreed to pay for the turbine-blade repairs; the court did not need the district court's alternative ground that refusal to tender excused it (supp. 111).
    - Remedy, reviewed for abuse of discretion (supp. 110): Virginia's adoption of the UCC "does not abrogate the maxim that specific performance is inappropriate where damages are recoverable and adequate" (Griscom). The district judge had repeatedly said damages would make Klein whole; Klein wanted the plane to resell for profit; "an increase in the cost of a replacement does not merit the remedy of specific performance" (Hilmor) (supp. 111).
    - Not unique: 21 other G-IIs on the market, three roughly comparable; Klein's chief pilot said others could be bought; UJS bought two and offered them to Klein; Klein bid on two others (supp. 111–112).
    - No "other proper circumstance": Klein did not buy another G-II because prices had risen and chose a G-III; "price increases alone are no reason" (supp. 112). Reversed and remanded for a trial on damages (supp. 112).
    - Footnote 1: statute of frauds, intended-beneficiary and rescission arguments rejected on the district court's reasoning (supp. 112).

## C. Limits on specific relief: certainty, fairness, supervision, personal service

12. Restatement § 362: no order unless the terms are "sufficiently certain to provide a basis for an appropriate order"; cmt. b: precision matters because of the contempt power, but courts use the usual aids and "courageous common sense", may order a promisor to choose among alternatives, and may supply missing terms where performance has begun by mutual consent.
13. Restatement § 364(1): relief "will be refused" if unfair because (a) the contract was induced by mistake or unfair practices, (b) relief would cause unreasonable hardship or loss to the party in breach or to third persons, or (c) the exchange is grossly inadequate or the terms otherwise unfair. § 364(2): relief will be granted despite a contract term if denial would cause unreasonable hardship to the party seeking relief or to third persons.
14. Restatement § 366 (in the Selections, not on the prompt's list): no specific enforcement if enforcement or supervision burdens are disproportionate to the advantages of enforcement and the harm from denial.
15. Restatement § 367(1): a promise to render personal service will not be specifically enforced. § 367(2): a promise of exclusive service will not be enforced by an injunction against serving another if the probable result is to compel performance involving personal relations whose enforced continuance is undesirable, or to leave the employee without other reasonable means of making a living.
16. De Rivafinoli v. Corsetti (N.Y. Ch. 1833), Note 3 at C&M 946: Chancellor Walworth refused to order an opera singer to perform, doubting that a court could judge the quality of the performance.
17. Construction contracts (C&M 946–947): courts are more willing to order performance where the construction is on the defendant's land with a conveyance to follow (Floyd v. Watson, W. Va. 1979). Northern Delaware Industrial Development Corp. v. E.W. Bliss Co. (Del. Ch. 1968): the court refused to order a contractor to add 300 workers for a second shift, citing the imprecision of the contract provision and the impracticability of enforcing a mandatory order keeping a specific number of workers on a steel-mill site; on reargument it relied on the principle that a personal-services contract, "even of a unique nature", will not be affirmatively enforced.
18. Note 4, C&M 947: an arbitral award granting specific performance that a court would not have ordered was held enforceable in Grayson-Robinson Stores v. Iris Construction (N.Y. 1960).

## D. Contracting for specific performance

19. Snow Phipps v. KCAKE (Del. Ch. 2021), tail at C&M 945: because Kohlberg breached an obligation that precedent and § 11.14 of the agreement deemed specifically enforceable, plaintiffs got an order to close. Note 1 (C&M 945): courts may order specific performance not only on their own discretionary or conventional grounds but "also when the parties' agreement expresses a clear intention for specific performance"; merger parties now commonly stipulate it (IBP). The Delaware three-part test (valid contract; plaintiff ready, willing and able; balance of equities) is on 944, outside the assignment.
20. Note 2 (C&M 945–946): Chancery's speed (Twitter v. Musk stay order); "an order of specific performance seldom results in performance within the time the contract requires", so damages for delay are usually appropriate (Osborn: buyer ordered to pay interest on the price from the original closing date). Compare § 358(3) and § 2–716(2).

## E. The injunction and specific performance distinction

21. Restatement § 357(2): an injunction against breach is granted in the court's discretion, subject to §§ 359–69, if (a) the duty is one of forbearance, or (b) the duty is one to act and specific performance would be denied only for reasons inapplicable to an injunction. Specific performance compels the promised act; an injunction forbids conduct that breaches the contract.
22. Walgreen enforces a negative promise (the landlord's promise not to lease to another pharmacy) by forbidding the lease to Phar-Mor; Bliss and De Rivafinoli show the problems of affirmative orders to act.

## F. Posner's cost-benefit framing in Walgreen

23. Facts (editors' bracket, C&M 947–948): Walgreen had a pharmacy in Southgate Mall for decades; the lease barred Sara Creek from leasing space to another store operating a pharmacy; in 1990 Sara Creek announced it would buy out its failing anchor tenant and install Phar-Mor, a deep-discount store with a pharmacy whose entrance would be within "a couple of hundred feet" of Walgreen's. The trial judge entered a permanent injunction against leasing to Phar-Mor until Walgreen's lease expired; Sara Creek's experts said the damages could be readily estimated, Walgreen's employees said they included intangibles such as good will.
24. Sara Creek's argument (C&M 948): damages are the norm; the breach may be "efficient"; injunctions issue only when the damages remedy is inadequate; projecting lost profits is routine.
25. Benefits of an injunction (C&M 948): it shifts the burden of valuing the defendant's conduct from the court to the parties (if Walgreen's damages are smaller than Sara Creek's gain, there is a price for dissolving the injunction), and markets price more accurately than a battle of experts.
26. Costs of an injunction (C&M 949): continuing supervision; costs to third parties; bilateral monopoly, which creates a bargaining range ($1 million cost to Walgreen versus $2 million benefit to Sara Creek, a hypothetical) whose negotiation may be costly or break down, leaving an inefficient result.
27. Damages mirror those costs: no supervision, third-party or bilateral-monopoly cost, but diminished accuracy and the parties' and court's costs of proving and evaluating damages (C&M 949).
28. Rule of decision: the judge weighs all these costs and benefits, "with the understanding that if the balance is even the injunction should be withheld"; the court affirms because the trial judge's approach was "broadly consistent with a proper analysis" (C&M 949).
29. Application (C&M 949–950): ten years left on the lease; Walgreen would have to project revenues and costs over ten years, the effect of Phar-Mor's competition, and discount to present value; all but the last step "fraught with uncertainty". Affirmed. (The excerpt ends here; facts about little supervision and Phar-Mor being a party are not in the assigned excerpt.)
30. Notes (C&M 950): preliminary injunctions weigh likelihood of success and irreparable harm on both sides; Note 2 asks whether Posner weighed the competing irreparable harms and distinguishes permanent from preliminary standards (Laycock).

## G. The economics of remedies (C&M 34–43)

31. Holmes's "bad man": the duty to keep a contract is a prediction that you must pay damages if you do not; the promisor chooses between performing and paying (C&M 34).
32. Efficient breach (C&M 35–36): cost $300, price $600, value to buyer $1,000; a second buyer offers $1,200. With $400 expectation damages the promisee is fully compensated, the seller nets $500 instead of $300, and the second buyer gets the machine. Pareto improvement (no one worse off) versus Kaldor-Hicks efficiency (gainers' gains exceed losers' losses, without actual compensation). The law, unlike Kaldor-Hicks, treats compensation of the promisee as relevant (C&M 36).
33. Efficient investment (C&M 37–38): weak remedies cause underinvestment in reliance; strong remedies, "like specific performance and damages that fully account for investment expenditures", cause overinvestment because the promisee plans as though performance were certain. The result is indeterminacy; foreseeability, certainty and uncompensated fees may curb overinvestment.
34. Morality and fairness also guide behavior (Fuller on the bad man worried about decrees but not friends and customers) (C&M 38).
35. Coase Theorem (C&M 39–41): with zero or low transaction costs and well-defined entitlements, parties bargain to an efficient allocation under any remedy, injunction or damages; Coase himself stressed that transaction costs are always present, so remedies matter.
36. Kansas v. Nebraska, 574 U.S. 445 (2015) (C&M 39–40, 42): Nebraska breached the Republican River Compact; Kansas lost $3.7 million; the Special Master found water far more valuable on Nebraska farmland and recommended $1.8 million partial disgorgement on top of compensation; the Court agreed; all Justices agreed to deny an injunction; Thomas agreed Nebraska breached "knowingly, but not deliberately". The editors' hypothetical ($18.7 million value to Nebraska, $15 million surplus) shows bargaining around an injunction.
37. Notes (C&M 41–43): compact framing and Lincoln's First Inaugural; willful breach and the Restatement ch. 16 Introductory Note; Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 39 gives a restitutionary remedy for "opportunistic breach" where the breach profits the promisor and damages give inadequate protection; liquidated damages and reliance investment; limits of Coase (dignity, the Thirteenth Amendment, externalities, distribution); bargaining failures with three or more parties.

## H. The civil-law contrast (Rowan, supp. 113–123)

38. Civil Code art. 1221 (2016): a creditor, after notice to perform, may seek performance in kind unless performance is impossible or there is a manifest disproportion between its cost to the debtor and its interest for the creditor; the 2018 version adds "acting in good faith" to the debtor (supp. 114). Art. 1341: a creditor may compel performance of a monetary obligation (supp. 114).
39. "Nearly all obligations are specifically enforceable in French law"; old art. 1142 (damages for obligations to do or not do) was read narrowly as confined to obligations of a personal character (supp. 114).
40. Monetary obligations: an "unfettered" right to the price in preference to damages or termination, with no judicial discretion; example of the distance-learning student ordered to pay the remaining 26 months of fees (supp. 115). The délai de grâce (art. 1343-5) lets a court defer payment up to two years for a debtor in difficulty; performance is delayed, not excused (supp. 115–116).
41. Non-monetary obligations: available "as of right"; sale of goods is specifically enforceable whatever the goods and whether or not unique; no duty to mitigate; substitute performance being possible is no ground for refusal (supp. 116–117). Building contracts are enforced, even demolition and reconstruction; supervision concerns "do not seem to have caused the same concern in France as in England" (airline ordered to keep a route open) (supp. 117). Negative promises are restrained (tenant ordered to restore altered premises even though improved) (supp. 117–118). Art. 1222 lets the creditor, with court authorization, destroy what was done in breach and claim the cost (supp. 118).
42. Three situations where specific enforcement is unavailable (supp. 118): (1) impossibility (e.g., the property already bought by a bona fide third party); (2) where enforcement would result in involuntary servitude, as with personal services (a commissioned painting); (3) manifest disproportion between the cost to the defaulting party and the injured party's interest, a new exception. Before the reform there was no discretion and no undue-hardship bar (the house 13 inches too low ordered rebuilt; the pool with three steps instead of four) (supp. 118–119). The 2018 good-faith amendment removes the exception for a party who breaches deliberately in bad faith (supp. 120). The exception is expected to be read narrowly, and specific enforcement is not ruled out merely because damages are adequate (supp. 120).
43. Unilateral promises to contract (art. 1124): revocation during the option period no longer prevents formation, so specific enforcement is now available (supp. 121).
44. The astreinte: an ancillary per-period monetary penalty to coerce compliance, "unequivocally punitive", uninsurable, assessed with regard to bad faith (supp. 121–122); the référé gives interim performance orders (supp. 122).
45. Reasons for wide availability (supp. 122–123): the binding force of contract (pacta sunt servanda); damages seen as inferior and a "favour" to the breacher; transactional security; risk of undercompensation and weak deterrence. Contrast item 2: C&M 929 says American specific relief aims primarily at compensation.
46. Replacement (art. 1222): the creditor may have the obligation performed by another within a reasonable time and at reasonable cost and recover the cost; a hybrid that resembles cost-of-cure damages from a common-law view (supp. 123).

## I. Commonwealth v. Cosby (Pa. 2021), excerpt

47. Facts as the excerpt gives them (supp. 124–127): D.A. Castor concluded a conviction was unlikely; he decided "as the sovereign" not to prosecute so that Cosby could not invoke the Fifth Amendment in Constand's civil suit; he testified that "agreement" was "the wrong word" and that the decision and Cosby's being required to testify "were not connected one to the other". The press release (Feb. 17, 2005) declined charges and said Castor "will reconsider this decision should the need arise". The decision was never reduced to writing. Cosby sat for four depositions without invoking the Fifth Amendment; the suit settled for $3.38 million; nearly a decade later the Commonwealth charged him; habeas petition denied; a mistrial, then conviction.
48. The court is bound by the trial court's findings that there was no formal immunity agreement and that Castor's act was "only ... a unilateral exercise of prosecutorial discretion", consistent with Castor's own insistence that there was no agreement, contract or quid pro quo (supp. 127).
49. Holding as stated: "when a prosecutor makes an unconditional promise of non-prosecution, and when the defendant relies upon that guarantee to the detriment of his constitutional right not to testify, the principle of fundamental fairness that undergirds due process of law ... demands that the promise be enforced" (supp. 127).
50. Contract doctrine used: "basic precepts of contract law ... inform the due process inquiry" (Puckett, McKeever, Martinez) (supp. 127). The court sets out the Pennsylvania elements of promissory estoppel (Crouse v. Cyclops): (1) the promisor acted in a manner he should reasonably have expected to induce the other party to take or not take action; (2) the other party actually did so; (3) injustice would result if the assurance were not enforced (supp. 128). This is the reliance theory of § 90, not bargain contract: the court says plea promises "differ in kind" from unilateral discretion, which "is not per se enforceable in the same way that a bargained-for exchange is under contract law" (supp. 128). Prosecutors "generally are bound by their assurances, particularly when defendants rely to their detriment" (supp. 128).
51. The constitutional anchor: the Fourteenth Amendment and Pa. Const. art. I, § 9; charging discretion is vast but not exempt from fundamental fairness (supp. 128).
52. Remedy link to this class: what the court enforces is the promise itself (non-prosecution), a form of specific enforcement, because the defendant's reliance cannot be undone by money. The specific disposition is not in the assigned excerpt.
