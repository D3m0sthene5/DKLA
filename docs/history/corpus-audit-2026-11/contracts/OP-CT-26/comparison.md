### E1. K-36 node d (vesting) drops two § 311(3) conditions and frames the test in First Restatement terms
- Location: tools/flowcharts/data/contracts.json, chart K-36, node "d", field "detail" (not shown in the surfaces file because it names no principal case).
- Current text: "Then identify a contractual reservation and the governing effect of assent, material reliance, or suit before the attempted change."
- Verdict: CHANGES RESULT.
- Counterexample: Holly and Fox make the Lawrence bargain. Lawrence hears of it and, on his own initiative, writes Fox that he accepts the promise. Holly and Fox then rescind. Under § 311(3) the parties' power to discharge ends only if the beneficiary "manifests assent to it at the request of the promisor or promisee," and only if the assent, reliance or suit comes "before he receives notification of the discharge or modification." Unrequested assent does not end the power, so the rescission is effective. A student following node d sees "assent" listed as protective and takes the edge "No effective revision or right vested" to node f, the opposite result. The phrase "identify a contractual reservation" also states the old rule: under First Restatement § 142 a donee's rights vested at once unless a power to change was reserved (C&M 1195), whereas under § 311(1)–(2) the parties keep the power unless a term of the promise removes it.
- Pinpoint: § 311(1)–(3) (Selections); C&M 1194–1195 (the editors' summary at 1195, "if the beneficiary assents to the promise, then the promise becomes irrevocable," itself omits the request condition while citing § 311(3) and cmt. h).
- Replacement: "First establish the right. Unless a term of the promise forbids it, the parties may discharge or modify the duty by later agreement (§ 311(2)); that power ends if, before notice of the change, the beneficiary materially changed position in justifiable reliance, sued, or assented at the promisor's or promisee's request (§ 311(3))."

### E3. K-37 node b treats any contractual restriction as making an assignment ineffective and omits the material-change limit
- Location: tools/flowcharts/data/contracts.json, chart K-37, node "b", field "detail".
- Current text: "Check transfer language, contractual restrictions, notice and defenses."
- Verdict: CHANGES RESULT (anti-assignment clause) and MISSING CONDITION (material change).
- Counterexample 1: B contracts with A to sell goods; the contract says "A may not assign any rights under this contract." A assigns his right to delivery to C. Under § 322(2)(b) such a term, unless a different intention is manifested, "gives the obligor a right to damages for breach of the terms forbidding assignment but does not render the assignment ineffective." Node b sends a student with "contractual restrictions" down "Transfer ineffective or defense applies" to node h ("the assignee cannot claim it"). If the clause instead bars assignment of "the contract," § 322(1) and UCC § 2–210(4) read it as barring only delegation of the assignor's duties. A right to damages for total breach, or a right earned by full performance, is assignable despite such a term (§ 322(2)(a); UCC § 2–210(2), last sentence).
- Counterexample 2: the casebook's starting example, § 334 ill. 3: B is to furnish all the oil A's family needs for a year; A assigns to C, whose house needs much more oil (C&M 1226–1227). § 317(2)(a) makes a right unassignable where substitution would "materially change the duty of the obligor, or materially increase the burden or risk imposed on him." Node b mentions none of this, so a student would likely take "Valid and enforceable transfer" to node e.
- Pinpoint: C&M 1226–1227; § 317(2)(a)–(c); § 322(1)–(2); UCC § 2–210(2), (4).
- Replacement: "Check transfer language, notice and defenses. A right cannot be assigned if substituting the assignee would materially change the obligor's duty or materially increase its burden or risk, if a statute or public policy forbids it, or if the contract validly precludes it (§ 317(2); UCC § 2–210(2)). Unless it shows a different intention, a term barring assignment of rights gives the obligor damages but does not make the assignment ineffective (§ 322(2)(b)); a ban on assigning 'the contract' bars only delegation (§ 322(1))."

### E4. The delegation concept node understates the rule that delegation never discharges the delegating party without the obligee's assent
- Location: src/data/seedData/nodes-45978ecb29/00388-delegation-5214bf6377.json, field "notes", first paragraph.
- Current text: "A transfer of performance responsibilities does not automatically prove that the original party was discharged; that is a separate question about the agreement and any substitution accepted by the obligee."
- Verdict: MISSING CONDITION.
- Counterexample: Holly delegates to Fox the duty to pay Lawrence (C&M 1216). Fox fails to pay. The Atlas wording ("does not automatically prove") leaves room to argue that the delegation plus Fox's assumption discharged Holly. § 318(3) says neither delegation "nor a contract to assume the duty made with the obligor by the person delegated discharges any duty or liability of the delegating obligor" unless the obligee agrees otherwise; UCC § 2–210(1) says the same for goods. Holly stays liable to Lawrence. The casebook (1217, a context page) calls the rule "so firmly established that any deviation from it can be regarded as an aberration" and says the obligee's assent yields a novation.
- Pinpoint: § 318(3); UCC § 2–210(1); C&M 1216 (Lawrence as delegation), 1217 (context).
- Replacement: "Delegation does not discharge the delegating party: unless the obligee agrees otherwise, the original obligor remains liable even if the delegate has promised to perform (§ 318(3); UCC § 2–210(1)), and the obligee's agreement to a substitution produces a novation."
- Same node, related wording: "whether the obligee has an important interest in that particular person's performance" should use the legal term: "whether the obligee has a substantial interest in having that particular person perform or control the acts promised (§ 318(2); UCC § 2–210(1))." Verdict: MISSING CONDITION (precise term).

### E6. Sisney v. State's rule line states South Dakota's statutory standard as a general rule
- Location: Effective brief CT-NEXT-CM-0184: rule; Chronology 'course-chronology-0' step 555 note.
- Current text: "clearly manifested at formation an intent to benefit that person"
- Verdict: MISSING CONDITION (jurisdiction).

## (c) Teaching gaps (assigned material no surface teaches)

1. The § 302 two-part test and the money limit in (1)(a). Home: third-party-beneficiaries concept node, "notes" (plus the K-36 node b change in E10). Draft: "Under Restatement § 302, a beneficiary is intended only if recognizing a right is appropriate to effectuate the parties' intention and either performance will satisfy the promisee's obligation to pay the beneficiary money (often called a creditor beneficiary) or the promisee intends to give the beneficiary the benefit (often called a donee beneficiary). Everyone else is incidental and acquires no right (§ 315). The First Restatement's creditor category also covered non-money duties; the Second limits (1)(a) to money or its near equivalent (cmt. b; C&M 1175)."
2. The First Restatement categories and why they mattered. Home: vesting-beneficiary concept node, "notes". Draft: "Under the First Restatement, a donee beneficiary's rights vested as soon as the promise was made unless the parties reserved a power to change them, while a creditor beneficiary was protected only after changing position in reliance (§ 142). The Second Restatement drops the distinction for vesting; Detroit Bank v. Chicago Flame Hardening applied the newer rule, so a rescission before vesting left Mrs. Scott with nothing (C&M 1195)."
3. The § 311 vesting rules in full, including § 311(4), the Michigan statute and the Feinberg problem. Home: vesting-beneficiary concept node, "notes" (replacing the sentence beginning "The syllabus assigns an introduction"). Draft: "Absent a no-modification term, promisor and promisee may change or cancel the beneficiary's right by later agreement. The power ends when, before notice of the change, the beneficiary materially relies, sues, or assents at the request of either party (§ 311(3)). If the promisee is paid for an ineffective discharge, the beneficiary can claim that payment (§ 311(4)). Michigan's statute instead vests most rights the moment the promise binds (C&M 1195–1196)."
4. Defenses that run against the beneficiary. Home: K-36 node f, "detail" (add after the first sentence), and the third-party-beneficiaries concept node. Draft: "The promisor may raise defenses arising from the contract itself, such as lack of consideration, the statute of frauds, mistake, incapacity, impracticability or failure of a condition, and claims for the promisee's breach of the same contract, but not claims arising from unrelated dealings with the promisee such as a setoff (§ 309; C&M 1196). A beneficiary who sues on the contract is usually bound by its arbitration clause."
5. An incidental beneficiary's possible reliance claim. Home: K-36 node g, "detail". Draft: "An incidental beneficiary acquires no contract right (§ 315), but § 90(1) protects reliance by 'a third person,' so a reasonably foreseeable, induced reliance may still support a promissory-estoppel claim against the promisor (C&M 1175–1176)."
6. Private rights of action and Astra USA. Home: public-beneficiaries concept node, "notes". Draft: "When a statute gives no private right of action, a claimant cannot get one by suing as a beneficiary of a government contract that merely incorporates the statute's obligations: in Astra USA v. Santa Clara County (2011) such a suit was 'in essence a suit to enforce the statute itself.' Astra does not bar suits on contract terms that do not arise from statutory duties (C&M 1177–1178). Courts generally presume members of the public incidental to government contracts absent a clear contrary intent (Orff, C&M 1199–1200)."
7. Statutory definitions and the direct-performance test. Home: third-party-beneficiaries concept node, "notes". Draft: "Some states define beneficiary rights by statute: Oklahoma and South Dakota protect contracts made 'expressly' for a third person, West Virginia a promise for a person's 'sole benefit,' and Michigan a promise to do something 'directly to or for' the person. The Restatement does not require that performance run directly to the beneficiary: where A secures B's promise to pay A's son's debt to D, both son and D are intended (§ 302 ill. 6; C&M 1176–1177)."
8. Lawrence v. Fox as a delegation, and the creditor's remaining claim against the debtor. Home: lawrence-fox map entry, "learning.topics" → "delegation". Draft: "Holly delegated to Fox his duty to pay Lawrence; the delegation did not release Holly, who remained liable to Lawrence unless Lawrence agreed otherwise (C&M 1216; § 318(3))."
9. Nondelegable duties illustrated. Home: delegation concept node, "notes" (second paragraph). Draft: "A money debt is the standard delegable duty. A performance that depends on who performs is not: an airport could refuse a substitute singer for Pavarotti, though it could not refuse a substitute tenant's rent (Metropolitan Airports Comm'n, C&M 1216). A party worried about delegation can bargain for a no-delegation term."
10. Assignment vocabulary, scope and limits. Home: assignment concept node, "notes" (replace "Also ask about any restriction on transfer, the nature of the right, notice, and defenses under the governing law."). Draft: "The obligee who transfers a right is the assignor, the transferee is the assignee, and the party who owes performance is the obligor. Most assignments of rights to payment are governed by UCC Article 9. A right is assignable unless substitution would materially change or burden the obligor's duty, a statute or public policy forbids it (for example, wage-assignment statutes), or the contract validly precludes it (§ 317(2); C&M 1226–1227)."

## (d) Challenger tests of the highest-risk statements

Each test states the Atlas claim, the strongest attack drawn from the assigned sources, and whether the claim survives.

1. K-36 node d, "the governing effect of assent, material reliance, or suit." Attack: § 311(3) protects only assent requested by promisor or promisee and only events before notice of the change. Fails (E1). The casebook's own summary at 1195 omits the request condition, so the Atlas wording has a casebook source, but the assigned black letter controls and the difference decides the hypothetical in E1.
