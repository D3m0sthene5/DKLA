## 1. What the chapter is about (1163)
1. Privity means the claim rests on a contract between the claimant and the promisor. The chapter studies two limits on that requirement: a third-party beneficiary may enforce a contract it is not party to, and an assignee may enforce a right transferred to it by a party ("the assignee stands in the shoes of the assignor").
2. Delegation is a third arrangement. A party delegates a duty, the delegate assumes it, and when the delegation is effective the person entitled to the performance is usually a third-party beneficiary of the delegate's promise.
3. Most contracts benefit non-parties (the restaurant example, 1163–1164). Life insurance is the most familiar contract made for a third person. Construction, will-drafting and government contracts are the other settings the chapter uses.

## 2. History of third-party rights (1164–1165)
4. Dutton v. Poole (K.B. 1677) let a daughter enforce a promise made to her father to pay her £1,000, although she gave nothing for it. Tweddle v. Atkinson (Q.B. 1861) disapproved that approach: a person cannot be a party for suing and not for being sued. English common law never developed robust beneficiary rights; the casebook says statutory intervention came "in 1979" (the casebook's date; the UK Act usually cited is 1999, so treat any Atlas date with care and cite the casebook).
5. The casebook dates modern U.S. beneficiary law from Lawrence v. Fox (1859).
6. The editors' introduction explains the trust problem. A trust requires a res (a specific asset). If A delivers plastic to B with instructions to make dolls for C, B is a trustee and C can enforce. If B instead promises A to buy plastic and deliver dolls to C, there is no trust asset, and some judges thought C could not sue. Restatement § 302 cmt. f compares trust, agency and beneficiary relationships.

## 3. Lawrence v. Fox (N.Y. 1859) (1165–1170)
7. Facts: Holly lent Fox $300 at Fox's request, saying he owed Lawrence that sum and had agreed to pay it the next day; Fox, in consideration, promised to pay Lawrence the next day. A bystander testified to this.
8. Fox's three nonsuit grounds: no proof Holly owed Lawrence; no consideration; no privity. Jury verdict for Lawrence for loan plus interest; General Term affirmed; Court of Appeals affirmed.
9. Gray's opinion: the bystander's evidence was competent to show the debtor-creditor relationship; Farley v. Cleveland settled that the promise was supported by consideration (the consideration moved from the promisee); the trust cases apply a broader principle and do not limit it, so "a promise made to one for the benefit of another, he for whose benefit it is made may bring an action for its breach." Whether Holly could have released Fox was "not now necessarily involved" because Lawrence had not released him.
10. Vote: 6–2 to affirm, but Johnson C.J. and Denio would rest on agency (Holly as Lawrence's agent; ratification), so only four judges endorsed the general beneficiary proposition (1165, 1168). Comstock dissented (privity required; Holly could countermand, release, assign; no trust because Fox borrowed the money as his own); Grover also dissented.
11. Notes: could the case rest on restitution (quasi-contract)? (Note 1, 1169–1170). Durability of rights: the dissent's point that the parties could divest Lawrence at will; the majority left it open; the vesting discussion at 1194–1196 takes it up (Note 2, 1170). Waters's history: the real promisee may have been Samuel Hawley and the loan for gambling (Note 3, 1170).

## 4. Seaver v. Ransom (N.Y. 1918) (1170–1174)
12. Facts: Judge Beman drafted his dying wife's will; she wanted the house to go to her niece, Marion Seaver; he promised that if she signed the will as drawn he would leave the niece enough in his own will to make up the difference; she signed; his will made no provision for the niece.
13. Trial court: recovery on a trust theory ($6,000, the value of the house). Court of Appeals (Pound, J.): no trust, because equity impresses a trust only on property obtained by the promise and Beman received only a life use of the house. The Appellate Division properly turned to the contract theory, and the Court of Appeals affirmed on that theory.
14. New York's then-existing categories: (1) a pecuniary obligation from promisee to beneficiary; (2) contracts for a party's wife, affianced wife or child; (3) public contracts where a municipality protects inhabitants; (4) promises running directly to the beneficiary at a party's request. The court saw no principled line between a child and a favorite niece: the contract was made for her benefit, "she alone is substantially damaged by its breach," and the wife's estate had no interest in enforcing it. "The doctrine of Lawrence v. Fox is progressive, not retrograde." The court reasoned by analogy to a devise on condition that the devisee pay a legacy, and said the American tendency is to let the donee beneficiary recover.
15. Note (1173–1174): Fourth Ocean Putnam (N.Y. 1985) read Seaver as resting partly on the fact that no one else could recover; the editors question that reading, citing § 307 (the promisee may sue for specific performance of a duty owed to an intended beneficiary where that remedy is otherwise appropriate) and Croker v. New York Trust Co.

## 5. Problems at 1174
16. Tobi's Tulips: buyer Lee agrees to pay Tobi's listed business debts, including the time-barred loan from Tobi's mother Ruth. Restatement § 302 ill. 1 treats a promise to pay a time-barred debt as making the creditor a (1)(a) beneficiary. The problem also asks whether a gift motive would strengthen or weaken Ruth's claim.
17. UK Ministry of Defence v. Trimble: the Air Force contracted with Trimble for goods to be supplied to the Ministry, which had prepaid; the Air Force declined to sue. Can the Ministry sue as beneficiary?

## 6. Restatement formulations (1174–1176)
18. First Restatement § 133: donee beneficiary (promisee's purpose is a gift or to confer a right not otherwise due), creditor beneficiary (no gift purpose, and performance satisfies an actual, supposed or asserted duty of the promisee), incidental beneficiary.
19. Those categories mattered historically to whether the parties could change the deal without the beneficiary's consent; some courts still use them. Corbin's critique led to the Second Restatement's two classes, intended and incidental.
20. § 302(1): a beneficiary is intended only if recognizing a right is "appropriate to effectuate the intention of the parties" and either (a) performance will satisfy the promisee's obligation to pay money to the beneficiary, or (b) the circumstances indicate the promisee intends to give the beneficiary the benefit of the promised performance. § 302(2): everyone else is incidental. § 315: an incidental beneficiary acquires no right against promisor or promisee. § 304: an intended beneficiary may enforce the promisor's duty.
21. Comment d: an overriding policy, possibly in a statute, may require recognition of a right regardless of the parties' intention.
22. Seaver fits (1)(b); Lawrence fits (1)(a). For these two cases the revision was only a rewording.
23. A substantive change: (1)(a) requires that the promisee's duty be to pay money or its near equivalent (cmt. b); First § 133(1)(b) had no such limit. Cmt. b also says a supposed or asserted duty, where the promisee was never under a duty, is not (1)(a); such cases go to (1)(b) or cmt. d.
24. § 90(1) covers reliance by "a third person," so even an incidental beneficiary may have a reliance claim against the promisor (1175–1176).
25. Agency and other routes around privity; collective bargaining agreements (Note, 1176).
26. Tenant Mix problem (Gianni): is a tobacco-selling co-tenant a beneficiary of Gianni's promise not to sell tobacco? Tusa v. Roffe (Fla.) denied the claim under a statute curtailing beneficiary rights.

## 7. Statutes and private rights of action (1176–1178)
27. Oklahoma (contract "made expressly for the benefit of a third person" enforceable before rescission), West Virginia (sole benefit), Michigan § 600.1405 (promisor undertook to do something "directly to or for" the person).
28. Note 1: § 302 ill. 6 (A secures B's promise to pay A's son C's debt to D; both C and D intended) versus the Michigan direct-performance test; Stowe v. Smith rejects a direct-performance requirement.
29. Note 2: when a statute gives no private right of action, private parties try to sue as beneficiaries of government contracts. Amaral v. Cintas (living-wage ordinance) succeeded. Astra USA v. Santa Clara County (2011): a suit on drug-pricing agreements that "simply incorporate statutory obligations" is in essence a suit to enforce the statute and fails; Astra does not bar suits on contract terms that do not arise from statutory obligations.
30. Problems (1178): owner suing on a subcontract; fan suing on the subcontract or general contract; manufacturer claiming to be beneficiary of a dealer's arbitration clause (Carranza).
31. Murray v. ILG (begins 1178; the holding that bar applicants were not beneficiaries of ILG's contract with the bar-admissions office is on 1179, outside the assigned range).

## 8. Eisenberg and vesting (1194–1196)
32. Eisenberg's critique of the intent test and his proposal (1193–1194; assigned from 1194).
33. Contracting parties may modify or cancel by mutual assent and cannot bind themselves not to. Once they create a beneficiary: § 311(1) a term can make modification ineffective; § 311(2) otherwise the parties keep the power to discharge or modify; § 311(3) the power ends when the beneficiary, before receiving notification of the discharge or modification, (i) materially changes position in justifiable reliance, (ii) sues on the promise, or (iii) manifests assent at the request of the promisor or promisee. § 311(4): consideration the promisee receives for an ineffective discharge belongs to the beneficiary.
34. The casebook compares vesting to the protection of offerees of unilateral contracts and to promissory estoppel; its summary that assent makes the promise irrevocable omits the § 311(3) condition "at the request of the promisor or promisee" (1194–1195).
35. Detroit Bank v. Chicago Flame Hardening (N.D. Ind. 1982): the First Restatement (§ 142) made a donee beneficiary's rights vest on creation unless a power to change was reserved, and protected a creditor beneficiary only after reliance; the court applied the Second Restatement approach instead, so the rescission deprived Mrs. Scott of her benefit.
36. Michigan's statutory vesting (Note 1): a Lawrence-type creditor right may be divested, but not with intent to hinder, delay or defraud, and only before the creditor takes legal steps; otherwise the right vests the moment the promise binds the promisor, without the beneficiary's knowledge, subject to contrary contract terms and the contract's infirmities.
37. Feinberg problem (Note 2): partial versus full vesting when reliance is worth less than the promised benefit; the Restatement appears to choose full irrevocability, but compare § 90's "remedy may be limited as justice requires."

## 9. Defenses against the beneficiary (1196–1197)
38. The beneficiary's rights derive from the contract and carry its limits: lack of consideration, statute of frauds, mistake, incapacity (§ 309(1)); discharge by impracticability, public policy, non-occurrence of a condition or failure of performance (§ 309(2)).
39. The promisor may assert claims arising from the promisee's breach of the same contract, up to the point that would extinguish the beneficiary's claim (Souza), but not unrelated claims against the promisee, such as a setoff from another contract (J.G.B. Enterprises) (§ 309(3)). The beneficiary is subject to defenses arising from its own conduct (§ 309(4)).
40. Arbitration clauses generally bind an intended beneficiary who sues on the contract (Labatt; Weekley Homes); not a claimant whose suit does not rest on the contract (Thompson v. Witherspoon). The editors say these cases rest more on "direct benefit estoppel" (1197, context page).

## 10. Government contracts and the two Sisney cases (1200–1206)
41. Orff (9th Cir.): parties that benefit from a government contract are generally presumed incidental absent a clear contrary intent. Eisenberg's policy test tends to favor enforcement of government contracts (1200).
42. Sisney v. State (S.D. 2008): food-service contract between the State and CBM, under which services were to be provided "to the State" and religious food substitutions were to be available. SDCL 53-2-6 requires a contract "made expressly for the benefit of a third person," judged by the parties' intent at execution; incidental beneficiaries do not qualify; private citizens are presumed not to be beneficiaries of public contracts, and a right of enforcement must come from plain and clear contract language. The complaint-resolution clause referred to a general institutional remedy and conferred no beneficiary status. Dismissal affirmed.
43. Sisney v. Reisch (S.D. 2008): settlement of Heftel's § 1983 suit, in which the DOC agreed "to provide a kosher diet to all Jewish inmates who request it," including prepackaged certified kosher meals. Standing may be conferred on an identifiable class; at the pleading stage the language raised an inference of intent to benefit that class; Sisney alleged membership, so the complaint stated a claim. The court also held the defendants' responsibility adequately pleaded and the claim not barred by sovereign immunity (that part omitted). Reversed and remanded.
44. Note: why the different results? Relief may vary with each defendant's sovereign immunity (1206).
45. Attorney-client contracts and third parties: Restatement (Third) of the Law Governing Lawyers § 51, Lucas v. Hamm; countervailing loyalty concerns (Rydde v. Morris) (1206–1207).

## 11. Delegation (1215–1217)
46. A delegable duty is one whose performance by someone other than the original obligor the obligee cannot refuse. A money debt is the paradigm (Langman; Lawrence v. Fox, where Holly delegated to Fox the duty to pay Lawrence).
47. Nondelegable duties: performance that depends on the identity of the performer (the Pavarotti hypothetical in Metropolitan Airports Comm'n). § 318(2): a promise requires performance by a particular person only to the extent the obligee has "a substantial interest in having that person perform or control the acts promised." Paying rent does not depend on the lessee's identity; other lease duties might.
48. § 318(1): delegation is improper if contrary to public policy or the terms of the promise. UCC § 2–210(1): delegation allowed unless otherwise agreed or the other party has a substantial interest in having the original promisor perform or control the acts.
49. Epstein's concern that a delegate may be more willing to breach, and the answer that the obligee can bargain for a no-delegation term (1216).
50. Delegation does not discharge the delegating obligor (§ 318(3); UCC § 2–210(1), second sentence). Discharge requires the obligee's assent, which produces a novation (1217, context page; the rule is also in the assigned sections).

## 12. Assignment (1226–1227)
51. Vocabulary: obligee/assignor, assignee, obligor; Article 9 governs most assignments of rights to payment (debtor, secured party, account debtor).
52. § 317(1): assignment extinguishes the assignor's right to the extent assigned and gives the assignee the right. § 317(2): a contractual right is assignable unless (a) the substitution would materially change the obligor's duty, materially increase its burden or risk, materially impair its chance of return performance, or materially reduce the value of that performance; (b) a statute or public policy forbids it (e.g., wage-assignment statutes); or (c) the contract validly precludes it. UCC § 2–210(2) parallels (a), subject to § 9–406, and makes damages for breach of the whole contract and rights from the assignor's full performance assignable despite contrary agreement.
53. § 334 ill. 3 (oil for A's family for a year; A assigns to C, whose house needs more oil): a requirements-type right whose assignment may materially change the obligor's duty (1226–1227).
54. Anti-assignment clauses: § 322(1) a ban on assigning "the contract" bars only delegation of the assignor's duties unless circumstances indicate otherwise; § 322(2) a ban on assigning rights, unless a different intention is manifested, does not reach a right to damages for total breach or a right earned by full performance, gives the obligor damages but does not make the assignment ineffective, and is for the obligor's benefit. UCC § 2–210(4) matches § 322(1). (The casebook text on these clauses is at about 1218 and is not assigned; the rule comes from the selections.)
55. § 328 and UCC § 2–210(5): an assignment of "the contract" or "all my rights" is an assignment of rights and a delegation of unperformed duties; the assignee's acceptance is a promise to perform them, and the obligor is an intended beneficiary of that promise. UCC § 2–210(6): the other party may treat a delegating assignment as grounds for insecurity and demand assurances.
56. Scher v. Bronco (1225–1226): a Michigan statute limited a wine supplier's power to withhold consent to transfer of a wholesaler's business; the court called Sally Beauty "not factually analogous or legally relevant."
