## (b) Findings

### (i) Findings that change a legal result

**E-1. Flowchart K-24 (Did the exercise of discretion violate good faith?) node e: `edges`.**
- Current text: `"label": "Duty recognized and breached",` (to f) and, in `detail`, "Compare the supplement’s purpose-based limits and identify any preemption issue."
- Verdict: CHANGES RESULT.
- Facts entered: Ginsberg. Minnesota law governs a frequent-flyer agreement and implies a covenant the parties cannot disclaim. The airline terminates a member "in its sole judgment," and the member alleges pretext (C&M 730–732).
- Path the chart takes: a, then "Different governing regime," then e, then "Duty recognized and breached," then f.
- Result displayed (f): "Remedy the obligation actually breached ... Award the promised process or proved contract loss."
- Source-supported result: the covenant claim is preempted by the Airline Deregulation Act, because under Minnesota law the covenant is a state-imposed obligation. No covenant remedy is available. A covenant claim escapes preemption only if the governing state lets the airline contract around it (C&M 732–733). Node e mentions preemption only as something to "identify," and no edge routes a preempted claim away from f. The alternative entry, a, then "Evaluation or satisfaction," then c, then "Process or good-faith standard breached," then f, reaches the same wrong result, because node c has no preemption check.
- Pinpoint: C&M 731 (editors' bracket stating the test), 732 (both Minnesota bases; "a breach of covenant claim is pre-empted").
- Minimal replacement (node e edges): change the f edge label to "Duty recognized, not displaced by federal law, and breached," and change the g edge label to "Duty absent, preempted (Ginsberg), or no breach." Detail, last sentence: replace "Compare the supplement’s purpose-based limits and identify any preemption issue." with "Ask whether federal law displaces the claim: under Ginsberg, the Airline Deregulation Act preempts an airline covenant claim when state law makes the covenant non-disclaimable or policy-based."

**E-4b. Map entry: learning.topics (bloor), key "ucc306".**
- Current text: "UCC 2-306(2)'s best-efforts duty in exclusive dealings is the statutory analogue of the express clause construed here."
- Verdict: OVERSTATED.

The section implies best efforts only from "[a] lawful agreement ... for exclusive dealing in the kind of goods concerned," and only "unless otherwise agreed." - Minimal replacement: "UCC § 2-306(2) implies best efforts only in exclusive-dealing agreements for goods, unless otherwise agreed; Bloor construed an express clause under New York law, and Feld, the § 2-306 output case Falstaff cited, supplied the contrast."

**E-5. Dalton: the rule line limits the arbitrary-or-irrational constraint to discretion a party "promised to exercise."**
- Surface: Effective brief CT-NEXT-CM-0106: rule.
  - Current text: "requires a party to actually exercise discretion it has promised to exercise and to do so neither arbitrarily nor irrationally"
- The same limit appears in:
  - Chronology 'course-chronology-0' step 441, `note` (same words);
  - Timeline 'good-faith' step 5, `note`: "A party must actually exercise a discretion it promised to exercise, and not arbitrarily";
  - Takeaway: "A party that promises to use its discretion has to actually use it, and not arbitrarily."
- Verdict: MISSTATES HOLDING. The trigger is narrowed: the court applied the limit to discretion "the contract contemplates," not only to discretion a party promised to exercise.
- Counterexample: a contract reserves to one party a power to terminate "in its sole judgment" (Ginsberg's clause, C&M 730) or a discretionary termination right (the PEB's example, C&M 730). The party promised nothing about how it would exercise the power. On the Atlas wording, good faith adds nothing because no exercise of discretion was promised. Dalton states the rule more broadly: "Where the contract contemplates the exercise of discretion, this pledge includes a promise not to act arbitrarily or irrationally in exercising that discretion" (C&M 721). ETS's power was itself a reserved "right to cancel" (C&M 720). Its separate duty to consider Dalton's material came from the contract's invitation to submit it (C&M 722). Whether the covenant reaches a particular case still depends on the governing law (and on preemption, as in Ginsberg).
- Pinpoint: C&M 720, 721, 722.
- Minimal replacements:
  - Rule (45 words): "Where a contract contemplates a party's exercise of discretion, the implied covenant forbids exercising it arbitrarily or irrationally, and a party must actually perform any evaluation the contract requires, such as considering relevant submitted material; the covenant cannot create obligations inconsistent with the contract's terms."
  - Timeline note: "Discretion the contract contemplates may not be exercised arbitrarily or irrationally, and a promised evaluation must actually occur, but good faith adds no duty the contract excludes (New York)."
  - Takeaway (2 sentences, 56 words): "When a contract gives a party discretion, it may not exercise it arbitrarily or irrationally, and it must carry out any evaluation the contract requires. The testing service had to genuinely consider the student's evidence, though it did not have to prove he cheated, and the court ordered a real review rather than releasing the score."

## (c) Teaching gaps ranked by exam importance

**1. Honesty in fact versus reasonable commercial standards, and which states use which.** Home: Timeline 'good-faith', a new step (id `ucc1-201b20`, year 2001, turn "widens"), with a cross-reference in good-faith node notes. Draft note: "The 2001 revision of UCC Article 1 defines good faith for all parties as honesty in fact plus observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing (§ 1-201(b)(20)); thirteen states, including New York, kept the old honesty-only definition, with the objective standard applying only to merchants under § 2-103(1)(b)." (C&M 724–725; Selections § 1-201 n.a, § 2-103 n.b.) This also answers the casebook's Dalton question at C&M 725: Dalton is a New York services contract, so the UCC standard does not apply to it directly.

Draft: "RLCC § 4 in the assigned Selections (Official Text § 5) reads a business's discretion as limited by good faith and makes a term granting absolute, unconstrained discretion unenforceable by the business; Illustration 5 lets an airline modify a frequent-flyer program prospectively but not unreasonably devalue perks already earned." See section (b)(ii).

**3. Three levels of effort obligation.** The levels are the implied good-faith effort, the express best-efforts clause and the § 2-306(2) implied best efforts in exclusive dealing in goods. Home: implied-efforts node notes, and Flowchart K-24 node b `detail`. Draft: "Without an efforts clause, a seller paid by royalty is owed a good-faith effort toward substantial sales (unless the contract offers a paid exit, as clause 2(a)(v) did); an express best-efforts clause adds more, barring profit-maximization without fair consideration of the brand's volume though not requiring self-bankruptcy (Bloor); for goods, § 2-306(2) implies best efforts only in exclusive dealing, unless otherwise agreed."

**4. Consequences of bad faith, and good faith as a non-independent duty.** Home: Flowchart K-24 node f `detail` (see E-2) and good-faith node notes. Draft: "Good faith is measured against the agreement and is not an independent cause of action (PEB Commentary No. 10; § 1-304 cmt 1); a bad-faith failure is a breach of the specific duty or bars the actor's own remedial right, as Market Street lost specific performance, and it does not support punitive damages unless specifically permitted."
