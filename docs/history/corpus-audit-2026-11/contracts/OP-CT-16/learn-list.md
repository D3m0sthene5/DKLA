## 1. What the duty is, where it comes from, and what it covers (C&M 719–720)

Rule. Every contract imposes on each party a duty of good faith and fair dealing in its performance and its enforcement (Restatement § 205). The UCC version: every contract or duty within the Code imposes an obligation of good faith in its performance and enforcement (UCC § 1-304). The casebook calls this "the implied duty of good faith in performance and enforcement," "also known as the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing," and classes it with gap fillers and warranties as a term the law adds to the parties' actual agreement (C&M 719–720).

Conditions and limits. The duty governs performance and enforcement, not negotiation and formation (C&M 720). Market Street repeats this: buying something you know the seller undervalues is "a question about formation, not performance" (C&M 734). Ng Giap confirms that English law and, for now, Singapore law reject a duty to negotiate in good faith (Walford v Miles; supp para 21, pp. 71–72). Under the UCC the obligation cannot be disclaimed by agreement, but the parties may set the standards by which performance is measured if those standards are not manifestly unreasonable (UCC § 1-302(b)).

The three questions the editors pose for the whole section (C&M 720): (i) what sort of contracts include a duty of good faith; (ii) how courts determine its content; (iii) the consequences of failing to act in good faith.

## 2. Dalton v. Educational Testing Service (N.Y. 1995) (C&M 720–723)

Facts. Dalton's November SAT score rose 410 points over his May score. Because the rise exceeded 350 points, the score fell into ETS's "Large Score Differences" category, and ETS's security office found disparate handwriting. The Registration Bulletin, which Dalton agreed to, reserved to ETS "the right to cancel any test score if ETS believes that there is reason to question the score's validity." It also promised that ETS would notify the test-taker and offer five options: additional information, a free retest, cancellation with a refund, third-party review by a receiving institution, or arbitration (C&M 720). Dalton chose to submit information: mononucleosis during the May test, consistent diagnostic results from a prep course, a proctor's statement, two students' statements, and his family's handwriting expert (C&M 720–721). ETS's Board of Review treated handwriting as the only issue and regarded the health and presence evidence as irrelevant. After a 12-day nonjury trial, the trial judge found that ETS failed "to make even rudimentary efforts to evaluate or investigate" Dalton's information and ordered the score released. The Appellate Division affirmed (C&M 721).

Holding and rule (Kaye, C.J.):
- The test-taker's acceptance of ETS's standardized form was a contract. "Implicit in all contracts is a covenant of good faith and fair dealing in the course of contract performance" (C&M 721).
- Content: a pledge that "neither party shall do anything which will have the effect of destroying or injuring the right of the other party to receive the fruits of the contract" (Kirke La Shelle). "Where the contract contemplates the exercise of discretion, this pledge includes a promise not to act arbitrarily or irrationally in exercising that discretion" (C&M 721).
- Limit: "no obligation can be implied that 'would be inconsistent with other terms of the contractual relationship'" (Murphy) (C&M 721).
- Application of the limit: the contract let ETS cancel on "reason to question" after offering the five options. Nothing required ETS to prove cheating, and ETS had no duty, express or implied, to run a field investigation, because such a duty would be inconsistent with the terms placing the burden on the test-taker (C&M 721).
- What ETS did owe: "The contract, however, did require that ETS consider any relevant material that Dalton supplied." Dalton triggered that obligation by exercising his option to submit information. The proctor's and students' statements were relevant to impersonation, which was the question ETS itself framed, and the medical and diagnostic evidence was germane to it (C&M 722).
- Standard of review, in two parts (C&M 722). (a) When ETS considers the relevant material and otherwise acts in good faith, ETS and not the court is the final arbiter of weight and validity, and a court will interfere only if the determination is arbitrary or irrational. (b) When ETS refuses to exercise its discretion in the first place by declining even to consider relevant material, the question is whether the refusal breached an express or implied term, not whether it was arbitrary or irrational. ETS's failure to consider the evidence meant it "failed to comply in good faith with its own test security procedures, thereby breaching its contract" (C&M 722).
- Remedy: specific performance of the promise actually made, which was good-faith consideration of Dalton's material. Dalton was not entitled to release of the score as though validated, because ETS never promised to release a score it believed invalid and validity was undetermined; the trial court had not decided whether Dalton took the test. The remaining options (third-party review, arbitration) remain available. The Appellate Division's order was modified and, as modified, affirmed (C&M 722–723).
- Dissent (two judges; Levine, J.): ETS acted within its discretion by continuing the security process after "considering and rejecting" Dalton's evidence, and there was no evidence that ETS acted arbitrarily (C&M 723, Note 1).

Teaching points. Good faith attaches to a discretionary power (here, the power to cancel scores) and requires that the power actually be exercised, on the relevant material, and not arbitrarily or irrationally. It cannot add duties that contradict express terms (no field investigation). The remedy fits the promise: a do-over, not the result. Note 1 (C&M 723) asks what criteria a court would use to define good faith if asked to hold ETS in contempt. The note at C&M 725 asks how the UCC § 1-201(b)(20) standard would analyze Dalton. Dalton is a services contract under New York law, so the UCC does not govern it; and New York is a state that kept the pre-2001 honesty-in-fact-only definition (Selections, § 1-201 footnote a).

## 3. Hilton Hotels v. Butch Lewis Productions (Nev. 1991), C&M Note 2 (C&M 723–724)

Rule taught. A party with no express duty to deliver a benefit (Dynamic Duo had no contractual duty to furnish Spinks) still has a duty not to interfere with the other side's expected benefit (it could not deliberately get Spinks stripped of his title in order to undermine the Unification Series and make more money elsewhere). Doing so "could be seen as a breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing implied in the parties' contract." Motive evidence (Lewis said he would pull Spinks "to make 'big money' elsewhere") was wrongly excluded. The judgment for the defendant was reversed and the case remanded. This is Summers's "interference with, or failure to cooperate in, the other party's performance" category and Burton's recapture of a forgone opportunity.

## 4. The UCC obligation and the two definitions of good faith (C&M 724, 725; Selections)

- § 1-304: "Every contract or duty within [the UCC] imposes an obligation of good faith in its performance and enforcement." This was formerly § 1-203 (C&M 729 n.*).
- Former § 1-201(19): good faith meant only "honesty in fact in the conduct or transaction concerned," which the casebook calls "a purely subjective test" (C&M 724).
- Former § 2-103(1)(b), for merchants: "honesty in fact and the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing in the trade" (C&M 724).
- The 2001 revision of Article 1 moved the heightened standard into the general definition and applied it "to all parties, whether or not they are merchants." Current § 1-201(b)(20): "honesty in fact and the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing" (C&M 724, 725). The current text drops the words "in the trade."
- Qualification the casebook omits but the Selections print. Some states kept the prior definition, which has no reference to reasonable commercial standards: Alabama, Arizona, Hawaii, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Maryland, Michigan, Nebraska, New York, Rhode Island, Utah and Virginia (§ 1-201 footnote a). Those states keep bracketed § 2-103(1)(b) for merchants (§ 2-103 footnote b). New York, whose law governs Dalton and Bloor, is one of them. A statement that the objective standard now applies to every party everywhere overstates the law.
- § 1-302(b): good faith cannot be disclaimed; the parties may fix standards that are not manifestly unreasonable.
- § 1-304 Comment 1 (which incorporates the PEB's 1994 amendment, C&M 730): the section "does not support an independent cause of action for failure to perform or enforce in good faith." A failure to perform or enforce a specific contractual duty in good faith "constitutes a breach of that contract or makes unavailable, under the particular circumstances, a remedial right or power." Comment 2: performance and enforcement include the exercise of rights created by the Code.

The distinction to learn. Honesty in fact is subjective: did the party actually believe it was acting properly? Observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing is objective: would the conduct pass muster with fair-dealing commercial actors, even if the party was sincere? Summers (C&M 727) explains why honesty alone is too narrow: abuse of bargaining power, undercutting the other's performance, or capricious action can be bad faith without any "guilty mind."

## 5. Comparative and international notes (C&M 724; supp 65–75)

- Germany: BGB art. 242 requires "performance according to the requirements of good faith [Treu und Glauben], common habits being duly taken into consideration." At the UCC's promulgation the notion was unfamiliar to the common law (Farnsworth, 30 U. Chi. L. Rev. 666) (C&M 724, Note 4).
- UNIDROIT Principles art. 1.7 requires each party to "act in accordance with good faith and fair dealing in international trade." The CISG has no comparable performance duty: art. 7(1) makes good faith only a principle for interpreting the Convention, a compromise recorded by Honnold (C&M 724, Note 5).
- Singapore: see point 13 (Ng Giap). Editors' update (supp 73): Canada, Wastech, 2021 SCC 7 (a mandatory duty of good faith that includes exercising contractual discretion reasonably); England, Braganza, [2015] UKSC 17 (courts sometimes imply a duty to exercise contractual discretion in good faith); and Yam Seng, [2013] EWHC 111 (QB) (a duty of good faith may be implied in "relational contracts").

## 6. What does good faith add beyond the terms? (C&M 724–725)

The editors' question: failure to live up to the terms is already breach, so "What duty is imposed by the obligation of good faith that is not already imposed by the terms of the agreement?" (C&M 725). The candidate answers the section develops are: limits on the exercise of discretion (Dalton; the PEB's termination example; RLCC § 4); a ban on interfering with the other side's benefit (Hilton); a ban on exploiting the other party's oversight about its rights under the contract (Market Street); a ban on recapturing forgone opportunities (Burton); and a minimum effort level when the counterparty's return depends on one's own performance (Bloor; Wood v. Lucy; Feld).

## 7. Summers's "excluder" analysis (C&M 725–728)

- Good faith "is a phrase without general meaning (or meanings) of its own and serves to exclude a wide range of heterogeneous forms of bad faith." Its specific meaning in a case comes from contrast with the form of bad faith the judge rules out (C&M 726). To find what a judge means, ask what the judge intends to rule out.
- The list of bad-faith forms and their good-faith opposites (C&M 727): concealing a defect, opposed to full disclosure; willful substantial-only performance, opposed to substantial performance without knowing deviation; abusing bargaining power to coerce a price increase; hiring a broker and then preventing the deal, opposed to cooperation; conscious lack of diligence in mitigating; arbitrary and capricious exercise of a termination power, opposed to acting with some reason; overreaching interpretation, opposed to fair interpretation; harassing for repeated assurances, opposed to accepting adequate assurances.
- Good faith is broader than honesty, and bad faith need not be immoral. A party may abuse bargaining power, undercut performance, or act capriciously without a guilty mind and still violate good faith (C&M 727–728).
- The Supreme Court quoted the excluder thesis through Tymshare (Scalia, J.) in Ginsberg (C&M 732), and Ng Giap cites it (supp para 15).

## 8. Burton's reply, and Restatement § 205 Comment a (C&M 728–729, 736)

- Summers's six (non-exhaustive) categories of bad-faith performance, as Burton lists them (C&M 728): evasion of the spirit of the deal; lack of diligence and slacking off; willful rendering of only substantial performance; abuse of a power to specify terms; abuse of a power to determine compliance; and interference with, or failure to cooperate in, the other party's performance.
- Restatement commentary (Comment a, quoted at C&M 728): good faith performance "emphasizes faithfulness to an agreed common purpose and consistency with the justified expectations of the other party; it excludes a variety of types of conduct characterized as involving 'bad faith' because they violate community standards of decency, fairness or reasonableness."
- Burton's position: courts generally do not use good faith to override the agreement. They use it "to effectuate the intentions of the parties, or to protect their reasonable expectations, through interpretation and implication." Courts override agreed terms through other doctrines (unconscionability or other unenforceability at formation, estoppel or waiver, impossibility or impracticability). Fairness may be used to interpret or supply terms only when the parties' intentions or reasonable expectations cannot reasonably be ascertained (C&M 728–729).
- Burton's "forgone opportunities" test (Harv. L. Rev., C&M 736): discretion in performance may be exercised for purposes the parties reasonably contemplated, including ordinary business reasons, but not to recapture opportunities forgone on entering the contract. The burden of careful drafting falls on the discretion-exercising promisor who wishes to depart from the norm. The editors ask whether ETS's conduct in Dalton would violate this standard (C&M 736).

## 9. PEB Commentary No. 10 (1994): good faith is not an independent cause of action (C&M 729–730)

- One acts in good faith "relative to the agreement of the parties." Good faith "merely directs attention to the parties' reasonable expectations; it is not an independent source from which rights and duties evolve." The obligation extends only to rights and duties resulting from the contract (C&M 729).
- A breach arising from a failure to act in good faith does not support punitive damages unless specifically permitted (C&M 729).
- The counterweight: it is "wrong to conclude that as long as the agreement allows a party to do something, it is under all terms and conditions permissible." The examples are an express quantity term that may be "not really" a quantity term but a projection, and whether a party with a discretionary termination right may expect the decision to rest on sound business criteria (C&M 729–730).
- Official Comment amendment (C&M 730; now § 1-304 Comment 1): no independent cause of action; breach of a specific duty performed without good faith is breach of the contract, or it bars a remedial right or power.

The central distinction of the session: good faith as an implied term that serves the parties' bargain (Burton, the PEB, the "effectuate intentions" states in Ginsberg, terms implied in fact in Ng Giap) versus good faith as a law-imposed or independent duty grounded in community standards (Summers, Restatement Comment a, Minnesota law in Ginsberg, terms implied in law in Ng Giap). The classification determines results. It decided preemption in Ginsberg, and it decided whether Singapore would imply the term in Ng Giap.

## 10. Northwest, Inc. v. Ginsberg, 572 U.S. 273 (2014) (C&M 730–733)

Facts. Ginsberg was a WorldPerks Platinum Elite member. Northwest terminated his membership under a clause allowing cancellation for "[a]buse of the ... program (including ... improper conduct as determined by [Northwest] in its sole judgment)." Northwest's letter cited 24 complaints, 9 of them about late bags, and its past compensation (C&M 730–731). He alleged that the termination was a cost-cutting measure tied to the Delta merger and brought a class action with four claims: breach of contract (revocation without valid cause); breach of the implied covenant (termination that contravened his reasonable expectations about how Northwest would exercise its discretion); negligent misrepresentation; and intentional misrepresentation (C&M 731). The district court held the covenant claim and both misrepresentation claims preempted by the Airline Deregulation Act, 49 U.S.C. § 41713 (C&M 731). The excerpt does not say how the district court disposed of the breach of contract claim.

Rule (Alito, J., for the Court).
- Framework, as stated in the editors' bracket: the covenant claim is preempted if the covenant is a state-imposed obligation and not preempted if it is an obligation the parties voluntarily undertook (C&M 731).
- States differ. Some use the doctrine "to effectuate the intentions of parties or to protect their reasonable expectations"; others use it to ensure a party does not "violate community standards of decency, fairness, or reasonableness" (Universal Drilling (Wyo.); Restatement § 205 Comment a). The Court cites Summers's excluder line through Tymshare (C&M 732).
- Under Minnesota law, the governing law, the covenant is state-imposed, for two independent reasons. First, the parties cannot contract out of it (respondent conceded this), and a state's unwillingness to allow disclaimer "shows that the obligation cannot be implied, but is law imposed" (Corbin). "When the law of a State does not authorize parties to free themselves from the covenant, a breach of covenant claim is pre-empted." Second, Minnesota applies the covenant to every contract except employment contracts, for policy reasons. When application of the covenant depends on state policy, the claim cannot be viewed as simply vindicating the parties' implicit understanding (C&M 732).
- Holding: the implied-covenant claim is preempted. A state's covenant rules escape preemption only if the state permits an airline to contract around them, and an airline governed by such a state's law can specify that the agreement does not incorporate the covenant (C&M 732–733).
- Policy: the ADA relies on the market. An airline with a reputation for mistreating its frequent flyers will lose customers to rival programs (C&M 733). The excerpt does not mention Department of Transportation enforcement or American Airlines v. Wolens.

Conditions and pitfalls. The holding is about ADA preemption of a state-law covenant claim, and the test turns on the law of the particular state (whether the covenant can be disclaimed, and whether it depends on state policy). The excerpt contains nothing on ERISA. Any Atlas linkage of Ginsberg to ERISA preemption is not supported by the assigned sources.

Note (C&M 733): how would the case come out if it involved the UCC duty in a sale of goods? The tension the student should see: the PEB and § 1-304 Comment 1 say the UCC duty is not an independent source of rights and runs only to the parties' agreement, which points toward "voluntarily undertaken"; but § 1-302(b) forbids disclaiming the UCC duty, which is Ginsberg's first marker of a state-imposed obligation.

Link to RLCC § 4. Illustration 5 is a frequent-flyer program. A clause letting the airline modify the program at its discretion is enforceable if exercised in good faith. A modification that unreasonably reduces the value of previously acquired perks violates good faith; one that reduces the value of prospectively acquired miles does not. And § 4(b) makes a term granting absolute discretion unconstrained by good faith unenforceable by the business.

## 11. Market Street Associates v. Frey (7th Cir. 1991) (C&M 733–736)

Facts. Paragraph 34 of the sale-leaseback required the GE Pension Trust to give reasonable consideration to Penney's (and its assignees') requests to finance improvements. If negotiations broke down, the lessee could buy back the property at a price that could be below market value when appreciation had exceeded 6 percent a year. Market Street's Orenstein sent letters requesting $4 million in financing without mentioning paragraph 34; he later testified that it had occurred to him that Erb might not know about it. Erb replied that the request was below the trust's $7 million minimum. Market Street then exercised the purchase option. The district court granted summary judgment for the trust, holding that Market Street had violated the duty of good faith (C&M 733–734).

Holding and reasoning (Posner, J.).
- The Wisconsin cases are "cryptic" about the meaning of good faith. Good faith does not make every contract a fiduciary relationship or make a signatory "his brother's keeper," especially toward the sophisticated GE Pension Trust (C&M 734).
- "The duty of honesty, of good faith even expansively conceived, is not a duty of candor." One can contract to buy what the seller undervalues, but that is formation. After signing, a party need not "become an altruist" and relax the terms if the other gets into trouble, because mere difficulty of performance does not excuse (C&M 734).
- The line: exploiting superior knowledge of the market, or refusing to spend money bailing out a partner, is permitted. Taking "deliberate advantage of an oversight by your contract partner concerning his rights under the contract" is "sharp dealing" (C&M 734–735).
- The essential issue was Orenstein's state of mind, which ordinarily cannot be resolved on summary judgment. If he believed Erb knew or would surely find out about paragraph 34, it was "not dishonest or opportunistic" to fail to flag it. A trial was necessary. Reversed and remanded (C&M 735).
- Remand (Note 1): after trial, Market Street was not entitled to specific performance. Orenstein realized the Trust was not operating under paragraph 34, did not bring it to the Trust's attention, and kept writing ambiguous letters until he wished to use the option. That breached his duty of good faith (817 F. Supp. 784, 788 (E.D. Wis. 1993)) (C&M 735).
- The consequence of bad faith here was the loss of a remedy. The party that acted in bad faith while enforcing its own option lost specific performance. This illustrates § 1-304 Comment 1's "makes unavailable ... a remedial right or power" (C&M 730) and the "enforcement" limb of § 205.

Opportunism framing (Note 2, C&M 735). Easterbrook, Industrial Representatives v. CP Clare: opportunism means either (1) exploiting the other party's sunk costs from performing first, a hold-up (Alaska Packers), or (2) taking advantage of the partner "in a way that could not have been contemplated at the time of drafting, and which therefore was not resolved explicitly by the parties" (Market Street). "Contract law does not require parties to be fair, or kind, or reasonable, or to share gains or losses equitably. It does require parties to avoid taking advantage of the opportunities that arise from sequential performance, when the contract does not cover a particular subject." Posner's own test in the opinion asks whether the failure to flag was "dishonest or opportunistic" (C&M 735).

Note 3 (C&M 736): Burton's forgone-opportunities test; the editors ask whether ETS's conduct in Dalton violated it.

## 12. Bloor v. Falstaff Brewing Corp., 601 F.2d 609 (2d Cir. 1979) (C&M 736–744; supp 53–64)

Contract (C&M 737; supp 54–55, 59). Falstaff bought Ballantine's labels, trademarks, accounts receivable, distribution systems and other property except the brewery, for $4,000,000 plus a royalty of $0.50 a barrel on Ballantine brands sold over six years. Clause 8(a): after closing, the Buyer "will use its best efforts to promote and maintain a high volume of sales under the Proprietary Rights." Clause 2(a)(v): if the Buyer "substantially discontinues the distribution of beer under the brand name 'Ballantine'" during the Royalty Period, it pays liquidated damages of $1,100,000 times the years remaining. "Proprietary Rights" are the brand names, trademarks, trade names and copyrights listed in Exhibit A (supp 53, § 1(a)). New York law governed by stipulation (C&M 739).

Facts. Ballantine had been losing money since 1965. Falstaff continued IFC's practices until 1975, when Kalmanovitz took control. He cut Ballantine advertising from $1 million to $115,000 a year, closed four of six distribution centers including North Bergen, and ended illegal sales practices (C&M 738). Ballantine sales fell 29.72% in 1975 and 45.81% in 1976 against 1974. Over the same years, all brewers except the top 15 gained 2.24% in 1975 and lost 13.08% in 1976 (C&M 739). Falstaff recovered financially: 1976 net income was $8.7 million (C&M 739).

Falstaff's argument. Under Feld v. Henry S. Levy & Sons (N.Y. 1975), a UCC § 2-306 output case, a bread-crumb seller could stop producing before cancellation only in good faith. Bankruptcy, or a genuine imperiling of the existence of the entire business, would justify cessation; yielding less profit than expected would not; and cessation before cancellation was justified only if losses from continuing would be "more than trivial," a question of fact. Falstaff argued that it therefore need not do anything causing more than trivial losses (C&M 740).

Footnote 7 (C&M 740). In 407 E. 61st Garage v. Savoy, the actual holding was only that an issue of fact existed. Van Valkenburgh: a publisher who, in bad faith, hired another author to write a very similar book and promoted it to the plaintiff's buyers was liable. Parev: a party with an exclusive right to sell may sell a similar product if necessary to meet outside competition, so long as it accounts for the plaintiff's resulting losses. Arnold Productions: best efforts means performing as well as "the average prudent comparable" brewer. "The net of all this is that the New York law is far from clear."

Holding (Friendly, J.).
- The trial judge did not require Falstaff to bankrupt itself or to sell Ballantine at a substantial loss (C&M 741).
- Kalmanovitz's policy ("We sell beer and you pay for it ... F.O.B. the brewery. You come and get it") did not satisfy the clause. "Once the peril of insolvency had been averted," the drastic drop "required Falstaff at least to explore whether steps not involving substantial losses could have been taken to stop or at least lessen the rate of decline" (C&M 741).
- The findings of misfeasance and nonfeasance were: closing North Bergen; distributor choices, including in New York a distributor that owned a competing brand; ignoring the Guinness-Harp/Metrobeer offer; the incentive to push Falstaff's own higher-priced, royalty-free brands; failure to treat Ballantine evenhandedly; ending sales goals; and stressing profit at the expense of volume. Even "taking account of Falstaff's right to give reasonable consideration to its own interests," these supported breach (C&M 741).
- The evenhandedness finding (Texas and Missouri advertising and pricing) did not warrant the inference drawn, but that error did not undermine the conclusion. The clause "clearly required Falstaff to treat the Ballantine brands as well as its own," but "it does not follow that it required no more." With its own brands, Falstaff could maximize profit even at a serious loss of volume. With Ballantine it could not, because the royalty "was an essential part of the purchase price" (C&M 742).
- Baseline without the clause: "Even without the best efforts clause Falstaff would have been bound to make a good faith effort to see that substantial sales of Ballantine products were made, unless it discontinued under clause 2(a)(v) with consequent liability for liquidated damages. Cf. Wood v. Duff-Gordon." Clause 8 imposed "an added obligation" (C&M 742).
- Limit: best efforts did not require Falstaff "to spend itself into bankruptcy," but it barred applying a profit-über-alles philosophy "without fair consideration of the effect on Ballantine volume" (C&M 742).
- Burden of proof: the plaintiff need not show what steps Falstaff could reasonably have taken. It sufficed to show that Falstaff "simply didn't care about Ballantine's volume and was content to allow this to plummet so long as that course was best for Falstaff's overall profit picture." The burden then shifted to Falstaff to prove there was nothing significant it could have done without financial disaster (C&M 742).
- Damages: certainty is not required, only "a stable foundation for a reasonable estimate." The Rheingold-Schaefer composite was an appropriate comparison, and the court rejected Bloor's preferred "all but the top 15" grouping (C&M 742–743).
- Liquidated damages: the court rejected the claim that Falstaff's conduct triggered clause 2(a)(v), so there was no substantial discontinuance. Affirmed; the plaintiff recovers two-thirds of his costs (C&M 743).

Notes (C&M 743–744).
- (1) How do best efforts and good faith connect, and would Falstaff's duties differ without the clause? (Friendly's own answer at C&M 742: a good-faith effort to see that substantial sales are made.)
- (2) Measuring "best": the behavior of others in like activities (the "average prudent comparable" brewer); UNIDROIT art. 5.1.4(2) ("such efforts as would be made by a reasonable person of the same kind in the same circumstances"); Lucas v. Hamm (a lawyer's ordinary skill); Olympia Hotels (Posner: working as hard for this customer as for other, noncomplaining customers).
- (3) Goetz and Scott: the joint-maximization or "as if a single firm" test; Tigg Corp. (3d Cir.): consider the best interests of the seller and oneself "as if they were one firm."
- (4) Exclusivity: does best efforts imply exclusivity? Van Valkenburgh says no as a general rule, but there may be a point where competing activity is "so manifestly harmful" to the author, and must have been seen to be so, that it breaches the covenant. Joyce Beverages v. Royal Crown: undertaking a competing line is not per se breach, but it was a breach where trade usage was one cola per distributor and Seven-Up's advertising denigrated RC.

UCC § 2-306 (Selections).
- (1) An output or requirements term means "such actual output or requirements as may occur in good faith," except that no quantity unreasonably disproportionate to a stated estimate, or, absent an estimate, to normal or comparable prior output or requirements, may be tendered or demanded. Comment 2: the measuring party must operate its plant or business in good faith and according to commercial standards of fair dealing in the trade; good-faith variations, even to the point of discontinuance, are permitted; "A shut-down by a requirements buyer for lack of orders might be permissible when a shut-down merely to curtail losses would not." Comment 3: an estimate is a center around which variation is expected; a stated minimum or maximum is a clear limit.
- (2) "A lawful agreement by either the seller or the buyer for exclusive dealing in the kind of goods concerned imposes unless otherwise agreed an obligation by the seller to use best efforts to supply the goods and by the buyer to use best efforts to promote their sale." Comment 5: the parties are held to have bound themselves impliedly to reasonable diligence as well as good faith; the exclusive agent must use "reasonable effort and due diligence" even without an express commitment; the principal must refrain from supplying other dealers in the exclusive territory.
- Scope point. Bloor is not a § 2-306 case. It involved an express best-efforts covenant in the sale of trademarks and brand assets, applied under New York common law. Feld, which Falstaff relied on, was the § 2-306 case. Section 2-306(2) is the statutory analogue of the Wood v. Lucy implied promise for exclusive dealing in goods, and it can be varied ("unless otherwise agreed").

## 13. Ng Giap Hon v Westcomb Securities Pte Ltd [2009] SGCA 19 (Singapore Court of Appeal; Andrew Phang JA) (supp 65–75)

Facts. A remisier (a self-employed securities agent who earns commissions through a broker) signed an Agency Agreement. Clause 6 paid him 40%, later 50%, of the commission the firm charged clients "on transactions that are dealt by or through the Remisier." Clause 12.1 made him an agent and not an employee. Clause 18 was an entire-understanding clause: there are no terms "expressed or implied" other than those contained in the agreement (supp 66). The firm sold IPO shares to clients he had introduced (S and A) without charging commission; a representative had told A's CEO to return its account-opening forms to him instead of to the appellant (supp 66–67). The trial judge found no express or implied duty to charge commission on IPO shares and rejected the claims based on custom, conspiracy and quantum meruit (supp 67).

Issues and holdings.
- First issue: the entire-agreement clause did not bar implication of terms (editors' bracket, supp 67).
- Two categories of implied terms (supp para 6, p. 67). Terms implied in fact fill a gap on the basis that the parties would have intended it filled. Terms implied in law fill a gap "on (broader) policy grounds." Implying a term in law sets a precedent for all future contracts of the same type, which calls for caution (supp para 12, p. 69).
- The appellant's First Implied Term (a general duty of good faith) falls within terms implied in law. The Second Implied Term (that the firm would not do anything to deprive him of his commission) is narrower and falls within terms implied in fact (supp paras 8–9, p. 68).
- Holding on the First Implied Term: a duty of good faith cannot be implied as a term implied in law. Implied terms are "the last desperate resort of counsel in distress" (Megarry), and sanctity of contract must not be undermined (supp para 11). The content of the term is controversial (supp para 12). Good faith is "a fledgling doctrine in English and (most certainly) Singapore contract law" (supp para 13). The court surveys the theories: Steyn's subjective threshold of honesty plus an objective element of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing (supp para 14); Summers's excluder and Burton's forgone opportunities (supp para 15); Bridge against (supp para 16); Carter and Peden (good faith as a principle governing implication and construction, not an implied term) (supp para 18); a possible softening of English hostility, driven by EU instruments that do not apply in Singapore (supp para 19); Furmston (supp para 20); Walford v Miles (no duty to negotiate in good faith, still good law in Singapore) (supp para 21); and the uncertainty in Australia, the United States (Hunter) and Canada (supp paras 22–25). "[U]ntil the theoretical foundations as well as the structure of this doctrine are settled, it would be inadvisable (to say the least) to even attempt to apply it in the practical sphere." The court calls this the "strongest reason" for refusing to endorse an implied duty of good faith in Singapore (supp para 26, p. 73).
- Not in the assigned excerpt: the court's ruling on the Second Implied Term (implied in fact) and the final disposition of the appeal.
- Editors' update (supp 73): later Canadian and English developments (Wastech; Braganza; Yam Seng). The court's statement is Singapore law as of 2009.

Comparative lesson. The United States treats good faith as a term in every contract (§ 205, § 1-304). Singapore in 2009 refused to imply a general good-faith term in law, while leaving room for specific terms implied in fact. That difference corresponds to the implied-term versus law-imposed distinction in Ginsberg.

## 14. RLCC § 4, Discretionary Obligations (Selections, 2019 TD)

- (a) A contract or term that grants the business discretion to determine its rights and obligations "must be interpreted, when reasonably susceptible to such interpretation, to provide that such discretion will be exercised in good faith."
- (b) A term that "purports to grant the business absolute and unlimited discretion ... unconstrained by the good-faith obligation is unenforceable by the business."
- Comment 1: the section covers two types of discretion: discretion to resolve an issue left open, and discretion to adjust obligations already specified (modification clauses, also governed by § 3). The comment cites UCC §§ 2-305 and 2-306 as models.
- Comment 2: unlimited discretion would fail mutuality and consideration (§ 77), so the good-faith reading saves the term. Comment 3: a term irreconcilable with good faith is illusory and not enforceable by the business. Illustrations 3 and 4 (cruise cancellation, hot-tub service) make the whole contract unenforceable by the business.
- Illustration 5: a frequent-flyer program modification. Unreasonably reducing the value of previously acquired perks violates good faith; reducing the value of prospectively acquired miles does not.
- Comment 5: a pattern of bad-faith exercise (a course of performance) makes the discretionary term unenforceable by the business (Illustration 6, an inaccessible arbitral body). Comment 6: asymmetry alone does not matter. Comment 7: unenforceable terms are replaced by gap fillers under § 9, and terms meant to benefit the consumer may be enforced by the consumer.
- Links: Dalton (ETS's discretion over scores); Ginsberg (the "sole judgment" clause); the PEB's discretionary-termination example.

## 15. Synthesis: what an exam answer must be able to do

1. Identify the discretion or power the defendant exercised, and whether an express term addresses it. Good faith cannot contradict express terms (Dalton/Murphy; Burton; PEB).
2. Choose the standard. At common law, the covenant protects the fruits of the contract and bars arbitrary or irrational exercise of discretion (Dalton). Under the UCC, the standard is honesty in fact plus reasonable commercial standards, except in states that kept the honesty-only definition for non-merchants, such as New York.
3. Classify the bad-faith conduct: Summers's categories; Posner's ban on exploiting the partner's oversight about its rights, without any duty of candor; Easterbrook's opportunism; Burton's forgone opportunities.
4. Separate best efforts from good faith. Good faith alone (Wood v. Lucy; Bloor's baseline) requires a good-faith effort to make the counterparty's return materialize. An express best-efforts clause adds more: the brand must be treated at least as well as one's own, profit cannot be maximized without fair consideration of the brand's volume, and the promisor must at least explore non-loss-making steps, though it need not spend itself into bankruptcy (Bloor). Under § 2-306(2), exclusive dealing in goods implies best efforts unless otherwise agreed. Under § 2-306(1), output and requirements quantities must be in good faith (Feld; Comment 2).
5. State the consequence. It may be breach with expectation damages (Bloor). It may be specific performance of the process promised and not the outcome (Dalton). It may be the loss of a remedial right (Market Street; § 1-304 Comment 1). There are no punitive damages unless specifically permitted (PEB).
6. Watch for forum and system issues: federal preemption turns on whether the covenant is state-imposed (Ginsberg); there is no general implied duty in Singapore as of 2009 (Ng Giap); the CISG has no performance duty, while UNIDROIT and German law do have one.
