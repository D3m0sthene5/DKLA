## 1. What an offer is

1. Restatement § 24: an offer is "the manifestation of willingness to enter into a bargain, so made as to justify another person in understanding that his assent to that bargain is invited and will conclude it." The test is objective: what the addressee is justified in understanding, not what the offeror privately intended (C&M 191; slide 4).
2. Corbin's alternative definition stresses the legal effect: an offer confers on the offeree a power to create contractual relations; it must lead the offeree "reasonably to believe" that power is conferred, which excludes invitations to deal, acts of mere preliminary negotiation, and acts evidently done in jest (C&M 191–192). The editors ask whether Corbin describes the offer's effect rather than defining it.
3. Restatement § 26: a manifestation is not an offer if the addressee knows or has reason to know the maker does not intend to conclude a bargain until a further manifestation of assent. This is the black-letter basis for "invitation to deal" and "preliminary negotiation" (Selections § 26).
4. Medium matters less than intent: an exchange of emails can form a contract, even without signed implementing documents, when the communications are "sufficiently clear and concrete" (Brighton Investment v. Har-ZVI, N.Y. App. Div. 2011, C&M 192).
5. Quotations and quantity. In Moulton v. Kershaw (Wis. 1884) a seller's letter "authorized to offer Michigan fine salt in full car load lots ... Shall be pleased to receive your order" was not an offer, because "the language is not such as a business man would use in making an offer to sell a definite amount of property" (C&M 198). The notes ask whether a stated quantity range ("one to 25 carloads") or "all ... you will order" would change the result, and what makes "we quote you" mean "offer" (Fairmount Glass, C&M 197–198, context).

## 2. Advertisements, auctions and rewards

6. The general rule is that an advertisement is not an offer but an invitation to the buyer to make an offer to purchase (C&M 199–200). One policy reason: if ads were offers, a seller whose stock ran out could be bound to more buyers than it has goods; French law instead treats such a proposal as an offer conditioned, for each offeree, on its not having been accepted first by a quicker offeree (C&M 200).
7. Lefkowitz v. Great Minneapolis Surplus Store (Minn. 1957), C&M 200–202. The store advertised "1 Black Lapin Stole ... worth $139.50 ... $1.00 FIRST COME FIRST SERVED." Lefkowitz was first at the counter, tendered $1, and was refused under a "house rule" limiting the offer to women. Held: an advertisement is an offer when "the facts show that some performance was promised in positive terms in return for something requested" (quoting Williston); where the offer is "clear, definite, and explicit, and leaves nothing open for negotiation," it is an offer, and whether a particular advertisement is an offer "depends on the legal intention of the parties and the surrounding circumstances." Being first and tendering the price was acceptance. The advertiser may modify the offer before acceptance, but may not after acceptance "impose new or arbitrary conditions not contained in the published offer." Judgment for the plaintiff for $138.50 (the stole's stated value less the $1 price) was affirmed (C&M 201–202).
8. Remedy detail. The trial court disallowed the claim on the first ad (fur coats "Worth to $100.00") because their value was speculative; recovery was on the second ad only (C&M 201). Note 1 says Lefkowitz was denied recovery on the first ad and asks "What aspect of an offer was missing from the store's first ad?" (C&M 203). The opinion's stated reason is uncertainty of value, a damages problem; the note invites an offer-definiteness reading.
9. Leonard v. Pepsico (S.D.N.Y.), distinguishing Lefkowitz: the commercial was not sufficiently definite because it reserved the details to a separate writing (the Catalog) and named no step needed to accept; Lefkowitz's ad identified "the person who could accept"; the absence of limiting words such as "first come, first served" made the alleged offer too indefinite (C&M 203).
10. Consumer protection runs separately from contract: in Geismar v. Abraham & Straus (N.Y. Dist. Ct. 1981) the ad was not an offer, so there was no breach of contract, but the shopper recovered $50 under a statute for advertising misleading in a material respect; the FTC Act and bait-and-switch rules (16 C.F.R. § 238.3) also apply (C&M 203; slide 4's "may be tortious or violations of consumer protection statutes").
11. Auctions and bids: inviting competitive bids is an invitation for offers; the bidder is the offeror (UCC § 2-328; Restatement § 28). In an auction "without reserve" the auctioneer makes the offer and may not withdraw after a bid, while the bidder may retract until the hammer falls; online sellers may set a reserve price (C&M 204). Problem: the Mint's coin announcement and the buyers' order forms (Mesaros v. United States, Fed. Cir. 1988) (C&M 204–205).
12. Advertised rewards: an offer of reward "may be accepted by anyone who performs the service called for when the acceptor knows that it has been made and acts in performance of it, but not otherwise" (Broadnax v. Ledbetter, Tex. 1907). In § 71(2) terms, the performance was not given in exchange for the promise; in assent terms, no manifestation of assent with reference to the offer (§§ 2(1), 23) (C&M 205).
13. Errors in advertised prices (Donovan v. RRL, Cal. 2001): the appellate court answered "possibly, yes, no" (an ad for a specific car at a stated price may be an offer; the statute matters; the error does not change the answer); the California Supreme Court reversed (C&M 212–213, 213 assigned). Foley v. Yacht Management: a "No Reserve" eBay listing posted by mistake (C&M 213).

## 3. Acceptance: definition, the master of the offer, and an unequivocal commitment

14. Restatement § 50(1): acceptance is "a manifestation of assent to the terms thereof made by the offeree in a manner invited or required by the offer" (C&M 213). Corbin: the offeror "has, in the beginning, full power to determine the acts that are to constitute acceptance"; the first step is to read the offer to see what kind of acceptance it invited (C&M 214). This is the source of the phrase "the offeror is master of the offer" (slide 6). The editors also note one limit on mastery: the offeror cannot make the offeree's silence an acceptance (C&M 235).
15. Restatement § 30(2) and UCC § 2-206(1)(a): unless the language or circumstances indicate otherwise (UCC: "unambiguously indicated"), an offer invites acceptance in any manner and by any medium reasonable in the circumstances. § 60: if the offer prescribes place, time or manner, those terms must be met; if it merely suggests one, other methods are not precluded.
16. An offeror may bargain for a performance or for a promise; economically significant transactions usually involve a promise (C&M 214). § 32: in case of doubt the offeree may choose to accept by promise or by performance.
17. Wucherpfennig v. Dooley (N.D. 1984), C&M 214–216. Elizabeth wrote to the estate's attorney: "Now if Don wants to buy my share of the real estate, I will sell it to him for $200 an acre, provided it is a cash deal & handled promptly." The attorney replied that Donald was "interested," then wrote on Feb. 17 that Donald "has made arrangements with the Federal Land Bank to secure funds ... and we are therefore ready to proceed with this transaction. Please let me know the exact dollar amount that you expect to receive" and asked whether she would sign an agreement relating to Special Use Valuation. She revoked on March 9. Held: no contract. An acceptance "must be absolute, unequivocal, and unconditional, and it may not introduce additional terms or conditions" (N.D. Cent. Code § 9-03-21); it must be "unequivocal ... of an intent to create thereby, without more, a contract." The Feb. 17 letter read as negotiations toward a future agreement. Donald's testimony that the exact amount would have been $37,200 and that she would not have signed at that figure confirmed the parties never assented to the same terms. Because no acceptance preceded the revocation, the court did not reach the other three grounds for dismissal (C&M 215–216). It was undisputed that the Jan. 4 letter was an offer (C&M 216).
18. Note (Hill's v. William B. Kessler, Wash. 1952): a manufacturer's form letter, "You may be assured of our very best attention to this order," where the order form said the manufacturer was not bound until an officer accepted; the note asks whether that was an acceptance under Wucherpfennig (C&M 216).

## 4. Acceptance by promise or by performance, and notice

18a. International Filter Co. v. Conroe Gin, Ice & Light Co. (Tex. Comm'n App. 1925), C&M 217–220. The seller's salesman submitted a "proposal" that "becomes a contract when accepted by the purchaser and approved by an executive officer of the International Filter Company at its office in Chicago" and was "submitted for prompt acceptance, and unless so accepted is subject to change without notice." Conroe's manager signed "Accepted Feb. 10, 1920"; the president wrote "O.K." on it in Chicago on Feb. 13; the seller's Feb. 14 letter thanked Conroe for its order and asked for a water sample; Conroe countermanded on Feb. 28. Held: (a) the buyer's signed proposal was the offer, and the president's "O.K." was the acceptance in the manner the offer prescribed; (b) the offeror may dispense with notification, and does so "if the form of the offer ... shows that this was not to be required"; the offer's own words made the contract complete on approval at Chicago, and "prompt acceptance" fixed only the time for that approval, so notice was not required; (c) alternatively, the Feb. 14 letter sufficiently communicated notice if notice was required, because notice may take any form that conveys the fact "by word or fair implication" to a reasonably prudent person. Reversed and remanded for the Court of Civil Appeals to decide the remaining questions (C&M 219–220). The court treated the case as one of acceptance by promise; it did not hold that approval was a unilateral-contract performance.
19. Notes: identify the offeror and the function of each act (buyer's notation, seller's "O.K.", seller's letter) (C&M 220); the lower court relied on Courtney Shoe, where "Your order ... will receive our prompt and careful attention" was not an acceptance (C&M 220–221); home-office approval clauses control salespersons' authority (C&M 221); note 4 asks how to reconcile International Filter with the rule that an offeree who accepts by promise must notify (§§ 54, 56; Kendel v. Pontious) (C&M 221).
20. Restatement § 56: except under § 69 or where the offer manifests a contrary intention, acceptance by promise requires that the offeree exercise reasonable diligence to notify the offeror or that the offeror receive the acceptance seasonably. § 57: where notification is essential, an equivocal acceptance binds only if the offeror reasonably understands it as one.
21. Problem, "Awesome" Acceptance? (CX Digital v. Smoking Everywhere, S.D. Fla. 2011), C&M 221–222: an instant-message exchange ("We can do 2000 orders/day by Friday if I have your blessing," "NO LIMIT," "awesome!") offered to show a modification lifting a 200-leads-per-day cap. Who made the offer, and what must "awesome!" be claimed to mean? (slide 10.)
22. White v. Corlies & Tifft (N.Y. 1871), C&M 222–224. After White's estimate and his signed assent to changed specifications, the defendants' bookkeeper wrote: "Upon an agreement to finish the fitting up of offices 57 Broadway in two weeks from date, you can begin at once." White did not reply; he bought lumber and began work; the next day the defendants countermanded. Held: the note was a proposal that had to be accepted before either party was bound. Where parties are not together, acceptance "must be manifested by some appropriate act." It need not come to the offeror's knowledge before he is bound, but it must be "put in a proper way to be in the usual course of events, in some reasonable time communicated to him" (the court gives the mailed letter of acceptance as the example). A mental determination not indicated by speech, or put in course of indication by act, is not acceptance; an act "as well referable to one state of facts as another" is no indication. White's lumber was "as fit for any other like work," and his work on it was what he would have done for any other job. The trial court's charge that White need not indicate acceptance was error. Reversed; new trial ordered (C&M 223–224).
23. Notes: two readings of "Upon an agreement" (the note demands a return promise, or it means "with reference to") (C&M 224); whether some activity could have accepted, and the risk allocation ("you can begin at once" suggests willingness to bear risk) (C&M 224–225).
24. Ever-Tite Roofing v. Green (La. App. 1955), C&M 225–227: the homeowners' signed proposal said it would bind "only upon written acceptance hereof, by the principal or authorized officer of the Contractor, or upon commencing performance of the work." After credit approval, the contractor loaded trucks and drove to the house; the owners had hired someone else and turned the workers away. Held: commencement of performance began with loading the trucks and transporting materials and workers, before any notice of withdrawal, so a contract was formed and the owners breached. No time limit was set, so a reasonable time applied, and the delay for credit approval was understood. Note 2 asks how to distinguish White (the "critical language of the offer") (C&M 227).
25. "Notification of Acceptance in Unilateral Contracts," C&M 227–229. For offers seeking a promise ("bilateral"), the offeree ordinarily must take steps to notify (§ 56). For offers seeking performance ("unilateral"), notice is less obviously needed. Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. (Eng. C.A. 1893): the £100 reward, backed by a £1,000 deposit "shewing our sincerity," was an offer, not a "mere puff"; notification of acceptance exists for the offeror's benefit and the offeror may dispense with it expressly or impliedly; in advertisement cases the dispensation is inferred from the transaction (C&M 228). Notes ask which act was the acceptance (purchase, use, or contracting influenza) and whether the promise was conditional (C&M 229). Bishop v. Eaton (Mass. 1894): ordinarily no notice is needed, "but if the act is of such a kind that knowledge of it will not quickly come to the promisor, the promisee is bound to give him notice of his acceptance within a reasonable time"; notice was found to have been given (C&M 229).
26. Restatement § 54: (1) where an offer invites acceptance by performance, no notification is necessary unless the offer requests it; (2) if the offeree has reason to know the offeror has no adequate means of learning of the performance with reasonable promptness and certainty, the offeror's duty is discharged unless the offeree uses reasonable diligence to notify, the offeror learns within a reasonable time, or the offer indicates notification is not required (slides 7, 13). The § 54(2) consequence is discharge of the offeror's duty, not failure of acceptance. UCC § 2-206(2) gives a goods offeror who is not notified within a reasonable time the right to treat the offer as lapsed.
27. Restatement § 45 (option created by beginning invited performance, offer for performance only) and § 62 (where offeror invites either, beginning performance is acceptance and operates as a promise) mark the consequences of starting performance; C&M 242 lists part performance as the third way to create an option.
28. Problem, Steinberg v. Chicago Medical School (Ill. 1977): who made the offer when a school's bulletin stated admission criteria and the applicant paid a fee? (C&M 229).
29. UCC § 2-206(1)(b): an order for prompt or current shipment invites acceptance by a prompt promise to ship or by prompt or current shipment of conforming or nonconforming goods; a nonconforming shipment is not an acceptance if the seller seasonably notifies that it is offered only as an accommodation (C&M 229).

## 5. Silence

30. "The general rule is that silence alone is not acceptance"; "even as the master of the offer," the offeror cannot make silence acceptance by writing "Unless I hear from you ... you will be deemed to have accepted" (C&M 235, citing Farnsworth). Restatement § 69 lists the exceptions "only": (a) taking the benefit of offered services with reasonable opportunity to reject and reason to know compensation was expected; (b) the offeror has said or given reason to understand that silence may manifest assent and the offeree, staying silent, intends to accept; (c) previous dealings or other circumstances make it reasonable that the offeree should notify if it does not intend to accept; and § 69(2), an act inconsistent with the offeror's ownership of offered property binds on the offered terms unless manifestly unreasonable.
31. Hobbs v. Massasoit Whip Co. (Mass. 1893, Holmes, J.): retaining eelskins sent by a seller who had sent them four or five times before and been paid, "for an unreasonable time," warranted a finding of acceptance; the editors ask "What, beyond mere silence," was present (C&M 235).
32. American Bronze v. Streamway Products (Ohio App. 1982): the buyer is the offeror; over twenty years the seller began production on receipt of written orders, so "absent a notice of rejection" the buyer could believe production had begun, and filling orders this way "as a regular practice" constituted acceptance (UCC § 2-204(1)) (C&M 236).
33. Unsolicited merchandise: the recipient who shelves it and does not use it incurs no liability; N.Y. Gen. Bus. L. § 396 deems it an unconditional gift; 39 U.S.C. § 3009 restricts the practice. Negative-option book and CD clubs rest on a prior subscriber agreement; Katz's efficiency argument and Patterson's "freedom from contract" (C&M 236).

## 6. Contract formation and antidiscrimination law

34. 42 U.S.C. § 1981(a) (Civil Rights Act of 1866, responding to the Black Codes) gives all persons "the same right ... to make and enforce contracts ... as is enjoyed by white citizens" (C&M 236–237).
35. Perry v. Command Performance (3d Cir. 1990): a Black customer booked a hair appointment by phone; a stylist refused to do her hair on racial grounds. The appellate court vacated summary judgment and remanded to determine whether the parties were still in the process of making a contract at the time of the incident or whether a contract was made when the appointment was scheduled; the editors ask the student to find the offer and acceptance and when they occurred (C&M 237). Formation timing mattered because at that time § 1981 was read to cover discrimination in the making of a contract but not discrimination after formation or under a contract (C&M 237–238).
36. Congress amended § 1981 in the Civil Rights Act of 1991: "make and enforce contracts" now "includes the making, performance, modification, and termination of contracts, and the enjoyment of all benefits, privileges, terms, and conditions of the contractual relationship" (§ 1981(b)) (C&M 238). The amendment reduces, but the notes do not say it eliminates, the importance of formation timing for § 1981.
37. Barfield v. Commerce Bank (10th Cir. 2007): a Black man refused change for a $50 bill as a non-account-holder, while a white non-customer was given change; the bank argued there was no consideration; the editors ask whether there was (C&M 238).

## 7. Termination of the power of acceptance

38. The power ends by (1) lapse, (2) revocation by the offeror, (3) the offeror's death or incapacity, or (4) the offeree's rejection (C&M 238); Restatement § 36(1) also lists counter-offer and the offeree's death or incapacity, and § 36(2) adds non-occurrence of a condition of acceptance. The editors say the practical import of the death rule has "been greatly reduced since the typical offeror is now often a deathless corporate entity" (C&M 238). § 48: death or incapacity of offeror or offeree terminates the power. (The Second Restatement keeps the rule even where the offeree does not know of the death; that discussion is on C&M 259, unassigned.)
39. Option contracts: an offeror can make a "firm offer" or create an option, which is not subject to revocation for a time; irrevocability is the defining characteristic (C&M 238). § 37: the power of acceptance under an option is not terminated by rejection, counter-offer, revocation, or the offeror's death or incapacity (slide 8: "the last three events may not terminate the power to accept an irrevocable offer"). C&M 242 lists four ways to create an option: a promise to hold open supported by consideration, a UCC § 2-205 firm offer, beginning invited performance under an offer for a unilateral contract, and reliance.
40. Lapse. An offer lapses at the time it states, or if none, after a reasonable time, which depends on the circumstances, and rapid price fluctuation shortens it (C&M 239; § 41(1)–(2)). Akers v. J.B. Sedberry (Tenn. App. 1955): an offer made in a face-to-face conversation ordinarily lasts only until the conversation ends (C&M 239). Loring v. City of Boston (Mass. 1844): a reward advertised for a week in 1837 had lapsed by the time of the 1841 arrest (C&M 239–240); contrast Carr v. Mahaska County Bankers Ass'n (Iowa 1936), where a standing reward aimed at future bank robberies continued (C&M 240, 242). § 41(3): an offer by mail is ordinarily timely accepted by mailing before midnight on the day received.
41. Tardy acceptance: may the offeror disregard the delay, or is the late acceptance a counter-offer? (C&M 240). § 70: a late acceptance may operate as an offer to the original offeror, but his silence is acceptance only under § 69. Newman v. Schiff (8th Cir. 1985): whether a rebroadcast of a TV challenge was a second offer, judged objectively (C&M 240). Problem, Courtroom Countdown (Yaros v. Trustees of Univ. of Pa., Pa. Super. 1999): a $50,000 settlement offer in a ten-minute recess, "You've got to get back to me," accepted seventy minutes later (C&M 240).
42. Revocation. The common-law rule is that an ordinary offer is freely revocable at any time before acceptance; some civil-law countries make offers irrevocable for a reasonable time unless the offeror says otherwise (C&M 238, 241). Revocation must be communicated: § 42 (effective when the offeree receives a manifestation of intention not to contract). Hoover Motor Express v. Clements Paper (Tenn. 1951): the offeror's statement on the phone, "Well, I don't know if we are ready... we might not want to go through with it," "brought home" that the offeror had not consented, and the later acceptance was too late (C&M 241). § 43: indirect revocation by definite action inconsistent with the offer when the offeree acquires reliable information.
43. General offers: an offer to the public is revoked by a notice given publicity equal to the offer (§ 46), and an offeree who missed the notice is still bound, in spite of the general requirement that revocation be communicated (C&M 241). Taking down a posted reward notice and Carr v. Mahaska (C&M 241–242); Shuey v. United States (revocation by publication of the Surratt reward) (C&M 242).
44. Rejection and counter-offer. Rejection terminates the power of acceptance unless the offeror manifests a contrary intention or the offeree says it is taking the offer under advisement (§ 38); a counter-offer terminates the power unless either party manifests a contrary intention (§ 39); a reply that purports to accept but is conditional on additional or different terms is a counter-offer (§ 59), while one that merely requests a change is still an acceptance (§ 61). A rejection or counter-offer by mail is effective on receipt but limits the power of acceptance (§ 40). Under an option, rejection probably does not end the power (Humble Oil, Tex. 1968, and § 37); the editors ask whether a UCC firm offer should be treated differently from a paid option (C&M 261). (The common-law "mirror image" statement in Minneapolis & St. Louis Ry. v. Columbus Rolling-Mill, and the UCC § 2-207 change for goods, are on C&M 260, unassigned.)

## 8. The mailbox rule and its limits

45. At common law an acceptance is effective on dispatch: after dispatch the offeror cannot revoke, the offeree cannot reject, and the risk of loss in transmission falls on the offeror (C&M 261). Origin: Adams v. Lindsell (K.B. 1818) (C&M 262).
46. Restatement § 63(a): "Unless the offer provides otherwise," an acceptance "made in a manner and by a medium invited by an offer" is operative "as soon as put out of the offeree's possession, without regard to whether it ever reaches the offeror." Comment a: the rationale is that the offeree needs a dependable basis for deciding whether to accept (not the post-office-as-agent fiction).
47. Limits: (a) the offer may provide otherwise; (b) the rule applies only to acceptance by an invited manner and medium, so acceptance by mail of an offer made by telegram would ordinarily be effective only on receipt (C&M 262; compare §§ 65–67: a reasonable medium, properly dispatched; an uninvited or improperly dispatched acceptance counts on dispatch only if it arrives when a proper one would have); (c) § 63(b): acceptance under an option contract is effective only on receipt; (d) revocation, rejection and counter-offer are effective only on receipt (§§ 40, 42; C&M 262); (e) § 64: acceptance by telephone or other substantially instantaneous two-way medium is governed by the rules for parties in each other's presence.
48. The overtaking rejection: Buyer mails an acceptance Tuesday, then phones a rejection Wednesday that arrives before the letter. Under the mailbox rule it is too late to reject and a contract formed on dispatch; a receipt rule would give Buyer a free option. But if Seller relies on the rejection (selling the goods elsewhere before the acceptance arrives), Buyer may be estopped to enforce the contract (§ 63 comment c and illustration 7) (C&M 262–263). Comment c also says an overtaking attempt to revoke an acceptance is ineffective, though it may operate as an offer to rescind, a repudiation, or an estoppel.
49. U.S. Life Ins. Co. v. Wilson (Md. Ct. Spec. App. 2011) introduces the mailbox rule's application to electronic payment of an overdue premium under a reinstatement clause (C&M 263; its analysis is on unassigned pages).
