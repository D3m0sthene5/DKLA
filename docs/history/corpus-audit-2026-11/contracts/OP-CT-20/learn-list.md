## 1. What a condition is

1. Definition. "A condition is an event, not certain to occur, which must occur, unless its non-occurrence is excused, before performance under a contract becomes due." § 224; C&M 806. Three working parts: an event; uncertainty; it qualifies a duty (§ 224 cmt. c: offer and acceptance are not conditions, though acceptance of an option is).
2. The passage of time is not a condition (no uncertainty), and an uncertain event referred to only to measure the passage of time is not a condition either. § 224 cmt. b; C&M 809 (introduction to Internatio-Rotterdam). The occurrence of an event within a period is a common kind of condition. C&M 809.
3. Effects of non-occurrence. A conditional duty cannot become due unless the condition occurs or its non-occurrence is excused (§ 225(1)); unless excused, non-occurrence discharges the duty once the condition can no longer occur (§ 225(2)); non-occurrence is not a breach unless the party was under a duty that the condition occur (§ 225(3)).
4. Source. An event becomes a condition by agreement (express) or by a term supplied by the court (constructive). § 226.
5. "Condition subsequent." The Restatement drops the term; an event that extinguishes a duty after performance is due is a "termination" governed by § 230, and "condition precedent" is simply "condition." § 224 cmt. e; C&M 806 Note. Where language says "terminated" but the event must ordinarily occur before performance can be expected, the event is a condition. § 224 cmt. e, § 227(3). The labels keep a separate significance for pleading and burden of proof (civil procedure). C&M 806.
6. § 230 (black letter): a terminating event discharges the duty, except where its occurrence results from the obligor's breach of good faith and fair dealing, or could not have been prevented because of impracticability and continuing the duty adds no materially increased burden, or the obligor promised beforehand to perform anyway and did not revoke before a material change of position.

## 2. Express conditions: strict compliance (Luttinger v. Rosen, Conn. 1972, C&M 806 to 808)

7. Facts that matter: $85,000 house, $8,500 deposit; contract "subject to and conditional upon" a first mortgage of $45,000, 20 years minimum, interest not over 8½%; buyers promised due diligence; refund and termination on timely notice. The bank committed at the prevailing rate "but not less than 8¾%." C&M 806 to 807.
8. Holdings. (a) Due diligence was satisfied by applying to the only institution that might meet the terms; "The law does not require the performance of a futile act." C&M 807. (b) The language was unambiguous; the condition did not occur; the buyers recover the deposit. C&M 807. (c) The sellers' offer to fund the interest difference "could be rejected," and testimony about it was properly excluded as irrelevant. C&M 807 to 808.
9. Notes. The condition protects the buyers' duty (Note 1: lottery question, the sellers could not refuse if the buyers chose to proceed). Making an event a condition allocates the risk of its non-occurrence (Note 2, § 224 cmt. b); pre-approval letters shift financing risk. Buyers have non-financial reasons to insist on an institutional lender (Note 3, Kovarik dissent). Effort standard: Informed Physician Services (Md. 1998), not "standing idly by" but no "extraordinary expense"; the note asks whether reasonable efforts or only good faith was required (Note 4). Barber v. Jacobs (Conn. App. 2000) follows Luttinger. If the buyers had not tried hard enough, would non-occurrence be "excused" (that is, would they lose the refund)? C&M 809. Treat these as questions, not holdings.

## 3. Interpretation: condition, duty, or both; the preference against conditions

10. Separate contracts. A breach of one contract does not let the other party terminate or suspend a different contract unless agreed (Northwest Lumber, Or. 1972); one test for "one contract or two" is separate, self-contained documents (Coplay Cement, 7th Cir. 1993); adequate assurance can link them. C&M 814 to 815.
11. Time of the essence. Parties may make performance by a stated date a condition ("drop dead" date), but stock phrases such as "time is of the essence" do not necessarily do so (§ 242 cmt. d), and a specific date does not by itself make time essential (Arkla, 10th Cir.). For option contracts courts presume time is of the essence (Livesey: exercise one day late ineffective). C&M 814.
12. Condition, duty, or both (Constable v. Cloberie hypothetical). Duty only: sailing late gives damages, the premium is still owed (ignoring constructive conditions). Condition only: no damages, no premium. Both ("promissory condition," Corbin): damages and no premium. C&M 815. § 227 cmt. d states the same three options.
13. Preference. If language is unclear, a court prefers an interpretation that imposes a duty rather than a condition, because that avoids harsh results and the obligor stays protected by the constructive-condition rules. C&M 816 Note. Restatement: § 227(2) prefers "duty only" where the event is within the obligee's control, except in contracts where only one party generally undertakes duties (insurance); § 227(1) prefers the interpretation that reduces the obligee's risk of forfeiture unless the event is within the obligee's control or the obligee assumed the risk; § 227(3) prefers condition over discharge. Citation detail: the casebook attributes the quoted sentence to "Comment c to Restatement § 227(1)"; in the Selections that sentence is in § 227 cmt. d and explains subsection (2).
14. § 227(1) is judged as of contract formation (risk of forfeiture viewed then), not by actual forfeiture later; actual forfeiture is § 229's job. § 227 cmt. b.

## 4. Pay-when-paid (Peacock Construction v. Modern Air Conditioning, Fla. 1977, C&M 816 to 818)

15. Clause: final payment "within 30 days after the completion of the work included in this sub-contract, written acceptance by the Architect and full payment therefor by the Owner." Owner in bankruptcy (fn. 1). Subcontractors' work had no deficiencies. C&M 816 to 817.
16. Holding. The clause is ambiguous (condition precedent or reasonable time for payment); interpretation is a question of law here because sub/GC contracts on large projects are a recurring relationship; the Court joins the majority view that owner payment is not a condition precedent but fixes a reasonable time for payment, because small subcontractors who need payment to stay in business ordinarily do not assume the risk of the owner's failure to pay. The parties may shift that risk, but only by unambiguous language; the burden of clear expression is on the general contractor. Gerrits overruled to the extent inconsistent; summary judgments for the subcontractors stand. C&M 817 to 818.
17. Restatement match: § 227 Illus. 1 (owner insolvent; GC must pay sub after a reasonable time).
18. Notes. DEC Electric (Fla. 1990): all such provisions are construed as a matter of law; clear and unambiguous means condition precedent, ambiguous means reasonable time, "nothing for the jury." C&M 818 to 819. Public policy: Wm. R. Clarke (Cal. 1997) held pay-if-paid unenforceable as a disguised waiver of mechanic's lien rights; only California, Nevada and New York courts have done so; some legislatures bar them; most jurisdictions enforce them if "clear and unequivocal." C&M 819. Brokers: commission "at time of closing" is generally a condition (Harding Realty, Fla. App. 1983; § 227 Illus. 4), and the note asks how the broker differs from the subcontractor. C&M 819. Problem: redraft to shift the risk unambiguously. C&M 819.

## 5. Satisfaction clauses (Gibson v. Cranage, Mich. 1878, C&M 820 to 823)

19. Gibson agreed the portrait of the defendant's deceased daughter would be satisfactory to the defendant, who could refuse to take or pay for it otherwise. Defendant was dissatisfied. Holding: under the agreement the defendant "was the only person who had the right to decide this question"; such an agreement violates no public policy and is free of fraud or mistake; a portrait others think excellent may still dissatisfy the person who ordered it. Judgment for the defendant affirmed. C&M 820 to 821.
20. Note questions: refusal to look at the portrait at all (the subjective standard still requires honest dissatisfaction, so a refusal to examine is the kind of fact that matters); portrait versus barn (taste versus utility; unjust enrichment if the barn is painted); did the court hold Gibson under a duty (it did not; satisfaction was a condition). C&M 821.
21. Forfeiture defined: "the denial of compensation" when non-occurrence of a condition causes the obligee "to lose his right to the agreed exchange after he has relied substantially on the expectation of that exchange, as by preparation or performance." § 227 cmt. b; C&M 821 Note 2. Gibson's forfeiture risk exceeded Hopper's in Mattei.
22. Standards. Subjective (honest, good-faith dissatisfaction) for matters of taste or personal judgment; objective (reasonable person) where practicable to determine, which is the preferred interpretation under § 228. Fursmidt Farms v. Cornett (Ala. 1979): potatoes "to buyer satisfaction"; dissatisfaction must be in good faith, not to escape a bad bargain; the buyer, a merchant, was held to the UCC good-faith standard including reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing; the jury need not accept the buyer's subjective claim; the current Article 1 extends that heightened definition beyond merchants. C&M 821 to 822.
23. Morin Building Products v. Baystone (7th Cir., Posner): a reasonable-person standard applied despite a clause that acceptability "shall rest strictly with" the buyer, for utilitarian goods; the note asks whether drafting could ever avoid that result. Devoine: only the decision contracted for is final. Mixed motives: Columbia Christian College (Or.): dissatisfaction must relate to the subject of the condition, but one genuine ground suffices; the note asks whether a decision driven almost entirely by a market drop is different. C&M 822 to 823.
24. Third-party satisfaction (architect, engineer). The architect's decision is "final" if rendered in good faith; the test is honest, not reasonable, satisfaction. RaDec (Neb. 1995): a gross mistake was "legally equivalent to bad faith." Rizzolo v. Poysher (N.J. 1916): architect who was ready to certify but refused because the owner wanted to cut payment and because the contractor hired counsel acted fraudulently; contractor recovered without the certificate. Anthony P. Miller (D. Del. 1959): gross miscalculation labelled "constructive fraud." C&M 823 to 825. Restatement: § 227 cmt. c and Illus. 5 to 8 (honest but unreasonable refusal binds; admitted satisfaction, no inspection, or gross mistake of fact do not).
25. Slap Shot problem (Giunto): judges' decisions "final in all matters" with a puck that ended "just slightly within the slot." C&M 824.

## 6. Excuse of express conditions

26. Forfeiture-avoiding interpretation first: a satisfaction condition read as reasonable satisfaction; a payment clause read as pay-when-paid. Interpretation cannot help if the drafter clearly intended forfeiture. C&M 824.
27. § 229: "To the extent that the non-occurrence of a condition would cause disproportionate forfeiture, a court may excuse the non-occurrence of that condition unless its occurrence was a material part of the agreed exchange." Discretionary ("may"), partial ("to the extent"), limited by "material part of the agreed exchange." Most states adopted it. Conley (N.J. App. Div. 2015): attorney-review notice by e-mail and fax rather than certified mail, telegram or personal delivery was excused because actual timely notice was received, the method was imposed by a consent decree, and losing the right would be a forfeiture. Courts also use "more traditional methods, some bordering on subterfuge." C&M 824 to 825 (825 context).
28. Waiver (§ 84; C&M 886). A promise to perform despite non-occurrence of a condition is binding without consideration unless occurrence was a material part of the agreed exchange (and the promisee had no duty that it occur) or the uncertainty was a risk the promisor assumed. Before the time for occurrence, a waiver of a condition within the other party's control can be retracted by notice unless reliance makes reinstatement unjust (estoppel) (§ 84(2), UCC § 2-209(5)). After the time has passed the waiver cannot be retracted even without reliance (election). "Intentional relinquishment of a known right" is not a useful formulation; what is excused is the non-occurrence of a condition.
29. McKenna v. Vernon (Pa. 1917, C&M 887 to 888): each payment conditioned on the architect's certificate; the owner made every progress payment but one without asking for a certificate and watched the work daily without complaint; he waived the certificate and cannot raise it against final payment "for work shown to have been honestly and substantially performed." Notes: Sethness-Greenleaf (lenience is not a promise to forbear indefinitely); could Vernon reinstate for later payments, and how; the consideration problem. Anti-waiver clauses are not always honored (Hovnanian, Md. 2011); waiver of a no-waiver clause needs clear and convincing evidence (Wis. Elec. Power, 7th Cir.). UCC § 2-208 cmt. 3 prefers "waiver" over course-of-performance reading where needed for flexibility. C&M 887.
30. Problems: Luttinger buyers promise to proceed with a relative's financing, then renege; does reliance by the Rosens matter (§ 84(2))? Mattei says he is not satisfied, then changes his mind within the period. Luttinger buyers made no attempt at all: may the Rosens keep $8,500, $12,750, as liquidated damages, "at their option"? C&M 888 to 889.
31. Prevention and hindrance (C&M 889 to 893). United States v. Peck: conduct of one party that prevents the other from performing excuses the other's nonperformance. Broker recovers when the seller's frustrating conduct (selling to a third party) prevents closing (Alaska 1986). Insurance: courts generally do not limit prevention to bad-faith conduct where the insurer's failure to pay is a breach, though prevention may only suspend the insured's duties. Ide v. British Airways (S.D.N.Y. 2021): obstacles to requesting refunds excused the condition. Shear v. NRA (D.C. Cir. 1979): no prevention where the contract authorizes the party to prevent the condition ("assumption of risk"), but Shear did not assume that risk. Kooleraire (N.Y. 1971): a party cannot rely on a condition it frustrated (board asked comptroller to withhold certificate). McCarthy v. Tobin: prevention coupled with waiver. Snow Phipps v. KCAKE (Del. Ch.): buyer who derailed debt financing cannot rely on its absence. Cooperation: implied duty to take steps essential to the other's performance (site availability; Kehm); limited to good-faith consideration where approval is discretionary (Curtis, Ind.); enthusiasm not required. Burden: the party invoking prevention must show the other's conduct was at least a partial cause; the other must then show its conduct did not contribute materially (Shear). Iron Trade v. Wilkoff begins at 893 (buyer's purchases from others made performance harder but did not prevent it; context).

## 7. Constructive conditions of exchange and order of performance

32. Section 2 frames the doctrine: constructive conditions and material breach; failure to render, or in some cases to offer, performance can excuse the other party's duty; prospective failure can too; "dependent" versus "independent" covenants. C&M 828.
33. Pre-Kingston baseline: mutual covenants independent unless expressed otherwise (1500 Year Book hypothetical; father must convey even if the groom marries another). C&M 829.
34. Kingston v. Preston (K.B. 1773, C&M 829 to 830). Apprentice was to give "good and sufficient security," approved by the mercer, "at and before the sealing and delivery of the deeds." Mansfield's three kinds of covenants: (1) mutual and independent, each sues for breach, the other's breach no defense; (2) conditions and dependent, one performance depends on prior performance of the other; (3) mutual conditions to be performed at the same time, where a party ready and offering to perform may sue the other who neglected or refused. Dependence is collected "from the evident sense and meaning of the parties," and precedency depends "on the order of time in which the intent of the transaction requires their performance." The essence was that the mercer should not trust the apprentice's personal security; the giving of security was a condition precedent; judgment for the defendant. Plaintiff's personal security was admitted to be worth nothing. C&M 830.
35. Drafting reprise: making sailing with the next wind a duty only is hard after Kingston because a court may treat the duty as a constructive condition anyway. C&M 830 to 831.
36. Time for performance (C&M 831). Fixing the time allocates the risk of performing first; holdup problem (party who sinks costs first can be held hostage). Default: "the doing must take place before the giving" (Coletti, N.Y. 1930); § 234 cmt. e: where work is to be done by one party and payment made by the other, the work must precede payment absent contrary intention. Holmes: implied conditions rest on judgments of policy.
37. § 234(1): performances that can be rendered simultaneously are due simultaneously unless language or circumstances indicate the contrary (cmt. a: maximum security and no financing burden). § 234(2): where only one party's performance requires a period of time, that party performs first. Comment b's five categories (different periods fixed means no simultaneity); Illus. 9 (concrete work, payment not due until finished) and 10 (bond to secure payment must come first, the Kingston pattern); Illus. 11 (progress payments as agreed). § 234 cmt. f: progress payment clauses and wage statutes reduce the rule's importance.
38. § 238 (not assigned): where performances are simultaneous, each party's duty is conditioned on the other's rendering or offering performance with manifested present ability (Mansfield's third category).
39. Stewart v. Newbury begins at 832 (no express payment term; monthly-payment claim disputed). The holding is on unassigned pages.

## 8. Substantial performance and material breach

40. Express versus constructive. Luttinger required strict compliance with an express condition; for constructive conditions the rule is often substantial performance. C&M 846. Substantial performance "applies only to constructive and not express conditions." C&M 851 Note 1; § 237 cmt. d (if the parties made full performance a condition, substantial performance is not sufficient, and relief must come through § 229). Oppenheimer v. Oppenheim (N.Y. 1995) rejects universal application of substantial performance where language leaves no doubt an express condition was intended. C&M 853.
41. Origins and setting. Boone v. Eyre; construction is the proving ground because labor is united to land, leases and land sales follow other rules, employees are paid at short intervals, goods can be resold, and construction parties lack specific performance. C&M 847.
42. Jacob & Youngs v. Kent (N.Y. 1921, C&M 847 to 851). $77,000 country house; $3,483.46 balance; Reading pipe specified; other brands installed through a subcontractor's oversight, identical except for the stamped name; replacing them meant demolishing substantial parts of the finished structure; architect refused the final certificate. Holding (Cardozo): the omission was neither fraudulent nor willful; an omission "both trivial and innocent" may be atoned for by an allowance of damages rather than treated as breach of a condition followed by forfeiture; the line between important and trivial is one of degree, for the jury if doubtful, for the court if the inferences are certain; weigh "the purpose to be served, the desire to be gratified, the excuse for deviation from the letter, the cruelty of enforced adherence"; substitution may matter more "in fields of art" than "of mere utility"; no change tolerated if so dominant or pervasive as to frustrate the purpose; parties may by "apt and certain words" make every term a condition, but the law will be slow to impute that purpose "where the significance of the default is grievously out of proportion to the oppression of the forfeiture"; "The willful transgressor must accept the penalty of his transgression"; a transgressor whose default "is unintentional and trivial may hope for mercy if he will offer atonement."
43. Measure of the allowance. Normally the cost of replacement (the money to complete), "unless the cost of completion is grossly and unfairly out of proportion to the good to be attained," in which case the difference in value, here "nominal or nothing." Vermont versus New Hampshire granite. The plaintiff never reached the stage of proving the allowance because the trial court excluded evidence of insubstantiality; an offer of proof directed to difference in value would not necessarily have been defective. Disposition: order affirmed and judgment absolute for the plaintiff on the stipulation. C&M 850.
44. Dissent (McLaughlin, with Pound and Andrews; 4 to 3): the failure was intentional or due to gross neglect amounting to the same; 2,000 to 2,500 feet installed, at most 1,000 complied; no proof of the cost of compliance; the owner was entitled to what he contracted for whatever his reason. C&M 850 to 851.
45. Notes. Bright-line variants: "structural defects" (Spence v. Ham) but Kizziar; a percentage rule (Rochkind, 10%) but Jardine. Serial objections (Cawley v. Weiner: an owner's list of defects does not estop proof of others unless the builder was harmed). "Willful": generally said to bar substantial performance; § 241 instead asks whether the party's behavior comports with good faith and fair dealing, weighed with other factors and not decisive; willfulness may also affect damages. Burden: some courts require the owner to prove defect damages; Vance (Tex. 1984) puts the cost of remedying on the builder. Problem: owner withheld $150,000 and has a $250,000 claim; contractor owes $100,000 (Kirk Reid). The actual J&Y contract had architect-certificate, brand-as-standard, written-approval, tear-down and difference-in-value terms; how could they be improved? Conclusiveness of certificate. C&M 851 to 854.
46. § 237: each party's remaining duties are conditioned on there being "no uncured material failure by the other party to render any such performance due at an earlier time" (except § 240). Material failure, including defective performance, operates as non-occurrence of a condition: it suspends, and if not cured in time (§ 242) discharges. Cmt. b: the central problem is which party made the first uncured material failure. Cmt. c: ignorance immaterial. Cmt. d: substantial performance is the same question stated from the builder's side; substantial means not material; contractor recovers the balance less damages; if not substantial, no contract claim, possibly restitution (§ 374). Cmt. e: separate contracts unaffected. Illus. 1 and 2: unpaid progress payment lets the builder stop; cure possible until too late.
47. § 241 factors: (a) deprivation of reasonably expected benefit; (b) adequacy of compensation; (c) forfeiture by the party failing; (d) likelihood of cure, including reasonable assurances; (e) good faith and fair dealing. Cmt. a: circumstances, not rules; a non-material failure can still be a partial breach. Cmt. b: no ratio rule; structural defects significant; sale of goods has a stricter perfect-tender standard. Cmt. d: failure late after substantial performance, or of work that cannot be salvaged (building on owner's land), less likely material; progress payments and restitution can reduce forfeiture. Cmt. f: "wilful" is a less precise term for factor (e); neither adherence nor non-adherence is conclusive. Illus. 6 = Jacob & Youngs: not material under (a), (c) and (e); A recovers $10,000 balance subject to B's damages claim. Illus. 7: builder who angrily refuses to build a $300 compressor cover still recovers $40,000 less damages; factor (e) did not make the failure material.
48. Section 4 framework (C&M 854 to 855). Steps: uncured breach; a duty within the exchange (§ 232 presumption unless a contrary intention is clearly manifested); performance due before the aggrieved party's (§ 237), otherwise which material breach came first; materiality under § 241. Non-material breach: the aggrieved party must keep performing and has a claim for partial breach only. Material breach: the aggrieved party may continue and sue for partial breach, or stop and treat the breach as total; materiality is not the aggrieved party's choice, but partial-or-total treatment is. Total breach: terminate own performance (§ 237) and recover damages for the whole contract (§ 236(1)). Trainer illustration: five minutes short in session six is not material; four missed sessions and no response is. Where appropriate the aggrieved party should suspend and allow cure before treating the breach as total; § 242 lists the circumstances for how long to wait; cf. UCC § 2-508(2).

## 9. Divisibility (Gill v. Johnstown Lumber, Pa. 1892, C&M 872 to 874)

49. Test (Parsons): if one party's performance consists of several distinct items and the price is apportioned to each item, or left to be implied by law, the contract is generally severable; if the consideration is single and entire, the contract is entire even if the subject has several items. C&M 872.
50. Holding: severable, because the price was apportioned by kind of timber and destination ($1 per thousand feet for oak, 75 cents for other logs, 3 and 5 cents per tie); Gill recovers at the stipulated rate for every log and tie delivered at the specified points; nothing for logs the flood swept past the boom, whether partly driven or untouched, because for each log the price was for the whole distance and not apportioned to parts of the drive (common-carrier analogy). Judgment reversed; new trial (venire facias de novo). The trial court had directed a verdict for the lumber company on the theory the contract was entire. C&M 872 to 873.
51. Notes. Halfway at 50 cents (no; not divisible by distance). Pennsylvania Exchange Bank (Ct. Cl. 1959): the first three steps were incidental to Step IV; not divisible; assignment for creditors was a total breach (implied condition that the promisor will not disable itself). Uses of divisibility, including Internatio-Rotterdam's paired Houston and Lake Charles duties. Grease Monkey v. Godat (Mo. App. 1996): divisibility can sever a fee-shifting clause from a contract whose financing condition failed; the seller paid the buyer's fees. C&M 873 to 874. § 240 (agreed equivalents) is the Restatement analogue (not assigned).

## 10. Restitution for the party in breach and after a failed condition

52. Traditional rule: no recovery by a party on its own breach (Lawrence v. Miller); Britton v. Turner is the watershed (unassigned pages). C&M 875.
53. Nelson v. Hazel (Idaho 1967, C&M 883 to 884). The contractor had been held not to have substantially performed, so no contract recovery; building contracts are an exception to the rule that a defaulting party recovers nothing, because the owner would be unjustly enriched; even without substantial performance, a negligent contractor recovers the benefit actually conferred to avoid forfeiture; where defects are remediable the owner's offset is the market cost of correction by another. Numbers: labor and materials $5,090.86, less $1,997.51 paid, less $2,494.50 repair set-off, judgment $598.85 for the contractor. "If the defective performance, though less than substantial, has conferred benefit on the homeowner in excess of his injury, he is under a quasi-contractual duty to pay that excess." The breach was negligent, not willful.
54. Notes: UCC § 2-718(2) (defaulting buyer's restitution); land down payments: Maxton (N.Y. 1986, keeps the rule for about 10%), Vines (Conn. 1980, non-willful purchaser has a restitution claim but a 10% liquidated-damages clause is presumptively reasonable), Kutzin (N.J. 1991, § 374(1), purchaser bears the burden). Restitution and conditions: when a financing condition fails, unless the contract says otherwise the seller cannot keep a partial payment (Blackacre hypothetical). C&M 884 to 885.

## 11. Distinctions the notes and problems teach (for gap checking)

- Express condition (strict, § 229 the only safety valve) versus constructive condition (substantial performance and § 241 materiality). C&M 846, 851; § 237 cmt. d.
- Condition versus duty versus both, and the remedy consequences of each. C&M 815; § 225(3).
- Pay-when-paid (timing term, reasonable time) versus pay-if-paid (condition, enforced in most states if clear; void by public policy in California, Nevada and New York courts). C&M 818 to 819.
- Subcontractor versus broker (closing as condition of commission). C&M 819; § 227 Illus. 4.
- Subjective versus objective satisfaction; portrait versus barn; party satisfaction versus independent third-party (architect) satisfaction tested by honesty, with gross mistake treated as bad faith. C&M 820 to 823; § 228; § 227 cmt. c.
- Waiver before versus after the time for the condition (retractable versus binding election). C&M 886; § 84.
- Prevention that excuses versus mere added difficulty (Peck versus Iron Trade), and contract-authorized prevention. C&M 889 to 893.
- Cost of completion versus difference in value. C&M 850.
- Innocent and trivial versus willful deviations; § 241(e) good faith weighed, not decisive. C&M 849 to 852; § 241 cmt. f, Illus. 7.
- Material breach (election to treat as total) versus non-material breach (must continue performing). C&M 854 to 855.
- Divisible by item versus not divisible by distance. C&M 873.
- Substantial performance (contract balance less damages) versus less-than-substantial performance (restitution of net benefit, Nelson). C&M 883 to 884; § 237 cmt. d.
