### E4. Timeline 'conditions': substantial performance presented as a possible substitute for an express condition

- Surface: Timeline 'conditions' summary fields, field now; Timeline 'conditions' step 4 (jacob-youngs), field context. Both are prior corrections (2026-11-17-timeline-redesign.json); the corrected text is itself inaccurate.
- Current text (now): "Courts may interpret language, recognize waiver or apply substantial-performance rules to prevent disproportionate loss. Those doctrines do not automatically replace an unmistakable express condition." Current text (context): "It does not generally substitute for strict compliance with an unmistakable express condition."
- Verdict: OVERSTATED ("automatically" and "generally" imply that substantial performance sometimes satisfies an express condition).
- Counterexample: in Luttinger a commitment at 8¾% met the amount and term but not the 8½% ceiling. A student who reads "not automatically" may argue the condition was substantially performed. The casebook says Luttinger "required strict compliance with the express condition" and that substantial performance "applies only to constructive and not express conditions"; Oppenheimer (N.Y. 1995) rejects applying it "when the language of the agreement leaves no doubt that an express condition precedent was intended." Relief from an express condition comes only through excuse (waiver, prevention, § 229).
- Pinpoint: C&M 846, 851 Note 1, 853 Problem 2; § 237 cmt. d ("there is no mitigating standard of materiality or substantiality applicable to the non-occurrence of that event"; relief "must be through excuse of the non-occurrence of the condition to avoid forfeiture. See § 229").
- Replacement (now): "A condition makes a duty depend on an event. Courts prefer to read doubtful language as a promise or a timing term. An express condition must occur unless its non-occurrence is excused, as by waiver, prevention or disproportionate forfeiture; substantial performance applies only to constructive conditions. The contract’s language and the governing law matter."
- Replacement (context): "Substantial performance applies to promises and constructive conditions in this setting. It does not satisfy an express condition; relief from one must come through excuse, such as § 229."

### E5. Jacob & Youngs rule line omits the express-condition proviso

- Surface: Effective brief CT-NEXT-CM-0127: rule (also shown as Chronology 'course-chronology-0' step 78, field note).
- Current text: "A trivial and innocent departure from a construction contract does not forfeit the builder's right to the price but is compensated in damages, measured by diminution in value when the cost of replacement is grossly and unfairly disproportionate to the benefit."
- Verdict: MISSING CONDITION.
- Counterexample: Problem 2 asks whether Kent's lawyer could have drafted around the result; Cardozo's dictum says parties may "by apt and certain words" make performance of every term a condition of recovery, and Oppenheimer applies that dictum to deny substantial performance. A student applying the rule line as written would let the builder recover under such a clause.
- Pinpoint: C&M 849 ("This is not to say that the parties are not free by apt and certain words..."), 853 Problem 2; § 237 cmt. d.
- Replacement (47 words): "Absent apt and certain words making full compliance a condition, a trivial and innocent departure from a construction contract does not forfeit the builder's right to the price; damages compensate it, measured by diminution in value when replacement cost is grossly and unfairly disproportionate to the benefit."

## (c) Ranked teaching gaps

Ranked by exam weight and by how often the distinction recurs in the assigned notes and problems.

G1. Material versus non-material breach and what the aggrieved party may do (Section 4 framework). Home: constructive-conditions concept, notes; and a new K-27 step between e and g. Draft: "If the other party's uncured breach is not material, you must keep performing and sue only for partial breach; if it is material, you may suspend, and once the time for cure has passed (§ 242) treat the breach as total, stop performing and recover damages for the whole contract (§§ 237, 236(1)). A trainer who cuts one session five minutes short commits only a partial breach; one who misses four sessions and ignores messages commits a material one." (C&M 854–855.)

G2. Express conditions are excused only, never substantially performed, and § 229 is the forfeiture safety valve. Home: forfeiture concept, notes; K-28 node e detail. Draft: "Restatement § 229 lets a court excuse a condition to the extent its non-occurrence would cause disproportionate forfeiture, unless occurrence was a material part of the agreed exchange; in Conley (N.J. App. Div. 2015) notice by e-mail and fax instead of certified mail was excused because the realtors had timely actual notice." (C&M 824–825; § 237 cmt. d.)

G3. Waiver rules by timing and limits. Home: waiver concept, notes; K-28 node c detail. Draft: "A promise to perform despite a condition's non-occurrence binds without consideration unless the condition was a material part of the agreed exchange or its uncertainty was a risk the promisor assumed (§ 84(1)). Made before the time for a condition within the other party's control, it can be retracted by timely notice unless the other party has materially changed position; made after that time, it is an election and cannot be retracted. Anti-waiver clauses do not always hold." (C&M 886–887; § 84.)

G4. Prevention authorities and the burden of proof. Home: prevention concept, notes (with E11); K-28 node d detail. Draft: "Conduct that prevents the other party's performance or the occurrence of a condition excuses it (Peck; Kooleraire; § 245), but conduct that only makes performance harder does not (Iron Trade), and there is no prevention where the contract authorizes the conduct (Shear). The party invoking prevention must show the other's conduct was at least a partial cause; the other must then show it did not contribute materially." (C&M 889–893.)

G5. Pay-if-paid versus pay-when-paid beyond Florida, and the broker contrast. Home: Peacock map notes; express-conditions concept. Draft: "Florida later held that a clear clause is a condition and an ambiguous one fixes a reasonable time, with nothing for the jury (DEC Electric); courts in California, Nevada and New York have voided pay-if-paid clauses on public-policy grounds; and a broker's commission payable "at time of closing" is generally a condition (Harding Realty; § 227 Illus. 4), because a broker, unlike a small subcontractor, is usually taken to assume that risk." (C&M 818–819.)

G6. Third-party and objective satisfaction. Home: satisfaction concept, notes (currently Mattei only); Gibson map notes. Draft: "An architect's or engineer's decision binds if honest, even if unreasonable, but not if fraudulent or grossly mistaken (Rizzolo; RaDec; § 227 Illus. 5–8). Where it is practicable to judge whether a reasonable person would be satisfied, § 228 prefers that standard; Fursmidt held a merchant buyer of potatoes to commercial good faith, and Morin applied a reasonable-person test to commercial goods despite a sole-discretion clause." (C&M 821–824.)

G7. Order of performance defaults. Home: constructive-conditions concept; K-27 node d (with E3). Draft: "Performances that can be exchanged at once are due simultaneously (§ 234(1)), so each party must tender to put the other in breach; where one performance takes time, it comes first, so work precedes payment (§ 234(2), cmt. e). Fixing the order allocates the risk of performing without being paid, which is why parties use progress payments and security, as in Kingston." (C&M 830–831.)

G8. Condition, duty or both, and the preference for duty. Home: K-27 node a detail; express-conditions concept. Draft: "If the language makes sailing with the next wind a duty only, late sailing gives damages but the premium is still owed; if a condition only, no damages but no premium; if both, damages and no premium. When the language is doubtful, courts prefer the duty reading if the event is within the obligee's control (§ 227(2)), except in contracts like insurance where only one party undertakes duties." (C&M 815–816; § 227 cmt. d, Illus. 9–11.)
