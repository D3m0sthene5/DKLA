## Part 1. Statements that change a legal result

### C-01. Speakers of Sport, Cold Call plain-English sentences, at 2.2

Current text: "doctrine (fraud based on a broken promise about the future)"

Verdict: CHANGES RESULT.

The gloss defines promissory fraud as a broken promise. The casebook defines it by the promisor's state of mind when the promise was made: "The law distinguishes between a promise that has not been performed and a promise that at the time of its making the promisor never intended to perform. The first is a simple breach for which the promisee is entitled to expectation damages. The second gives rise to an action ... called promissory fraud" (C&M 519). Restatement § 171(2) likewise treats a promise as an assertion that the promisor intends to perform.

Counterexample. In Junk v. Aon (C&M 519–520) the claim rests on evidence that Aon had not started development or allocated funds when its vice-president spoke, which goes to intent at the time of the promise. Conversely, a promisor who sincerely intends to perform and later fails has broken a promise and committed no fraud. A student using the Atlas gloss would classify every unkept promise as promissory fraud and would expose an ordinary breach to the punitive damages that the casebook associates only with promissory fraud (C&M 519).

Pinpoint: C&M 519; C&M 523 ("allegedly fraudulent promise"); Restatement § 171(2).

Replacement: "Separately, Illinois \"promissory-fraud\" doctrine (fraud based on a promise that the promisor did not intend to keep when making it) required a \"scheme to defraud\" evidenced by more than the allegedly fraudulent promise itself, and Speakers showed none."

### C-02. Speakers of Sport, Takeaway (tools/r19/data/takeaways.json)

Current text: "is sales talk, not a promise, when third parties decide whether to sign him"

Verdict: CHANGES RESULT (overstated into a categorical rule).

The court did not hold that a statement about third-party action is sales talk for that reason. It said the opposite: "It is possible to make a binding promise of something over which one has no control; such a promise is called a warranty" (C&M 522). The decisive question was the "only reasonable meaning" of what ProServ said, read as "a sales pitch that is intended, and that a reasonable person in the position of the 'promisee' would understand, to be aspirational rather than enforceable" (C&M 522–523). Third-party control was one contextual reason for that reading, together with the absence of any contention that Rodriguez understood ProServ to be "legally obligated to make up the difference" (C&M 522).

Counterexample. An agent who says "if sponsors pay you less than $2 million, I will pay the difference" is promising a result that third parties control, yet the court's own reasoning treats that as a possible warranty (C&M 522–523). Rodi v. Southern New England School of Law (C&M 521), where accreditation was "solely in the hands of the ABA," is presented as a question of what the statements conveyed and what the dean knew, which the takeaway's rule would answer automatically.

Pinpoint: C&M 522–523.

Replacement: "ProServ's statement that it could get an athlete $2 million to $4 million in endorsements was sales talk (puffing), not a guarantee: a reasonable person in his position would understand it as optimism that ProServ would try, because advertisers would decide whether to pay and nothing suggested ProServ promised to make up a shortfall. A party can bind itself to a result it does not control (a warranty), so the outcome turns on what the statement reasonably conveyed."

### C-03. Speakers of Sport, Cold Call plain-English sentences, at 2.0

Current text: "the kind of sales hype no one takes literally"

Verdict: CHANGES RESULT.

Posner said the endorsement statement was puffing "not in the most common sense of a cascade of extravagant adjectives but in the equally valid sense of a sales pitch that is intended, and that a reasonable person in the position of the 'promisee' would understand, to be aspirational rather than enforceable—an expression of hope rather than a commitment" (C&M 522). The gloss substitutes the "extravagant adjectives" sense the court expressly set aside.

Counterexample. ProServ's statement contained a specific dollar range ($2 million to $4 million) that a listener could take literally as a figure. Under the gloss, a specific numerical statement is not "hype no one takes literally," so a student would conclude it is not puffery, which is the opposite of the holding.

Pinpoint: C&M 522.

Replacement: "The Seventh Circuit read ProServ's pitch as aspirational \"puffery\": a sales pitch that ProServ intended, and that a reasonable person in Rodriguez's position would understand, as an expression of hope rather than an enforceable commitment. ProServ was saying it would try to get endorsements and was optimistic about the result, not guaranteeing it."

### C-04. Swinton, Takeaway (tools/r19/data/takeaways.json)

Current text: "There had to be a misleading statement, concealment, or duty to disclose."

Verdict: CHANGES RESULT.

Swinton's declaration alleged that the bank "falsely and fraudulently concealed from the plaintiff its true condition," and it failed. The court explained: "The charge is concealment and nothing more; and it is concealment in the simple sense of mere failure to reveal, with nothing to show any peculiar duty to speak. The characterization of the concealment as false and fraudulent of course adds nothing in the absence of further allegations of fact" (C&M 510). The kind of concealment that counts is conduct by which the seller "by any means prevented the plaintiff from acquiring information as to the condition of the house" (C&M 510), which Restatement § 160 calls action "intended or known to be likely to prevent another from learning a fact."

Counterexample. Swinton itself: a student told that "concealment" suffices would hold the Swinton declaration sufficient, because it pleaded concealment in those words.

Pinpoint: C&M 510; Restatement § 160.

Replacement: "There had to be a misleading statement, active concealment that prevented the buyer from learning the facts, or a duty to disclose."

### C-05. Swinton, Effective brief CTR-037: rule; Chronology 'course-chronology-0' step 112 note (appears 2 times)

Current text: "In an arm's-length sale of real estate, a seller's bare nondisclosure of a known hidden defect creates no liability absent a false statement, half-truth, active concealment, or a relationship imposing a duty to speak."

Verdict: CHANGES RESULT (a 1942 Massachusetts tort rule stated as a universal present rule covering every remedy) and MISSING CONDITION (sources of a duty to speak).

Three problems. First, the sentence has no jurisdiction or date. The assigned notes report that other courts and a statute impose the duty this rule denies: Hill v. Jones (Ariz. App. 1986) held "that seller has duty to disclose the existence of termite damage which materially affects the value of the property" (C&M 511–512); Virginia requires home sellers to disclose known defects unless the property is sold "as is" (C&M 511); Weintraub (N.J.) and Holcomb (N.D.) rejected Swinton and ordered rescission (C&M 511–512); in Johnson v. Davis (Fla.) the sellers "were required to repay the amounts they had received from the buyers, with interest" (C&M 512). Kannavos itself notes that "The Swinton case may not represent the law elsewhere" (C&M 514 n.1). Restatement § 161(b), comment d and Illustration 5 treat a seller's knowing silence about a termite-riddled house as "equivalent to an assertion that the house is not riddled with termites," so that avoidance is governed by § 164.

Second, "creates no liability" covers every remedy. Swinton decided only a demurrer to "a declaration in an action of tort" (C&M 514, describing Swinton), and the casebook leaves rescission and specific performance open (C&M 511, Note 1).

Third, Swinton did not confine the duty to speak to a relationship. It separately asked whether there was a fiduciary relation, whether the plaintiff "stood in a position of confidence toward or dependence upon the defendant," and whether there was "any peculiar duty to speak" (C&M 510). A statutory duty, such as Virginia's, is a duty to speak that comes from no relationship.

Counterexample. A buyer in Arizona, or a buyer seeking avoidance under the Restatement on Swinton's facts, loses under the Atlas rule and wins under Hill v. Jones or § 161(b) and Illustration 5.

Pinpoint: C&M 510–512, 514 n.1; Restatement § 161(b), cmt. d, illus. 5; § 164.

Replacement: "Under the Massachusetts rule applied in Swinton (1942), a seller's bare nondisclosure of a known, nonapparent defect in an arm's-length sale of real estate did not support the buyer's tort action, absent a false statement, a half-truth, conduct preventing the buyer from acquiring information, a fiduciary relation or relation of confidence or dependence, or another peculiar duty to speak. Other jurisdictions, statutes and Restatement § 161 impose disclosure duties, and the decision did not resolve rescission."

### C-06. Swinton, Map entry: notes

Current text (a): "is the standard statement of caveat emptor: absent a false statement, a fiduciary relationship or a special duty to speak, a seller's silence about a known, non-obvious defect in an arm's-length sale is not actionable"

Current text (b): "The sale took place on September 12, 1938 and the infestation was discovered on August 30, 1940"

Verdict: (a) CHANGES RESULT and MISSING CONDITION; (b) OVERSTATED.

(a) The list of exceptions drops two that Swinton itself named: "the uttering of a half truth which may be tantamount to a falsehood" and conduct by which the defendant "by any means prevented the plaintiff from acquiring information" (C&M 510). It also drops the relation of "confidence toward or dependence upon" the seller (C&M 510). It states the rule in the present tense without jurisdiction, which the sources contradict (see C-05). The phrase "the standard statement of caveat emptor" has no support in the assigned sources, which present Swinton as a rule that some courts reject (C&M 511–512). Caveat emptor ("let the buyer beware," standard legal Latin) is the rule that a seller need not disclose defects.

Counterexample. Kannavos involved no false statement (everything said "was substantially true") and no fiduciary relationship (C&M 513–514), yet the buyers rescinded because of a half-truth. A student working from the note's three-item list would find no listed exception and deny relief.

(b) These dates come from the declaration ("on or about September 12, 1938"; "learned of them about August 30, 1940") and were never found as facts, because the case was decided on a demurrer (C&M 510–511).

Pinpoint: C&M 510–514.

Replacement (a): "the decision states the Massachusetts rule of 1942: absent a false statement, a half-truth, conduct preventing the buyer from acquiring information, a fiduciary relation or relation of confidence or dependence, or another peculiar duty to speak, a seller's bare nondisclosure of a known, nonapparent defect in an arm's-length sale did not support the buyer's tort action. Other courts and statutes require disclosure."

Replacement (b): "The declaration alleged a sale on or about September 12, 1938 and discovery of the infestation about August 30, 1940"

### C-07. Kannavos, Cold Call plain-English sentences, at 2.1

Current text: "(failing to mention a fact) is not fraud"

Verdict: CHANGES RESULT.

The full plain sentence reads: Swinton "was a precedent holding that mere 'nondisclosure' (failing to mention a fact) is not fraud." This is the "silence is never fraud" error. Swinton held only that bare nondisclosure, in an arm's-length deal with no fiduciary relation, no relation of confidence or dependence and no peculiar duty to speak, did not support a Massachusetts tort action (C&M 510–511). Restatement § 161 lists cases in which nondisclosure is "equivalent to an assertion," including a relation of trust and confidence (§ 161(d)) and a known mistake as to a basic assumption where silence breaches good faith and fair dealing (§ 161(b)).

Counterexample. A trustee who sells property to a beneficiary while silent about a known defect is within § 161(d) and within Swinton's own reservation for a "fiduciary relation" (C&M 510). The gloss would tell a student that this silence is not fraud.

Pinpoint: C&M 510–511, 514; Restatement § 161(b), (d).

Replacement: "Swinton was a precedent holding that a seller's bare \"nondisclosure\" (failing to mention a fact), in an arm's-length deal with no fiduciary relationship or other special duty to speak, did not support the buyer's tort claim under Massachusetts law."

### C-08. Vokes, Cold Call plain-English sentences, at 2.0

Current text: "the listener's trust, unequal ability to judge the subject"

Verdict: CHANGES RESULT.

The narrative sentence says "trust," and the gloss turns that into the listener's state of mind. The court's qualification is relational: the fact-opinion rule "does not apply where there is a fiduciary relationship between the parties" (C&M 525). Restatement § 169(a) requires that the recipient "stands in such a relation of trust and confidence to the person whose opinion is asserted that the recipient is reasonable in relying on it." Trust that one party places in the other, with no such relationship, does not qualify. Vokes repeats that parties "dealing on a contractual basis at arm's length" are generally left "where [the courts] find themselves" (C&M 525).

Counterexample. The used-car shopper in the Williams problem (C&M 517), or Rodriguez in Speakers, trusted the seller's pitch. Under the gloss, that trust alone would make an opinion actionable. Under Vokes and § 169 it does not.

Pinpoint: C&M 525; Restatement § 169(a).

Replacement: "\"Superior knowledge\" by the speaker, a relationship of trust between the parties (the court named a fiduciary relationship), unequal ability to judge the subject, and \"devices\" (tricks) making an opinion misleading could support \"relief.\" That was so even when the statements were framed as opinions."

### C-09. Vokes, Cold Call plain-English sentences, at 3.2

Current text: "separately applied Illinois's special requirement for fraud claims based on a fraudulent scheme"

Verdict: CHANGES RESULT.

The scheme requirement applied to a promise: "even if it was a promise (or a warranty), it cannot be the basis for a finding of fraud because it was not part of a scheme to defraud evidenced by more than the allegedly fraudulent promise itself" (C&M 523). The gloss extends it to "fraud claims" generally and garbles its direction, since it reads as a rule about claims that are already "based on a fraudulent scheme."

Counterexample. A knowingly false statement of present fact, such as "two sponsors have already signed," is an ordinary misrepresentation, not a promise. The Atlas's own Speakers narrative (episode 5) says such a statement "would present different representations." Under the gloss a student would demand proof of a scheme for it.

Pinpoint: C&M 523.

Replacement: "That court \"construed\" (interpreted) a forecast of endorsement deals as aspirational sales talk, and separately applied Illinois's requirement that a promissory-fraud claim (one based on a promise made without intent to keep it) show a scheme to defraud beyond the promise itself."

### C-10. Speakers of Sport, Timeline 'misrepresentation' step 5, note (not in prior corrections; only `effect` was corrected)

Current text: "Illinois promissory fraud needs a scheme to defraud beyond one broken promise"

Verdict: MISSING CONDITION (the error is the same as in C-01, in a shorter form).

Promissory fraud is a promise made with no intention to perform (C&M 519). It is not a broken promise. The scheme must be "evidenced by more than the allegedly fraudulent promise itself" (C&M 523). As written, the note implies that a broken promise plus a broader scheme is fraud, even where the promisor meant to perform when it spoke.

Pinpoint: C&M 519, 523.

Replacement: "Aspirational sales talk is puffery, not fraud, and Illinois promissory fraud needs a scheme to defraud evidenced by more than the allegedly fraudulent promise itself."

### C-11. Flowchart K-21 node c (appears 2 times)

Current text: "Did expertise make the assurance misleading?"

Verdict: MISSING CONDITION (this omission produces wrong result 6 in the branch test).

The node is the only opinion branch in the served chart, and it tests only expertise. Vokes lists four qualifications to the fact-opinion rule: "a fiduciary relationship between the parties," "some artifice or trick employed by the representor," parties who "do not in general deal at 'arm's length'," and a representee who "does not have equal opportunity to become apprised of the truth" (C&M 525). It adds superior knowledge (C&M 525). Restatement § 169 adds a relation of trust and confidence (a), special skill, judgment or objectivity (b) and special susceptibility (c). Section 168(2) lets the recipient read an opinion as an assertion that the speaker knows facts justifying it. The casebook also notes that a misrepresentation of law may support relief where the speaker has superior competence, as a lawyer does with a client (C&M 518).

Counterexample. A trustee's opinion that an investment is "sound," or a misleading opinion procured by a trick, reaches h because no expertise is shown.

Pinpoint: C&M 518, 525; Restatement §§ 168(2), 169.

Replacement label: "Was the opinion or prediction actionable in context?" Replacement detail, first sentence: "Test superior knowledge or skill, a relationship of trust and confidence, an artifice or trick, dealing not at arm's length, unequal opportunity to learn the truth, or special susceptibility (Vokes; Restatement § 169)." Keep the existing Vokes and Speakers sentences.

### C-12. Flowchart K-21 node d (appears 2 times)

Current text: "Test half-truth, active concealment, correction, or any independent disclosure duty." The edge reads "No disclosure duty on these facts" → h.

Verdict: MISSING CONDITION (this omission produces wrong result 5 in the branch test).

The node asks whether "the bargain [can] be avoided," but its only authority for "no duty" is Swinton's tort holding. The casebook expressly leaves open whether Swinton could have rescinded (C&M 511, Note 1). Restatement § 161 names the cases in which nondisclosure is equivalent to an assertion for avoidance: (b) a known mistake as to a basic assumption where silence fails good faith and fair dealing; (c) a known mistake as to the contents or effect of a writing; and (d) a relation of trust and confidence. Illustration 5 applies (b) to a termite-ridden house. Statutory duties also exist (Va. Code, C&M 511; slide 12). "Any independent disclosure duty" gestures at these duties but does not tell a student that Swinton's facts can satisfy § 161(b).

Pinpoint: C&M 511–512; Restatement § 161 and cmt. d, illus. 5.

Replacement detail: "Kannavos's apartment-income promotion was misleading without the zoning facts. Swinton's bare termite nondisclosure did not state the Massachusetts tort claim; it did not decide rescission. Test half-truth, active concealment, a known mistake as to a basic assumption where silence breaches good faith and fair dealing, a known mistake about the writing, a relation of trust and confidence, or a statutory duty (Restatement § 161)."

### C-13. Flowchart K-21 node g

Current text: "A release’s scope, the particular allegation and the governing rule decide whether it defeats reliance." The edge reads "Disclaims the asserted reliance and governs" → h.

Verdict: MISSING CONDITION (this omission produces wrong result 7 in the branch test).

Under the New York law applied, "when the facts at issue are 'peculiarly within the misrepresenting party's knowledge,' even a specific disclaimer will not defeat a claim of reasonable reliance." The exception failed in Psenicska because the plaintiffs faced no high cost of verification and could have insisted on written terms or walked away (409 F. App'x at 371; Supp. 33–34). The district court also treated specificity as the "touchstone": the plaintiffs argued that a merger clause "too general" could not preclude fraud, and the court enforced Paragraph 5 because it named "the nature of the Film or the identity of any other Participants" (Supp. 29–30). A merger clause (also called an integration clause; standard term) is a clause stating that the writing is the parties' entire agreement. A no-reliance clause is a clause stating that the signer is not relying on statements outside the writing. Node g mentions neither the exception nor the general-clause distinction.

Pinpoint: Supp. 29–30 (2008 WL 4185752, at *6); Supp. 33–34 (409 F. App'x at 371).

Replacement detail: "The Borat analysis turned on a specific disclaimer of reliance on statements about the film's nature and participants. Under the New York law applied, a disclaimer of reliance on the very matter alleged ordinarily defeats reliance, unless the facts were peculiarly within the speaker's knowledge and could not cheaply be verified or written into the contract; a general entire-agreement clause was not the basis of decision."

### C-14. Vokes, Takeaway (tools/r19/data/takeaways.json)

Current text: "The alleged expert flattery could support a fraud claim"

Verdict: MISSING CONDITION (remedy). This is the error of confusing a tort damages claim with rescission.

Vokes sought "cancellation of her dance contracts, an accounting, and recovery of the portion of her payments not chargeable to instruction already given" (Atlas brief, Parties). The court framed the relief as equitable: "a Court exercising inherent chancery power" (C&M 524) and "the equitable arm of the Court" (C&M 525). The defendants' argument concerned when "contracts can only be rescinded for fraud or misrepresentation" (C&M 524). "A fraud claim" suggests a deceit action for damages, which the casebook treats as a separate question (C&M 516, Note 1; C&M 518, on scienter in tort versus rescission).

Pinpoint: C&M 524–525.

Replacement: "The alleged expert flattery could support her equitable claim to cancel the contracts for fraud or misrepresentation"

### C-15. Lecture notes record, topic "Liability for non-disclosure"

Current text: "and “failure to act in good faith and in accordance with reasonable standards of fair dealing” (Restatement § 161)"

Verdict: MISSING CONDITION.

Slide 11 abbreviates § 161(b) in the same way, and the record repeats it accurately. As a statement of § 161, though, it drops the conditions that the speaker "knows that disclosure of the fact would correct a mistake of the other party as to a basic assumption on which that party is making the contract." It also omits that § 161 applies "in the following cases only" and omits clause (c) (a known mistake about the writing).

Counterexample. Restatement § 161, Illustration 7: a seller who knows the buyer is unaware of a planned shopping center has no duty, because the buyer's mistake "is not one as to a basic assumption." Illustration 10 reaches the same result for minerals discovered through government surveys. A student working from the abbreviated clause would ask only whether the silence was unfair.

Pinpoint: Restatement § 161(b), (c), cmt. d, illus. 7, 10; slide 11.

Replacement: "and where the person knows that disclosure would correct the other party's mistake as to a basic assumption and silence amounts to a “failure to act in good faith and in accordance with reasonable standards of fair dealing” (Restatement § 161(b)); § 161(c) adds a known mistake as to the contents or effect of a writing"

### C-16. Kannavos, Map entry: summary; Swinton, Cold Call plain-English sentences, at 3.1

Current text (Kannavos summary): "Advertising property as an income-producing apartment investment made silence about known zoning violations a misleading half-truth."

Current text (Swinton ≈ 3.1): "the sellers' ads for the property as a rental made their silence about zoning limits misleading"

Only then did it conclude that "enough was done affirmatively to make the disclosure inadequate and partial" (C&M 515). Advertising alone was not held sufficient.

Replacement (summary): "Advertising, income figures, statements about continued apartment use and the sale of apartment furnishings, by sellers who knew the buyer meant to keep renting, made silence about known zoning violations a misleading half-truth."

Replacement (≈ 3.1): "the sellers' ads, income figures and statements presenting the property as a continuing rental made their silence about zoning limits misleading"

### C-17. Kannavos, Effective brief CTR-038: rule; Chronology step 204 note (appears 2 times)

Current text: "a person who speaks on a subject must disclose the material facts needed to keep the communication from being deceptive"

Verdict: MISSING CONDITION (low).

The rule quoted by the court is limited to facts "that lie within his knowledge," and it is framed as a duty to "divulge all the material facts bearing upon the point" (C&M 514). The Atlas omits the knowledge limit, which matters for a claim built on intentional deception, as Kannavos's was ("intentionally deceptive and fraudulent," C&M 515). The Map Reasoning field already includes the limit ("within its knowledge").

Replacement: "a person who speaks on a subject must disclose the material facts within the speaker's knowledge that bear on that subject, so that the communication is not deceptive"

### C-18. Vokes, Effective brief CTR-040: rule; Chronology step 196 note (appears 2 times)

Current text: "where the speaker has superior knowledge, a relationship of trust exists, the parties are unequally able to evaluate the subject, or a device makes the opinion misleading"

### C-19. Swinton, Cold Call plain-English sentences, at 2.1

Current text: "to reveal anything affecting the other's view of the value"

The court's concern was a duty to disclose "any nonapparent defect known to him ... which materially reduces its value and which the buyer fails to discover," and the parallel duty for a buyer's "nonapparent virtue ... which materially enhances its value" (C&M 510).

Pinpoint: C&M 510.

Replacement: "to reveal any known, nonapparent defect or virtue that materially changed the value of what was sold"
