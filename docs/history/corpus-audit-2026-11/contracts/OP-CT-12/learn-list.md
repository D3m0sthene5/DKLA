## 2. Law versus conscience; the baseline of no general duty to disclose (C&M 508–509)
- Cicero's Rhodes grain merchant: conscience may demand disclosure; the question is what the law demands (508).
- Laidlaw v. Organ (U.S. 1817): buyer who knew hours early of the Treaty of Ghent bought tobacco; seller sought rescission for fraud; Marshall, C.J., in dictum, said the buyer was not bound to communicate the information because "it would be difficult to circumscribe the contrary doctrine within proper limits" (508). Note: the casebook labels this statement dictum.
- Note 1, investment in knowledge (509): dealers in antiques or rare coins are not expected to disclose the elements of their evaluations, because expertise would not be worth acquiring without bargaining advantage. Kronman: deliberately acquired, socially useful information is "in essence a property right"; casually acquired information is not. Hirshleifer: "discovery" deserves more protection than "foreknowledge." Cooter & Ulen: "productive" versus "redistributive" information.
- Note 2, Kidd Creek (509): Texas Gulf Sulphur bought mineral rights without disclosing an aerial-survey anomaly; officers who traded stock were liable under securities laws; a landowner sued for nondisclosure. The landowner suit's outcome is not in the excerpt (the note breaks off at the end of p. 509).

## 3. Bare nondisclosure: Swinton v. Whitinsville Sav. Bank (Mass. 1942) (C&M 510–511)
- Facts as alleged: seller bank knew the house was termite-infested; buyer could not readily observe it; buyer sued for repair and termite-control expense (510). Kannavos describes it as "an action of tort" (514).
- Holding: order sustaining the demurrer affirmed; judgment for defendant (511). "The rule of nonliability for bare nondisclosure" (511), adopted in Restatement of Torts § 551.
- What was absent (510): no false statement; no half-truth "tantamount to a falsehood"; nothing showing the seller prevented the buyer from acquiring information (active concealment); no fiduciary relation or confidence/dependence; an arm's-length deal. "Concealment in the simple sense of mere failure to reveal." Calling the concealment "false and fraudulent" adds nothing without further facts.
- Reasoning (510–511): a duty here would make every seller liable for any known nonapparent defect that materially reduces value, and every buyer liable for any known nonapparent virtue; the law has not imposed "a standard so idealistic"; the court concedes moral appeal but "the law cannot provide special rules for termites."
- Note 1 questions (511): whether a tort holding forecloses rescission; whether the seller could get specific performance. Open questions; the next case suggests an answer.

## 4. The latent-defect trend and remedies (Notes after Swinton, C&M 511–512)
- Statutes: Va. Code §§ 55-517 et seq. requires home sellers to disclose known defects unless the property is sold "as is" (511). Some courts require disclosure of dangerous conditions; some impose a builder's implied warranty of fitness for habitation (511).
- Courts rejecting Swinton: Weintraub v. Krobatsch (N.J. 1974) (Swinton "does not represent our sense of justice or fair dealing"); Holcomb v. Zinke (N.D. 1985) (caveat emptor "no longer an expression of American mores"). Contra: Johnson v. Davis (Fla. 1985) (Boyd, C.J., dissenting: blurring misrepresentation and nondisclosure will distort the market). Compass Point (Ala. 1994): no duty to disclose water damage, no fraudulent concealment; distinguished its abrogation of caveat emptor for new homes. Hill v. Jones (Ariz. App. 1986): duty to disclose termite damage that materially affects value (511–512).
- Remedies for concealment (Note 3, 512): Weintraub (buyers sued for specific performance counterclaimed for rescission); Johnson (sellers repaid amounts received, with interest); Holcomb and Weintraub (rescission; Holcomb charged sellers with buyers' cure expenses and charged buyers with reasonable rental value); Compass Point (punitive damages claimed); Virginia statute ("actual damages suffered").
- Note 4 (512): Jewish Ctr. of Sussex County v. Whale (N.J.): employer rescinded a rabbi's contract for concealment of a fraud conviction and disbarment (summary judgment; affirmed on misrepresentation). Fuller v. De Paul (Ill. 1938) posed as a question. DNA child-support problem (512): open questions.

## 5. Half-truths; partial disclosure: Kannavos v. Annino (Mass. 1969) (C&M 513–516)
- Facts (513): seller converted a single-family house to eight apartments in knowing violation of zoning and without a building permit; ads offered it as an income property ("ideal for couple to live free with excellent income"); broker supplied income and expense figures; buyer, without a lawyer, made no inquiry; no statements were made about zoning; everything said was "substantially true."
- Court assumes that wholly silent vendors could not have been found to misrepresent, citing Swinton (513). Swinton is distinguished, not overruled.
- Rule (514): one who speaks on a point, voluntarily or on request, "is bound to speak honestly and to divulge all the material facts bearing upon the point that lie within his knowledge. Fragmentary information may be as misleading as active misrepresentation, and half-truths may be as actionable as whole lies." The court says Swinton itself assumed this principle (514).
- Difference (a), affirmative conduct (515): ads offering investment property, income figures, an express assertion that No. 11 was "being rented to the public for multi-family purposes," sale of apartment furnishings, and knowledge of the buyer's purpose made the disclosure "inadequate and partial, and, in the circumstances, intentionally deceptive and fraudulent." "Because the vendors did as much as they did do, they were bound to do more" (516).
- Difference (b), character of defect (515): zoning is public regulation discoverable through public records or counsel; nonetheless, where there is reliance on fraudulent representations, lack of due diligence is not a bar (Yorke v. Taylor, which allowed rescission for an innocent but false, negligent misrepresentation of assessed value).
- Holding and disposition (516): the vendors' conduct entitled the vendee to rescind; decree overruling the demurrer affirmed; final decree reversed for further consideration of relief because of a fire at No. 11. Footnote 1 (514): Swinton "may not represent the law elsewhere."
- Notes (516): Note 1 — whether Kannavos could also sue in deceit; tort requisites are left to torts. Note 2, Hand v. Dayton-Hudson (6th Cir. 1985): employee retyped a release to carve out his claims; question whether he may keep the money and his claims or is bound to the release as D-H understood it (reformation).

## 6. No-reliance ("big boy") clauses (C&M 516–517; supp. 22–36)
- A no-reliance clause tries to remove the reliance element of a later fraud claim by a declaration that the party relied only on its own judgment (516–517). Extra Equipamentos v. Case Corp. (7th Cir., Posner, J.; printed as "541 F.3d 719 (7th Cir. 2011)"): promissory-fraud claim about a distributorship promise; question whether evidence of the promise is admissible. Factors (Forest Oil v. McAllen, Tex. 2008): boilerplate or negotiated; counsel; business sophistication (517). Compare the parol evidence rule (517).
- Psenicska (district court, Preska, J., Sept. 3, 2008) (supp. 22–31): motions to dismiss granted. (i) "Documentary-style film" is not ambiguous; the operative word is "style"; Borat is told in the style of a true story (28–29). (ii) Fraudulent inducement is foreclosed because each plaintiff disclaimed reliance on "any promises or statements made by anyone about the nature of the Film or the identity of any other Participants" (Agmt. ¶ 5), under Danann Realty v. Harris (29). Elements of fraudulent inducement (FN14, 29): material representation or omission of fact, made with knowledge of falsity and intent to defraud, and damages from reasonable reliance. (iii) The clause is not "too general": it is specific to the very matters alleged (30). (iv) Plaintiffs cannot escape by "restyling their allegations of misrepresentation as allegations of omission" under a duty-to-disclose theory (30). The court calls ¶ 5 a "merger clause" (27, 30).
- Second Circuit summary order (Nov. 17, 2009; 409 Fed. Appx. 368) (supp. 32–34): affirmed. Reasonable reliance is an essential element of fraudulent inducement; a specific disclaimer "destroys" reliance allegations (Danann) (33). The "peculiar-knowledge" exception (facts peculiarly within the misrepresenting party's knowledge) addresses high costs of determining truth and does not apply where the party could have insisted that the writing reflect the oral undertaking; plaintiffs took no cost-free steps such as asking to meet the "reporter" or learn his name, and could have walked away without collateral harm (33–34). Reasonable reliance is also essential to duty-to-disclose or fraudulent-concealment claims; nothing in the Agreement suggested a documentary for Belarus television (34). The court adds that the conduct, if true, does defendants no credit (34).
- The release itself (supp. 35–36): ¶ 1 consent to a "documentary-style film"; ¶ 3 irrevocable; ¶ 4 waiver of listed claims including (n) "fraud (such as any alleged deception or surprise about the Film or this consent agreement)"; ¶ 5 entire-agreement plus non-reliance; ¶ 6 New York forum and law.
- Slides 18–20: three distinct devices — merger clause, statement of non-reliance, waiver of right to sue (slide 19); whether plaintiffs assented and whether Fox had legitimate reasons (slide 18); arguments on contracting out of fraud liability: makers of representations can mitigate known inaccuracies cheaply; autonomy favors enforcement; limiting liability may limit litigation costs (especially where non-legal sanctions are potent) and agency costs (slide 20).
- Unresolved: neither court rested on ¶ 4's fraud waiver alone; both rested on the ¶ 5 reliance disclaimer. The release footnote (35) refers to a different suit by "two men" dismissed Feb. 23, 2007.

## 8. Elements: tort versus contract (C&M 518)
- Concealment and nondisclosure are varieties of misrepresentation, which is both a ground for rescinding a contract and the predicate for a tort action.
- Tort (deceit) requires scienter: knowledge of falsity or at least reckless disregard for truth. In contract law scienter generally has not had the same force, partly because of rescission's equitable character (Halpert v. Rosenthal, R.I. 1970). As a rule a party may avoid a contract induced by an innocent misrepresentation. (Context: Restatement § 164(1): "fraudulent or a material" misrepresentation plus justified reliance makes the contract voidable; § 162 defines fraudulent and material.)
- Misrepresentation of law: sometimes held innocuous, but inequality of competence (lawyer to client, insurance agent to customer) can justify relief (518, citing Kannavos). Fina Supply v. Abilene Nat'l Bank (Tex. 1987) (519): a representation about the legal effect of a document is ordinarily opinion; where parties have equal bargaining position and equal access to legal advice, the misrepresentation-of-law-as-fact doctrine does not apply; judgment for Fina reversed.
- Materiality is required; standards vary; prominent in insurance; serves judicial control of juries (518).
- Diligence and reliance (518): some diligence is required, depending on the victim's capacities, the nature of the transaction, and the plausibility of the representation; distinguish whether the plaintiff relied at all — "reliance and verification are incompatible" (Hayat Carpet, L. Hand, J.). Preferred formulation: justifiable reliance. (Context: Restatement § 172: fault in not discovering facts does not make reliance unjustified unless it amounts to a failure of good faith and fair dealing.)
- Traditional rule: misrepresentation must be of fact, not opinion; "rattled" by Vokes (518).

## 9. Promissory fraud (C&M 519–521)
- An unperformed promise is a breach (expectation damages); a promise the promisor never intended to perform at the time made is promissory fraud, at the intersection of tort and contract, for which punitive as well as compensatory damages may be recovered (519). Key feature: the promisor lacked any intention to perform at the time of the representation (Junk v. Aon).
- Rationales (519–520): lying about intent may be worse than not performing; UCC § 2-609 comment (parties bargain for security of performance); Ayres & Klass: liability promotes credible transfer of information about intent; punitive damages deter misrepresenting the probability of performance.
- Notes (520–521): Junk v. Aon (vice-president's promise about software not yet in development; at-will clause; motion to dismiss denied; appellate result posed as a question); Hoffman v. Red Owl ("negligent promising" versus promissory fraud); Byrd v. Lamar (Ala. 2002) (for the student: dean knew there were no instructors or equipment); Rodi v. S. New Eng. Sch. of Law (1st Cir. 2008) (for the school; facts listed for analysis: catalogue disclaimer, ABA's sole control, "no cause for pessimism" wording, dean a "flawed prognosticator").

## 10. Puffery and promises of uncontrolled results: Speakers of Sport v. ProServ (7th Cir. 1999) (C&M 521–523)
- Facts (521–522): Rodriguez had one-year, terminable-at-will contracts with Speakers; ProServ promised $2–4 million in endorsements; got him none of significance; Speakers sued ProServ, charging that the promise was fraudulent and induced him to terminate; summary judgment for ProServ; affirmed (523).
- Competition is not a tort and supplies the competitor's privilege against the tort of improper interference; the privilege does not cover fraud, but allowing juries to find that unfulfilled promises were knowingly false would make luring clients a grave legal risk (522).
- Puffing defined (522): not only "a cascade of extravagant adjectives" but "a sales pitch that is intended, and that a reasonable person in the position of the 'promisee' would understand, to be aspirational rather than enforceable — an expression of hope rather than a commitment." Contrast a promise to employ and pay $2 million a year.
- Warranty (522–523): a binding promise of something over which one has no control is a warranty; not plausible ProServ was guaranteeing a minimum; the only reasonable meaning was that it would try and was optimistic, so the "promise" was not a promise at all (523).
- Even if a promise or warranty, it "cannot be the basis for a finding of fraud because it was not part of a scheme to defraud evidenced by more than the allegedly fraudulent promise itself" (523).
- Note (523): distinguish puffing, fraud, promissory fraud, misrepresentation; why Speakers did not sue Rodriguez. Slide 8: grounds of suit; the promise; false statements; Posner's definition; why puffing is not actionable.

## 11. Opinion made actionable: Vokes v. Arthur Murray (Fla. App. 1968) (C&M 523–526)
- Facts (523–524): 51-year-old widow; fourteen courses, 2302 hours, $31,090.45 in under sixteen months; flattery, "dance aptitude tests," medal eligibility; complaint alleged the representations were known false and made to induce purchases.
- Posture (524): trial court dismissed the complaint with prejudice for failure to state a cause of action; allegations taken as true; reversed — "plaintiff is entitled to her day in Court" (525). Relief sought is equitable; defendants' argument was framed in terms of rescission (524); the excerpt does not otherwise specify the prayer for relief.
- Rule and qualifications (525): generally a misrepresentation must be of fact rather than opinion; the rule does not apply (1) where there is a fiduciary relationship, (2) where the representor used some artifice or trick, (3) where the parties do not deal at arm's length, or (4) where the representee lacks equal opportunity to learn the truth. A statement of a party with superior knowledge may be treated as fact although it would be opinion between equals (Ramel); defendants could reasonably be supposed to have superior knowledge of her potential and progress.
- Half-truth (525): a party with no duty to disclose who undertakes to speak must disclose the whole truth.
- Characterization (524): conduct went beyond "the unsavory, yet legally permissible, perimeter of sales puffing" into "undue influence, the suggestion of falsehood, the suppression of truth, and the free exercise of rational judgment"; it would be "unconscionable" for a court of chancery to let the contracts stand. Arm's-length deals without unfair practices: courts "leave the parties where they find themselves" (525).
- Notes (526): how Vokes differs from Kannavos; what is objectionable; Gordon's "grotesque" bargain; California Dance Studio Act (contracts made in reliance on willful false or misleading information "void and unenforceable"; lessons must begin within six months), upheld in People v. Arthur Murray (Cal. App. 1965). Slide 9: remedy, exception features, incentives for studios; slide 10 (Hong Kong banker) has only a headline.
- Restatement §§ 168–169 (slides 6–7): an opinion expresses only a belief without certainty as to a fact, or a judgment of quality or value; it may reasonably be read as an assertion that the speaker knows no incompatible facts and knows facts sufficient to justify it; reliance on opinion is justified only with a relation of trust and confidence, reasonable belief in the speaker's special skill, judgment or objectivity, or special susceptibility.

## 12. When silence is an assertion (slides 11–16; Restatement § 161 context)
- Slide 11: at common law silence is treated as an assertion for concealment; failure to correct a previous assertion (half-truth); fiduciary or trust-and-confidence relationship; failure to act in good faith and in accordance with reasonable standards of fair dealing (citing § 161). § 161 text: (a) disclosure needed to prevent a previous assertion from being a misrepresentation; (b) known mistake as to a basic assumption plus bad faith; (c) known mistake as to the contents or effect of a writing; (d) relation of trust and confidence. Concealment by action is § 160.
- Slide 12: statutes impose disclosure liability (securities, franchising, truth-in-lending).
- Slides 13–16: contracting around the rules — buyers can obtain broad representations and warranties ("no material defects"; "no condition ... likely to have a material adverse effect") (slide 14); sellers can use an "AS IS" clause disclaiming warranties (slide 16); Swinton/Kannavos incentive and due-diligence questions (slides 13, 15).
