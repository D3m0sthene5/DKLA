## A. Framing (pp. 1073–1074)

1. A court will "occasionally—but only very occasionally—excuse or suspend a contract obligation" (p. 1073). Mistake (Sections 1–2) concerns facts existing but unknown at formation; impracticability and frustration (Sections 3–4) concern events after formation (p. 1073). Impracticability looks to the cost of performance; frustration looks to the value or purpose of performance (pp. 1073–1074). It is "not sufficient that the party's performance is simply more difficult or costly than anticipated" (p. 1073).
2. Background principle: pacta sunt servanda; regret on fresh information is not enough (p. 1074). Watkins & Son v. Carrig dictum (p. 477): an excavator who hits rock has no mutual-mistake defense (pp. 1074, 1101 Note 3: "if the plaintiff was unwise in taking chances, it is not relieved, on the ground of mistake").

## B. Mutual mistake (§§ 151, 152, 154, 157, 158)

3. Definition, § 151 and cmt a: a mistake is a belief not in accord with the facts existing when the contract is made. A wrong prediction about future events is not a mistake (Illus. 1, boat slips off reef after contracting; Illus. 2, earnings forecast). If the boat had already slipped when the contract was made, it is a mistake governed by § 152 (cmt a). Facts include the law in force at formation (cmt b, Illus. 3).
4. § 152(1) black letter: a mistake (a) of both parties, (b) at the time the contract was made, (c) as to a basic assumption on which the contract was made, (d) that has a material effect on the agreed exchange of performances, makes the contract voidable (e) by the adversely affected party, (f) unless he bears the risk under § 154. § 152(2): materiality is judged after taking account of reformation, restitution or other relief (cmt d; Illus. 10, 11).
5. Basic assumption, § 152 cmt b: same meaning as in §§ 261, 265, 266 and UCC § 2-615(a). Market conditions and the parties' financial situation are ordinarily not basic assumptions. A basic assumption can exist though the parties never consciously considered alternatives (annuitant already dead, Illus. 6).
6. Material effect, § 152 cmt c: it is not enough that the party would not have contracted had he known; he must show an imbalance "so severe that he can not fairly be required to carry it out," ordinarily by showing the exchange is less desirable to him and more advantageous to the other. Hardship with no advantage to the other party is ordinarily left to impracticability and frustration (Illus. 9, dredge unfit for B's special purpose: not voidable; see § 266(2)). Illus. 8: 110 acres at $1,000 an acre, not voidable without more.
7. Mistakes as to different assumptions go to § 153, not § 152 (cmt h).
8. Parol evidence does not bar prior or contemporaneous agreements offered to prove mistake (§ 152 cmt a; p. 1078 Stees Note 4), but the trier examines the evidence "with particular care."
9. Who bears the risk, § 154: (a) allocated by agreement (Illus. 1, quitclaim-type "only such title as he has"); (b) conscious ignorance: the party knows at formation that his knowledge of the relevant facts is limited and treats it as sufficient (cmt c; Illus. 2, seller refused a cancellation clause for surveyor error); (c) allocated by the court because reasonable in the circumstances (cmt d), e.g., seller of farm land that turns out to contain minerals (cmt a; p. 1081 Renner Note 2); builder who meets subsoil needing drainage (Illus. 5; compare § 266 Illus. 8); gravel below water level at 20% added cost (Illus. 4; compare § 266 Illus. 5); bidder's misestimate of labor (Illus. 6). § 154 cmt a rejects the "intrinsic/extrinsic" and "identity/attributes" distinctions in favor of risk allocation.
10. Fault, § 157: a mistaken party's negligent failure to discover the facts does not bar avoidance unless it amounts to a failure to act in good faith and in accordance with reasonable standards of fair dealing. Renner n.2: failure to investigate the water thoroughly does not preclude rescission where the risk was not allocated (p. 1079).
11. Relief, § 158: restitution under §§ 240, 376, and, if those rules and Chapter 16 will not avoid injustice, relief "on such terms as justice requires including protection of the parties' reliance interest."

Teaching case: Stees v. Leonard (Minn. 1874), pp. 1074–1078.
12. Facts: builders contracted under seal to "erect and complete" a building per annexed plans and specifications (prepared by architects; specs called for two-foot foundation walls on six-inch footings). The building fell twice at three stories because the site was quicksand; the builders then refused to perform. The owners sued for $5,214.80 (installments paid $3,745.80, $1,000 loss of use, $469 damage to an adjacent house). Jury for owners; denial of new trial affirmed (pp. 1074–1077).
13. Rule (Young, J.): one who binds himself "by a positive, express contract, to do an act in itself possible" must perform "unless prevented by the act of God, the law, or the other party"; "No hardship, no unforeseen hindrance, no difficulty short of absolute impossibility, will excuse him" (p. 1075). Following School Trustees v. Bennett and Dermott v. Jones: a loss from latent soil defects falls on the contractor (p. 1076). Conformity to the specified footings is no defense: having promised to "erect and complete," the builders had to supply stronger foundations or drain the land (p. 1076). The prior parol agreement that the owners would drain was inadmissible to vary the writing; later drainage promises were excluded for want of alleged consideration or reliance (pp. 1076–1077).
14. The court did not decide a mistake claim; the editors say it "would have been deaf to that appeal" (p. 1074). § 154 Illus. 5 reaches the same allocation under modern mistake doctrine.
15. Notes: (1) Stees sought restitution-type recovery (installments paid, plus loss of use and property damage), not expectation; the test hypothetical asks whether a builder who refused before starting would owe the owner's added cost of a substitute builder (p. 1077). Fried calls the result "harsh and silly." (2) Spearin: where the owner supplies the plans, "the contractor will not be responsible for the consequences of defects in the plans and specifications"; design specification (owner specifies design, materials, methods; impliedly warrants adequacy) versus performance specification (contractor must produce a result by means of its choosing) (p. 1077). In Stees the owners' argument was "foreclosed by the pleadings." (3) Act of God: if a nearly finished building burned after lightning, the builder would still have to rebuild (Butterfield v. Byron) (pp. 1077–1078). (4) Parol evidence and § 152 cmt a (p. 1078).

Teaching case: Renner v. Kehl (Ariz. 1986), pp. 1078–1081.
16. Facts: purchasers contracted to buy leases on more than 2,000 acres near Yuma at about $100 an acre, having made clear that they wanted the land only for jojoba and required adequate water; both sides believed sufficient water was available. Five test wells failed. Trial court: rescission and reimbursement of $309,849.84 ($80,200 down payment plus $229,649.84 development costs) (pp. 1078–1079).
17. Liability holding: Arizona permits rescission for a mutual mistake of material fact constituting "an essential part and condition of the contract"; the water belief was "a basic assumption on which both parties made the contract" (§ 152 cmt b) and the mistake "ha[d] such a material effect on the agreed exchange of performances as to upset the very bases of the contract" (cmt a); the contract was voidable (p. 1079). n.2: inadequate investigation does not bar rescission where the risk was not allocated (p. 1079). The sufficiency challenge failed because no trial record was supplied (n.1).
18. Remedy holding: "absent proof of breach for fraud or misrepresentation a party who rescinds a contract may not recover consequential damages" (here, reliance development expenses) (p. 1079). Restitution measure: rescinding party returns the property and pays fair rental value for the occupancy (§ 384(1)(a)); recovers the down payment plus the amount by which its efforts enhanced the land's value (§§ 376 cmt a, 371(b)) (p. 1080). Awarding the $229,649.84 "would shift the entire risk of mistake onto the petitioners" (p. 1080). Disposition: affirmed in part, reversed in part, remanded (p. 1081).
19. Notes: (1) had the court ruled against mistake, sellers could recover price-based damages but specific performance remains discretionary (McKinnon v. Benedict) (p. 1081). (2) Converse case: if drilling revealed valuable minerals, the sellers could not avoid, § 154 cmt a (p. 1081).

## C. Unilateral mistake (§ 153), p. 1081 introduction only

20. § 153: one party's mistake at formation as to a basic assumption on which he made the contract, with a material effect on the agreed exchange adverse to him, makes the contract voidable by him if he does not bear the risk under § 154 AND either (a) enforcement would be unconscionable, or (b) the other party had reason to know of the mistake or his fault caused it. Cmt b: he must first meet every § 152 requirement. Cmt c: the bidder must show the loss on performance and the profit he would have made without the mistake. § 154 Illus. 6: a bid error from a misestimate of labor (judgment) is allocated to the bidder, unlike a clerical computation error.
21. Casebook: relief is most commonly sought for erroneous bids (Elsinore v. Kastorff, p. 209); Sumerel shows the doctrine applied to formation and to avoidance (p. 1081). Sumerel's holding is not in the assigned excerpt.

## D. Impracticability (§§ 261–264, 266(1), 269, 270; UCC §§ 2-613 to 2-615)

22. Existing versus supervening impracticability (p. 1100 Note 1, continued p. 1101): existing impracticability (facts at formation) may also support a mistake claim; § 266(1) governs it (no duty arises if the party had no reason to know of the fact and its non-existence was a basic assumption, unless language or circumstances indicate the contrary). Mineral Park (gravel below water at prohibitive cost) is the "impracticable" landmark: "a thing is impracticable when it can only be done at an excessive and unreasonable cost" (p. 1100, quoted p. 1109).
23. Note 2 (p. 1101): efficiency framing (compelled performance costing more than its value is inefficient) versus fairness. Note 3 (p. 1101): courts look to agreement or context to find the risk allocated; if unallocated, the court makes its own allocation.
24. Paradine v. Jane (1647) (p. 1101): duty "absolute"; reasons: the party "might have provided against it," and one who takes "casual profits" must bear "casual losses." Taylor is "an early departure" (p. 1102).
25. § 261 elements: (1) after the contract is made, (2) a party's performance is made impracticable, (3) without his fault, (4) by the occurrence of an event the non-occurrence of which was a basic assumption on which the contract was made; then his duty is discharged (5) unless the language or circumstances indicate the contrary. Cmt a: applies only to discharge a duty; does not affect a breach already arisen; yields to agreements assuming greater or lesser obligations. Cmt b: market shifts and financial inability are ordinarily not basic-assumption events (Illus. 2 bank failure, Illus. 4 receivership); foreseeability, even actual foresight, does not necessarily defeat basic assumption. Cmt c: an implied assumption of the greater obligation may be found from the circumstances (ability to draft a clause, standardized forms, trade practice of insuring, middleman versus producer); Illus. 5 experienced salvor who drafted the contract may be held to an absolute duty. Cmt d: "fault" includes breach and negligence, not only willful wrongs; event caused by the obligee is that party's breach; impracticable means "extreme and unreasonable difficulty, expense, injury, or loss"; "'impracticability' means more than 'impracticality'"; ordinary cost increases are the risk a fixed-price contract covers; a party must use reasonable efforts to surmount obstacles (Illus. 11, no variance sought). Disproportionate risk of injury to persons or property can suffice (Illus. 7, 8). Cmt e: "I cannot do it" ordinarily does not discharge; a party ordinarily bears the risk that a third party it depends on fails (Illus. 12 milk from own herd; Illus. 13 molasses "of the usual run from the C sugar refinery"). Cmt f: alternative performances: no discharge while one remains practicable. Illus. 9 and 10 restate Transatlantic: no discharge when the canal closed after departure but a Cape route remained; discharge if the ship was in the canal when it closed.
26. §§ 262–264 supply the basic-assumption conclusion for death or incapacity of a necessary person, destruction or failure of a specific thing necessary for performance, and governmental regulation or order. § 269: temporary impracticability suspends rather than discharges unless later performance would be materially more burdensome (p. 1131–1132, Autry). § 270: partial impracticability.
27. Taylor v. Caldwell (K.B. 1863), pp. 1102–1105. Facts: May 27, 1861 agreement to give plaintiffs the use of the Surrey Gardens and Music Hall on four days (June 17, July 15, Aug. 5, Aug. 19) for concerts and fêtes at £100 a day; defendants to supply a band and other amusements; plaintiffs to take entrance money. The Hall burned on June 11, 1861 without the fault of either party; plaintiffs sued for their advertising and preparation expenses. The agreement was not a lease. Holding (Blackburn, J.): the absolute-contract rule applies only to positive contracts "not subject to any condition either express or implied"; where the parties must have known the contract could not be fulfilled unless a particular thing continued to exist, "in the absence of any express or implied warranty that the thing shall exist," the contract is subject to an implied condition that the parties are excused if performance becomes impossible "from the perishing of the thing without default of the contractor" (p. 1103). Both parties are excused: plaintiffs from paying, defendants from furnishing the Hall; verdict for defendants (p. 1105). Sources: Roman law (Stichus), Pothier, Williams v. Lloyd (bailee's horse died), personal-performance and bailment cases.
28. Taylor notes: (1) "Tiger days excepted" (Scott & Sons v. Del Sel, 1922) ridicules the implied-term fiction; Restatement Ch. 11 Introductory Note: an extraordinary circumstance may alter "the essential nature of that performance," and the court decides "whether justice requires a departure from the general rule that the obligor bear the risk" (pp. 1105–1106). (2) Death or illness excuses a personal-service duty (Buccini, Oneal, Wasserman/Walter Huston, apprehension of illness) (p. 1106). (3) Undertaking a specific result versus reasonable efforts (Mound View v. Walijarvi; Tamarac; UNIDROIT 5.1.4–5.1.5) (pp. 1106–1107).
29. UCC Article 2 box (p. 1107): § 2-613: where the contract requires goods identified when the contract is made and they suffer casualty without fault of either party before risk of loss passes, total loss avoids the contract; partial loss lets the buyer avoid or accept with allowance. It applies only where the destroyed goods are the only goods that would satisfy the contract; analogous to literal impossibility. § 2-615(a): seller's delay or non-delivery is not a breach if performance as agreed has been made impracticable by a contingency whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption, or by good-faith compliance with a governmental regulation, "Except so far as a seller may have assumed a greater obligation," subject to § 2-614; seller must allocate partial capacity fairly (b) and notify seasonably (c). Cmt 4: increased cost alone does not excuse unless due to an unforeseen contingency altering the essential nature of performance; market rise or collapse is not enough. Cmt 5: failure of an exclusive or contemplated source by casualty is within § 2-615, but only if the seller "employed all due measures to assure himself that his source will not fail" (citing Canadian Industrial Alcohol); the excused seller must turn over rights against the source. Cmt 8: exemptions do not apply when the contingency was "sufficiently foreshadowed" to be among the dickered business risks. § 2-614(1): if the agreed carrier, facilities or manner of delivery becomes commercially impracticable without fault and a commercially reasonable substitute is available, the substitute "must be tendered and accepted."
30. Transatlantic Financing Corp. v. United States (D.C. Cir. 1966), pp. 1107–1113. Facts: voyage charter executed Oct. 2, 1956 (after Egypt's July 26 nationalization of the canal, during the crisis) for a full cargo of wheat from a Gulf port to Iran; the charter named termini, not route. The CHRISTOS sailed Oct. 27 on a Suez course; Israel invaded Oct. 29; the canal was blocked Nov. 2. Transatlantic sought instructions and extra pay from an unauthorized USDA employee, sailed around the Cape, arrived Dec. 30, and sued for $43,972 above the $305,842.92 contract price for a voyage lengthened about 3,000 miles beyond roughly 10,000. Held: dismissal affirmed.
31. Test (Wright, J.): (1) a contingency, "something unexpected," must have occurred; (2) the risk of the unexpected occurrence must not have been allocated "either by agreement or by custom"; (3) occurrence of the contingency must have rendered performance "commercially impracticable" (p. 1109). Step 1 met: absent a stated route the parties expected the usual and customary route, Suez. Step 2: no express or implied condition on Suez, no custom; the Cape was generally regarded as an alternative; the circumstances arguably allocated the risk to Transatlantic, but the court does "not deem the risk of closure so allocated": "Foreseeability or even recognition of a risk does not necessarily prove its allocation" (p. 1111). Awareness of the crisis showed "a willingness by Transatlantic to assume abnormal risks," which justifies judging impracticability "in stricter terms" (p. 1111). Step 3 not met: cargo not harmed by the southern route, vessel and crew fit, Transatlantic no less able than the United States to insure and better placed to do so; added expense alone; "there must be more of a variation between expected cost and the cost of performing by an available alternative than is present in this case" (p. 1112). Footnote 1: impracticability assumes performance was physically possible; footnote 13: an objective test.
32. Relief dictum: if the contract had been impossible it would be "a nullity," and the proper theory would be quantum meruit for the entire trip, not the extra cost while keeping the contract price; "There is no interest in casting the entire burden of commercial disaster on one party in order to preserve the other's profit" (pp. 1112–1113).
33. Notes (pp. 1113–1114): (1) a cost spike alone is unlikely to be a contingency; identify the event (environmental regulation, construction boom, forest fires) and ask which risk was allocated to the seller. (2) Wegematic (2d Cir. 1966, Friendly, J.): a seller of a "revolutionary" computer bore the risk that the breakthrough failed; "the appropriate exculpatory language is well known"; § 2-615's opening exception recognizes assumption of greater obligation; was mistake a better defense? (3) The Eugenia: no excuse for cost increase unless "positively unjust to hold the parties bound"; back-to-back carrier and seller.
34. Foreseeability (pp. 1114–1115): Restatement Ch. 11 Introductory Note (parties may not have thought the risk important enough to bargain over); UCC § 2-615 cmt 8 "foreshadowed"; unforeseeability is evidence that non-occurrence was a basic assumption. Opera Co. of Boston v. Wolf Trap (4th Cir. 1987): foreseeability "is at best but one fact to be considered"; trial court's rule that foreseeability absolutely barred the defense was reversed. Belk v. Le Chaperon Rouge (N.D. Ohio 2020): a settlement signed March 12, 2020, the day schools closed, was not excused because the COVID-19 financial difficulties could reasonably have been foreseen.
35. Risk-bearing analyses (pp. 1116–1117): Posner and Rosenfield's superior risk bearer (better able to estimate loss and probability, to insure or self-insure by diversification, to avert or mitigate); Elofson's rebuttal; Winstar's citation; Schwartz on inflation risk.
36. Canadian Industrial Alcohol (caption on p. 1120; opinion p. 1121, context): a seller of molasses "of the usual run" from a named refinery that never contracted with the refinery is not excused; § 2-615 cmt 5; § 261 Illus. 13.
37. Temporary impracticability and Westinghouse (pp. 1131–1132): Gene Autry's wartime military service suspended rather than breached his acting contract; when may a party terminate because of the other's short-term disability? Westinghouse's uranium contracts: the trial judge found Westinghouse "not wholly excused"; claims settled.

## E. Force majeure clauses (pp. 1117–1120)

38. Definition: a term, introduced by a party anticipating events it cannot readily prevent, that excuses performance if the impediment arises; "force majeure clause" is the generic name (p. 1117). Drafting risk: ejusdem generis narrows general words to the class of the listed items (p. 1117).
39. OWBR v. Clear Channel (D. Haw. 2003): a clause naming terrorism did not cover cancellation caused by the post-9/11 economic downturn and sponsor withdrawal; the court used § 261 to interpret the clause and found an unexpected drop in market prices not covered; a convention held right after 9/11, when travel was disrupted, would have had a stronger argument; summary judgment for the hotel (pp. 1117–1118).
40. Kama Rippa Music v. Schekeryk (2d Cir. 1975): a list of a dozen events ending "for any similar or dissimilar reason"; the trial court disregarded the catch-all, which the appellate court said was supported by New York law, and alternatively could have read it ejusdem generis (p. 1118).
41. ICC Force Majeure Clause (2020) (p. 1119, partly lost in OCR): the invoking party must prove it could not reasonably have avoided the event's effects, but under the passage shown need not prove the event was beyond its reasonable control or unforeseeable.
42. COVID leases (p. 1119): 1600 Walnut v. Cole Haan (E.D. Pa. 2021): the force majeure clause governed the closures, and its proviso that it shall not "relieve Tenant from the obligation to pay Rent" (a "hell or high water clause") allocated pandemic risk to the tenant; BP Prucenter v. Saks (Mass. Land Ct. 2022): a similar clause without that proviso excused rent after mandated closure. So a clause can both expand and contract the common-law excuse; a specific clause displaces the default doctrine.
43. Notes (pp. 1119–1120): (1) UCC § 2-207 cmt 5 treats a clause modestly enlarging the seller's § 2-615 exemption as non-material; § 2-615 cmt 8: § 2-615 "provides a minimum beyond which agreement may not go" (minimum of obligation or of exemption?). (2) Drafting "strike" to cover lockouts or labor disputes. (3) Brighten Sheet Metal problem: clause excused failure from "labor unions, fires, floods, earthquakes, or acts of God, or other conditions or contingencies beyond its control"; the contractor's only qualified sheet-metal worker was hospitalized indefinitely; it backed out and the owner hired another firm at a higher price; "What result?" (the casebook gives no answer). The problem tests whether a catch-all is read ejusdem generis with the listed events and whether the loss of one employee is a contingency "beyond its control" or the ordinary "I cannot do it" risk (§ 261 cmt e).

## F. Frustration of purpose (§§ 265, 266(2))

44. § 265 elements: (1) after the contract is made, (2) a party's principal purpose is (3) substantially frustrated (4) without his fault (5) by an event the non-occurrence of which was a basic assumption; then his remaining duties are discharged (6) unless language or circumstances indicate the contrary. Cmt a: frustration differs from impracticability because "there is no impediment to performance by either party"; the purpose must be so completely the basis of the contract that, "as both parties understand, without it the transaction would make little sense"; substantial frustration means more than reduced profit or a loss; foreseeability is a factor but does not compel the conclusion. Illus. 1 (parade window), 2 (yacht-race program: discharged, printer's claim under § 272(1)), 3 (hotel burns), 4 (sign lighting prohibited: discharged), 5 (export ban, other reasonable disposition available: not discharged), 6 (traffic change, station still operable at a loss: not discharged), 7 (failure to apply for exemption: not discharged). Cmt b: fault and contrary-indication limits; existing frustration goes to § 266(2).
45. Krell v. Henry (C.A. 1903), pp. 1132–1134. Facts: by letters of June 20, 1902 the defendant agreed to take Krell's flat at 56A Pall Mall for the daytime of June 26 and 27 for £75 (£25 paid, £50 due June 24). The letters did not mention the coronation, but an announcement on the premises advertised windows to view the coronation procession, and the housekeeper offered the rooms for that purpose for the days only. The King's illness postponed the processions; the defendant declined to pay the balance. Holding (Vaughan Williams, L.J.): Taylor's principle is not limited to destruction of the subject matter or of a state of things expressly specified; the court ascertains, from necessary inferences from surrounding circumstances recognized by both parties, the substance of the contract and whether it "needs for its foundation the assumption of the existence of a particular state of things"; the use was licensed "for a particular purpose and none other"; the processions on the proclaimed days along the proclaimed route were regarded by both parties as the foundation; the defendant was not liable for the balance. Appeal dismissed (pp. 1133–1134). The excerpt does not report the deposit counterclaim.
46. Derby Day dictum: a cab hired at an enhanced price to go to Epsom on Derby Day: the race is not the foundation because "the cab had no special qualification for this particular occasion. Any other cab would have done as well" (p. 1134). The distinguishing facts are the rooms' unique suitability, the vendor's own advertising of the purpose, and the limited daytime license.
47. Krell notes: (1) Lloyd v. Murphy (car dealer's lease; wartime restrictions on new-car sales; frustration "is not a form of impossibility ... Performance remains possible but the expected value of performance to the party seeking to be excused has been destroyed by a fortuitous event"); SVAP III v. Fitness Int'l (COVID gym closure analysed in part as frustration); Kel Kim (insurance-market crisis); "The landlord prevailed in each case" (p. 1135). (2) Efficiency: frustration changes the value of performance to the recipient rather than cost; buyer's factory burns before machine delivered (p. 1135). (3) Was the coronation's postponement so inconceivable? (p. 1135, text truncated).
48. Problems (p. 1136): (1) Homecoming: alumni booked lodging in Touchdown at more than double the usual rates for Gridiron U.'s homecoming, which was cancelled after a tornado; recovery of deposits; support in Krell, distinguish the Derby Day cab, dispute the dictum? The problem turns on whether the hotel-keeper shared the homecoming purpose as the foundation (doubled rates, small town, purpose known) or whether, like the cab, the rooms had no special qualification and could serve any visitor. (2) Wedding dress: P promises for $500 to provide [a dress] by June 1 for D's wedding to X a week later; X dies when the dress is all but ready; is D excused? The problem tests whether the seller shared the purpose as a basic assumption and, if D is discharged, P's restitution or reliance claim under § 272 and § 377 for work done.

## G. Relief after excuse (§§ 272, 377; Renner; Taylor; Transatlantic)

49. § 272(1): either party may claim relief including restitution under §§ 240 and 377. § 377: a party whose duty is discharged by impracticability or frustration is entitled to restitution for any benefit conferred by part performance or reliance. § 272(2): if those rules and Chapter 16 will not avoid injustice, the court may grant relief "on such terms as justice requires including protection of the parties' reliance interests." § 265 Illus. 2: the printer whose customer is discharged "may have a claim under the rule stated in § 272(1)."
50. Applications: Taylor's plaintiffs recovered nothing for advertising and preparation; both sides were excused (p. 1105). Renner: restitution of down payment plus enhancement minus rental value; no reliance-expense recovery absent fraud or misrepresentation (pp. 1079–1080), a holding about rescission for mistake under Arizona law that contrasts with § 158(2)'s discretionary reliance protection. Transatlantic: quantum meruit for the whole performance is the remedy if the contract becomes a nullity, not cost overrun on top of price (p. 1112).

## H. Distinctions the problems and notes test

51. Mistake versus impracticability versus frustration: timing (existing fact versus later event; § 151 cmt a; § 266), and cost versus value (pp. 1073–1074; § 152 cmt c sends no-advantage hardship to Chapter 11).
52. Design versus performance specification (Stees Note 2) determines whether the owner warranted the plans.
53. Specific identified goods or specific source versus fungible or general supply (§ 2-613 vs § 2-615; § 261 Illus. 12–13; Canadian Industrial Alcohol).
54. Foreseen versus allocated (Transatlantic; § 261 cmt c; Wolf Trap; Belk; § 2-615 cmt 8).
55. Unique foundation versus ordinary purpose (Krell vs Derby Day cab; homecoming problem; § 265 Illus. 5–6).
56. Force majeure clause wording controls and can displace the default rule in either direction (Cole Haan vs Saks; OWBR; Kama Rippa; Brighten).
57. Suspension versus discharge (§ 269; Autry).
