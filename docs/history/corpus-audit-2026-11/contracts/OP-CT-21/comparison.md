## (c) Teaching gaps

1. **Unilateral mistake (§ 153).** No map entry, chart or brief teaches it; only the rules entry and the incomplete lecture sentence do. Home: `mistake` node notes, and K-29 node b (see E1). Draft: "If only one party was mistaken, that party may avoid only by meeting every § 152 element, not bearing the risk under § 154, and showing either that enforcement would be unconscionable or that the other party had reason to know of the mistake or caused it by fault (§ 153). Clerical errors in construction bids are the usual case (Elsinore, C&M 1081); a bidder's misjudgment of the labor required is a risk the court allocates to the bidder (§ 154 Illus. 6)."
2. **The three § 154 routes, and court allocation examples.** Home: K-29 node d detail and `risk-allocation` node notes. Draft: "A party bears the risk of a mistake when the agreement allocates it, when he knew at formation that his knowledge was limited and treated it as sufficient (conscious ignorance), or when the court allocates it as reasonable (§ 154). Courts allocate to a seller of farm land the risk that it contains minerals (Renner Note 2, C&M 1081) and to a builder the risk of subsoil needing drainage (§ 154 Illus. 5; Stees). Where the owner supplied a design specification, the owner impliedly warrants it (Spearin, Stees Note 2, C&M 1077)."
3. **Existing impracticability and frustration (§ 266) and its overlap with mistake.** Home: `impracticability` node notes. Draft: "When the impediment existed at formation, § 266 provides that no duty arises if the party had no reason to know of the fact and its non-existence was a basic assumption; Mineral Park's gravel below water is the casebook example, and the editors ask what difficulties a mistake claim would have faced (C&M 1100–1101)."
4. **§ 261 elements and limits.** Home: K-29 node e detail. Draft: "Discharge requires a later event, not caused by the party's fault, whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption, making performance impracticable, with no contrary language or circumstances (§ 261). Market shifts and a party's financial inability are ordinarily not such events (§ 261 cmt b, Illus. 2, 4), 'impracticability' means more than 'impracticality,' and the party must use reasonable efforts to overcome the obstacle (cmt d)."
5. **UCC §§ 2-613, 2-614, 2-615 distinctions.** Home: `impracticability` node notes. Draft: "In a sale of goods, § 2-613 avoids the contract only when goods identified at formation are totally destroyed without fault before risk of loss passes; § 2-614 requires a commercially reasonable substitute manner of delivery to be tendered and accepted; and § 2-615 excuses a seller for a contingency whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption, but the seller must allocate partial supply fairly and notify the buyer seasonably. A rise in cost alone is not enough (cmt 4), and a failed source excuses only a seller who took all due measures to secure it (cmt 5; Canadian Industrial Alcohol)."
6. **The foreseeability debate and assumption of greater obligation.** Home: Transatlantic map `learning.topics.foreseeability` and `risk-allocation` node. Draft: "Foreseeability is 'at best but one fact to be considered' (Wolf Trap, C&M 1115): an event foreshadowed at contracting may be among the dickered risks (UCC § 2-615 cmt 8; Belk, where a settlement signed the day schools closed for COVID-19 was not excused), while an unforeseeable event supports a finding that its non-occurrence was a basic assumption. A seller who promotes a 'revolutionary' product assumes the risk that it cannot be built (Wegematic, C&M 1113)."
7. **Force majeure clause cases.** Home: `force-majeure` node notes. Draft: "Read the clause's listed events and catch-all: a clause naming terrorism did not cover a cancellation caused by the economic downturn after 9/11 (OWBR, C&M 1117–1118), and general words may be confined to the class of listed events (ejusdem generis; Kama Rippa, C&M 1118). A clause can also narrow the default excuse: a lease clause stating that it shall not relieve the tenant of rent allocated COVID-19 closure risk to the tenant (1600 Walnut v. Cole Haan), while a similar clause without that proviso excused rent (BP Prucenter v. Saks) (C&M 1119)."
8. **Relief after discharge (§§ 272, 377).** Home: K-29 node g detail. Draft: "After discharge for impracticability or frustration, either party may recover restitution for benefits conferred by part performance or reliance (§§ 272(1), 377), and the court may protect reliance where that will not otherwise avoid injustice (§ 272(2)). Taylor's promoters recovered nothing for advertising; Transatlantic says a discharged carrier's claim would be quantum meruit for the whole voyage, not the extra cost (C&M 1105, 1112)."

Draft: "Material effect means an imbalance so severe that the party cannot fairly be required to perform, ordinarily because the exchange is both less desirable to him and more advantageous to the other party; hardship with no advantage to the other side is left to impracticability and frustration (§ 152 cmt c)."

Draft: "A temporary obstacle suspends the duty while it lasts and discharges it only if later performance would be materially more burdensome (§ 269); Gene Autry's wartime service suspended his studio contract (C&M 1131–1132)."

Draft: "Frustration 'is not a form of impossibility': performance remains possible, but its expected value to the party seeking excuse has been destroyed by a fortuitous event (Lloyd v.
