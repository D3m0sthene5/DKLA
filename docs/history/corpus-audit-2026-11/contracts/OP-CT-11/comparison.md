## (c) Teaching gaps worth adding (ranked by exam importance)

1. **§ 89(a) content beyond the black letter.** Surface: R&A restatement-89 (add comment lines) and LN "Modern doctrine." Draft: "Under § 89(a), 'fair and equitable' requires more than the absence of coercion: there must be an objectively demonstrable reason for seeking the modification, arising from circumstances not anticipated when the contract was made. An event foreseen only as a remote possibility can qualify if the contract did not adequately cover it. Once such a reason exists, the parties' relative financial strength, the formality of the modification, and how far it has been performed or relied on bear on whether there was imposition or unfair surprise. The Restatement rejects the 'rescission' theory as fictitious when rescission and new agreement are simultaneous. Illustration 1 (the Watkins facts) is binding; Illustration 5 (a supplier who raises the price from 50 to 75 cents and a buyer who 'is forced to agree' because substitutes would take months) is not." Pinpoint § 89 cmt b, illus. 1, 5.

2. **§ 2-209 comment 2 detail.** Surface: R&A ucc-2-209 (add comment lines). Draft: "A modification under § 2-209(1) needs no consideration but must meet the Code's good-faith test. Extorting a 'modification' without legitimate commercial reason is ineffective, and a merely technical consideration cannot save a bad-faith modification. Between merchants, good faith includes observing reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing in the trade and may require an objectively demonstrable reason for seeking a modification; a market shift that makes performance unprofitable can be such a reason even if it would not excuse performance under §§ 2-615 and 2-616." Pinpoint § 2-209 cmt 2; § 2-103(1)(b); C&M 473–474 Note 3.

3. **N.Y. GOL § 5-1103 entry and contrast with § 2-209.** Surface: new R&A entry for § 5-1103 (node `ny51103`). Draft: "Section 5-1103 makes an agreement to modify or discharge any contract, obligation, lease, mortgage or security interest valid without consideration, but only if it is in writing and signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought, or by that party's agent. Section 2-209(1) also dispenses with consideration but is limited to contracts for the sale of goods, requires no writing of its own force (subsections (2) and (3) can impose one), and is policed by good faith. Neither statute removes the duress defense." Pinpoint Supp. p. 9; C&M 473 Note 2, 473–474 Note 3; § 2-209(1)–(3).

4. **RLCC § 3 entry.** Surface: new R&A entry (the LN topic lists node "gap-rlcc"). Draft: the black letter of § 3(a)–(c), plus "Comment 7: § 3(c) targets opportunistic modifications imposed on consumers who are locked in, and generally bars modifying fully performed or short-term contracts unless circumstances arose that the parties did not anticipate. Illustration 14: raising a fixed $100 snow-plowing price to $150 mid-contract fails both because termination would cost the consumer value and because it undermines the promised price." Pinpoint RLCC § 3, cmts 1, 6, 7, illus. 14.

5. **Duress inducement and degree.** Surface: Austin brief Reasoning or a duress node. Draft: "Inducement is subjective: a threat induces assent if it substantially contributes to the decision, judged in light of the victim's age, background and relationship to the threatener. Courts' talk of a threat that 'overcomes the will' is often metaphorical; as Holmes put it, choosing the lesser of two evils does not exclude duress, and 'the question is one of degree.' A 'feeble threat' to litigate is not duress (Denbow v. Tesch). Economic duress between businesses is also called 'business compulsion.'" Pinpoint § 175 cmt c; C&M 481–482, 487.

6. **Hawaiian Housing facts.** Surface: LN "Problem: Hawaiian Housing." Draft: "The subcontractor's $49,000 bid rested on its own error (galvanized metal instead of zinc alloy), and it waited about a month after discovering the error before telling Richards. After two months of hard bargaining, in which Richards insisted the bid bound the subcontractor, Richards signed at $62,000, paid about $50,000, never intended to pay more, and then invoked the pre-existing duty rule and Alaska Packers. Compare § 89 illus. 2 (a bid error corrected at cost, after the buyer checked other suppliers, binds)." Pinpoint C&M 483; slide 16; § 89 illus. 2.

7. **Waiver and no-oral-modification mechanics.** Surface: LN "Ways to forestall modification" or the waiver node. Draft: "Under § 2-209(4), a modification that fails a no-oral-modification clause or the statute of frauds can still operate as a waiver, and comment 4 says the clause cannot limit the legal effect of the parties' later conduct. Under § 2-209(5), a waiver of an executory term can be retracted by reasonable notice that strict performance will be required, unless retraction would be unjust in view of a material change of position in reliance. Restatement § 89 comment d applies the same retraction rule to reliance-based modifications." Pinpoint § 2-209(4)–(5), cmts 3–4; § 89 cmt d; slides 11–12.

8. **New consideration as an escape.** Surface: LN "The pre-existing duty rule." Draft: "Any new consideration, however small, takes a modification outside the pre-existing duty rule: early payment, payment at a different place, or payment in property (Pinnel's 'horse, hawk, or robe'). Restatement § 73 accepts a performance similar to the one owed if it differs 'in a way which reflects more than a pretense of a bargain.' Foakes v. Beer, much criticized, held that paying a judgment's principal did not support the creditor's promise to forgo interest." Pinpoint C&M 483–484.

9. **Economic rationale from the notes.** Surface: LN "Holdup and the economics of modification." Draft: "Judge Posner described the Alaska Packers fishermen as holding a temporary monopoly on labor and called the exploitation of temporary monopolies 'the functional meaning of the legal concept of economic duress.' In Selmer he asked whether the statement that induced the promise is 'the kind of offer to deal that we want to discourage,' and said that refusing to enforce holdup modifications assures parties that 'signing a contract is not stepping into a trap.'" Pinpoint C&M 473 Note 1, 482–483.

10. **Other pre-existing duties.** Surface: modification node or LN. Draft: "The rule also applies to duties imposed by law: a spouse's statutory duty of support was no consideration for a promise of property (Borelli v. Brusseau), and a promise to testify truthfully, already required by the broken cooperation agreement and owed by every citizen, could not support a modified plea deal (Schneir v. State)." Pinpoint C&M 474 Note 4, 487.

11. **Schwartzreich and the reason for § 5-1103.** Surface: ny51103 node or Watkins CC episode-6. Draft: "New York's Law Revision Commission cited Schwartzreich as a source of commercial uncertainty, because juries could not predictably tell a rescission-and-new-contract from a modification, and that study led to the signed-writing statute." Pinpoint C&M 476 Note 1.

12. **Austin note questions.** Surface: Austin map Reasoning. Draft: "The casebook asks whether a buyer that agreed to a price increase without protest, while secretly not meaning to pay, would itself act in bad faith (Crane Co. v. Progressive Enterprises), and poses Totem Marine: a settlement extracted from a near-bankrupt carrier, to be distinguished from Austin." Pinpoint C&M 492.

Lower priority: Alabama's rejection of the rule (Winegardner) and CISG art. 29 (C&M 473); Hillman's proposal (C&M 474); the slide-3 checklist; § 2-209 cmt 3 (unilateral termination is not "modification").
