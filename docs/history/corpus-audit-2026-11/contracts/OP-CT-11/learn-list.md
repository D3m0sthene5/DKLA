# OP-CT-11 learn-list: Contracts Class 11, "Contract modifications"

Sources: C&M (10th ed.) printed pp. 470–484 and 487–492 (assigned); pp. 467–469, 485–486, 493–497 read as context only. Supplement p. 9 (N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 5-1103). ## A. Framework (slides)

A1. The professor frames every modification problem with the course checklist: is there an enforceable promise (mutual assent, consideration, definiteness, defenses including duress), has it been performed, was performance excused, how should it be enforced. Slide 3.

A2. Slide 2 lists six topics: the pre-existing duty rule; its revision; economic duress and good faith applied to modifications; the RLCC approach; economic implications of enforcing modifications; ways to forestall modification. Slide 2.

## B. The pre-existing duty rule

B1. Rule. "Performance of a legal duty owed to a promisor which is neither doubtful nor the subject of honest dispute is not consideration." Restatement § 73, quoted C&M 470; slide 5. Consequence: a promise to pay more for exactly the performance already owed lacks consideration and is unenforceable. Conditions: the duty must be owed to the promisor, and must be neither doubtful nor honestly disputed.

B2. Criticism. Patterson called the rule the adjunct of consideration "which has done most to give it a bad name." C&M 470. § 89 cmt b: the rule's "modern justification" is in promises "made by mistake or induced by unfair pressure"; its application where those elements are absent "has been much criticized." Traditional rationale (Williston): prevents "overreaching and blackmail"; Fried: modern trend toward enforcing promises "so long as the sincerity of the obligation is clear and the commitment freely made." C&M 477 (Note 4). Corbin: courts should "separate the sheep from the goats" (enforce for the honest contractor, refuse for the extortionate one). C&M 477.

B3. Caveats the professor stresses (slide 5): (i) mutual promises to rescind are supported by consideration, so the rule does not stop parties from rescinding and replacing a contract; (ii) a modification supported by even minimal consideration is enforceable.

B4. Rejection or reform elsewhere. Alabama rejects the rule by decision: "an executory contract may be modified by the parties without any new consideration other than mutual consent." Winegardner v. Burns (Ala. 1978), C&M 473 Note 2. New York suspends it by statute (§ 5-1103, below). UCC § 2-209(1), CISG art. 29 and UNIDROIT Principles art. 3.2 permit modification without consideration. C&M 473.

B5. Pre-existing statutory/marital duty. Borelli v. Brusseau (Cal. Ct. App. 1993): wife's promise to nurse husband at home was no consideration for his promise of property because spousal support was already a duty under Cal. Civ. Code §§ 5100, 5132; dismissal affirmed; court also invoked policy against "deathbed negotiating." Dissent: strangers could have so contracted; "public policy should not be equated with coerced altruism." C&M 474 Note 4.

B6. Duty owed to the state / "or otherwise." Schneir v. State (Fla. App. 2010): a promise to testify truthfully, already required by the broken cooperation agreement "as well as by the obligation imposed on any citizen," is not consideration for a modification; "a promise to perform what one is already required to do by an existing contract or otherwise is not valid consideration." C&M 487 (facts begin on unassigned p. 486).

B7. Reliance question left open: if the rule relieves the promisor, can the promisee enforce on reliance? C&M 474 Note 5. (Answered in part by § 89(c); see D3.)

B8. Problem "Work Reduction" (Ever-Tite variation): roofer persuades homeowners to initial "gutter installation eliminated"; are they bound, and does it matter whether Article 2 applies? C&M 475. Unresolved in sources; teaches that a reduction of the promisor's duty without new consideration raises the same issue, and that Article 2's application changes the answer (§ 2-209(1)).

## C. Alaska Packers' Ass'n v. Domenico (9th Cir. 1902), C&M 470–473

C1. Facts the court treated as decisive: workers signed to sail to Pyramid Harbor, Alaska, as sailors and fishermen for "$50 for the season, and two cents for each red salmon"; on arrival they "stopped work in a body" and demanded $100, threatening to return to San Francisco; substitutes were impossible to get; company had about $150,000 invested in a cannery; season "extremely short"; superintendent signed. C&M 470–471.

C2. Nets. Trial court found the claim that nets were "rotten and unserviceable" not sustained; appellate court would not disturb that finding on conflicting evidence; therefore workers "were not justified in refusing performance." C&M 471. Slides 4, 6, 8 ask what if nets were defective or the workers believed they were (open questions).

C3. Holding: the promise of extra pay was "without consideration" because it rested "solely upon the libelants' agreement to render the exact services, and none other, that they were already under contract to render"; they "willfully and arbitrarily broke that obligation." C&M 471. Judgment for workers reversed. C&M 473.

C4. No waiver: the company knew nothing of the breach until return, and the superintendent told the men he "had no power to alter the original or to make a new contract," so he also had no authority to waive. C&M 471. (Ties to slide 12: limits on agents' authority as a way to forestall modification.)

C5. Reliance/estoppel rejected: per King v. Railway Co., a party "cannot lay the foundation of an estoppel by his own wrong"; the promise "cannot be legally enforced, although the other party has completed his contract in reliance upon it." C&M 472. Lingenfelder: enforcing would "offer a premium upon bad faith"; nudum pactum. C&M 472–473. Slide 4/6/8: "Was it relevant that the promise was relied on?"

C6. Posner (Trompler v. NLRB): workers had a "temporary monopoly"; "[t]he exploitation of temporary monopolies is the functional meaning of the legal concept of economic duress." C&M 473 Note 1. (Casebook spells it "Trampler".)

C7. Slide questions: could the workers have provided consideration; why didn't they make pay conditional on net quality; why did Alaska Packers agree; effect of decision on incentives to threaten to quit, neglect nets, negotiate wages up front; how could the parties have made the modification enforceable. Slides 4, 6, 8.

## D. Avoiding the rule: rescission-and-substitution, Restatement § 89

D1. Schwartzreich v. Bauman-Basch (N.Y. 1921): designer at $90/week got a $115 offer; employer promised $100; parties signed a new contract identical but for pay and tore signatures off the old one; jury verdict at $100 reinstated on appeal. Theory: "an existing contract is terminated by consent of both parties and a new one executed in its place" (a "substituted agreement"); a mere change of compensation and a rescission plus new contract differ "in principle." C&M 475–476. Note 1 questions whether timing of the tearing matters. C&M 476.

D2. Schwartzreich was cited by the N.Y. Law Revision Commission as a source of "commercial uncertainty" (unpredictable jury findings on rescission vs. modification), leading to the N.Y. written-modification statute. C&M 476 Note 1.

D3. Restatement § 89: "A promise modifying a duty under a contract not fully performed on either side is binding (a) if the modification is fair and equitable in view of circumstances not anticipated by the parties when the contract was made; or (b) to the extent provided by statute; or (c) to the extent that justice requires enforcement in view of material change of position in reliance on the promise." Sel. § 89; slide 11; C&M 476–477 Note 3.
- Executory limit: contract "not fully performed on either side." C&M 476 asks why.
- (a) "fair and equitable" "goes beyond absence of coercion and requires an objectively demonstrable reason for seeking a modification" (cmt b). The reason must rest in circumstances not "anticipated" as part of the context; an event "may be unanticipated for this purpose if it was not adequately covered, even though it was foreseen as a remote possibility" (cmt b). Once a reason exists, relative financial strength, formality, extent of performance or reliance and other circumstances may show or negate "imposition or unfair surprise" (cmt b).
- The Restatement rejects the rescission theory as "fictitious when the 'rescission' and new agreement are simultaneous," and because it "might uphold unfair and inequitable modifications." § 89 cmt b.
- Illustrations: 1 (Watkins facts, rock at reasonable unit price nine times original: binding); 2 (laundry chute bid error, $300→$1,000 after B checks other suppliers: binding); 3 (Schwartzreich facts: binding, clause not stated); 4 (steel-strike threat, $60→$70, B pays 1,500 roofs without protest: binding); 5 (castings 50¢→75¢ on metal cost, substitutes only after months' delay, B "forced to agree" to keep plant running: not binding).
- (b) statutes: UCC § 2-209 and N.Y. § 5-1103 (slide 11). Cmt c: under 2-209 an ineffective modification "can operate as a waiver"; extortion without legitimate commercial reason violates good faith; a similar limit "may be applicable" under signed-writing statutes; some states flatly forbid extra pay to government contractors.
- (c) reliance: applies § 90; promise may become binding "in whole or in part"; reliance makes modification binding as to performance by the promisee and return performance; "the original terms can be reinstated for the future by reasonable notification ... unless reinstatement would be unjust in view of a change of position" (cmt d; compare § 2-209(5)); illus. 6–8.
- C&M 476–477 asks whether Schwartzreich's $115 offer is an unanticipated circumstance under (a), or whether (b) or (c) apply. Unresolved in sources.

D4. Waiver (slide 11): "Modifications may be enforceable as waivers, but only until retracted, unless they have been relied upon."

## E. Watkins & Son v. Carrig (N.H. 1941), C&M 477–480

E1. Facts: written contract to excavate a cellar for a stated price; solid rock encountered soon after; meeting; oral agreement to remove rock at a unit price "about nine times greater"; rock was about two-thirds of the space; referee found the oral agreement "superseded" the written one; verdict for plaintiff. C&M 477.

E2. No mutual-mistake defense: no understanding that no rock would be found, no inquiry by plaintiff, no misleading by defendant, no reservation for unexpected conditions; contract required "all material" removed and "to excavate" was unqualified. C&M 477–478. (Plaintiff bore the rock risk under the original contract.)

E3. The court said it did not matter whether the contract was rescinded and replaced or modified: "A modification involves a partial rescission," and a promise unsupported by consideration is "as tenable" either way. C&M 478. It rejected Williston's view that calling it rescission does not avoid consideration, as clothing consideration "with insistence of control beyond its proper demands." C&M 479.

E4. Ground of decision: defendant "intentionally and voluntarily yielded" to the demand; the special-price promise "necessarily imported a release or waiver" of his right to the lower price; "the element of discharge was distinct in precedence of the new promise." C&M 479–480. Decisive facts: the special price was "fair and the defendant received reasonable value"; the promise "was not an assumption of the burden"; defendant wanted no delay; plaintiff proceeded "on the strength of the promise"; defendant "yielded to the threat without protest." C&M 478–480. Policy: "Changes to meet changes in circumstances and conditions should be valid"; "law according to justice." C&M 479–480. Exceptions overruled (plaintiff wins). C&M 480.

E5. Notes: why "insistent request" was not as coercive as Alaska Packers; how good is the changed-circumstances argument given the "Granite State"; did Watkins assume the risk? C&M 480 Note 1. Transactional constraints on opportunism (repeat dealings, reputation, substitution, liquidated-damage clauses, specific relief, damages exposure) (Aivazian, Trebilcock & Penny). C&M 480 Note 2. Slide 10: what if price exorbitant; what if defendant said "I don't want to do this, but if you insist…"; why did he agree; what should he do in future.

## F. Economics of modification (slides 7, 9; C&M 480, 482–483)

F1. Holdup example: cannery has $150,000 invested, perishable fish worth $1M on hand, $10,000 contracted wages; slide asks the maximum the cannery should pay and the minimum the workers should accept. Slide 7. (Inference, not on slide: if workers quit and are judgment-proof the cannery gets $0 from the fish; if they stay at $X it nets $1M − X, so it would pay up to about $1M; the $150,000 is sunk.)

F2. Enforceability: may reduce drafting costs ex ante; presumably makes parties better off at the time of modification; may be needed to protect reliance; but the prospect of a successful holdup gives incentives to "bluff," "shirk" (avoid precautions against breach) and "lowball"; these are "arguably, coercive, unfair and inefficient," and anticipating them "may discourage contracting." Slide 9.

F3. Selmer Co. v. Blakeslee-Midwest (7th Cir. 1983) (Posner): subcontractor's financial difficulties did not present a factual issue of duress; the fundamental issue is whether the statement is "the kind of offer to deal that we want to discourage"; avoiding modifications like Alaska Packers "assures prospective contract parties that signing a contract is not stepping into a trap." C&M 482. Question what Alaska Packers could do in later seasons if bound. C&M 483.

## G. Ways to forestall modification (slide 12; UCC § 2-209(2), (4), (5))

G1. Contractual limits on modification are ineffective: "Those who make a contract may unmake it." Slide 12.

G2. No-oral-modification (NOM) clauses are made effective by statute: UCC § 2-209(2) ("A signed agreement which excludes modification or rescission except by a signed writing cannot be otherwise modified or rescinded"; except between merchants, such a term on a merchant's form "must be separately signed by the other party"); slide 12 adds N.Y. GOL § 15-301 (not reproduced in assigned materials). Cmt 3: (2) and (3) protect "against false allegations of oral modifications"; (2) lets parties "make their own Statute of Frauds."

G3. NOM clauses may be construed narrowly and qualified by waiver and estoppel. Slide 12. § 2-209(4): an attempted modification that fails (2) or (3) "can operate as a waiver"; cmt 4: (4) prevents NOM clauses "from limiting in other respects the legal effect of the parties' actual later conduct." § 2-209(5): a waiver "affecting an executory portion" may be retracted "by reasonable notification received by the other party that strict performance will be required," unless retraction "would be unjust in view of a material change of position in reliance on the waiver."

G4. Statute of frauds applies to the contract as modified if within § 2-201. § 2-209(3). Cmt 3: "modification" for the future cannot be "conjured up by oral testimony" if price is $500 or more.

G5. Other devices: limits on agents' authority; commitments to transfer value to third parties if modified (e.g., most-favored-nation clauses). Slide 12.

## H. Statutes dispensing with consideration

H1. N.Y. GOL § 5-1103: an agreement to change, modify or discharge "any contract, obligation, or lease, or any mortgage or other security interest" "shall not be invalid because of the absence of consideration, provided that" it is "in writing and signed by the party against whom it is sought to enforce the change, modification or discharge, or by his agent." Supp. p. 9; C&M 473 Note 2. Conditions: writing + signature of the party to be charged (or agent). Applies to all contracts, not only goods. C&M 473 asks how Alaska Packers would come out under it (the superintendent "signed an agreement," C&M 470, but had disclaimed authority, C&M 471; unresolved).

H2. UCC § 2-209(1): "An agreement modifying a contract within this Article needs no consideration to be binding." Scope: sale of goods (Article 2). No writing requirement in (1) itself, but (2) and (3). C&M 473 asks how this differs from § 5-1103 (inference from texts: § 5-1103 requires a signed writing and covers all contracts; § 2-209(1) requires neither writing nor consideration but is limited to Article 2 and policed by good faith).

H3. Good-faith limit. § 2-209 cmt 2: modifications "must meet the test of good faith imposed by this Act"; "the extortion of a 'modification' without legitimate commercial reason is ineffective as a violation of the duty of good faith"; "Nor can a mere technical consideration support a modification made in bad faith." Between merchants good faith includes "observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing in the trade" (§ 2-103) and "may in some situations require an objectively demonstrable reason for seeking a modification"; "a market shift which makes performance come to involve a loss may provide such a reason even though there is no such unforeseen difficulty as would make out a legal excuse" under §§ 2-615, 2-616. Cmt 1: the section seeks to make effective "all necessary and desirable modifications ... without regard to the technicalities." C&M 473–474 Note 3; slide 13. § 2-103(1)(b) (selections): merchant good faith = "honesty in fact and the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing in the trade." C&M 474 asks which part an extorted modification violates.

H4. "Modification or rescission" includes abandonment by mutual consent but not unilateral "termination" or "cancellation." § 2-209 cmt 3.

H5. Duress nonetheless: a modification valid under § 2-209(1) or § 5-1103 may still be voidable for duress if induced by a threat to break the contract. C&M 473–474 Note 3. Hillman: § 2-209 dismissed the pre-existing duty rule "too summarily"; would put a party relying on a concession to proof that it was not unfairly coerced. C&M 474.

## I. Duress (C&M 467–469 context; 481–483; 487–492; §§ 175–176)

I1. § 175(1): "If a party's manifestation of assent is induced by an improper threat by the other party that leaves the victim no reasonable alternative, the contract is voidable by the victim." Elements: improper threat; inducement (causation); no reasonable alternative. Remedy: voidable. Sel. § 175; C&M 468.
I2. § 175 cmt c: inducement test is subjective: duress induces assent "if the duress substantially contributes to his decision"; "did the threat actually induce assent"; all circumstances (age, background, relationship) count; "Persons of a weak or cowardly nature are the very ones that need protection"; availability of disinterested advice and the time between threat and assent are relevant evidence. Illus. 8 (easily frightened B: voidable), 9 (separation agreement: voidable). § 175(2): third-party duress; contract voidable unless the other party in good faith and without reason to know gives value or relies materially.
I3. § 176(1): a threat is improper if (a) what is threatened is a crime or tort, or the threat itself would be one if it obtained property; (b) criminal prosecution; (c) use of civil process in bad faith; (d) "the threat is a breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing under a contract with the recipient." § 176(2): improper "if the resulting exchange is not on fair terms, and" (a) the threatened act would harm the recipient and not significantly benefit the threatener, (b) effectiveness is significantly increased by prior unfair dealing, or (c) it is otherwise "a use of power for illegitimate ends." The selections print no comments to § 176.
I4. Slide 13 elements: improper threat (history: confinement or bodily harm → "duress of goods" → economic harm "including breach of contract"; includes unjust, inequitable or unlawful behavior; can include threat to breach good faith and fair dealing); "absence of reasonable alternative/deprivation of free will"; "unfair terms [less relevant if threat is sufficiently improper]." A threat to breach "without legitimate commercial reason" can be characterized as bad faith (§ 2-209 cmt 2). Compare § 176(1) (no fairness requirement) vs. (2) (fairness required).
I5. History and limits (context pp. 467–468, not assigned but bear on I4): threats of lawful action generally not wrongful (Chouinard), but "an unjust and inequitable threat is wrongful" though not independently actionable (McCubbin v. Buss). C&M 468.
I6. Yielding to threat (C&M 481–482): Denbow v. Tesch: brother's threat to "law and law it" a "feeble threat" insufficient. "Ordinary firmness" standard (Carrier); stricter "free will"/"volition" formulas (Raymundo, Konsuvo) are "often somewhat metaphorical" and appear "largely in cases where relief was granted ... where no such total mastery ... existed." Holmes (Union Pacific): choosing the lesser of two evils "does not exclude duress"; but an honorable agreement may be made with one facing a "repugnant alternative" (Tidwell); "The question is one of degree" (Hellenic Lines). Note 1: trend from "brave man" to "ordinary firmness" to subjective test (Rubenstein: unlawful threats that in fact overcome the will; age, sex, capacity, relationship considered; may be proved by party's own testimony). Note 2: In re Baby Boy L. (maternal pressure not duress; "emotionally difficult choice"). C&M 481–482. Watkins homeowner "seemed to cave quickly" (C&M 481).
I7. "Business compulsion" is the name for economic duress between businesses. C&M 487.

## J. Austin Instrument v. Loral Corp. (N.Y. 1971), C&M 487–492

J1. Facts: July 1965 Loral won a $6,000,000 Navy radar contract with a delivery schedule, liquidated-damages clause for late delivery and cancellation-on-default clause; Austin got a subcontract for 23 of about 40 gear components. May 1966 second Navy contract; on July 15 Loral said Austin would get only items on which it was low bidder; Austin refused less than all 40 and the next day said it would cease deliveries under the existing subcontract unless Loral agreed to substantial price increases, retroactive and prospective, and gave it all 40 parts; Austin stopped delivery. Loral contacted 10 manufacturers (its entire "approved vendors" list), none could deliver in time (best: commence sometime in October); July 22 letter: "we are left with no choice or alternative but to meet your conditions." Loral got parts in August, met Navy commitments; after Austin's last delivery under the second subcontract (July 1967) Loral notified it would seek recovery. Austin sued for more than $17,750 due on the second subcontract; Loral sued for about $22,250 (price increases under the first subcontract). C&M 487–488. Loral made no claim on the second subcontract (n.2).
J2. Procedural posture: trial court awarded Austin its sum and dismissed Loral's claim for failure to show it could not get the items elsewhere in time; closely divided Appellate Division affirmed. Court of Appeals (Fuld, C.J.) treated application of law to undisputed facts as a question of law (n.3). C&M 488–489.
J3. Rule: "A contract is voidable on the ground of duress when it is established that the party making the claim was forced to agree to it by means of a wrongful threat precluding the exercise of his free will." Economic duress is shown by proof that "immediate possession of needful goods is threatened," or that one party "threatened to breach the agreement by withholding goods unless the other party agrees to some further demand." But "a mere threat ... to breach the contract by not delivering the required items, though wrongful, does not in itself constitute economic duress. It must also appear that the threatened party could not obtain the goods from another source of supply and that the ordinary remedy of an action for breach of contract would not be adequate." C&M 489.
J4. Decisive facts for "no free will"/no alternative: staggered Navy deliveries, liquidated damages, cancellation risk, dependence on government business (not "self-imposed, undisclosed and subjective"); best alternative vendor would have made Loral late; Loral need not have asked the Navy for an extension (uncertain length, subcontractor default no excuse); contacting all manufacturers "it believed capable" "was all the law requires"; suing for breach inadequate because Loral would still have had to get gears elsewhere. C&M 489–491. Loral had the burden of showing it could not obtain parts elsewhere within a reasonable time. C&M 490.
J5. Promptness: one who would recover money paid under duress "must act promptly"; Loral's delay until three days after Austin's last delivery was reasonable because it feared another stoppage. C&M 491.
J6. Disposition: "a classic case, as a matter of law"; order modified by reversing dismissal of Loral's claim; remanded for computation of damages; otherwise affirmed. 4–3. C&M 489, 491–492.
J7. Dissent (Bergan, J., with Breitel and Jasen): duress is "normally a routine type of factual issue" resolved against Loral below; Austin's version: no threat, a request tied to Loral's failure to carry out an understanding and to a customary vacation closing; Loral admitted many suppliers in a trade registry but used only familiar ones, so reasonableness was "at least a fair issue of fact." C&M 491–492.
J8. Note: would Loral have been justified in renouncing the price increases or the second purchase order a year earlier, and what does § 2-209(1) say; if Loral agreed without protest but did not mean to pay, bad faith (Crane Co. v. Progressive)? Article 2 "contains no rules with respect to duress," so duress comes in through UCC § 1-103(b). C&M 492. Problem "Financial Stress" (Totem Marine v. Alyeska): Alyeska proposes a settlement offer (figure illegible in the scan, a six-figure sum beginning "$10") against Totem's $300,000 bill, where Totem says it will likely go bankrupt unless paid; will the settlement be upheld, and how does it differ from Austin? C&M 492 (no answer in sources).
J9. Slide 14: what doctrine determined enforceability; how court defined economic duress; what made threat wrongful; what if modification was prompted by a cost increase; why Loral's alternatives inadequate; what Austin would have done had it known the modification was unenforceable.

## K. New consideration as an escape (C&M 483–484)

K1. "[A]ny consideration for the new undertaking, however insignificant," suffices (Levine v. Blumenthal): early payment, payment at a different place, payment in property, composition with creditors. Pinnel's Case: a lesser sum cannot satisfy a greater, but "the gift of a horse, hawk, or robe" can. C&M 483–484. § 73: a "similar" performance is consideration "if it differs from what was required ... in a way which reflects more than a pretense of a bargain." C&M 484.
K2. Partial payment: Foakes v. Beer (H.L. 1884): Mrs. Beer agreed to forgo interest on a judgment; Dr. Foakes paid principal in installments; she later recovered interest; "payment of a lesser sum on the day ... cannot be any satisfaction of the whole." The rule is "often ... doubted or denounced" ("relic of antique law," Rye v. Phillips); Lord Blackburn had reservations ("prompt payment of a part ... may be more beneficial"). C&M 484. (Patterson coda and De Cicco on unassigned p. 485–486.)

## M. RLCC § 3 (consumer standard-term modifications)

M1. § 3(a): a standard term in a consumer contract "governing an ongoing relationship" is modified if (1) reasonable notice of the proposed term and reasonable opportunity to review; (2) reasonable opportunity to reject and continue under the existing term, with reasonable notice of that opportunity; and (3) consumer either (A) manifests assent or (B) does not reject and continues after the rejection period. Sel. RLCC § 3; slide 15.
M2. § 3(b): the contract may set a reasonable modification procedure but may not, to the consumer's detriment, exclude (a), except that it may replace the opportunity to reject with "a reasonable opportunity to terminate the transaction without unreasonable cost, loss of value, or personal burden."
M3. § 3(c): enforceable "only if it is proposed in good faith and if it does not have the effect of undermining an affirmation or promise made by the business that was made part of the basis of the original bargain." Cmt 7: good faith (related to § 89's "fair and equitable" test and § 2-209); aimed at "opportunistic modifications" against locked-in consumers; (c) "will generally bar the modification of contracts fully performed, or of short-term contracts ... unless circumstances are present that were not anticipated." Cmt 10: consistent with § 2-209 and § 89. Illus. 8 (forced "I Accept": not adopted), 14 (snow plow $100→$150: not reasonable opportunity to terminate and undermines promise), 18 (retroactive device limit violates (c)).
M4. Slide 15 quotes § 3(c) as including "if it is fair and equitable in view of circumstances not anticipated by the parties when made." That clause is not in the § 3(c) black letter printed in the Selections; it appears only in the comment's description of § 89 and of short-term contracts (cmt 7). Unresolved discrepancy between slide and selection text.
