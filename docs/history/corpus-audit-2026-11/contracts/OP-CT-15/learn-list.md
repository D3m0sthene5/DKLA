## 1. Whose meaning prevails, and misunderstanding (R2d §§ 20, 200, 201)

- **Interpretation defined.** Interpretation of a promise, agreement or term is "the ascertainment of its meaning." R2d § 200.
- **Shared meaning controls.** Where the parties attached the same meaning to a term, it is interpreted in accordance with that meaning. R2d § 201(1). This answers the C&M question whether Frigaliment would come out the same "if both parties had meant broilers although they had said 'chicken'" (C&M p. 581, Note 1). The Restatement answer is that the shared meaning governs even if it departs from the "external signs." C&M p. 599 presents the contrary objectivist position (Learned Hand in Eustis Mining, 239 F. 976, 984 (S.D.N.Y. 1917)): intent "makes not the least difference," and the casebook cites § 201 in the same passage.
- **One party's meaning controls when that party is the less-at-fault one.** Where the parties attached different meanings, the term is interpreted in accordance with one party's meaning if, *at the time the agreement was made*, (a) that party did not know of any different meaning attached by the other and the other knew the first party's meaning, or (b) that party had no reason to know of any different meaning and the other had reason to know the first party's meaning. R2d § 201(2). "Reason to know" is the fault standard. Time of formation is the measuring point.
- **Otherwise neither party is bound.** Except as stated in § 201, neither party is bound by the meaning the other attached, "even though the result may be a failure of mutual assent." R2d § 201(3). Comment d: the result may be an entire failure of agreement or a failure to agree on a term. There may still be a binding contract if the term is not essential or can be supplied (citing § 204). In some cases a party can waive the misunderstanding and enforce the contract on the other's understanding.
- **Formation-side rule (§ 20).** There is no manifestation of mutual assent if the parties attach *materially* different meanings and (a) neither knows or has reason to know the other's meaning, or (b) each knows or each has reason to know the other's meaning. R2d § 20(1). The manifestations operate on one party's meaning on the same knowledge/reason-to-know conditions as § 201(2). R2d § 20(2). Comment b: perfect identity of connotation is never required. A "core of common meaning sufficient to determine their performances with reasonable certainty" is enough (citing § 33). Comment c: interpretation arises both in deciding whether a contract was made and in deciding what obligations it imposes. The Chapter 9 interpretation rules determine what each party "knows or has reason to know."
- **Teaching problem.** "Is Silence Golden?" (C&M pp. 581-582; Hamann v. Crouch, 508 P.2d 968 (Kan. 1973)). Hamann knew the college read the reinstatement clause as conditional on an open similar position. His lawyer read it otherwise, and he accepted the leave without saying anything. The casebook asks what one should think of the lawyer's advice and gives no answer. Applying § 201(2)(a) to the stated facts, the college's meaning would prevail if the college did not know of Hamann's different meaning and Hamann knew the college's. That is an inference from the Restatement. The excerpt does not report the Kansas court's holding.

## 2. Frigaliment: ambiguity, evidence of meaning, and the burden of proving the narrower meaning

Frigaliment Importing Co. v. B.N.S. International Sales Corp., 190 F. Supp. 116 (S.D.N.Y. 1960) (Friendly, J.), C&M pp. 575-580.

- **Posture and law.** The Swiss buyer sued the New York seller for breach of the warranty that goods correspond to their description, under N.Y. Personal Property Law § 95 (C&M p. 576). Both parties accepted that New York law applied. The case predates New York's adoption of the UCC; Note 2 asks whether the buyer would have succeeded under the UCC (C&M p. 583).
- **Holding.** "[P]laintiff has the burden of showing that 'chicken' was used in the narrower rather than in the broader sense, and this it has not sustained." C&M p. 580 (announced at p. 576). The complaint was dismissed. The court expressly did not decide whether plaintiff subjectively intended broilers: "it is unnecessary to determine that issue" (p. 580).
- **Why the seller's meaning sufficed.** The seller's subjective intent "would not be significant if this did not coincide with an objective meaning of 'chicken.'" It coincided with one dictionary meaning, with the USDA regulation definition "to which the contract made at least oblique reference," with "at least some usage in the trade," with "the realities of the market," and with what plaintiff's spokesman had said. C&M p. 580.
- **Steps of the analysis.** "Chicken" standing alone is ambiguous (p. 576). (i) Contract text: the buyer argued that because the 1½-2 lb birds had to be young, the 2½-3 lb birds must be young too; the court called this "unpersuasive" with the two-sizes-of-apples example (p. 576). (ii) Pre-contract cables: the buyer stressed that it used the English word "chicken" in German-language cables. That argument was "largely drained away" by Bauer's testimony that Stovicek said "any kind of chickens" and confirmed "Huhn" (p. 577). (iii) Trade usage: see point 3. (iv) Incorporated USDA regulation, 7 C.F.R. § 70.301, which lists "Hen or stewing chicken or fowl" as a class of chickens. There is "force" in the argument that the contract "made the regulations a dictionary," because the reference to Government grading was already in plaintiff's own initial cable (p. 579). (v) Market price: broilers cost 35-37 cents a pound and the contract price was 33 cents, and plaintiff "could not have expected defendant deliberately to incur a loss" (p. 579). (vi) Post-shipment conduct: the buyer's May 29 cable telling the seller to ship despite its "fowl" protests might be "highly relevant" to the measure of damages, but the court "cannot give it weight on the issue of interpretation" (p. 580). The seller's point that plaintiff resold birds as "poulets" had "little force" given plaintiff's "immediate and consistent protests" (p. 580).
- **Holmes framing and Note 1.** Friendly says the case illustrates Holmes's remark that contract depends on the parties "having said the same thing" (p. 575). Note 1 asks whether that is really the issue (p. 581).
- **Note 2, corporate intent** (p. 581). Would it matter if Bauer had long trade experience? The note cites Franklin Life Ins. Co. v. Mast, 435 F.2d 1038 (9th Cir. 1970), on an agent's intent.
- **Note 3, incorporation by reference** (p. 581). Wilcox v. Wilcox, 406 S.W.2d 152 (Ky. 1966): "age of majority" meant the statutory age (21) at the time of the agreement, even after the statute later lowered it to 18. The note also asks whether a bare clause incorporating the UNIDROIT Principles suffices.
- **Note 4** (p. 581). The buyer's usage evidence "would be impressive if there were nothing to the contrary," but there was. See UCC § 1-303.
- **Context, judge and jury** (pp. 574-575, partly outside the assignment). The meaning of language is strictly a question of fact, but interpretation of writings is often treated as a question of law for the judge. Traynor in Parsons: it is a judicial function unless interpretation turns on the credibility of extrinsic evidence. Friendly in Meyers v. Selznick favors jury resolution of ambiguities in light of extrinsic evidence. On scope of review, Ram Construction (3d Cir. 1984) holds that a trial court's interpretation of a factual matter is reviewed for clear error (p. 575). Paragon Resources (5th Cir. 1983) holds that ambiguity is a question of law reviewed first, and the interpretation in light of extrinsic evidence is then reviewed for clear error (p. 575 n.f).

## 3. Trade usage and a party who is not a member of the trade

- **Pre-Code rule applied in Frigaliment.** When one party is not a member of the trade, "his acceptance of the standard must be made to appear." The proof is either actual knowledge of the usage, or a usage "so generally known in the community that his actual individual knowledge of it may be inferred" (9 Wigmore § 2464). C&M p. 577. The seller showed it "was only beginning in the poultry trade in 1957." There was "no proof of actual knowledge," and the seller's belief "was to the contrary" (p. 577). New York's alternative test demands a usage "of so long continuance, so well established, so notorious, so universal and so reasonable in itself, as that the presumption is violent that the parties contracted with reference to it" (Walls v. Bailey, 49 N.Y. 464 (1872)). C&M pp. 577-578.
- **Weight of usage witnesses.** Strasser's opinion was "considerably weakened" because in his own deals he specified "broiler" or "fowl." Under the Lord Mansfield/Wigmore line, "a witness' consistent failure to rely on the alleged usage deprives his opinion testimony of much of its effect" (p. 578). The buyer's published-quotation evidence was met by contrary witnesses (Weininger, Fox, Sadina) and by GSA specifications and industry statistics (pp. 578-579).
- **UCC rule.** Usage of trade binds as "usage of trade in the vocation or trade in which they are engaged *or of which they are or should be aware*." UCC § 1-303(d). Comment 6: subsection (d) gives effect to usages of which the parties "are or should be aware."
- **Nanakuli's statement of the rule** (C&M p. 588). A party "is always held to conduct generally observed by members of his chosen trade," because the other party is justified in assuming so "unless he indicates otherwise." He is held to more general business practices "to the extent of his actual knowledge of those practices or to the degree his ignorance of those practices is not excusable: they were so generally practiced he should have been aware of them." The court quotes White & Summers: usage binds "members of the trade involved or persons who know or should know about it," including those who regularly deal with the trade and members of a second trade that commonly deals with it (p. 588). Applied: Shell dealt "constantly and almost exclusively on Oahu with one asphalt paver" and "should have been aware" of pavers' fixed-price bidding and suppliers' price protection (pp. 588-589).
- **Note 3 comparisons** (C&M p. 584). In Foxco (5th Cir. 1979), a buyer was bound by a trade association usage on "first quality" though it was not a member and did not know of the usage. In Flower City (2d Cir. 1979), a "neophyte minority painting contractor" did not have reason to know of the trade usage. In Martin Rispens (Ind. 1993), a seed seller and a crop farmer were "not in the same trade," so the seller could exclude the implied warranty by usage under § 2-316(3)(c) only by showing the farmer "was or should have been aware" of the usage. The note asks whether the farm was in the business of buying seeds, and what if the Frigaliment buyer had been a fried-chicken chain.
- **Proof requirements.** The existence and scope of a usage "must be proved as facts," and if the usage is embodied in a trade code or similar record, interpreting the record is a question of law. UCC § 1-303(c). A party offering usage evidence must give notice "sufficient to prevent unfair surprise." § 1-303(g); Comment 9. Nanakuli: "The extent of a usage is ultimately a jury question" (p. 589).
- **Regularity, not universality.** The ancient English tests for custom are abandoned. A usage need not be "ancient or immemorial" or "universal," and new usages and those observed by "the great majority of decent dealers" qualify. § 1-303 Comment 4, quoted in Nanakuli at C&M p. 589. Nanakuli adds that trade usages "still must be well settled" (p. 591). Commercial acceptance makes a prima facie case of reasonableness, and the unconscionability policy polices usages. Comment 5. Where a usage's precise variation is unsettled, the party relying on it gets at least the minimum variation demonstrated. Comment 8.
- **Place of performance.** A usage applicable where part of the performance is to occur may be used as to that part. § 1-303(d), second sentence. This is relevant to the "What Is 'Slaw'?" problem (C&M p. 584; Williams v. Curtin, 807 F.2d 1046 (D.C. Cir. 1986)), which poses a Georgia usage against an interstate coleslaw-market usage. The excerpt does not give the answer.

## 4. Hurst: usage can give special meaning to facially unambiguous language

Hurst v. W.J. Lake & Co., 16 P.2d 627 (Or. 1932) (Rossman, J.), C&M pp. 582-583.

- **Facts and posture.** The contract was for horse-meat scraps, "minimum 50% protein," with a $5/ton discount if any scraps analyzed "less than 50% of protein." The buyer took the discount on 140 tons at 49.53-49.96% protein. The seller alleged that both parties were members of a trade group and that the buyer knew of a usage treating 49.5% or more as 50%. The trial court gave the buyer judgment on the pleadings (p. 582).
- **Holding.** The court rejected the rule that "when a contract is expressed in language which is not ambiguous upon its face the court will receive no evidence of usage." The court found none of the rule's supposed justifications sufficient (simplicity, protection against perjured custom testimony, and serving the parol evidence rule), and so would not exclude usage evidence "even though the instrument is nonambiguous upon its face." Reversed. C&M p. 583. The examples are the bricklayer's "thousand," the shingle packs, and 100 dozen rabbits as "a thousand" (pp. 582-583).
- **Scope.** The case reverses a judgment on the pleadings, so the seller now has to prove the usage at trial. Note 1 asks how. Note 1 also gives Holmes's endorsement of class usage, as distinguished from private codes (p. 583). Hurst's pleaded facts include trade membership and the buyer's knowledge, so the case does not test a non-member.
- **UCC parallel.** § 2-202 Comment 1(c) rejects any requirement that the court first find the language ambiguous before admitting course of dealing, usage or course of performance. Note 2 asks how § 1-303(e) differs from a plain-meaning rule (C&M pp. 583-584).

## 5. Hierarchy: express terms, course of performance, course of dealing, usage of trade

- **Definitions.** Course of performance is a sequence of conduct in the *particular* transaction. It exists when (1) the agreement involves repeated occasions for performance by a party and (2) the other party, with knowledge of the nature of the performance and opportunity to object, accepts or acquiesces without objection. § 1-303(a). Course of dealing is conduct in *previous* transactions between the parties that is "fairly to be regarded as establishing a common basis of understanding." § 1-303(b); Comment 2. Usage of trade is defined at § 1-303(c). Nanakuli restates the distinction: course of performance is the parties' action in carrying out the contract at issue, and course of dealing is their relations before signing it (C&M p. 589).
- **Function.** Each "is relevant in ascertaining the meaning of the parties' agreement, may give particular meaning to specific terms of the agreement, and may supplement or qualify the terms of the agreement." § 1-303(d); C&M p. 585 (Section 4 introduction). Under the UCC, "agreement" is the bargain in fact, including course of dealing, usage and course of performance (former § 1-201(3), quoted in Nanakuli at C&M p. 590 and in Figgie at C&M p. 595).
- **Order of precedence.** Except as provided in subsection (f), express terms and any applicable course of performance, course of dealing or usage "must be construed whenever reasonable as consistent with each other." Only if that construction is unreasonable does the order apply: (1) express terms prevail over all three, (2) course of performance prevails over course of dealing and usage, and (3) course of dealing prevails over usage. § 1-303(e). The Restatement states the same order as a "standard[] of preference," with "greater weight" language. R2d § 203(b). Consistent construction "wherever reasonable" is R2d § 202(5). Course of performance "accepted or acquiesced in without objection is given great weight" (R2d § 202(4)). Nanakuli: "[C]ourse of dealing controls usage of trade" (C&M p. 590), and the Code "looks to the actual performance of a contract as the best indication" of meaning (pp. 585-587).
- **Other R2d aids** (no comments in the selection). Circumstances and principal purpose, R2d § 202(1). Writing read as a whole, § 202(2). Generally prevailing meaning, and technical terms in their field, "[u]nless a different intention is manifested," § 202(3). Effective meaning to all terms, § 203(a). Specific over general, § 203(c). Negotiated over standardized terms, § 203(d).

## 6. When usage or dealing can supplement or qualify, and when it contradicts express terms

- **Nanakuli Paving & Rock Co. v. Shell Oil Co.**, 664 F.2d 772 (9th Cir. 1981), C&M pp. 585-592.
  - *Holding on consistency.* The express term was "Shell's Posted Price at time of delivery," F.O.B. Honolulu (p. 585). A reasonable jury could find that a trade usage of price protection was incorporated and was "reasonably consistent" with that term. The court reasoned that "[a] total negation of that term would be that the buyer was to set the price." An unstated exception at price increases, charging the old price for a period or for tonnage already committed on non-escalating contracts, is "a less than complete negation." Such a usage "forms a broad and important exception to the express term, but does not swallow it entirely" (p. 591). The court followed "the Code's mandate to proceed on the assumption that the parties have included those usages unless they cannot reasonably be construed as consistent with the express terms" (p. 591). Performance, usages and prior dealings "are important enough to be admitted always, even for a final and complete agreement" (pp. 590-591). The court labels the holding as "under these particular facts" (p. 591).
  - *Scope of the trade.* The trial judge "did not abuse his discretion" in defining the trade as the Hawaii asphaltic paving trade rather than the sale of asphalt alone (p. 586). That definition let in aggregate suppliers' routine price protection. Chevron, the largest asphalt supplier, also price protected (p. 587). The practice rested on government agencies' refusal to accept escalation clauses (p. 587).
  - *Disposition.* JNOV for Shell was reversed and the $220,800 jury verdict was reinstated, because the jury could have found for Nanakuli under either theory (pp. 585, 592). The standard of review was substantial evidence for reasonable jurors (p. 585).
  - *Kennedy, J., concurring specially* (p. 592). The opinion should not let juries import price protection or a similar specific term "from a concept of good faith that is not based on well-established custom and usage or other objective standards of which the parties had clear notice." Uncontradicted usage evidence and the parties' knowledge or reason to know were "necessary predicates for either theory."
  - *Notes* (pp. 592-593). Note 1 asks whether usage was used to give meaning, to supplement or to qualify, and what language was being interpreted. It compares Naval Institute (industry custom invoked to ship before the "not sooner than October" date). It also asks whether the 1970-71 increases were comparable to the $44-to-$76 jump. Note 2 asks whether usage is measured at formation or performance; the court did not decide whether post-signing usage evidence is admissible to show justified reliance (664 F.2d at 785 n.17). Note 3 asks whether usage can fill a case the contract omits, such as the irrevocability of a sub-bid (Drennan; Albert v. R.P. Farnsworth).
- **Columbia Nitrogen Corp. v. Royster Co.**, 451 F.2d 3 (4th Cir. 1971) (Butzner, J.), C&M pp. 596-599.
  - *Rule.* The Code restates the rule that usage and course-of-dealing evidence "should be excluded whenever it cannot be reasonably construed as consistent with the terms of the contract." But the test of admissibility "is not whether the contract appears on its face to be complete in every detail, but whether the proffered evidence ... reasonably can be construed as consistent with the express terms" (p. 597).
  - *Reasons for consistency* (pp. 597-598). The contract did not expressly exclude usage and dealing. It was "silent about adjusting prices and quantities to reflect a declining market," and that "neutrality" was a "fitting occasion" for usage. Quantities were stated as "Products Supplied Under Contract," not "Purchased." The default clause covered only the buyer's failure to pay. The parties had rejected both a $10/ton liquidated-damages clause and a market-price clause, leaving a "hiatus" on refusal to take delivery. Before allowing damages, the court "must first determine whether the buyer has in fact defaulted," by supplementing the agreement with consistent usage and dealing.
  - *Merger clause.* The clause read: "No verbal understanding will be recognized ... this contract expresses all the terms and conditions." It did not exclude usage or dealing, because these "are not synonymous with verbal understandings, terms and conditions." § 2-202 limits consistent additional terms when the writing is complete and exclusive, but places "no similar limitation" on usage and dealing. Under § 2-202 Comment 2, those are admissible to supplement "any writing" unless "carefully negated." They "should not be conclusively rejected by reading them into stereotyped language that makes no specific reference to them." C&M pp. 598-599 and n.l.
  - *Disposition.* The judgment was vacated and the case remanded (p. 599), so the evidence goes to the factfinder. The court did not hold that the usage exists or that Columbia had no obligation. The evidence was expert testimony that express price and quantity terms in the mixed-fertilizer industry are "mere projections," plus six years of the parties' dealings, mainly Columbia's nitrogen sales to Royster, showing "repeated and substantial deviation" (pp. 596-597).
  - *Note* (p. 599). Would the result differ if the merger clause had named "usage of trade, or course of dealing"? The note does not answer, but § 2-202 Comment 2 ("unless carefully negated") points toward exclusion.
- **Figgie International, Inc. v. Destileria Serralles, Inc.**, 190 F.3d 252 (4th Cir. 1999) (Traxler, J.), C&M pp. 593-596.
  - *Posture.* The seller of bottle-labeling equipment sought a declaratory judgment that the buyer was limited to repair, replacement or return. The original agreement was lost. The buyer's copy referred to terms "printed on the reverse," but the reverse was blank. Figgie conceded a factual dispute over whether its standard terms were part of the agreement (pp. 594-595).
  - *Holding.* Summary judgment for Figgie was affirmed. Figgie's affidavits showed that sellers in the industry "always limit" remedies to repair, replacement or return and exclude consequential damages. Serralles said it did not "acquiesce" but "offered no evidence to contradict the affidavits." So usage of trade "would limit Serralles to the exclusive remedy" (p. 596). Usage *supplemented* the agreement with a remedy limitation under § 2-719 (pp. 595-596).
  - *What the excerpt leaves out.* It shows no separate finding that Serralles, a rum bottler, was in the bottle-labeling trade or knew or should have known of the usage. The statute quoted requires "the vocation or trade in which they are engaged or of which they are or should be aware" (p. 595). The failure-of-essential-purpose issue appears in Chapter 8 (C&M p. 1044) and is not in this excerpt.
  - *Note* (p. 596). Same result if the original agreement were found and said nothing about limited remedies? The excerpt does not answer.
- **UCC § 2-202.** Terms set forth in a writing intended as a final expression "may not be contradicted by evidence of any prior agreement or of a contemporaneous oral agreement but may be explained or supplemented (a) by course of performance, course of dealing, or usage of trade (Section 1-303); and (b) by evidence of consistent additional terms unless the court finds the writing to have been intended also as a complete and exclusive statement." Comment 1 rejects three things: (a) the assumption that a writing final on some matters includes all matters agreed, (b) meaning fixed by legal rules of construction rather than commercial context, and (c) a threshold ambiguity finding for paragraph (a) evidence. Comment 2 reads writings on the assumption that prior dealings and usages "were taken for granted," and "[u]nless carefully negated they have become an element of the meaning." Comment 3: if additional terms "would certainly have been included in the document in the view of the court," evidence of them is kept from the trier of fact.
- **Contrast in degree of departure.** In Hurst, usage gives special meaning to the express words. In Nanakuli, it creates an exception that qualifies the express price term short of total negation. In Columbia Nitrogen, it supplements terms on a matter the contract is silent on. In Figgie, it supplies a remedy limitation the writing, as proved, may not contain. Section 1-303(e)(1) remains the outer limit: when consistent construction is unreasonable, express terms prevail.

## 7. Waiver versus course of performance

- **Code text.** Subject to § 2-209, a course of performance "is relevant to show a waiver or modification of any term inconsistent with the course of performance." § 1-303(f). Section 1-303(e) is expressly "[e]xcept as otherwise provided in subsection (f)."
- **Nanakuli** (C&M pp. 586, 589-590). Shell argued that its 1970 and 1971 price protection was a "mere waiver[]" of the price term. The court held the jury "could reasonably have found" that the two acts "were not ambiguous and therefore indicated Shell's understanding of the terms." A single occasion is not a course of performance (former § 2-208 Comment 4). Two occasions sufficed because they were "the only occasions before 1974 that would call for such conduct." Blee's 1970 memo spoke of the need to "bargain" over the extent of protection, which indicated a perceived legal right. Former § 2-208 Comment 3 prefers "waiver" where it is difficult to tell whether an act sheds light on meaning or waives a term, "to preserve the flexible character of commercial contracts and to prevent surprise or other hardship." The court held that this preference "only applies ... where acts are ambiguous," and that ambiguity was for the jury. Shell's own evidence of price protection in 1977 and 1978 bolstered the course-of-performance finding. The excerpt does not say whether the post-1974 conduct's relevance to the 1969 contract was contested.
- **Why the label matters.** The selection states only part of this. A waiver can be retracted through "the provisions on the reinstatement of rights waived," which the excerpt mentions in Comment 3 at p. 590 but does not reprint as § 2-209. A course of performance instead becomes part of the meaning of the agreement.
- **Course-of-dealing evidence excluded below.** The district judge excluded Nanakuli's course-of-dealing evidence, and the appeal turned on usage and course of performance (p. 589).

## 8. Good faith in setting price (Nanakuli, second theory)

- A price to be fixed by the seller means a price to be fixed in good faith (former § 2-305(2)). For a merchant, good faith includes "the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing in the trade" (former § 2-103(1)(b)). A posted price satisfies good faith "in the normal case" (§ 2-305 Comment 3), but that qualifier means not in all circumstances. The dispute was over the *manner* of the increase, not its amount. Chevron gave at least six weeks' notice, while Shell's $32 increase took effect January 1 by a letter written December 31 and received January 4. The jury could find a breach of good faith in both the timing and the refusal to protect work already bid. C&M pp. 591-592.
- Kennedy's concurrence limits this theory to objective, well-established standards of which the parties had clear notice (p. 592).
- UCC § 1-304 (in the selection near § 1-303) imposes good faith in performance and enforcement. Its Comment 1 says it does not support an independent cause of action and directs interpretation within the commercial context. The selection supplies this section, but the session assignment does not list it. It bears on whether the Nanakuli good-faith theory is an independent claim. The excerpt does not resolve that tension.

## 9. Omitted essential terms (R2d § 204)

- "When the parties to a bargain sufficiently defined to be a contract have not agreed with respect to a term which is essential to a determination of their rights and duties, a term which is reasonable in the circumstances is supplied by the court." R2d § 204. The conditions are (i) a bargain sufficiently defined to be a contract, (ii) no agreement on the term, and (iii) the term is essential to determining rights and duties. The court then supplies a term that is reasonable in the circumstances. The selection prints no comments.
- Cross-reference: R2d § 201 Comment d. A failure to agree on a term does not defeat the contract if the term is not essential or can be supplied under § 204.
- Course connection, which is inference and not stated in the sources. Columbia Nitrogen's "hiatus" on the consequences of refusal to take delivery (p. 598) and Nanakuli Note 3 on "Usage and Omitted Cases" (p. 593) both concern gaps. The Code approach in Columbia fills the gap first from usage and dealing consistent with the express terms, and the court refused to fill it directly with "the general law of contracts" (p. 598). The sources do not say how § 204's "reasonable in the circumstances" term relates to a proved usage.

## 10. What the sources leave unresolved

- Whether Frigaliment's buyer would win under § 1-303 (Note 2, p. 583). Section 1-303(c) drops the "universal"/"notorious" test the court applied, but § 1-303(d) still requires that the seller be in the trade or be or should be aware of the usage. Friendly also found the existence of the usage contested on the evidence.
- Whether, on Frigaliment's facts, § 20(1)(a) would mean no contract if neither party had reason to know the other's meaning. Friendly avoided deciding plaintiff's intent and rested on burden. The casebook does not connect the case to § 20.
- Which of two competing usages governs (Williams v. Curtin, p. 584). Whether usage is measured at formation or performance (Nanakuli Note 2, p. 593). Whether usage can make a sub-bid irrevocable (Note 3, p. 593).
- Whether a merger clause expressly naming usage and course of dealing would exclude them (Columbia Note, p. 599). Whether Figgie survives if the original agreement is silent on remedies (p. 596).
- How many occasions make a course of performance. Comment 4 rules out one occasion and the court said the Comment "does not further delineate" (p. 589).
- The Hamann outcome (p. 582) and the Naval Institute comparison (p. 592) are posed as questions only.
