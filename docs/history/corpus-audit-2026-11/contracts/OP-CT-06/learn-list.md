## 1. Three questions the chapter separates (C&M 169–171)

The editors split what courts often call "mutual assent" into three questions. First, assent: did the parties manifest a shared understanding of what is to be exchanged? Restatement (Second) § 3 defines an agreement as "a manifestation of mutual assent on the part of two or more persons", and its comment says the definition carries "no implication that legal consequences are or are not produced" (C&M 169). Holmes's line that assent rests "not on the parties' having meant the same thing but on their having said the same thing" states the outward focus. Second, consent: was the participation voluntary, meaning legal capacity and no impermissible coercion? The robber's "your money or your life" is perfectly understood by both sides but is not a contract (C&M 169; consent is deferred to Chapter 4). Third, intent regarding legal enforceability: an intention to be legally bound is not required, but a bargain will not be enforced "if there is an indication from the circumstances or the parties that they intend it not to be legally enforceable" (C&M 169, citing § 21). The editors warn that cases blur the three (C&M 170, quoting the Arizona Supreme Court).

Restatement (Second) § 17(1) requires a bargain with "a manifestation of mutual assent to the exchange and a consideration"; § 18 says each party must make a promise or begin or render a performance; § 22 says assent "ordinarily takes the form of an offer ... followed by an acceptance", and § 22(2) allows formation even when neither offer nor acceptance can be identified (Selections pp. 20–21; C&M 170).

## 2. The objective theory of assent (C&M 171–173, 176, 178)

"Objective theory" is standard vocabulary: a party's assent is judged by the reasonable meaning of what the party said and did, not by an unexpressed state of mind. "Subjective theory" (also called the "actual intent", "meeting of the minds" or "will" theory) looks to what the parties actually intended. Judge Learned Hand stated the strong objective view: a contract "has, strictly speaking, nothing to do with the personal, or individual, intent of the parties", and a party who used words with their ordinary meaning would be held "if ... it were proved by twenty bishops" that he meant something else, unless there were mutual mistake or the like (Hotchkiss v. National City Bank, C&M 171–172). Judge Jerome Frank answered that the objectivists "went too far" by treating all contracts alike and by excluding "the actual intention of the parties or either of them" as legally irrelevant (Ricketts v. Pennsylvania R. Co., C&M 172–173). The Tenth Circuit's line that contracts "are not formed by comparing mental states; they are formed by what the parties communicate" (Navair, C&M 176) restates the objective view. Gilmore's point is evidentiary: a subjective test turns every case into an extended inquiry into minds (C&M 178). The editors then note that subjective intent is not unprovable: discovery, jury credibility judgments and conflicting organizational sources can test a witness's claim about intent (C&M 178).

Restatement (Second) § 19 states the rules for conduct as assent. Conduct can manifest assent (§ 19(1)); it is effective only if the actor "intends to engage in the conduct and knows or has reason to know that the other party may infer from his conduct that he assents" (§ 19(2)); and conduct may manifest assent "even though he does not in fact assent", in which case the contract may be voidable for fraud, duress, mistake or another invalidating cause (§ 19(3)). Section 20 supplies the misunderstanding rules: no assent where the parties attach materially different meanings and neither (or each) knows or has reason to know the other's meaning; one party's meaning controls where that party does not know (or has no reason to know) of the other's meaning and the other knows (or has reason to know) the first party's meaning (§ 20(1)–(2)). "Reason to know" is a term of art meaning the person has information from which a reasonable person would infer the fact.

## 3. Lucy v. Zehmer (Va. 1954), C&M 173–176, and the jest notes, C&M 176–178

Facts. W.O. Lucy offered A.H. Zehmer $50,000 for the 471.6-acre Ferguson farm at Zehmer's restaurant on December 20, 1952. Zehmer wrote on a restaurant check, first "I hereby agree to sell", then, after Lucy objected that Mrs. Zehmer must sign too, "We hereby agree to sell to W.O. Lucy the Ferguson Farm complete for $50,000.00, title satisfactory to buyer"; both Zehmers signed (C&M 173–175). The talk lasted forty minutes or more and covered what the sale included and title examination (C&M 175). Lucy pocketed the paper and offered $5 to bind the bargain; Zehmer refused, and by his own account only then told Lucy it was a joke (C&M 174, 175–176). Zehmer whispered to his wife that it was a joke so that Lucy would not hear (C&M 176). The next day Lucy arranged for his brother to take a half interest; the day after, he hired a lawyer to examine title; Lucy testified that Zehmer told him "for the first time" the following night that he would not sell (C&M 175).

Intoxication is a separate question. A contract is not invalidated for intoxication unless the party was "intoxicated to the extent of being unable to comprehend the nature and consequences of the instrument he executed"; the record showed Zehmer was not, and his counsel conceded at oral argument that Zehmer "was not too drunk to make a valid contract" (C&M 174).

Holding and test. The court reversed the dismissal and remanded for a decree of specific performance (an order requiring the sellers to convey the farm, rather than pay damages) (C&M 176). "We must look to the outward expression of a person as manifesting his intention rather than to his secret and unexpressed intention" (C&M 175). Quoting Restatement (First) § 71: "If the words or other acts of one of the parties have but one reasonable meaning, his undisclosed intention is immaterial except when an unreasonable meaning which he attaches to his manifestations is known to the other party" (C&M 176). "So a person cannot set up that he was merely jesting when his conduct and words would warrant a reasonable person in believing that he intended a real agreement" (C&M 176). The court also found that Lucy actually believed the deal serious and "was warranted in believing" it (C&M 175). Whether the Zehmers accepted seriously or "in secret jest", there was a binding contract (C&M 176).

What the test produces in the variations the casebook and slide 8 pose:
- Zehmer privately joking, Lucy reasonably taking it seriously: contract (the holding).
- Lucy actually knew Zehmer was joking, even though the joke was not objectively apparent: no contract under the knowledge exception the court quoted (C&M 176); Note 4 poses this case and discusses how the knowledge would be proved (C&M 178).
- Lucy did not know but a reasonable person in his position would have recognized a joke: no contract, because Zehmer's words would not "warrant a reasonable person in believing that he intended a real agreement" (C&M 176). This matches § 20(2)(b) (one party's meaning controls when the other has reason to know it). Keller and Leonard are the casebook's illustrations.
- Lucy honestly but unreasonably believed the deal serious: his honest belief alone does not bind Zehmer (Note 1 asks "Why is Lucy's honest belief alone not sufficient to bind Zehmer? What more does the court require?", C&M 176); the court required that the belief be warranted.

Keller v. Holderman (Mich. 1863): Holderman gave a $300 check for a watch worth about $15 as "a frolic and banter"; the trial court found that neither side expected a sale at that price, and the appellate court held that "no contract was ever made" (C&M 177). The price is used as evidence that no one was serious, which the casebook contrasts with the "peppercorn" (a nominal consideration) at Note 4, p. 59 (C&M 177). Leonard v. Pepsico (S.D.N.Y. 1999, aff'd 2d Cir. 2000): a television commercial showed a Harrier jet for 7,000,000 Pepsi Points; Leonard sent 15 points and $700,000 (points could be bought at 10 cents each); the court held for Pepsico because "no objective person could reasonably have concluded that the commercial actually offered consumers a Harrier Jet", citing the ad's "zany humor", the jet's military function and a $23 million real price (C&M 177–178). The casebook asks whether a price too good to be true always means no offer was made (C&M 178). Note 2 also distinguishes jesting from "bluffing or daring": the Zehmers claimed the writing was a bluff to make Lucy admit he lacked $50,000, and the notes ask what result follows if Lucy, knowing this, had "called their bluff" by raising the money (C&M 177). The casebook leaves that question open.

## 4. Intention to be legally bound: § 21, Cohen, social settings and Balfour

Restatement (Second) § 21: "Neither real nor apparent intention that a promise be legally binding is essential to the formation of a contract, but a manifestation of intention that a promise shall not affect legal relations may prevent the formation of a contract." Comment c: "In some situations the normal understanding is that no legal obligation arises, and some unusual manifestation of intention is necessary to create a contract. Traditional examples are social engagements and agreements within a family group" (Selections p. 21). The editors draw two qualifications from the broad first clause: some agreements, such as social and family arrangements, lack the usual presumption of enforceability, so parties must do more to show they meant legal effect; and agreements that would otherwise be enforceable may be denied enforcement because of a manifested intent not to be bound (C&M 183).

Cohen v. Cowles Media (Minn. 1990), C&M 183–185. Two reporters promised Dan Cohen anonymity in exchange for documents about a candidate; their editors overruled them and named him. The court said "we seemingly have all three" elements (offer, acceptance, consideration) "plus a breach" (C&M 184), but held a contract cause of action "inappropriate for these particular circumstances" because "the law ... does not create a contract where the parties intended none", reporter and source do not ordinarily believe they are making a legally binding contract, and contract would put "an unwarranted legal rigidity" on an ethical relationship (C&M 185). The casebook's bracketed introduction notes that the same promises were later held enforceable through promissory estoppel (C&M 183). Slide 6's quotation comes from the U.S. Supreme Court's opinion reversing the Minnesota court (C&M 169 n.a: 501 U.S. 663 (1991); the slide prints "50 U.S. 663").

Social settings, C&M 186–187. The editors say that where promises are social, courts are less likely still to find contractual liability "unless the parties go out of their way to indicate an intention to be legally bound" (C&M 186). Professor Patterson's test: a good rule should give "freedom to contract" to non-lawyers who clarify what they want, without making contracting "so easy that it hooks the unwary signer or the casual promisor", which he calls "freedom from contract" (C&M 186). Note 1 asks about dinner invitations, expensive preparations and a broken promise to marry (Ferraro v. Singh) (C&M 186); Cohen itself cites Minnesota's abolition of the action for breach of promise to marry (C&M 185). Mitzel v. Hauck (S.D. 1960): a friend's ride on a duck-hunting trip was not a contract that took the passenger out of guest status; "it is not every agreement that results in a binding, legally enforceable contract" (C&M 187). Note 3: the strict traditional view of spousal agreements "persists in judicial reluctance" to enforce them but has been relaxed for unmarried couples (Boland v. Catalano, Conn. 1987: "ordinary contract principles are not suspended for unmarried persons living together") (C&M 187). Note 4 raises the lawyer's ethical limits in preparing a client's testimony about past intent (Model Rule 1.2(d)) (C&M 187).

Balfour v. Balfour, [1919] 2 K.B. 571 (C.A.), Supp. pp. 11–20. Facts: married in 1900 and living in Ceylon, where the husband was a civil engineer and Director of Irrigation, the couple came to England in 1915 on his leave. When he returned in August 1916, she stayed on medical advice (rheumatic arthritis). They worked out her needs together, and he promised £30 a month until she returned (Supp. pp. 11–12 (571–572)). She conceded the arrangement was "one which might be made by a couple in amity" and that they had not agreed to live apart until later differences arose; he later wrote suggesting they remain apart; she obtained a decree nisi for restitution of conjugal rights and an alimony order (Supp. pp. 11–12 (571–572)). Sargant J. held for her: the husband owed support, the parties had contracted to fix its amount, and her consent was sufficient consideration (Supp. p. 12 (572)). Her counsel argued she gave up her right to pledge his credit for necessaries (Supp. pp. 13–14 (573–574)).

The Court of Appeal allowed the appeal. Warrington L.J.: a contract could be shown only by express terms or "necessary implication"; there was "no bargain on the part of the wife at all"; implying one would bind her to accept £30 whatever happened and him to pay indefinitely; "These two people never intended to make a bargain which could be enforced in law"; the husband was "bound in honour" only. He did "not for a moment say" that such a contract between spouses is impossible (Supp. pp. 14–15 (574–575)). Duke L.J.: the question was whether the wife gave consideration; he did not dissent "as at present advised" from the proposition that spouses could contract, and he distinguished a genuine separation agreement, which "does involve mutual considerations"; the wife's power to pledge her husband's credit was an agency he could withdraw, so giving it up was not consideration; treating domestic promises as contracts would be "a possible fruitful source of dissension and quarrelling" and "lead to unlimited litigation" (Supp. pp. 15–18 (575–578)). Atkin L.J.: some agreements, such as an agreement to take a walk or an accepted invitation, are not contracts; spousal allowance arrangements, "or many of them, do not result in contracts at all ... even though there may be what as between other parties would constitute consideration", "because the parties did not intend that they should be attended by legal consequences"; "each house is a domain into which the King's writ does not seek to run"; "the onus was upon the plaintiff", who had not established a contract (Supp. pp. 18–20 (578–580)). The casebook summarizes Balfour by quoting Atkin's intention ground (C&M 187).

The decisive facts are that the spouses were living in amity, expecting reunion, when the promise was made, and that no separation agreement existed. Two of the three judges (Warrington and Atkin) expressly found no intention to create legal relations; Duke rested on absence of consideration and policy. None held that spouses can never contract.

## 5. Notice and assent online: Specht v. Netscape (2d Cir. 2002), C&M 178–183

Facts. Netscape offered free software. Five of six named plaintiffs downloaded Communicator, whose installation automatically displayed a scrollable license and required clicking "Yes"; that "clickwrap" license (defined in the casebook footnote as one requiring the user to click an icon to manifest assent before the product can be obtained or used) did not mention SmartDownload (C&M 179). SmartDownload was downloaded by clicking a "Download" button at the bottom of the screen. The only reference to its license was on the next screen, visible only by scrolling: "Please review and agree to the terms of the Netscape SmartDownload software license agreement before downloading and using the software"; clicking that invitation led to a separate "License & Support Agreements" page (C&M 180). Plaintiffs sued under federal privacy statutes; Netscape moved to compel arbitration under the license (C&M 179).

Law and holding. State law governs formation of an arbitration agreement; the district court applied California law and no party appealed that choice (C&M 180). California law: "an offeree, regardless of apparent manifestation of his consent, is not bound by inconspicuous contractual provisions of which he is unaware, contained in a document whose contractual nature is not obvious" (C&M 180). The duty to read applies when a party signs a contract, but not "when the writing does not appear to be a contract and the terms are not called to the attention of the recipient" (C&M 181). "Inquiry notice" (standard vocabulary: notice imputed to a person who has facts that would lead a prudent person to inquire) was argued and rejected: a scroll bar showing that more of the page remained "does not mean that they reasonably should have concluded that this portion contained a notice of license terms" (C&M 182). Plaintiffs' testimony that they were unaware was unrefuted (C&M 182). Holding: "Reasonably conspicuous notice of the existence of contract terms and unambiguous manifestation of assent to those terms by consumers are essential if electronic bargaining is to have integrity and credibility"; a reasonably prudent offeree would not have known of the terms before downloading, so downloading was not acceptance of the license terms (C&M 182). The court affirmed the denial of the motion to compel arbitration (C&M 182). The excerpt omits the full opinion's discussion of Fagan's separate download route, the Communicator clause's scope and direct-benefit estoppel against Specht.

The notes. Note 1 contrasts Lucy (was there any contract?) with Specht (no doubt there was a contract for the deal as a whole; the question was assent to a particular term), and asks whether it matters that the party disputing assent is the offeror in one case and the offeree in the other (C&M 182–183). Note 2 asks whether the offeree must actually read the terms, and compares Hancock v. AT&T (10th Cir. 2012), where a technician displayed the terms on a laptop and the buyer could not proceed without clicking "I Accept" or "I Reject" (C&M 183).

## 6. Restatement of Consumer Contracts § 2(a), (c), in each edition

The Selections print the April 2019 Tentative Draft; the professor's slide 16 and the Atlas use the 2024 Official Text. Section 2 keeps its number in both editions (crosswalk, OP-CT-RLCC).

2019 draft (Selections p. 477). (a) A standard contract term is adopted "if the consumer manifests assent to the transaction after receiving: (1) a reasonable notice of the standard contract term and of the intent to include the term as part of the consumer contract, and (2) a reasonable opportunity to review the standard contract term." (c) "If the consumer manifests assent to the transaction, a contract exists even if some of the standard contract terms are not adopted. In such case, the terms of the contract are those adopted under subsections (a) and (b), and, if the consumer elects, the unadopted standard terms, along with any terms supplied by law." Comment 2 separates assent to the transaction from adoption of terms; Comment 3 says assent is usually manifested by signing, paying or clicking "Purchase Now" or "I Agree" after reasonable notice that the act creates legal obligations, and "Absent such effective communication—for example, when the consumer may reasonably think that proceeding with the activity does not create a set of legally binding obligations—the consumer does not manifest assent to a consumer contract" (Selections pp. 478–479). Illustration 5: an underlined link "in large font and contrasting color" immediately beside "Purchase Now" adopts the linked terms (Selections p. 480).

2024 Official Text (Complete Restatement, PDF p. 26). (a) is the same in structure but begins "if the business demonstrates that the consumer manifested assent ... after receiving: (1) reasonable notice of the term and of the intent to include the term in the consumer contract, and (2) reasonable opportunity to review the term." The business therefore bears the burden of showing adoption; the 2019 text is silent on burden. (c): "a contract is not rendered unenforceable if some of the standard contract terms are not adopted"; its terms are the adopted terms, "if the consumer elects, any of the unadopted standard terms, along with terms, whether standard or not, to which both parties agreed, and terms supplied or incorporated by law." Comment 3 adds the sentence slide 16 quotes: "This Section does not address the formation of the contract or the assent to nonstandard terms. Once the contract is formed, this Section determines which standard contract terms are adopted." That sentence is not in the Selections' excerpt.

Applied to Specht, both editions separate the transaction from the term. If downloading manifested assent to a transaction, the arbitration term was not adopted for lack of reasonable notice, and § 2(c) keeps the contract without it, which matches Note 1's framing (C&M 182–183). A second reading is possible under 2019 Comment 3: a user who reasonably thought that downloading free software created no legal obligations may not have manifested assent to any consumer contract at all. The casebook does not resolve which reading applies.

## 7. What the Apple EULA illustrates (Supp. pp. 2–8)

The Licensed Application End User License Agreement shows a drafter reciting assent rather than securing it. It says the apps are "licensed, not sold", that "Your license ... is subject to your prior acceptance of this ... Standard EULA", and that "By using this software in connection with an iTunes Store account, you agree to the latest iTunes Store Terms and Conditions and Usage Rules", reached through a URL (Supp. pp. 2, 4). Those recitals do not by themselves establish adoption: under Specht and § 19(2), the act relied on as assent counts only if the user had reasonable notice that it signified agreement, and under RLCC § 2(a) each term needs reasonable notice and a reasonable opportunity to review. The clause binding the user to "the latest" iTunes terms also previews the modification problem of RLCC § 3 (Class 11). Other clauses show what is at stake in adoption: consent to collection of technical data (¶ b, a topic parallel to the privacy claims in Specht), automatic termination "without notice" (¶ c), a capitalized warranty disclaimer (¶ e, which connects to slide 11's "conspicuousness" factor), a $50 cap on liability (¶ f), and a California choice-of-law clause (¶ i), the same law that governed formation in Specht. The EULA has no arbitration clause.
