## 1. Definiteness and gap-filling

1. Restatement § 33(1): even an intended offer cannot be accepted so as to form a contract unless the terms are "reasonably certain." § 33(2): terms are reasonably certain "if they provide a basis for determining the existence of a breach and for giving an appropriate remedy." § 33(3): leaving terms open or uncertain "may show" that a manifestation was not intended as an offer or acceptance. The test is functional (can a court find breach and frame a remedy), not a checklist of terms. Slide 5 pairs this with the older formulation "parties must have reached agreement on all essential terms."
2. § 34 (Selections, not named in the prompt, but it completes § 33): terms may be reasonably certain though one party is empowered to select terms in performance; part performance may remove uncertainty; reliance may make a contractual remedy appropriate even though uncertainty is not removed.
3. C&M p. 352 gives the two functions of definiteness: a court must know the terms to decide whether there was a breach, and must know the promise to measure expectation damages. Varney v. Ditmars (N.Y. 1916, p. 352–353): a promise of "a fair share of my profits" was too indefinite; the amount was "pure conjecture."
4. Sources that can cure indefiniteness (p. 353): preliminary negotiations, prior communications, external sources such as regulations and trade usage, course of dealing and course of performance (UCC § 1-303), and terms implied by law (the reasonable-efforts term in Wood v. Lucy). "Good faith" and "reasonable efforts" are definite enough if their content can be fixed by an external standard.
5. UCC § 2-204(3): a contract for sale of goods does not fail for indefiniteness, even though terms are open, if (a) the parties intended to make a contract and (b) there is a reasonably certain basis for giving an appropriate remedy. Comment: "The more terms the parties leave open, the less likely it is that they have intended to conclude a binding agreement." Both conditions are required; § 2-204(3) does not supply intent.
6. UCC § 2-305(1): "if they so intend," parties can conclude a contract though the price is not settled; the price is then a reasonable price at the time for delivery if (a) nothing is said, (b) the price is left to be agreed and they fail to agree, or (c) the price is to be fixed by an agreed market or other standard set by a third person and it is not so set. § 2-305(4): if the parties intend not to be bound unless the price is fixed or agreed, and it is not, there is no contract (restitution of goods and price follows). Comment 1 rejects the formula that "an agreement to agree is unenforceable" for cases within (1). Comment 2: whether the parties intended to be bound is usually a question for the trier of fact. § 2-305 applies only to sales of goods.
7. Restatement § 352 (slide 5): damages are not recoverable beyond what the evidence permits to be established with reasonable certainty. § 362 (slide 5; quoted in Oglebay at p. 368): specific performance or an injunction will not be granted unless the terms are sufficiently certain to provide a basis for an order; comment b: the court first uses usage and terms supplied by law. C&M Note 4, p. 370: courts usually need more precision for specific relief than for damages, because disobedience is contempt.
8. Two kinds of incompleteness (pp. 361–362, Ayres & Gertner): missing terms (gaps), which courts often fill with default rules or implied terms; and failure to provide for future contingencies, which courts seldom repair. Reasons parties leave agreements incomplete (p. 362, list (a)–(e)); slide 10 asks which of these reasons a court should condone by enforcing. Output and requirements contracts and terms specified by one party (UCC § 2-311; Fairmount Glass, p. 361) are not indefinite merely because quantity or particulars are fixed later.
9. Relational contracting (pp. 362–363): Macaulay, Macneil, Goetz and Scott; repeat players and reputation make complete specification less necessary.
10. Price mechanisms (pp. 363–365): open price under § 2-305; price schedules; designated third party or arbitrator; escalator clauses; cost-plus; most-favored-nation clauses. A mechanism can still fail through an unanticipated contingency.

## 2. Sun Printing versus Oglebay

11. Sun Printing & Publishing Ass'n v. Remington Paper & Power Co. (N.Y. 1923, Cardozo, J., pp. 353–358). Contract for 16,000 tons of newsprint, 1,000 tons a month, Sept. 1919–Dec. 1920. Price fixed for 1919; for the balance, price "and length of terms for which such price shall apply" were to be agreed, the price "in no event to be higher than" the Canadian Export Paper Co. contract price to large consumers. Remington refused to deliver in 1920. Holding: no contract for the 1920 deliveries. Reasoning: two subjects were left open, price and the duration of each price term. If only price had been left open, the buyer might have been viewed as holding an option to buy at the Canadian maximum. The term was also open, and agreement on the term was "as essential to a completed contract as agreement in respect of price"; "While the term was unknown, the contract was inchoate." The result was "an agreement to agree." The court refused to supply a reasonable term or a monthly term: "We are not at liberty to revise while professing to construe." The seller's motive did not matter ("exercised its legal right"). The complaint could have been amended to allege that the Canadian price was fixed for the year. Dissent (Crane, J., with Hogan, J.): the parties intended a binding contract; the court should hold the seller to the Canadian price (as of Dec. 15, or month by month, or for a reasonable period). The case was decided before the UCC; § 2-305(1)(b) would now supply a reasonable price if the parties intended to be bound (Notes and slides ask whether the result changes). Notes: Cardozo's lecture (certainty in commercial life outweighs equity of the particular case); Macaulay's paper-industry anecdote; Cardozo and Crane "trading places" in Varney and Wood.
12. Oglebay Norton Co. v. Armco, Inc. (Ohio 1990, pp. 366–369). 1957 long-term shipping contract, extended four times to 2010; Oglebay invested heavily ($95 million program). Primary price mechanism: the regular net contract rate recognized by leading iron ore shippers (published in Skillings Mining Review). Secondary: if no such rate, the parties "shall mutually agree upon a rate," taking into consideration rates charged by leading independent vessel operators. After 1985 neither mechanism could work (no published rate; competitor data unavailable). Holdings: (1) whether the parties intended to be bound despite the failure of both mechanisms is a question of fact, and ample evidence (long and close relationship, joint ventures, interlocking directorates, Armco's stock ownership, dedication of Oglebay's fleet) supported the finding of intent; (2) because the parties intended to be bound, the trial court could set a reasonable rate, citing Restatement § 33 comment e and UCC § 2-305(1) by analogy (the contract is for shipping services, not goods), basing the rate on course of dealing, detriment to the parties and market comparisons; the trial court set $6.00 plus $0.25 for self-unloading vessels, which the court of appeals and the opinion describe as $6.25; (3) the trial court could retain equitable jurisdiction and order the parties to negotiate and, failing agreement, to mediate each season through 2010, as specific performance, because long-term damages would be too speculative (§ 362; § 360(a) comment b), and the order neither added to nor detracted from the parties' obligations.
13. The distinction: in Sun Printing the parties left a term open with no standard to fix it and the court found no intent to be bound absent agreement on duration; in Oglebay the parties had a mechanism that failed after decades of performance, and the trier of fact found intent to be bound despite the failure. The decisive variable is the parties' intent to be bound (UCC § 2-305(1) "if they so intend" and § 2-305(4)), shown by the relationship and performance; a court can fill a price gap only after that finding. Note 2, p. 369–370 asks whether the intent finding would differ if the price failure had occurred at formation. Note 3: express default pricing (PSI Energy v. Exxon Coal: the seller's last offer controls absent a valid competing firm offer).

## 3. Tribune Type I and Type II, and the duty to negotiate in good faith

14. Teachers Ins. & Annuity Ass'n v. Tribune Co., 670 F. Supp. 491 (S.D.N.Y. 1987) (Leval, J.). The casebook does not print the opinion; it describes the two types through the Second Circuit's summary in Adjustrite Systems v. GAB Business Services, 145 F.3d 543, 548 (2d Cir. 1998) (p. 190, Note 1). Slide 14 quotes Tribune itself. The facts of Tribune (TIAA's loan commitment to Tribune, Tribune's refusal to close after an accounting change) are not in the assigned excerpt.
15. Type I: parties agree on all points that require negotiation but intend to memorialize in a more formal document; "fully binding"; "preliminary only in form"; binds both sides to the ultimate contractual objective. Slide 14 adds "including the agreement to be bound" and "The second stage is not necessary; it is merely considered desirable."
16. Type II ("binding preliminary commitment"): parties agree on certain major terms but leave other terms open; they accept a mutual commitment to negotiate the open issues in good faith "within the agreed framework." No right to demand performance of the transaction; if no final contract is reached, the parties may abandon the deal "as long as they have made a good faith effort to close the deal and have not insisted on conditions that do not conform to the preliminary writing."
17. Slide 15 factors: against binding force, an express disclaimer of intent to be bound, indefiniteness, a complex transaction; for binding force, formality, part performance, reliance. Slide 13: background rules are "No general duty to negotiate in good faith" and "Recovery in restitution may be available for benefits conferred."
18. p. 189 context and p. 190 Note 2: deal breakers (A/S Apothekernes; Budget Marketing); p. 190–191 Note 3: Pennzoil v. Texaco. Agreement to negotiate versus agreement to agree (Keystone Land v. Xerox, p. 189 context).
19. Meaning of bad faith (p. 352, Note 2, PSI Energy, Easterbrook, J.): the obligation "nixes trickery and certain forms of obduracy," but does not require revealing strategy or reservation price, disclosing every useful fact, or refraining from taking advantage of opportunities. Open questions: reneging on an agreed term; negotiating with others; terminating without warning to contract with another (as in Cyberchron).
20. Remedy for breach of an agreement to negotiate (pp. 351–352, Note 1): reliance; possibly lost opportunities; expectation is conventionally refused because the court cannot know what agreement would have been reached (Goodstein, N.Y. 1992), but Venture Associates v. Zenith (7th Cir. 1996, Posner, J.) says that if the plaintiff proves a final contract would have been made but for the bad faith, the lost benefit is recoverable as consequential damages, subject to the practical difficulty of proof. A court may even grant specific performance of a promise to negotiate (Oglebay, cross-referenced at p. 352).

## 4. Restitution and misrepresentation in failed negotiations (pp. 326–327)

21. Restitution: benefit conferred during negotiations may have to be restored (down payment on land). Services rarely recovered: Songbird Jet v. Amax (S.D.N.Y. 1984) (no unjust enrichment from "the common grist of negotiations"; services designed to protect the claimant's own interests); Precision Testing v. Kenyon (S.D.N.Y. 1986) (recovery where services were designed to benefit the other party). Restitution leaves reliance that produced no benefit uncompensated.
22. Misrepresentation: a party may not misrepresent its intention to come to terms; liability includes reliance losses (Markov v. ABC Transfer, Wash. 1969: lessor fraudulently promised to renew and negotiate rent in good faith while secretly selling; damages included moving costs and profits lost when the main customer left). Slide 13 lists misrepresentation and promissory fraud as torts.
23. p. 327: the next four cases (Hoffman, Dixon, Cyberchron, Channel) suggest "more fruitful bases" than restitution or misrepresentation; "there appear to be two" doctrinal bases (promissory estoppel and express contract). In none did the parties agree to the terms of a contract.

## 5. Promissory estoppel in negotiations

24. Dixon v. Wells Fargo, N.A. (D. Mass. 2011, Young, J., pp. 333–340). On motion to dismiss. Facts alleged: Wells Fargo orally agreed that the Dixons would take the steps necessary for a loan modification, told them to stop making payments, and requested financial information, which they supplied; Wells Fargo then started foreclosure. Massachusetts treats reliance-based recovery as contract, requiring all elements but consideration, including a promise that is a commitment and definite. Wells Fargo argued an agreement to agree is unenforceable. The Dixons disclaimed specific performance of a modification; they sought enforcement only of a promise to consider them for modification. The court characterized it as closer to an agreement to negotiate, and noted Massachusetts treats agreements to negotiate as unenforceable variants of agreements to agree. Despite indefiniteness, Massachusetts cases enforce an indefinite promise made during preliminary negotiations where the promisor's words or conduct "were designed to take advantage of the promisee" ("dangled the other side on a string"; Greenstein; Cohoon); the promisor need not have acted fraudulently or in bad faith. Wells Fargo should have known and must have intended that the Dixons would default; its promise implied an undertaking not to foreclose on facts created solely by that promise. Holding: the complaint states a promissory estoppel claim; if proved, damages "will be confined to the value of their expenditures in reliance." The court said the concern about reading in a duty to bargain in good faith is minimized by limiting recovery to reliance; the Dixons did not allege a good-faith duty, so the court did not address it; the existing mortgagor-mortgagee relationship supported the conclusion. Note 1: two dozen other 2011 district courts rejected similar homeowner claims. Note 2: the court suggested restoring the loan to non-default status, with missed payments added to the balance.
25. Cyberchron Corp. v. Calldata Systems Development, Inc. (2d Cir. 1995, Mahoney, J., pp. 340–345). Negotiations over rugged computer equipment for a Marine Corps program; no agreement on weight and weight penalties, which the district court held were essential, so no contract (and no quantum meruit, for lack of benefit). New York promissory estoppel: clear and unambiguous promise; reasonable and foreseeable reliance; injury, sometimes required to be "unconscionable"; some courts add that enforcement must be necessary to avoid injustice. From mid-July 1990, Grumman's Wilhelm told Cyberchron to "proceed" as if the weights were approved and definitization would come later, refused to put it in writing, and pressed for performance while secretly negotiating with Codar; it then terminated and bought heavier, inferior equipment. Holding: affirmed promissory estoppel liability from mid-July onward; no recovery for expenses before any promise sufficient to support the claim; reliance damages for labor and materials ($162,824.19); vacated and remanded to allow reasonable overhead (with a demonstrable history of ongoing operations, allocated under standard cost accounting) and shutdown costs incurred in reliance. Cyberchron did not seek benefit-of-the-bargain damages on appeal. Note 1 asks the scope of the enforced promise and whether bad faith had to be proved. Note 2: UNIDROIT art. 2.15(2) (liability for negotiating or breaking off in bad faith); American courts protect freedom to negotiate without precontractual liability imposed by law; a party entering negotiations bears the risk of loss if the other breaks off (pp. 344–345).
26. Context, not assigned: Hoffman v. Red Owl (Wis. 1965, pp. 328–331): § 90 does not require a promise so definite as to be an offer; reliance damages. Notes on Green Industries, Grouse, Kajima (p. 332–333; the end of the Kajima note on p. 333 is assigned: promissory estoppel used to further public policy; lost profits barred).

## 6. Express agreement to negotiate: letters of intent

27. Channel Home Centers v. Grossman (3d Cir. 1986, Becker, J., pp. 345–352), Pennsylvania law. Grossman, acquiring a mall and seeking financing, asked Channel for a letter of intent he could show to banks. Channel's detailed letter of intent stated: "To induce the Tenant to proceed with the leasing of the Store, you will withdraw the Store from the rental market, and only negotiate the above described leasing transaction to completion." Grossman signed. Lease execution was subject to stated contingencies (Grace approval, title, permits). Both sides worked toward the lease; Grossman then negotiated with competitor Mr. Good Buys, terminated citing a 30-day limit not in the letter, and leased to Mr. Good Buys at higher rent. Channel did not claim the letter bound the parties to a lease; it claimed a binding agreement to negotiate in good faith. Holding: an agreement to negotiate in good faith, if otherwise meeting the requisites of a contract, is enforceable; test is (1) intent to be bound, (2) terms sufficiently definite, (3) consideration. The record would support findings of intent (letter plus conduct), definiteness (the promise read with the detailed letter covering most significant lease terms is definite enough "to be specifically enforced"), and consideration (Channel's signing and tender of the letter was a bargained-for benefit to Grossman, who wanted it for financing). Reversed and remanded for trial: the evidence permits but does not require a finding of intent; whether there was an oral 30-day limit must be decided; if none, a reasonable time applies. The court did not reach promissory estoppel (footnote). Good faith in bargaining differs from the § 205 duty of good faith in performance and enforcement (footnote 2).
28. Measure of damages for Channel (Note 1): expectation, reliance, or lost opportunities; Goodstein versus Venture Associates.

## 7. French Civil Code art. 1112 (supplement p. 21; slide 17)

29. First paragraph: the commencement, continuation and breaking-off of precontractual negotiations are "free from control," but "must mandatorily satisfy the requirements of good faith." So French law imposes a general good-faith duty in negotiations without any agreement to negotiate; the common-law starting point is the opposite (slide 13: no general duty; C&M pp. 344–345).
30. Second paragraph: for fault during negotiations, reparation "is not calculated so as to compensate the loss of benefits which were expected from the contract that was not concluded." The recoverable loss excludes the expected benefits of the unconcluded contract (expectation); the text does not itself say what is recoverable, but excludes that head. Comparison: this matches the reliance limit in Dixon and Cyberchron, and departs from Venture Associates' willingness to award the lost bargain where causation is proved. Slide 16 asks for each case "Would the result be different under French law?"

## 8. Distinctions a student must be able to draw

- Agreement to agree (no obligation; Sun Printing; Dixon's quotation of Rosenfield) versus agreement to negotiate (process obligation; Keystone; Channel; Tribune II).
- Tribune I (bound to the deal) versus Tribune II (bound only to negotiate in good faith within the framework; may walk away after good-faith effort).
- Contract-based liability for failed negotiations (Channel: express promise, consideration) versus promissory estoppel (Dixon, Cyberchron: no consideration, reliance-limited recovery) versus restitution (benefit conferred) versus misrepresentation (reliance including consequential losses).
- Price left open with no intent to be bound absent agreement (Sun Printing; § 2-305(4)) versus intent to be bound with a failed mechanism (Oglebay; § 2-305(1)(b)–(c)).
- Goods (UCC §§ 2-204(3), 2-305 apply directly) versus services or shipping (Oglebay applies § 2-305 by analogy through § 33 comment e).
- Definiteness for damages (§ 33(2), § 352) versus heightened definiteness for specific relief (§ 362; Oglebay Note 4).
- Scope of the enforced promise in promissory estoppel: Cyberchron recovers only from mid-July; Dixon's promise was to consider, not to modify.
- Common-law freedom from precontractual liability (absent agreement, estoppel, fraud) versus French art. 1112's mandatory good faith with a ban on recovering the expected benefits.
