## 1. The two families of agreed remedies

1. Parties may agree in advance on what happens after breach in two different ways. They may limit or replace the remedies the law supplies (for example, a buyer's remedies are limited to repair, replacement, or refund), or they may fix a sum or formula for money damages ("liquidated damages"). They may not stipulate a punishment; a clause that does so is a "penalty" and is unenforceable. Punitive damages are never available by agreement. (C&M 1043 intro; Restatement § 356 cmt. a; § 355.)
2. A limitation of remedy and a liquidated-damages clause are tested by different rules. A sales limitation is tested under UCC § 2-719 (exclusivity, failure of essential purpose, unconscionability of a consequential-damages exclusion). A stipulated sum is tested under § 2-718(1) (sales) or Restatement § 356(1) (everything else) for reasonableness. (§ 2-719(1) opening words make it "subject to" § 2-718.)

## 2. Limitation of remedy under UCC § 2-719

3. The agreement may add to or substitute for the Code's remedies, for example by limiting the buyer to return and refund or to repair and replacement (§ 2-719(1)(a)).
4. An agreed remedy is optional unless the parties expressly agree that it is exclusive (§ 2-719(1)(b)). Comment 2 presumes that remedy clauses are cumulative and requires exclusivity to be "clearly expressed."
5. In Figgie (4th Cir. 1999, South Carolina UCC), proved and unrebutted trade usage supplied the exclusive remedy of repair, replacement, or return (C&M 1045, referring back to 593–596).
6. Failure of essential purpose. Where circumstances cause an exclusive or limited remedy to fail of its essential purpose, the buyer may use the Code's ordinary remedies (§ 2-719(2)). The test quoted in Figgie from Bishop Logging and § 2-719 cmt. 1: an apparently fair and reasonable clause that, because of circumstances, fails in its purpose or deprives either party of the substantial value of the bargain must give way (C&M 1045). Comment 1 adds that a sales contract must leave "at least minimum adequate remedies" and "a fair quantum of remedy."
7. Figgie's holding on this point: a repair-or-replacement remedy can fail when repair is unsuccessful, but this buyer's remedy was repair, replacement, or return, and the seller first tried repair and then took back the machine and refunded the price. That sequence was what § 2-719(1)(a) and the agreement contemplated. A seller does not give up the refund alternative by trying repair first, and there was no evidence that the return-and-refund remedy, once invoked, failed (C&M 1045–1046). The decisive fact is that the refund alternative was actually carried out; the case does not decide what happens if the seller refuses to refund.
8. Consequential-damage exclusions. Consequential damages may be limited or excluded unless the limitation is unconscionable. Limiting consequential damages for injury to the person from consumer goods is prima facie unconscionable; limiting damages where the loss is commercial is not (§ 2-719(3)). Comment 3 calls such terms an allocation of unknown or undeterminable risks.
9. Notes after Figgie (C&M 1046): § 2-719(3) speaks only of consequential damages, so the notes ask whether incidental damages (the buyer's expenses of handling, inspecting, transporting, or covering, § 2-715(1)) are tested by the same standard. Anderson explains that consequential exposure (usually lost profits) is open-ended and depends on the buyer's operations, while direct damages are capped by contract or market price. Kearney & Trecker (N.J. 1987) calls the commercial seller's exclusion "a beneficial risk-allocation device." The notes ask whether that reasoning applies with equal force to consumers.

## 3. Liquidated damages versus penalties

10. Restatement § 356(1): damages may be liquidated "only at an amount that is reasonable in the light of the anticipated or actual loss caused by the breach and the difficulties of proof of loss." An unreasonably large amount is unenforceable on public-policy grounds. The rest of the agreement stays enforceable and ordinary damages apply (cmt. a; § 184(1); Illus. 1).
11. Comment b treats the two factors as combining on a sliding scale. The amount is reasonable if it approximates the actual loss from this breach (even if it would not fit other breaches, Illus. 2) or the loss anticipated at formation (even if it does not match actual loss, Illus. 3). The harder loss is to prove, the more latitude; the easier, the less. If it is clear that no loss at all occurred, a substantial sum is a penalty (Illus. 4: race track could not open anyway).
12. UCC § 2-718(1) adds a third factor, "the inconvenience or non-feasibility of otherwise obtaining an adequate remedy," and says an unreasonably large term is "void as a penalty." Section 2-718(2)–(3) separately gives a breaching buyer restitution of payments above the seller's valid liquidated damages or, absent such a term, above the lesser of 20% of the buyer's total performance or $500, subject to offsets.
13. Labels do not control (§ 356 cmt. c; Gustafson, Wasserman's). A sum that is unreasonably small is outside § 356 but may be refused as unconscionable under § 208 (cmts. a, d). Penalties in bonds are enforced only up to the loss caused (§ 356(2), cmt. e, Illus. 6).
14. Disguised penalties. Courts look to substance when a clause is drafted as an alternative performance, a discount for prompt performance, or a valuation; the relative value of the alternatives may be decisive (§ 356 cmt. c, Illus. 5). Problem 2 at C&M 1049 (penalty, liquidated damages, or an option to deliver or pay $10,000 for a machine worth $1,000 more than the price) and Note 3 at C&M 1063 (a bonus for early completion; a promise not to compete "or, in the alternative," pay $100,000) test this. Prenalta (10th Cir. 1991), quoted in Note 2 at C&M 1055, treats a take-or-pay "pay" option as an alternative performance rather than liquidated damages.

## 4. The principal cases

15. Dave Gustafson & Co. v. State (S.D. 1968), C&M 1047–1048. A graduated per-day delay charge ($210 a day for a contract between $500,000 and $1,000,000; 67 days; $14,070) was liquidated damages. South Dakota's formulation: a stipulated sum "will ordinarily be sustained if it appears that at the time the contract was made the damages in the event of a breach will be incapable or very difficult of accurate estimation, that there was a reasonable endeavor by the parties as stated to fix fair compensation, and that the amount stipulated bears a reasonable relation to probable damage and not disproportionate to any damages reasonably to be anticipated." Reasons: highway-delay damages are "impossible of measurement"; the graduated scale shows an endeavor to fix fair compensation; a larger project involves more loss and each day adds to it. The old road stayed open, which raised the question of actual loss; the excerpt does not discuss actual loss beyond that.
16. Gustafson Problem 1 (C&M 1049): if a bridge built by another contractor was also closed for the same 67 days, so that the highway could not have been used anyway, would the result change? The editors cite Massman (5th Cir. 1945) against Sun Ship (9th Cir. 1986) and Southwest Engineering (8th Cir. 1965), signalling a split over whether actual absence of loss defeats a clause valid at formation.
17. Lake River Corp. v. Carborundum Co. (7th Cir. 1985, Illinois law), C&M 1049–1054. The minimum-guarantee formula (full contract price for the guaranteed 22,500 tons minus what had been paid) was a penalty. Illinois test: a reasonable estimate at contracting of likely damages, with the need for estimation shown by the likely difficulty of measuring actual damages after breach; a penalty if damages would be easy to determine or if the estimate greatly exceeds a reasonable upper estimate; doubtful cases are resolved toward penalty. The formula was "invariant to the gravity of the breach," and because most of Lake River's costs were saved on breach and the formula ignored that saving, it always gave more than lost profits (about 400% of expected profit for an immediate breach, 130% near the end). Mitigation did not cure the defect. Remedy: Lake River gets common-law damages, the unpaid $241,000 minus the variable costs saved on the 45% never bagged, citing § 356 cmt. a; remand (C&M 1054, image).
18. Posner's critique (C&M 1051–1052). He would question, if free to do so, refusing to enforce penalties against substantial corporations: a penalty is an earnest of performance that makes a promise credible; arguments about creditors, bankruptcy, and the business cycle are "makeweights"; the efficient-breach argument (a penalty may deter a breach that would leave the promisee, once compensated, no worse off and the promisor better off) overlooks that competent parties weigh that cost when choosing the clause. He nonetheless applied Illinois law because a federal court in diversity must not import its own policy views. The bracketed lien holding (C&M 1051) is that Lake River had no lien for services never performed.
19. Notes after Lake River: Note 1 (whether a promisor who bargained for a lower price by accepting a high stipulated sum is unjustly enriched when the court refuses enforcement; Elliott on high highway assessments raising bid prices); Note 2 (take-or-pay); Note 3 (Cal. Civ. Code § 1671: a clause is valid unless the challenger shows it was unreasonable at formation; not applicable against consumers); Note 4 (Cellphone Termination Fee Cases: a flat $150–$200 fee charged regardless of when the customer terminated was a penalty; Dodd-Frank limits on mortgage prepayment penalties).
20. Wasserman's Inc. v. Township of Middletown (N.J. 1994), C&M 1056–1062. The lease's cancellation clause required improvement reimbursement plus 25% of one year's average gross receipts. Reasonableness under the totality of the circumstances is the single test; difficulty of estimating damages is an element of reasonableness rather than an independent test. The modern trend assesses reasonableness either at formation or at breach, and actual damages bear on whether the parties' prediction was reasonable. Stipulated-damages clauses in commercial contracts between parties of comparable bargaining power are presumptively reasonable, and the challenger bears the burden of proving unreasonableness (production and persuasion, C&M 1062). The court did not reach consumer contracts. A clause is unreasonable if it does more than compensate for approximate actual damages; gross receipts ignore ordinary and breach-specific expenses, and Jo-Ro's tax returns ($3,649, $414, then a $323 loss) suggested unreasonableness of $290,310.18 "to the extent" they reflected actual results. Enforceability is a question of law that may depend on underlying facts. Disposition: lease enforceable; $55,748.27 renovation award affirmed; gross-receipts clause remanded with listed factors (gross receipts regardless of cancellation date, one year's average instead of a figure for each remaining year, the parties' reasons, mitigation, fair market rent and replacement space) (C&M 1062, image).
21. Notes after Wasserman's (C&M 1062–1063): Note 1 (arguments on remand; the tenants lost twelve years of use at $5,500 a year of property worth about $600,000). Note 2 (second look versus single look: § 2-718's "anticipated or actual harm" against § 2A-504(1)'s "anticipated harm" only; Kelly v. Marx (Mass. 1999) adopted the single look and let a house seller keep a 5% deposit although the seller suffered no loss). Note 3 (subterfuge and alternative performances). Note 4 (an arbitration award upholding a clause binds even if a court might call it a penalty, Associated General Contractors (N.Y. 1975)).
22. Problems at C&M 1063–1064: Circle B v. Steinke (a contract that calls a $100-a-day delay charge a "penalty," with the owner claiming the whole balance plus $6,000); Turner-Schraeter (100% forfeiture for a consumer travel cancellation 13 days before departure); Yockey v. Horn (a $50,000 clause for a law associate's public criticism where the firm suffered no harm and even gained clients).

## 5. Swiss law (supplement 129–138; machine translations)

23. BGE 114 II 264 (June 22, 1988), supp. 129–130. Under Art. 163 OR the parties may set a contractual penalty at any amount, but the judge must reduce an excessive penalty in his or her discretion. Restraint is required because of freedom of contract and good faith; intervention is justified only when the amount exceeds what is reasonable and compatible with justice and equity, in particular where there is a gross disproportion between the amount and the creditor's interest in full enforcement. Relevant circumstances: nature and duration of the contract, severity of fault and breach, the creditor's interest in compliance, the parties' economic situation (especially the obligor's), dependence, and business experience; reduction is more justifiable for an employee or weaker party than between equal, business-savvy parties. Timing: freedom-of-contract limits look at formation, but a penalty's justification can only be assessed after breach. A penalty is not excessive merely because it exceeds the damages the creditor could claim; "the maximum possible damage," not the actual damage, indicates excessiveness. The penalty is an independent obligation due even without damage (Art. 161(1) OR). The debtor must assert and prove the grounds for reduction.
24. Swiss Federal Supreme Court, September 24, 2024, supp. 130–138. Clause 5.3 of a December 2020 purchase agreement set CHF 100,000 for each violation of a name and trademark non-competition clause, with permitted informational exceptions. A letter to candidates went beyond the necessary notice (takeover of the "operational business" in two places, use of the logo and company name, and "leading headhunter" advertising). The lower court found at least conditional intent, a very minor single-occasion breach with no risk of recurrence, and no substantiated damage or risk of damage; it reduced the penalty to CHF 10,000, keeping some penalty because the underlying transaction was financially significant and a penalty always also serves a punitive function, and because the defendant did not ask for zero. The Federal Supreme Court upheld this: the gross-disproportion test is applied to the creditor's interest at the time of breach; trademarks, mutual restrictions, and business experience did not justify CHF 50,000–100,000; absence of damage may be considered in fixing the amount even though damage is not required for the penalty to fall due; CHF 1,000 urged by the defendant was not required.
25. Comparison. In the U.S. cases a penalty is unenforceable and the victim recovers ordinary compensatory damages (Lake River; § 356 cmt. a). Swiss law treats the penalty as valid and due without damage, and the court cuts an excessive amount down while allowing the reduced sum to retain a punitive function. The closest U.S. analog in the assigned selections is § 356(2), which enforces a bond penalty up to the actual loss.
