## 1. Framing (pp. 744–748)

- Section 5 shifts from protecting one party against the other to protecting "the public at large against imposition by both parties" (744).
- Two categories (745). (i) "Illegal contracts": contracts that violate specific criminal laws; a court "will not generally enforce" them; criminal statutes rarely address enforceability, except usury and gambling statutes, which typically declare contracts "void." (ii) No explicit prohibition: courts derive policy from related legislation even when it is silent on enforceability, or from their own sense of social or legal norms, and sometimes find insufficient authority, so performance is not excused.
- Richardson v. Mellish (1824), Burrough J.: public policy is "a very unruly horse" (745).
- Wagenseller (Ariz. 1985): constitution and statutes embody public policy, but policy is not found only there and not every statutory statement is policy; quoting Lucas v. Brown & Root (8th Cir. 1984): courts are also sources, "always subject to legislative correction," with less freedom as legislation occupies a field (745–746).
- Positive public policy can also be implemented through contract (procurement preferences, Section 508 accessibility) and privatization (746).
- Note 1 (746): Friedman's empirical study: defenses appealing to specific statutes or regulations were twice as successful as those appealing to general notions of public policy.
- Note 2 (747): Hubner v. Spring Valley Equestrian Ctr. (N.J. 2010): Equine Activities Act; the N.J. Supreme Court reversed the grant of summary judgment, holding the claim fell within the Act's explicit exceptions, and an exculpatory agreement used to escape liability the Act authorizes (5:15-9) is denied enforcement "absent further direction from the Legislature."
- Problem (747–748), Saints bounty program: teaches (a) the contractual structure is a unilateral contract (promise of $1,500 for a "knockout," $1,000 for a "cart-off," accepted by performance); (b) a promise made in return for, or conditioned on, tortious or criminal conduct is unenforceable because of its tendency to induce the conduct (§ 178 cmt d; § 192); (c) sources of policy include criminal and tort law and a private source, the NFL CBA's "Non-Contract Bonuses" ban on payment for on-field misconduct. The casebook poses questions; it gives no answer.
- (A) intro (748): two contrasting positions: Bartlett v. Vinor (1692), Lord Holt: every contract about a matter prohibited by statute is void even if the statute only imposes a penalty; Gellhorn (1936): judges void only contracts whose enforcement, after studying the policy, "will disserve the general interest as it has been indicated by the legislature." Questions framed: performance that contributes to an illegal act but is not itself prohibited; contracts that are the product of bribery or antitrust conspiracy; statutes with their own penalties.

## 2. Restatement § 178 balancing (selections pp. 87–90; quoted at C&M 750, 752, 763, 765/768)

- § 178(1): a term is unenforceable on grounds of public policy if (a) legislation so provides, or (b) "the interest in its enforcement is **clearly outweighed** in the circumstances by a public policy against the enforcement of such terms."
- § 178(2) (interest in enforcement): (a) the parties' justified expectations; (b) any forfeiture that would result if enforcement were denied; (c) any special public interest in enforcement of the particular term.
- § 178(3) (policy against enforcement): (a) strength of the policy as manifested by legislation or judicial decisions; (b) likelihood that refusal to enforce will further the policy; (c) seriousness of any misconduct and extent to which it was deliberate; (d) directness of the connection between the misconduct and the term.
- Cmt b: legislation rarely declares terms unenforceable; usually the court derives policy from its own perception of public welfare or from related legislation (§ 179). Grave cases (serious crime or tort) are plain; trivial contraventions plainly do not preclude enforcement; in doubtful cases, "careful balancing." Enforcement is denied "only if the factors that argue against enforcement clearly outweigh" the interest in expectations, the abhorrence of unjust enrichment, and any public interest in enforcement.
- Cmt d: a promise may be unenforceable because of its *tendency* to induce offensive conduct, even if it does not actually induce it; misconduct "so remote or collateral" that refusal to enforce will not deter it does not bar enforcement.
- Cmt e: reliance (preparation or performance) strengthens the interest in enforcement; serious or deliberate misconduct defeats the promisee's claim to protection of expectations; possibility of restitution is relevant.
- Illus. 12 (from Sirkin): seller bribes buyer's purchasing agent; buyer's promise to pay is unenforceable (§ 193). C&M 761.
- Blossom quotes only the § 178(3) factors (752). Blossom's paraphrase of the balancing ("interest in the enforcement of the particular promise against the policy against enforcement") is cmt b. Note 1 at 763 points to § 178(2)(b) forfeiture.

## 3. Illegality as a defense, and its limits

- **Who may raise it.** A party to an illegal agreement may set up the illegality as a defense "even though the party may be alleging his or her own turpitude." Early Detection Center v. Wilson (Kan. 1991), quoted at 753 and 761. The court "will not aid either party to an illegal agreement" (761).
- **In pari delicto** (753, Blossom note 1): in circumstances of equal fault, the defendant's position is the stronger (in pari delicto potior est conditio defendantis); parties are left as the court found them. The casebook says the Blossom court "in effect" so found, without using the phrase. Claimants often argue unequal culpability.
- **Restitution.** General rule: illegality precludes enforcement and also restitution (763 note 1; § 197). Exceptions: § 197 disproportionate forfeiture (cmt b; "if the claimant has threatened grave social harm, no forfeiture will be disproportionate"), reformulated in R3RUE § 32(2) (753); § 198 excusable ignorance or not equally in the wrong; § 199 withdrawal before improper purpose achieved, or ending a continuing situation contrary to public interest, for a party who did not engage in serious misconduct. § 197 illus. 2: the bribing seller has no restitution claim against the buyer.
- **Clean hands** (753–754): North Pacific Lumber v. Oliver (Or. 1979): the misconduct "must bear a certain kind of relationship to the subject matter of the suit"; employer's eavesdropping unconnected to the employee's work, so no bar; improper-profit scheme connected; because the employee "participated in the ill-gotten gains," the court reversed his attorney's-fee award. Affirmed in part, reversed in part.
- **Improper intended use, § 182** (selections p. 90). If the promisee has substantially performed, the promisor's intended improper use does not preclude enforcement *unless* the promisee (a) acted for the purpose of furthering the improper use, **or** (b) knew of the use and the use involves grave social harm. Blossom (750) paraphrases only prong (a).
- **Degrees of involvement** (756–757): Graves v. Johnson (Mass. 1901) (Holmes): seller may exhibit "understood indifference"; a lawful sale is not connected with the buyer's later unlawful conduct "by the mere fact that the seller correctly divines the buyer's unlawful intent"; connection too remote absent communicated desire to cooperate. Fineman v. Faulkner (N.C. 1917): credit sale of phonograph to a known prostitute enforceable; seller "did not sell to aid" any illegal purpose. Phillips v. Hooker (N.C. 1867).
- **Direct connection** (761–762): McConnell v. Commonwealth Pictures (N.Y. 1960): agent who obtained the contract by bribing the producer's agent $10,000 cannot recover his commission from an innocent distributor, even though he could prove a prima facie case without exposing the bribe; "There must at least be a direct connection between the illegal transaction and the obligation sued upon"; "not every minor wrongdoing" in performance bars recovery.
- **Commercial bribery** (761): Sirkin v. Fourteenth St. Store (App. Div. 1908): seller who bribed the buyer's purchasing agent denied recovery of the price; guidance taken from a misdemeanor statute (fine up to $500, up to a year); two dissenters objected to judicially adding punishment. Problem: the buyer keeps the goods without paying (761); the bribed agent is accountable to the employer (762); disgorgement to the state proposed but not adopted in the United States (762).
- **Official bribery** (757–760): Campbell County v. Howard & Lee (Va. 1922): contract to pay for influencing legislation by means other than argument to legislators' reason is illegal; World Duty Free v. Kenya (ICSID 2006): $2 million to President Moi; claims "based on contracts of corruption or on contract obtained by corruption cannot be upheld"; Davis calls it "zero tolerance"; Adler v. Nigeria (9th Cir. 2000); FCPA (1977) antibribery and accounting provisions, criminal and civil; State v. Strickland (Md. App. 1979): briber cannot recover the bribe; "left where they are found, to stew in their own juice"; L-3 Communications v. United States (Fed. Cl. 2007): bid-preparation costs claim stated after Druyun.
- **Licensing** (762–764): Sirkin's dictum that an unlicensed party may never recover "goes too far": when the licensing purpose is raising revenue rather than protecting public welfare, want of a license is generally not a bar (§ 181: unenforceable only if the requirement has a regulatory purpose *and* the interest in enforcement is clearly outweighed). Trade-Winds v. Stewart (E.D. La. 2008): unlicensed mold remediator ($750,000 estimate, $9,000,000 invoice, $7,500,000 paid) lost on summary judgment; statute's stated protective purpose; no emergency executive suspension had been issued. Town Planning & Eng'g v. Amesbury (Mass. 1976): uncertified engineer recovered the contract price on a "vector of considerations," including whether the characteristics that gave the act its value were the same as those that made it a violation (Gardner). Kansas City Community Center (8th Cir. 1992) (architect) is a "compare" cite; In re Lake Providence distinguishes claims as contractor from claims as joint venturer.

## 4. Blossom Farm Products Co. v. Kasson Cheese Co. (Wis. Ct. App. 1986), C&M 748–753

- Facts: Blossom (salesman Podell), sole U.S. distributor of PTX's Isokappacase, sold it to Kasson from August 1981 to February 13, 1984; sued on open account for $138,306 for the last purchase (748–749). Isokappacase was labeled a starter medium and bacteriophage preventive but, at over 75% caseins, was characteristically a yield enhancer (749). Kasson put it directly into cheese milk and sold the product as real mozzarella and provolone without the imitation/analog labeling required by the federal standards of identity, which Wisconsin adopted (secs. 97.03, 97.09) (749–750 and n.2). Real-cheese price $1.40/lb versus 70¢ as analog (n.6). Kasson ordered about one hundred times the starter quantity; as a starter it would have needed nearly a billion pounds of milk per month against its roughly 60 million (751 & n.7). 2,699,200 pounds shipped; Blossom grossed over $5,000,000 (n.4). Kasson stopped about three weeks after a letter from the Wisconsin Department of Agriculture (n.5).
- Procedure: trial court held the contract "illegal and unenforceable" because both parties knew of and benefited from the illegal use (749). Court of Appeals affirmed, but "restrict[ed] our holding to whether the contract is unenforceable," following §§ 178 and 182, which speak of unenforceability, not illegality (750).
- Sale itself legal: use of a yield enhancer is legal if the end product is labeled as imitation; the use becomes improper only with intent to sell as real cheese (751). Even with Kasson's improper purpose, enforcement is not precluded "without knowledgeable involvement by Blossom" (751).
- Evidence: Podell knew of yield-enhancer use from volumes; Silverman (PTX president) solved a clotting problem at Kasson, suggested a pump, told Kasson's president the product would not be real cheese, inferred mislabeling and told Podell (751–752). Kasson's president never directly told them about the labeling and thought it a "grey area" (752). The court found "direct knowledge" of the yield-enhancer use and "tacit knowledge" of the misbranding; they "knew or should have known" because Kasson could not have survived selling at imitation prices (752).
- Holding (753): the state legislation enforces the policy of distinguishing imitation from real cheese; Podell "tacitly knew" of the misbranding "because of the economics of the situation"; Blossom "chose to overlook it and continued to supply volume shipments," a "course of dealing which facilitated Kasson in its improper conduct" and benefited both; "the transaction which anticipated such improper conduct is unenforceable." Affirmed.
- Precise teaching: the seller of a lawful product who has fully performed loses its price claim only because of knowledgeable involvement, shown here by continued volume sales with knowledge plus facilitation (technical help, a course of dealing), which the court treats as "acting for the purpose" of furthering the improper use under § 182(a) and cmt b. Mere knowledge, under Graves and Fineman, is not enough. The court did not rely on § 182(b) (knowledge plus grave social harm). The result is no recovery of the $138,306; Kasson keeps the goods.

## 5. X.L.O. Concrete Corp. v. Rivergate Corp. (N.Y. 1994), C&M 754–757

- Facts: X.L.O. (concrete subcontractor) and Rivergate (general contractor) contracted May 12, 1983 for a Manhattan concrete superstructure; contract $16,544,125; X.L.O. fully performed and sued for $844,125.07 balance (breach of contract, account stated, unjust enrichment). Rivergate refused, saying the contract was integral to the "Club": La Cosa Nostra's Commission, seven concrete companies and the District Council union; the Commission allocated jobs over $2 million, collected 2% for "labor peace," rigged bids, enforced through labor unrest or violence. X.L.O. joined in May 1981, the last concrete contractor to do so; paid the 2% and a $50,000 "gift." Rivergate negotiated "with full knowledge of the Club and its rules" (754–755).
- Procedure: trial court (Supreme Court) dismissed the complaint; Appellate Division reinstated it; Court of Appeals affirmed and answered the certified question in the affirmative (755–756). The illegality defense did not bar the claim as a matter of law; the case goes to trial.
- Rule (755): antitrust defenses in contract actions are "not favored" (Kelly v. Kosuga) because they tend to enrich parties who take the benefits and avoid the burdens (Viacom). They are upheld where the judgment "would result in enforcement of the 'precise conduct made unlawful by the Act.'" Beyond that, courts follow the policy "of preventing people from getting other people's property for nothing when they purport to be buying it" (Holmes, dissenting in Continental Wall Paper). "[A] contract which is legal on its face and does not call for unlawful conduct in its performance is not voidable simply because it resulted from an antitrust conspiracy." Critical question: whether the contract is "so integrally related to the agreement, arrangement or combination in restraint of competition that its enforcement would result in compelling performance of the precise conduct made unlawful by the antitrust laws"; whether it was "an indivisible, effectuating component of an illegal arrangement that would be consummated by granting the judgment sought" requires trial.
- Remand considerations (755–756): (1) whether and how far the price is excessive, discriminatory, and above fair market value because of the unlawful attempt to stifle competition, and the resulting anticompetitive effects; (2) the equities: avoid upholding the defense where it would work a substantial forfeiture on one party while unjustly enriching the other; whether the contract would be void entirely or quantum meruit recovery remains; relative culpability, bargaining power, knowledge; (3) policy of discouraging schemes like the Club, a danger reduced where statutory remedies exist and the Attorney General can attack violations directly. Per se illegality under the Donnelly Act rejected (756).
- Note 1 questions (756): were the parties in pari delicto; may further findings support recovery for unjust enrichment but not the contract balance; the New York Attorney General filed an amicus brief opposing the result.
- Jurisdiction: New York law (Donnelly Act, Gen. Bus. Law § 340), using federal Sherman Act precedent (Kelly).

## 6. Shadis v. Beal (3d Cir. 1982), assigned pp. 764 and 769 only

- No-fees provisions in Title XX funding contracts (PDPW–PLSC–CLS) barring CLS from requesting fees in suits against the Commonwealth; held void as contrary to the policy of the Civil Rights Attorney's Fees Awards Act, 42 U.S.C. § 1988; affirmed (764, 769). The reasoning (§§ 178–179 balancing; federal law governs) is on 765–768, which are not assigned. Notes (769): federalism (whose policy governs a state-law contract); whether a court should impose a limit Congress did not enact.

## 7. Judicially created policy and restraint of trade (pp. 769–770; §§ 179, 186–188)

- § 179: policies derived from (a) legislation or (b) the need to protect some aspect of the public welfare, e.g. restraint of trade (§§ 186–188), impairment of family relations (§§ 189–191), other protected interests (§§ 192–196) (769). Cohabitation: Hewitt (Ill. 1979) denied enforcement and deferred to the legislature; Marvin (Cal.) made judicial policy; Thomas v. LaRosa (W.Va. 1990): enforceable if the business part can be separated from the personal part (769–770).
- § 186(1): a promise is unenforceable if it is *unreasonably* in restraint of trade. § 186(2): a promise is in restraint of trade if its performance would limit competition in any business or restrict the promisor in exercising a gainful occupation.
- § 187: a promise to refrain from competition that is *not ancillary* to an otherwise valid transaction or relationship is unreasonably in restraint of trade (unenforceable without further balancing).
- § 188(1): an ancillary restraint is unreasonable if (a) it is greater than needed to protect the promisee's legitimate interest, or (b) the promisee's need is outweighed by the hardship to the promisor and the likely injury to the public. § 188(2): ancillary restraints include those by a seller of a business, an employee or agent, and a partner.
- § 188 cmt b: a post-employment restraint is not needed for the employer to get the value of what it acquired; it "must usually be justified" by a legitimate interest in preventing appropriation of valuable trade information and customer relationships; courts are more willing to uphold sale-of-business covenants than employment covenants. Cmt c: hardship (livelihood) and public injury (mobility, unavailability of skills); some restraints increase efficiency. Cmt d: extent limited by activity, area and time; if any one is broader than necessary the restraint is unreasonable "without the necessity of weighing the countervailing interests." Cmt g: post-employment restraints scrutinized "with particular care" (unequal bargaining power, standardized form); line between general skills and information peculiar to the employer; customer-contact justification depends on the nature, extent and locale of contacts; a restraint can be ancillary to at-will employment. Illus. 6 (contact-lens fitter with customer relationships: enforceable), 7 (advertising manager with no customer contact or confidential information: unenforceable), 8 (dance instructor), 9–10, 12–14 (doctor in a remote area: public injury).
- § 184(2) (partial enforcement): a court may treat only part of a term as unenforceable if the party seeking enforcement obtained it in good faith and in accordance with reasonable standards of fair dealing. This is the Restatement's version of the "partial enforcement" approach that Hassler rejects for Wyoming.

## 8. Hassler v. Circle C Resources (Wyo. 2022), C&M 770–778

- Facts: Circle C provides day and residential habilitation services to disabled clients in Natrona and Converse counties and is authorized in five more counties (770). Hired Charlene Hassler, a CNA, March 17, 2015, to provide residential habilitation care in her Converse County home for one long-term adult client (770–771). ¶ A: no competing, no similar services, no services to Circle C's clients, during employment and 24 months after, in any area where Circle C marketed in the year before separation, "including but not limited to" seven counties. ¶ B: 24-month non-solicitation. ¶ C: employee agrees the restrictions are reasonable and that a court will substitute the maximum reasonable restrictions (771). One month of training. Circle C received $121,142.10 per year through Medicaid waiver programs and paid Hassler about $26,400: net $94,742.10 (772). The client's mother and guardian, dissatisfied with Circle C's **day** habilitation services, changed providers; January 7, 2017 notice; the client stayed in Hassler's home under another Medicaid provider, which paid Hassler significantly more (772). May 31, 2017 cease-and-desist letter demanding she stop "for at least 12 consecutive months." Hassler stopped paid services from August 7, 2017 until August 2018; her husband and daughter were paid through Medicaid for the client's care (772). Circle C sued in July 2019 for damages.
- District court: agreement enforceable if narrowed to Natrona and Converse counties and 12 months; applying "the blue pencil rule," narrowed it; awarded $94,742.10 (projected profit for the modified 12-month term) on summary judgment (772).
- Standards (773–774): de novo review; reasonableness of a noncompete is a question of law (Hopper). Contracts contrary to public policy are not recognized and parties are left as found; Wyoming typically declares such contracts void rather than revising them. Balance: public interest in free competition, freedom of contract, employee's freedom to work. Noncompetes "strictly construed and rigidly scanned" and void unless necessary for reasonable protection of the employer; no protection from "ordinary competition." Hopper elements: (1) in writing; (2) part of a contract of employment; (3) based on reasonable consideration; (4) reasonable in duration and geographical limitations; (5) not against public policy. A noncompete is "prima facie invalid"; the employer must prove special circumstances making it reasonably necessary, i.e., necessity to protect legitimate business interests.
- Three approaches (774, quoting Skaf): "all or nothing"; "blue pencil" (enforce reasonable terms if the covenant remains grammatically coherent once unreasonable provisions are excised); "partial enforcement" or liberal blue pencil (reform and enforce to the extent reasonable unless bad faith or deliberate overreaching). Before Hopper (1993), unreasonable noncompetes were unenforceable in Wyoming; Hopper adopted partial enforcement (774–775).
- Reasons for overruling (775–777): stare decisis yields when precedent is unjust, contrary to plain principles, or unworkable; the liberal rule strays from black-letter rules; employer drafts from a superior bargaining position; the rule encourages overbroad drafting and gives a "free ride"; in terrorem effect on employees and competitors; ¶ C cannot enlist the court ("parties ... cannot contractually obligate a court to blue pencil," quoting 23 LTD v. Herman (Colo. App. 2019)); uncertainty for both sides.
- Holding (777–778): "We, therefore, overrule Hopper's adoption of the liberal blue pencil rule. ... a noncompete agreement which includes unreasonable restrictions on trade violates public policy and is invalid. The employer has the duty of proving all the terms of the noncompete agreement are reasonable." No dispute that the original duration and geography were unreasonable (Circle C did not justify the seven counties, sought enforcement only in two, and did not challenge the ruling that it had not shown 24 months reasonable); "the entire agreement is void in violation of public policy." Reversed and remanded for entry of summary judgment for Hassler. Footnote q: the court did not decide whether Circle C proved a legitimate business interest (778).
- Precision points: (a) the opinion's formal overruling is of the *liberal* blue pencil (partial enforcement); its opening and closing language says courts may no longer "use the blue pencil rule to modify" or "make" noncompetes reasonable; the excerpt does not separately analyze strict grammatical excision, but the holding ("includes unreasonable restrictions ... is invalid"; employer must prove "all the terms" reasonable) is all-or-nothing in effect. (b) The 12-month narrowing was the district court's, not Circle C's drafting. (c) Hassler did not originally argue unreasonableness of duration and geography; she raised it in supplemental briefing the court requested (773). (d) This is Wyoming law; the Restatement (§ 184(2)) and many states permit partial enforcement for a good-faith employer.
- Notes (778–780): (1) physicians: Mohanty (Ill. 2006) noted the adverse effect on patients if enforcement were denied; Blatchford (Ind. App. 2009) upheld non-enforcement where few suitable alternatives existed. (2) type of work and bargaining power; low-wage workers; Uniform Restrictive Employment Agreement Act (2021); the FTC's proposed rule of January 5, 2023 (text quoted). (3) "essential worker" enforcement: some states, particularly New York; minority view. (4) DeMuth v. Miller (Pa. Super. 1995): accountant fired "for cause" (cause defined to include homosexuality) after a TV appearance for a gay and lesbian coalition; liquidated payment of 125% of prior 12 months' charges from clients taken; enforced because no Pennsylvania statute or constitutional provision made the discrimination actionable; argument directed to the legislature. (5) Howard v. Babcock (Cal. 1993): law-partnership agreement making a withdrawing competitor's profit share discretionary held enforceable (reversed), "rules and habits of commerce have permeated the legal profession"; Kennard J. dissented.

## 9. Federal regulatory picture (C&M 778–779; supplement 102–107)

- Casebook: on January 5, 2023 the FTC *proposed* a rule declaring it an unfair method of competition to enter, maintain, or represent a non-compete with a worker (778–779).
- Supplement (91 FR 6507, published and effective February 12, 2026): the FTC published its final Non-Compete Rule May 7, 2024: unfair method of competition under FTC Act § 5 to enter non-competes with workers on or after September 4, 2024; existing non-competes of senior executives could remain in force; existing non-competes with other workers were not enforceable after September 4, 2024 (supp. 104). Challenges: ATS Tree Services (E.D. Pa. 2024) denied a preliminary injunction; Properties of the Villages (M.D. Fla. 2024) entered a preliminary injunction limited to the plaintiff (major questions); Ryan, LLC v. FTC (N.D. Tex. 2024) held the rule unlawful and set it aside under APA § 706(2) as in excess of statutory authority and arbitrary and capricious (supp. 104). On September 5, 2025 the Commission voted 3–1 to dismiss its appeals (5th and 11th Cir.) and accede to vacatur (supp. 104). The February 12, 2026 rule removes and reserves 16 C.F.R. part 910 (supp. 104–105).
- Teaching consequence: there is no federal rule; noncompete enforceability is governed by state law (Hassler-type common law, Restatement §§ 186–188, statutes). The supplement says nothing about FTC case-by-case § 5 enforcement or state statutes; any Atlas claim about those is not in the assigned sources.
