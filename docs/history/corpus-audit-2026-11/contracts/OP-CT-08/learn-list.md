## A. Limits on the power of revocation

1. **Default rule.** An offer stays revocable until acceptance even if it states a deadline, unless something makes the promise to hold it open binding (Dickinson v. Dodds background, C&M 246–247; Dawson quotation, C&M 247).
2. **Option contract.** An option contract is a promise that meets the requirements for forming a contract and limits the promisor's power to revoke an offer (Restatement § 25, SEL). The traditional routes are consideration and the seal (§ 87 cmt. a).
3. **§ 87(1) signed recital.** An offer binds as an option if it (a) is in writing and signed by the offeror, recites a purported consideration for making the offer, and proposes an exchange on fair terms within a reasonable time, or (b) is made irrevocable by statute (§ 87(1), SEL). Courts split on a recital of consideration never paid; § 87 treats the recital as making the offer irrevocable, with the signed-writing and fair-exchange qualifications (C&M 248 Note 3; § 87 cmts. b–c: "The signed writing has vital significance as a formality, while the ceremonial manual delivery of a dollar or a peppercorn is an inconsequential formality").
4. **§ 45 option by part performance.** Where an offer invites acceptance by performance and does not invite a promissory acceptance, an option contract arises when the offeree tenders or begins the invited performance or tenders a beginning of it; the offeror's duty stays conditional on completion or tender of the full performance (§ 45(1)–(2), SEL). Points tested: the offeree is not bound to finish (cmt. e); what is begun must be part of the actual invited performance, and preparation is different (cmt. f); a reservation of a power to revoke after performance begins means there is no offer at all (cmt. b). The Brooklyn Bridge hypothetical and Wormser's recantation illustrate it (C&M 251); one cannot "tender" a performance that takes time, such as crossing a bridge (C&M 251). § 45 Illustration 6 (SEL) is close to the Move to Maine problem: a mother's letter offering her farm if her daughter gives up her home and cares for her creates an option once the daughter gives up her home, moves, and begins caring.
5. **§ 62 contrast.** Where the offer lets the offeree choose between promise and performance, beginning the performance is an acceptance, and it operates as a promise to complete (§ 62, SEL; § 45 cmt. a and d). The offeree is then bound, unlike under § 45.
6. **§ 87(2) reliance before acceptance.** An offer that the offeror should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance of a substantial character by the offeree before acceptance, and that does induce it, binds as an option contract to the extent necessary to avoid injustice (§ 87(2), SEL). Comment e: it matters chiefly for reliance that is not part performance; "the reliance must be substantial as well as foreseeable." C&M 258 Note 2: Drennan's rationale is reflected in § 87(2); would it protect the bridge-crosser who spent time and money preparing but had not begun?
7. **§ 90.** A promise the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance, which does induce it, binds if injustice can be avoided only by enforcement; the remedy may be limited as justice requires; charitable subscriptions and marriage settlements need no proof of reliance (§ 90(1)–(2), SEL). Drennan applied the First Restatement wording, which required action or forbearance "of a definite and substantial character" (C&M 255).
8. **Death of the offeror.** Death or incapacity of offeror or offeree ends an ordinary power of acceptance, even without notice (§ 48, SEL; C&M 259). Death does not terminate the offeree's power of acceptance under an option contract (C&M 259). The Move to Maine problem asks what happens if the offeror dies after the offerees sold their Missouri home and business but before they moved (C&M 252).

## B. Drennan v. Star Paving (Cal. 1958), C&M 252–259

9. **Background: Baird (2d Cir. 1933).** Learned Hand held promissory estoppel inapplicable to a bargain offer seeking a promise in return; the subcontractor could revoke, and contractors can protect themselves by "insisting upon a contract before they used the figure" (C&M 252–253).
10. **Facts.** Star telephoned a $7,131.60 paving bid on bid day; Drennan used it, named Star as required, posted a bid bond, and won; next morning, before Drennan communicated acceptance, Star said the bid was a mistake and refused to perform for less than $15,000; Drennan hired L & H Paving for $10,948.60; judgment $3,817 (C&M 253–254).
11. **Holding and reasoning.** No option supported by consideration and no bilateral contract existed (C&M 254–255). Reasonable reliance resulting in a foreseeable prejudicial change of position supports an implied subsidiary promise not to revoke an offer for a bilateral contract, by analogy to First Restatement § 45 and comment b, under § 90 (C&M 255–256). Star had reason to expect, and to want, its bid to be used; fairness requires that the general have "at least an opportunity to accept defendant's bid after the general contract has been awarded to him" (C&M 256).
12. **Limits stated in the opinion.**
   - Had the bid "expressly stated or clearly implied that it was revocable at any time before acceptance we would treat it accordingly" (C&M 255).
   - A general contractor "is not free to delay acceptance after he has been awarded the general contract in the hope of getting a better price," and cannot "reopen bargaining with the subcontractor and at the same time claim a continuing right to accept the original offer" (C&M 256–257); Drennan promptly told Star it would be awarded the subcontract (C&M 257).
   - Mistake: the rescission cases involved mistakes that were known or should have been known, with the offeree restorable to the status quo; Drennan had no reason to know of the mistake given a usual 160 percent variance between high and low paving bids, and the loss falls on the party who made the mistake (C&M 257).
13. **Editors' notes.** Traynor did not find the promise in § 45 (Note 1); § 87(2) reflects Drennan (Note 2); Holman Erection v. Madsen (Minn. 1983) rejects the reverse duty, so a general's use of a sub-bid does not accept it (Note 3, C&M 258–259).
14. **Slide 10 question "How would this case be resolved in New York?"** From the assigned sources only: § 5-1109 needs a signed writing stating the offer is irrevocable, and Star's bid was telephoned; § 2-205 needs a merchant's signed goods offer, and paving is a service. Whether New York courts would protect reliance on an oral bid is not answered by the assigned materials.

## C. Firm offers under Article 2 and N.Y. G.O.L. § 5-1109

15. **§ 2-205 elements.** (i) an offer by a merchant (ii) to buy or sell goods (iii) in a signed writing (iv) that by its terms gives assurance it will be held open; it is not revocable for lack of consideration during the stated time, or a reasonable time if none is stated, (v) never more than three months; (vi) an assurance term on a form supplied by the offeree must be separately signed by the offeror (§ 2-205, SEL).
16. **§ 2-205 comments.** Cmt. 2: oral firm offers stay revocable "despite settled courses of dealing or usages of the trade," because authentication by a writing is the essence of the section; initialing a clause usually suffices. Cmt. 3: a promise for a longer period binds only for the first three months; a firm offer supported by consideration may last as long as the parties specify, because § 2-205 deals only with unsupported offers. Cmt. 4: separate authentication protects against inadvertent signing on the offeree's form.
17. **Merchant.** A merchant is one who deals in goods of the kind or holds himself out by occupation as having knowledge or skill peculiar to the practices or goods involved, or to whom that skill is attributable through an agent (§ 2-104(1), SEL). C&M 247 Note 1 asks whether a manufacturer buying factory equipment or selling a used machine is a merchant for § 2-205; Comment 2 to § 2-104 says professional status may rest on knowledge of goods, of business practices, or both.
18. **N.Y. G.O.L. § 5-1109 (SUPP 9; C&M 247–248).** "Except as otherwise provided in section 2-205 … with respect to an offer by a merchant to buy or sell goods," an offer made in a writing signed by the offeror or agent that states it is irrevocable during a period or until a time is not revocable during that period for lack of consideration; a writing stating irrevocability with no period is irrevocable for a reasonable time. It has no merchant, goods, or three-month requirement; merchant offers for goods stay under § 2-205. The supplement also prints §§ 5-1103, 5-1105, 5-1107, 5-1113 and 5-1115 (SUPP 9–10), which are context for this class.

## D. Scope of Article 2 (C&M 248–250)

19. Article 2 applies to "transactions in goods" (§ 2-102); goods are things movable at the time of identification to the contract (§ 2-105(1)). Whether Article 2 or common law governs can change the result (compare Dickinson with § 2-205) (C&M 248).
20. Hybrid transactions (air conditioner with installation; navigation device with services; radio-station assets) (C&M 248–249). Tests: Bonebrake's predominant factor or purpose (goods with incidental labor versus services with incidental goods); Foster's bifurcation; Anthony Pools' gravamen of the claim; Frantz as an implicit gravamen case (C&M 249–250). The 2022 amendments' proposed § 2-106(5) definition and § 2-102(2) rule, with enactment at an early stage in April 2023 (C&M 250).

## E. Formation under Article 2 (C&M 269–283)

21. **Common law.** Mirror-image rule: a reply varying the offer is a rejection and counteroffer (C&M 269; Ardente). Mitigations: implied terms; precatory suggestions; an inquiry is not a counteroffer (Rios, § 39 Illus. 2) (C&M 269–271). Last-shot consequence: performance accepts the last form (C&M 271; MPS v. Dresser-Rand, C&M 271–272). Macaulay on how forms are exchanged unread (C&M 273–274).
22. **§ 2-204.** A contract for sale may be made in any manner sufficient to show agreement, including conduct by both parties recognizing a contract; the moment of making may be undetermined; open terms do not defeat it if the parties intended a contract and a reasonably certain basis for a remedy exists (§ 2-204(1)–(3), SEL).
23. **§ 2-206.** Acceptance in any reasonable manner and medium; an order for prompt shipment invites a prompt promise to ship or shipment, and a nonconforming shipment both accepts and breaches unless the seller seasonably notifies that it is an accommodation (§ 2-206(1)); where beginning performance is a reasonable mode of acceptance, an offeror not notified within a reasonable time may treat the offer as lapsed (§ 2-206(2)); cmt. 3 preserves the common-law possibility that beginning performance temporarily bars revocation.
24. **§ 2-207(1).** "A definite and seasonable expression of acceptance or a written confirmation which is sent within a reasonable time operates as an acceptance even though it states terms additional to or different from those offered or agreed upon, unless acceptance is expressly made conditional on assent to the additional or different terms." A response differing radically in dickered terms such as price or quantity may fail as an expression of acceptance (C&M 276, Koehring). Two formation methods: an expressly conditional response followed by the offeror's assent gives a traditional agreement; a response not expressly conditional forms a contract without the offeror's assent (C&M 274).
25. **"Expressly made conditional."** Dorton: the proviso reaches only an "acceptance which clearly reveals that the of[feree] … with the transaction unless he is assured o[f] … additional or different terms therein" (C&M 279; the scan's right margin is cut off, so the missing words are marked rather than supplied), and the condition must be "distinctly stated or expressed rather than impl[ied]". Itoh's language qualified: "Seller's acceptance is, however, expressly conditional on Buyer's assent to the additional or different terms" (C&M 281).
26. **§ 2-207(2).** Additional terms are proposals for addition. Between merchants (both chargeable with merchants' knowledge, § 2-104(3)) they become part of the contract unless (a) the offer expressly limits acceptance to its terms, (b) they materially alter it, or (c) objection has been or is given within a reasonable time after notice. Cmt. 3: a materially altering term is not included "unless expressly agreed to by the other party"; cmt. 4: material means "surprise or hardship if incorporated without express awareness"; cmt. 5 lists non-material examples; cmt. 6 treats conflicting confirmations as mutual objections. Where either party is not a merchant only the first sentence applies, so the addition is a mere proposal requiring acceptance (C&M 284 preview; Dorton Note 2, C&M 281).
27. **§ 2-207(3).** Conduct by both parties recognizing a contract establishes one although the writings do not; its terms are "those terms on which the writings of the parties agree, together with any supplementary terms incorporated under any other provisions of this Act," which may be gap-fillers in Articles 1 and 2 and implied warranties (C&M 275). Cmt. 7: where goods are shipped, accepted and paid for, the only question is the terms, and (3) governs.
28. **Knockout and default terms.** The treatment of "different" terms (knockout, offer's term controls, or Comment 3 treatment as additions) is previewed at C&M 275 and taught on slides 16–17 but is developed in Northrop at C&M 284 and after, which belongs to Class 9. Under (3), every term on which the writings do not agree drops out, whether it is additional or conflicting, and Code gap-fillers supply the defaults (Itoh, C&M 282).

## F. Dorton v. Collins & Aikman (6th Cir. 1972), C&M 276–281

29. Facts in the excerpt: The Carpet Mart (Tennessee) bought carpets from Collins & Aikman in 55 transactions over three years (editors' bracket); orders were telephoned; acknowledgment forms said the acceptance was "subject to all of the terms and conditions on the face and reverse side," listed ways the order "shall become a contract," including retention for ten days without objection; the reverse required New York arbitration; the buyer took delivery and paid without objecting; it sued for fraud over fiber content; the seller moved to stay pending arbitration; the district court, applying § 2-207(3), denied the stay (C&M 276–277).
30. Holding: "subject to" language does not make acceptance expressly conditional; the proviso requires that the acceptance clearly reveal the offeree's unwillingness to go on with the transaction without assurance of the offeror's assent (a paraphrase consistent with the surviving words at C&M 279), and silence or inaction (retaining the form) could never be assent (C&M 279 & n.2). Because the acknowledgments were not within the proviso, a contract was recognized under (1) and arbitration was a proposal under (2); both parties were merchants, so the clause entered unless it materially altered the oral offers, a question for the district court on remand; if material, it could not enter "unless expressly agreed to" (C&M 280, cmt. 3). The remand also required findings whether oral agreements preceded the forms (making the forms confirmations) and whether the oral orders already included arbitration (C&M 278–279, 281). The district court was wrong to apply (3) directly (C&M 280 n.4). Whether arbitration is material rests on the facts of each case (C&M 280 n.6). Notes: acceptance versus written confirmation; non-merchant buyers (C&M 281).

## G. C. Itoh (America) v. Jordan International (7th Cir. 1977), C&M 281–283

31. Facts: Itoh's purchase order for steel coils; Jordan's acknowledgment expressly conditional on buyer's assent to its terms, one of which was arbitration with no counterpart in the order; steel delivered and paid for; Itoh sued for defects and late delivery; Jordan moved to stay; denial appealed (C&M 281–282).
32. Holding: the acknowledgment came within the proviso, so no contract formed under (1) and Jordan's form became a counteroffer; performance by both parties formed a contract under (3); its terms are those on which the writings agree plus supplementary terms, which are limited to Article 2's standardized gap-fillers; arbitration is not a gap-filler, so it is not in the contract (C&M 282). Policy: a seller using an expressly conditional clause may walk away before performing, or withhold delivery until assent; if it performs without assent it bears the risk that (3) omits its terms, because it injected the ambiguity (C&M 282–283). Notes: did acceptance of and payment for the steel constitute assent? Compare the Dorton trial court's move to (3) (C&M 283).

## H. Route-test variables that decide results in this class

- Offer invites performance only (§ 45 option) / either (§ 62 acceptance) / a promise (§ 87(2) reliance, Drennan).
- Beginning performance versus preparation (§ 45 cmt. f).
- Bid silent versus expressly revocable; prompt acceptance versus bid shopping; mistake known or unknown (Drennan).
- Merchant and goods and signed writing and three months and separate signature (§ 2-205); signed writing stating irrevocability (§ 5-1109).
- Goods versus services versus hybrid (Article 2 scope).
- Response "subject to" terms versus expressly conditional on assent (Dorton, Itoh).
- Both merchants versus one non-merchant (§ 2-207(2)); material versus non-material; expressly agreed or not.
- Offeror's express assent versus conduct only versus no performance (§ 2-207(1), (3)).
