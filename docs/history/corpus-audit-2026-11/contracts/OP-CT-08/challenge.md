## Part A. Challenged statements (errors in existing text)

### A-01. `ucc207-conduct` calls the § 2-207(3) terms rule "the knock-out rule" and limits it to conflicting terms

- Surface and field: **ucc207-conduct** concept entry, `notes`.
- Current text: "Conflicting terms in the two forms therefore drop out (the knock-out rule) and the Code's gap-fillers take their place."
- Verdict: **CHANGES RESULT**.
- Counterexample (C. Itoh, decisive fact as in the case). Jordan's acknowledgment expressly conditions acceptance on Itoh's assent; Itoh's purchase order says nothing about arbitration; both parties perform. A student told that under (3) "conflicting terms ... drop out" sees no conflict, because the buyer's form is silent, and keeps the arbitration clause. The court held the opposite. The terms of a (3) contract "consist of those terms on which the writings of the parties agree," and "the disputed additional terms (i.e., those terms on which the writings of the parties do not agree) which are necessarily excluded from a Subsection (3) contract" by that language "cannot be brought back into the contract under the guise of 'supplementary terms'" (C&M 282). A term found in only one form drops out whether or not it conflicts with anything. "Knockout" is the slides' and the `knockout` entry's name for a disputed reading of *different* terms in a contract formed under subsection (1) (slide 16: "majority 'knockout' rule"). The `knockout` entry itself says the two routes "can resemble each other in result while arising from different statutory questions." The sentence therefore contradicts a neighbouring entry and the Itoh holding.
- Pinpoint: C&M 275 ("Such 'supplementary terms' may consist of 'gap-fillers' provided in Articles 1 and 2 as well as implied warranties"), 282; `sel.txt` § 2-207(3); slide 15.
- Replacement: "Any term on which the writings do not agree drops out, whether it conflicts with the other form or appears in only one of them, as C. Itoh's arbitration clause did. The Code's supplementary terms, including its gap-fillers and implied warranties, then complete the contract (C&M 275, 282). This is the text of subsection (3). It is not the knockout rule, which is a disputed reading of different terms in a contract formed under subsection (1)."

### A-02. K-13 node e: Drennan's limits (reserved revocation, knowledge of mistake, bid shopping) and the "substantial" requirement are missing

- Surface and field: **Flowchart K-13 (Can the offer still be revoked?) node e**, `detail`; target node g `detail`.
- Current text: e "Drennan protected the general contractor that reasonably used a definite bid in its own bid. Baird rejected reliance protection on its approach. Preparations alone can raise the § 87(2) reliance question without starting the requested performance."; g "Measure the actual protected period. Drennan gave the general a reasonable opportunity to accept after award."
- Verdict: **CHANGES RESULT** (three routes).
- Route 1 (decisive fact changed: reservation). Star's telephoned bid ends "subject to withdrawal at any time before acceptance." Path: a →(Preparations or subcontractor bid) e →(Reliance protection under governing rule) g. Displayed result: "Withdrawal cannot defeat timely acceptance." Source result: Star may revoke. "Had defendant's bid expressly stated or clearly implied that it was revocable at any time before acceptance we would treat it accordingly" (C&M 255).
- Route 2 (decisive fact changed: bid shopping). After the award Drennan tells Star he will use it only if it cuts its price, waits two weeks, then tries to accept the original $7,131.60. Path as above, to g, where "Measure the actual protected period" gives no answer. Source result: no protection. "A general contractor is not free to delay acceptance after he has been awarded the general contract in the hope of getting a better price. Nor can he reopen bargaining with the subcontractor and at the same time claim a continuing right to accept the original offer" (C&M 256–257).
- Route 3 (decisive fact changed: obvious error). Star's figure is a third of every other paving bid, and Drennan has reason to know it is a mistake. Path: e reads "reasonably used a definite bid," and the student takes the reliance edge to g. Source result: no protection. The court distinguished cases where the "bidder's mistake was known or should have been known," and said that a general with reason to believe the bid was in error could not "justifiably rely on it, and section 90 would afford no basis" for enforcing it (C&M 257; the OCR drops some words of this passage). Drennan won because there was "usually a variance" between the highest and lowest paving bids.
- The § 87(2) sentence also omits a statutory condition. The reliance must be "of a substantial character," the offeror must reasonably expect it, and the offer binds only "to the extent necessary to avoid injustice" (`sel.txt` § 87(2); cmt. e: "the reliance must be substantial as well as foreseeable"). The First Restatement § 90 that Traynor applied required action "of a definite and substantial character" (C&M 255).
- Pinpoint: C&M 255–258; `sel.txt` § 87(2) and cmt. e.
- Replacement (e detail): "Drennan protected a general contractor that reasonably used a definite bid in its own bid. Protection fails if the bid expressly or clearly reserved revocation, or if the general knew or had reason to know of the mistake when relying. After the award the general may not delay acceptance to seek a better price, or reopen bargaining while claiming the original bid. Baird rejected reliance protection. Under § 87(2) the reliance must be substantial, reasonably expected by the offeror, and before acceptance; preparations can qualify without starting the requested performance." Node g needs no change once e carries these limits.

### A-03. K-13 node c and the `firm-offers` timeline step `ny51109`: New York § 5-1109 shown without its exception for merchants' goods offers

- Surfaces and fields: **Flowchart K-13 node c**, `detail`; **Timeline 'firm-offers' step ny51109**, `note`.
- Current text: c "... Compare New York § 5-1109's requirements for signed written offers."; timeline "New York: a signed written offer that states it is irrevocable binds for the stated period, or a reasonable time, without consideration."
- Verdict: **CHANGES RESULT**.
- Counterexample. A New York steel distributor signs a written offer to sell coils, "irrevocable for six months." On day 100 it revokes. The timeline says the offer "binds for the stated period," and node c invites the student to apply § 5-1109. Path: a →(Signed offer under statute) c →(Statutory requirements met) g. Displayed result: the withdrawal is ineffective. Source result: the revocation is effective. Section 5-1109 opens "Except as otherwise provided in section 2-205 ... with respect to an offer by a merchant to buy or sell goods" (supp. 9; C&M 247–248). Under § 2-205 "in no event may such period of irrevocability exceed three months," and a longer promise "will operate under this section to bind the offeror only for the first three months of the period" (`sel.txt` § 2-205 and cmt. 3). "Binds" also overstates the statute's effect: the offer "shall not be revocable ... because of the absence of consideration," which makes it irrevocable but forms no contract.
- Pinpoint: supp. 9; C&M 247–248; `sel.txt` § 2-205 and cmt. 3.
- Replacement (timeline note): "New York: except for a merchant's offer to buy or sell goods, which UCC § 2-205 governs, a signed written offer stating that it is irrevocable cannot be revoked for lack of consideration during the stated period, or for a reasonable time if none is stated." Node c, append: "Section 5-1109 does not reach a merchant's offer to buy or sell goods. Section 2-205 governs that offer, so the three-month cap applies even to a longer stated period (cmt. 3)."

### A-04. K-16 node b: a "definite ... expression of acceptance" is treated as satisfied by any non-conditional reply

- Surface and field: **Flowchart K-16 (Whose terms govern under UCC § 2-207?) node b**, `detail`.
- Current text: "A definite and seasonable expression of acceptance can form a contract despite variant terms unless expressly conditional on assent to them. Dorton requires a clear demand for assent, beyond a reference to printed terms."
- Verdict: **CHANGES RESULT**.
- Facts entered (Dorton, decisive fact changed). The Carpet Mart telephones an order for 1,000 yards at $5. Collins & Aikman's acknowledgment, with no expressly conditional language, lists 600 yards at $7. The Carpet Mart rejects the delivery. Path: a →(Article 2) b. The reply is not expressly conditional, so the student takes "Effective acceptance or confirmation" → d →(Different) g, where knockout or another rule picks a quantity and price. Displayed result: a contract on the surviving or gap-filled terms. Source result: no contract through the writings. The C&M note says that not all responses operate as acceptance: the response must be "an expression of acceptance," that is, "sufficiently responsive to the offer to indicate the intent to enter into a contract." It adds that a response differing radically from the offer, "particularly with regard to dickered terms, such as price or quantity," can fail as "an acceptance and end the inquiry into contract formation" (C&M 276, citing Koehring Co. v. Glowacki; the OCR drops a few words of this sentence, which are paraphrased). Dorton's own statement of the rule includes "provided that the offeree's intent to accept the offer is definitely expressed" (C&M 278). Slide 16 lists "Not a definite expression of acceptance" as a separate route to a counteroffer and to § 2-207(3). The node folds that route into the conditional-acceptance proviso.
- Pinpoint: C&M 276, 278; slides 16, 19; `sel.txt` § 2-207(1).
- Replacement detail: "A response operates as an acceptance only if it is a definite and seasonable expression of acceptance. One that departs radically from the offer's dickered terms, such as price or quantity, may not be one (C&M 276). Such an expression forms a contract despite additional or different terms unless acceptance is expressly made conditional on assent to them. Dorton requires language that clearly shows the offeree will not proceed unless assured of the offeror's assent; 'subject to' its printed terms is not enough."

### A-05. C. Itoh takeaway: "says the deal is conditional on its extra terms" also describes Dorton's "subject to" form

- Surface and field: **Takeaway (tools/r19/data/takeaways.json)** for `citoh`.
- Current text: "When a seller's form says the deal is conditional on its extra terms, the buyer never agrees, and both sides perform anyway, the contract is made by their conduct."
- Verdict: **CHANGES RESULT** (blurs the Dorton/Itoh distinction).
- Counterexample. Dorton's acknowledgment said the order was "given subject to all of the terms and conditions on the face and reverse side hereof" (slide 18; C&M 277). In ordinary English that "says the deal is conditional on its extra terms." A student who relies on the takeaway sends Dorton to § 2-207(3) and drops the arbitration clause, which repeats the error for which the district court was reversed (C&M 280 n.4). C&M 283, Note 2 asks the reader to compare Itoh with the Dorton trial court, which "was reversed for making the same move." The trigger is narrower. Acceptance must be "expressly made conditional on assent to the additional or different terms," and Jordan's form said "Seller's acceptance is, however, expressly conditional on Buyer's assent to the additional or different terms" (C&M 281). Dorton requires that the condition be "distinctly stated or expressed rather than implied" (C&M 279).
- Pinpoint: C&M 277, 279–283; `sel.txt` § 2-207(1).
- Replacement (55 words, two sentences): "When a seller's form expressly makes its acceptance conditional on the buyer's assent to its extra terms, the buyer never assents, and both sides perform anyway, the contract is made by their conduct under § 2-207(3). Its terms are only those both writings agree on, plus the Code's gap-fillers; the seller's extra terms drop out."

### A-06. `firm-offers` timeline `now`: § 2-205 stated without the assurance-of-firmness requirement

### A-07. The Move to Maine (`problem-maine`): death "after preparations" assumes the answer to the problem's question

- Surface and field: **problem-maine** concept entry, `notes`.
- Current text: "Vary the language to "agree to move" and consider death after preparations but before arrival."
- Verdict: **CHANGES RESULT** (presupposes the classification).
- Counterexample. The casebook asks: "What if Hodgkin had died after the Brackenburys had sold their home and business in Missouri and begun the journey to Maine?" (C&M 252); slide 6 asks the same. The offer reads "If you move to Maine and take care of me." Whether selling the home and beginning the journey is preparation or the beginning of the invited performance (moving to Maine) decides the case. If it is the beginning, § 45 creates an option, and "the death or incapacity of the offeror does not terminate the offeree's power of acceptance under an option contract" (C&M 259). If it is only preparation, the offer is an ordinary one and dies with the offeror (§ 48, C&M 259). Labelling the facts "preparations" sends the student to the second answer without asking the question. Comment f to § 45 states the factors: whether the conduct is "clearly referable to the offer," "the definite and substantial character of that conduct," and its benefit to the offeror. Illustration 6 (a daughter who gives up her home and moves to care for her mother) finds an option.
- Pinpoint: C&M 252, 259; `sel.txt` § 45 cmts. d, f and illus. 6; slide 6.
- Replacement: "Vary the language to 'agree to move.' Then consider death after the Brackenburys sold their home and business and began the journey but before they arrived. Was that preparation or the beginning of the invited performance (§ 45 cmt. f)? An offeror's death ends an ordinary offer but not an option (C&M 259)."

### A-11. K-16 node a: the common-law branch understates the mirror-image rule

- Surface and field: **Flowchart K-16 node a**, `detail`.
- Current text: "Under common law, a response that makes assent contingent on new conditions may instead be a counteroffer. A mere inquiry can leave acceptance intact."
- Verdict: **OVERSTATED** (in favour of formation).
- Counterexample. A services contract (outside Article 2). The offeree replies, "I accept; work to start June 2," where the offer said June 1, and frames the change as no condition. The detail suggests that only a response "contingent on new conditions" fails, so the student finds a contract. The casebook states the traditional rule more strictly: an acceptance must be "absolute, unconditional, and identical with the terms of the offer," and any "departure from those terms invalidates the offer" (C&M 269, quoting Nix). The rule is softened in two ways: the apparent variation may already be implied in the offer, or the language may be "only suggestive, or precatory" (C&M 269–270). Neither softening turns on whether the reply was framed as a condition.
- Pinpoint: C&M 269–271.
- Replacement: "Under the common-law mirror-image rule, a reply that departs from the offer's terms is a rejection and counteroffer. Courts soften the rule where the apparent variation was already implied in the offer or the added language is only precatory. A mere inquiry about other terms is not a counteroffer (Rios)."
