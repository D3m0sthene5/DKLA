## (b) Errors

Ordered by effect on an exam answer. Each finding gives the surface and field, the exact current text, the verdict, a
counterexample, the pinpoint, and a minimal replacement.

### E1. Plain-English glosses define "unconscionable" as merely "grossly unfair"

- **Surfaces and fields:**
  - Williams, Cold Call plain-English sentence at "1.0", `plain`: "meaning that a contract is grossly unfair"
  - Williams, Cold Call plain-English sentence at "1.1", `plain`: "unconscionable, grossly unfair, at the time it was made"
  - Stoll, Cold Call plain-English sentence at "1.1" (pleadings), `plain`: "the clause was unconscionable (grossly unfair)"
  - Stoll, Cold Call plain-English sentence at "1.1" (trial court), `plain`: "so unfair it could not be enforced"
- **Verdict:** MISSING CONDITION.
- **Why:** each gloss defines the term by substantive unfairness alone. Williams defines unconscionability as "an absence
  of meaningful choice on the part of one of the parties together with contract terms which are unreasonably favorable to
  the other party" (C&M 685). Stoll applies the same pair through Barnes (C&M 696). Comment 1 to § 2–302 states the
  principle as "the prevention of oppression and unfair surprise ... and not of disturbance of allocation of risks because
  of superior bargaining power." § 208 cmt d says a bargain is not unconscionable "merely because the parties to it are
  unequal in bargaining position," and RLCC (TD) § 5(b) requires both prongs.
- **Counterexample:** Zapatha v. Dairy Mart (C&M 702). A franchisor may terminate a franchise without cause on 90 days'
  notice. A student working from "unconscionable = grossly unfair" asks only whether that term is harsh. The court asked
  about surprise as well: the clause was not obscure, it was pointed out before signing, and the franchisee declined to
  consult a lawyer, so there was "no potential for unfair surprise." Combined with the inventory-repurchase obligation,
  that defeated the claim. Williams Note 1 (C&M 688) asks the same question directly: would explaining the clauses to Mrs.
  Williams have changed the holding?
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 685, 696, 702; UCC § 2–302 cmt 1; § 208 cmt d; RLCC (TD) § 5(b).
- **Minimal replacements:**
  - Williams 1.0: replace "meaning that a contract is grossly unfair" with "meaning a contract made without meaningful choice whose terms unreasonably favor the other side".
  - Williams 1.1: replace "unconscionable, grossly unfair, at the time it was made" with "unconscionable (made without meaningful choice, on terms unreasonably favoring the other side) at the time it was made".
  - Stoll 1.1 (pleadings): replace "the clause was unconscionable (grossly unfair)" with "the clause was unconscionable (one-sided terms they had no meaningful choice about)".
  - Stoll 1.1 (trial court): replace "so unfair it could not be enforced" with "so one-sided, and so poorly understood by the buyers, that it could not be enforced".

### E2. The Williams Cold Call glosses "as a matter of law" as "without facts found first"

- **Surface and field:** Williams, Cold Call plain-English sentence at "4.0", `plain`: "meaning without facts found first"
- **Verdict:** MISSING CONDITION (it misstates who decides and on what record).
- **Why:** in Williams the phrase means the appellate court could not decide unconscionability itself because the record
  lacked findings (C&M 686). As a general definition the gloss is wrong for this doctrine. Under § 2–302(1) the court
  decides unconscionability "as a matter of law," and under § 2–302(2) it does so after the parties have had a "reasonable
  opportunity to present evidence" of commercial setting, purpose and effect. Comment 3 says that evidence is "for the
  court's consideration, not the jury's," and § 208 cmt f says "Incidental findings of fact are made by the court rather
  than by a jury." A decision "as a matter of law" therefore often rests on facts the court has found.
- **Counterexample:** a student told that a UCC court must decide unconscionability "as a matter of law" would conclude,
  from this gloss, that no evidence is taken. That is the opposite of § 2–302(2). Stoll also decided the issue as a matter
  of law, but on a summary-judgment record of deposition evidence about language ability and litter value (C&M 694–697).
- **Pinpoint:** C&M 686, 696; UCC § 2–302(1), (2), cmts 1, 3; § 208 cmt f.
- **Minimal replacement:** "The lower courts believed they had no power to refuse enforcement, so they made no findings on
  unconscionability. The \"appellate\" record (what the appeals court had before it) was not enough for that court to
  decide the question itself \"as a matter of law\" (as a legal ruling for the judge). The trial court first had to find the
  facts."

### E3. The Anderson rule line generalizes California's implied-covenant limit into a formation rule

- **Surfaces and fields:** Anderson, Effective brief CT-SUP-0094: rule; and Chronology 'course-chronology-0' step 626,
  `note` (identical text): "Under ordinary state contract-formation principles, an online amendment adding an arbitration
  clause binds existing users only with actual or constructive notice, and a unilateral modification clause silent about
  accrued claims does not reach claims that accrued before the amendment."
- **Verdict:** MISSING CONDITION.
- **Why:** the notice half is a formation rule under the law of the users' states (Supp. 97–98). The retroactivity half is
  not a formation principle. It is Peleg's California rule that the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing
  restricts a unilateral-modification clause silent on the point so that changes "do not apply to" claims that are
  "accrued or known" (Supp. 100). The rule line also drops "known." The brief's own Reasoning and Cold Call episode 6 state
  the source correctly.
- **Counterexample:** an employer's handbook reserves a unilateral power to amend, and the amendment adds arbitration. An
  employee knew of a claim that had not yet accrued when the amendment took effect. Read literally, the rule line reaches
  only "accrued" claims, so the student would send the known claim to arbitration; Peleg as quoted in Anderson excludes
  "accrued or known" claims. A student would also treat the rule as general formation doctrine in every state rather than
  as an implied-covenant rule the court took from California law.
- **Pinpoint:** Supp. 97–98 (choice of formation law), 98 (actual or constructive notice), 100 (Peleg; "accrued or known").
- **Minimal replacement (41 words):** "Under the state law governing formation, an online amendment adding arbitration binds
  existing users only with actual or constructive notice; under California's implied covenant of good faith (Peleg), a
  unilateral-modification clause silent about known or accrued claims does not reach them."

### E4. K-25 node a treats lack of constructive notice as enough to keep later website terms out

- **Surface and field:** Flowchart K-25 node a, `detail`: "later website terms without constructive notice, can fail at this stage"
- **Verdict:** MISSING CONDITION.
- **Why:** Anderson says "Reasonable notice requires that a user have either constructive or actual notice" (Supp. 98),
  and the elided holding found actual notice for all plaintiffs by June 8, 2020 (Supp. 99). Lack of constructive notice
  defeats incorporation only if the user also lacked actual notice. In Anderson the pre-amendment claims ultimately failed
  on retroactivity, which chart K-10 node f covers and K-25 does not.
- **Flowchart branch:** Facts entered: a user agreed to terms by clickwrap; the company later posted an arbitration
  amendment without individual notice; the user learned of it (actual notice) and kept using the service; the dispute
  accrued after that. Path: node a, where the detail invites the edge "No adequate notice or assent", to node h ("Term cannot
  govern on this route"). Displayed result: the arbitration term is out. Source-supported result: notice is satisfied by
  actual notice (Supp. 98). The term may enter if continued use is accepted as assent under the governing law, and then the
  questions move to node b (unconscionability or other objections). Anderson does not decide those later claims (the
  excerpt omits them).
- **Pinpoint:** Supp. 98, 99 (bracketed summary), 100.
- **Minimal replacement:** "later website terms of which the user had neither actual nor constructive notice, can fail at this stage"

### E5. The lecture-notes conflict says least-favorable substitution needs independent support; the assigned RLCC supplies it

- **Surface and field:** Lecture notes record, `conflicts[0].body`: "The slide’s least-favorable substitution is a policy
  option and requires independent support under the governing law."
- **Verdict:** MISSING CONDITION. The slide itself is not on file, so I cannot check what it says.
- **Why:** UCC § 2–302 and § 208 authorize only refusal, severance and limiting the clause, and the note is correct about
  them. The assigned RLCC selection goes further. Under TD § 5(a), an unconscionable term is unenforceable "to the extent
  stated in § 9." TD § 9(b)(3) lets a court that enforces the remainder replace the term, "if the contravening term was
  placed by the business in bad faith, [with] a term that is calculated to give the business an incentive to avoid placing
  such terms in consumer contracts." Official Text § 10(b)(3) keeps the option for terms placed "not in good faith" or
  effecting "extreme derogation." The option still needs adoption by the governing jurisdiction, but the Restatement
  assigned for this class states it.
- **Counterexample:** asked which authority supports a deterrent replacement term for a bad-faith consumer clause, a student
  relying on this note would answer "none in the assigned materials." RLCC TD § 9(b)(3) is the answer.
- **Pinpoint:** RLCC (TD) § 5(a), cmt 10; § 9(a)–(b) (sel. 514).
- **Minimal replacement:** "The slide’s least-favorable substitution goes beyond those two provisions. The RLCC draft in
  the Selections (§ 9(b)(3); § 10(b)(3) in the 2024 text) allows a deterrent replacement for terms placed in bad faith, but
  a court must still adopt that approach under the governing law."

## (c) Ranked teaching gaps

Each gap gives a home (surface and field) and a draft sentence. Takeaway-length limits are noted where a takeaway is the home.

**G1. The RLCC position is absent.**
Home: gap-rlcc map entry, `notes` (it currently only describes the files on hand). Secondary home: K-25 node e, `detail`.
Draft (gap-rlcc notes): "The 2019 draft printed in the Selections (§ 5; § 6 in the 2024 Official Text) treats a consumer
term as unconscionable only if, when the contract was made, it was both substantively unconscionable (fundamentally unfair
or unreasonably one-sided) and procedurally unconscionable (unfair surprise or no meaningful choice), on a sliding scale
in which more of one requires less of the other. Comment 6(a) treats a non-negotiable standard form alone as the lowest
quantum of procedural unconscionability, which must be matched by a high degree of substantive unconscionability. The 2024
text adds that, in appropriate circumstances, a sufficiently high degree of one factor is enough. Subsection (c) lists
terms that are substantively unconscionable, including cross-collateral clauses (cmt 4(b), Illus 11) and terms that
unreasonably limit the consumer's ability to seek redress. Comment 4(c) expressly leaves open preemption under the Federal
Arbitration Act."
Draft (K-25 node e, appended): "In a consumer case, the RLCC applies a sliding scale; under UCC § 2–302 and § 208, follow the
jurisdiction's case law."

**G2. Who decides, and the § 2–302(2) evidentiary hearing, are never connected to the Williams remand.**
Home: K-25 node f, `detail`; R&A entry ucc-2-302 (add a Comment 3 line).
Draft (node f): "The court, not a jury, decides unconscionability as a matter of law, but UCC § 2–302(2) requires giving the
parties a reasonable opportunity to present evidence of the contract's commercial setting, purpose and effect. Williams
remanded because no such findings had been made."
Draft (R&A, Comment 3): "The present section is addressed to the court, and the decision is to be made by it. The commercial
evidence referred to in subsection (2) is for the court's consideration, not the jury's."

**G3. Remedy granularity is missing.**
Home: K-25 node g, `detail`.
Draft: "Comment 2 to § 2–302 ties refusal of the whole contract to a contract 'permeated' by unconscionability; otherwise
strike or limit the offending clause. Under § 208 comment g, denying specific performance is the simplest remedy, as in
Campbell Soup. Denying damages may be appropriate only if the contract is entirely executory, and because the policy is not
penal, a party who has performed ordinarily recovers at least the reasonable value of that performance. Frostifresh limited
the seller to net cost, a reasonable profit and necessary charges."
Exam use: with Campbell Soup facts the remedy is refusal of equitable relief; the court said it was not suggesting "any
excuse for the grower" (C&M 680).

**G4. Price unconscionability, beyond Stoll, is missing.**
Home: Stoll map entry, `notes`; or a new takeaway (12–70 words, at most two sentences).
Draft takeaway (58 words): "Inadequate price alone ordinarily does not make a bargain unconscionable, though a gross
disparity in values is an important factor (§ 208 cmt c). Stoll paired a hidden, conscience-shocking effective price with
buyers who could not understand the clause, and the Nez Perce problem asks whether paying $2.97 an acre for land worth $4
is unconscionable."
Draft (notes, appended): "Compare New York's price-gouging statute (§ 396-r), under which a seller can rebut a prima facie
case by showing that the increase preserved its prior profit margin or reflected added costs, and RLCC comment 8, which
calls for 'extra care' in applying unconscionability to price."

**G5. The FAA framework for unconscionability challenges to arbitration clauses is thin.**
Home: unconscionability timeline, `guide`; or K-25 node b, `detail`.
Draft: "FAA § 2 makes arbitration agreements enforceable 'save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the
revocation of any contract,' so unconscionability applies (Circuit City). Armendariz refused a one-sided employment clause,
while Oblix enforced one and said no state may apply to arbitration 'any novel rule.' Shubin and Samaniego found procedural
unconscionability in pressure and in the refusal of a translation."

**G6. Commercial-party limits are absent.**
Home: K-25 node i, `detail`.
