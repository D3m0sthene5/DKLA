## 1. The statutory and Restatement rules

**1.1 UCC § 2–302(1) (text).** "If the court as a matter of law finds the contract or any clause of the contract to have
been unconscionable at the time it was made the court may [a] refuse to enforce the contract, or [b] it may enforce the
remainder of the contract without the unconscionable clause, or [c] it may so limit the application of any
unconscionable clause as to avoid any unconscionable result." Conditions: (i) the court decides; (ii) as a matter of
law; (iii) measured at the time of contracting; (iv) the three remedies are discretionary ("may"). Scope: Article 2
(sales of goods). Restatement § 208 cmt a: § 2–302 is "literally inapplicable" outside sales of goods but "very
influential in non-sales cases," used "by analogy." (sel. § 2–302; C&M 679; § 208 cmt a, quoted in Stoll, C&M 696.)

**1.2 UCC § 2–302(2): evidentiary opportunity.** "When it is claimed or appears to the court that the contract or any
clause thereof may be unconscionable the parties shall be afforded a reasonable opportunity to present evidence as to its
commercial setting, purpose and effect to aid the court in making the determination." Mandatory ("shall"); triggered when
the claim is made or when the issue "appears to the court." Cmt 1: subsection (2) "makes it clear that it is proper for
the court to hear evidence upon these questions." Teaching use: Williams's remand (record insufficient, no findings) is
the common-law analogue of the § 2–302(2) hearing.

**1.3 Comment 1: the basic test and the principle.** "The basic test is whether, in the light of the general commercial
background and the commercial needs of the particular trade or case, the clauses involved are so one-sided as to be
unconscionable under the circumstances existing at the time of the making of the contract." "The principle is one of the
prevention of oppression and unfair surprise ... and not of disturbance of allocation of risks because of superior
bargaining power." Prior policing was covert ("adverse construction of language, ... manipulation of the rules of offer
and acceptance or ... determinations that the clause is contrary to public policy"); § 2–302 lets the court "pass
directly" on unconscionability and make "a conclusion of law." (C&M 679 quotes part; sel. cmt 1.)

**1.4 Comment 2: remedies in the court's discretion.** The court "may refuse to enforce the contract as a whole if it is
permeated by the unconscionability, or it may strike any single clause or group of clauses which are so tainted or which
are contrary to the essential purpose of the agreement, or it may simply limit unconscionable clauses so as to avoid
unconscionable results." (Whole-contract refusal is tied to permeation.)

**1.5 Comment 3: who decides.** "The present section is addressed to the court, and the decision is to be made by it. The
commercial evidence referred to in subsection (2) is for the court's consideration, not the jury's. Only the agreement
which results from the court's action on these matters is to be submitted to the general triers of the facts." C&M 681:
"under UCC § 2–302 the issue of unconscionability is not to be submitted to a jury." Stoll (C&M 696): "The question of
unconscionability is one of law for the Court to decide" (Phillips Machinery; Oklahoma Code Comment).

**1.6 Restatement (Second) § 208 (text).** "If a contract or term thereof is unconscionable at the time the contract is
made a court may refuse to enforce the contract, or may enforce the remainder of the contract without the unconscionable
term, or may so limit the application of any unconscionable term as to avoid any unconscionable result." Same three
remedies, same time of measurement, all contracts. C&M 680 Note 1: § 208 states a rule "in virtually the same terms as
UCC § 2–302(1)"; "without parallel in the First Restatement"; Campbell Soup is the basis of Illustration 1.

**1.7 § 208 comments.**
- cmt a (scope): determination made "in the light of its setting, purpose and effect"; relevant factors include
  "weaknesses in the contracting process like those involved in more specific rules as to contractual capacity, fraud,
  and other invalidating causes"; overlaps with public-policy rules; for standardized agreements the court may "pass
  directly" on unconscionability "rather than to avoid unconscionable results by interpretation."
- cmt b (historic standard): "such as no man in his senses and not under delusion would make on the one hand, and as no
  honest and fair man would accept on the other" (Hume, quoting Earl of Chesterfield v. Janssen); at law, damages limited
  to what the party was "equitably" entitled to; in equity, specific performance refused where the bargain was "too hard a
  bargain for a court of conscience to assist" (Campbell Soup).
- cmt c (overall imbalance): "Inadequacy of consideration does not of itself invalidate a bargain, but gross disparity in
  the values exchanged may be an important factor"; may "corroborate indications of defects in the bargaining process";
  "Theoretically it is possible for a contract to be oppressive taken as a whole, even though there is no weakness in the
  bargaining process and no single term which is in itself unconscionable. Ordinarily, however, an unconscionable
  contract involves other factors as well as overall imbalance." (Quoted by Eisenberg, C&M 681.)
- cmt d (weakness in bargaining process): "A bargain is not unconscionable merely because the parties to it are unequal in
  bargaining position, nor even because the inequality results in an allocation of risks to the weaker party. But gross
  inequality of bargaining power, together with terms unreasonably favorable to the stronger party, may confirm
  indications that the transaction involved elements of deception or compulsion, or may show that the weaker party had no
  meaningful choice, no real alternative, or did not in fact assent or appear to assent to the unfair terms." Factor list:
  stronger party's belief of no reasonable probability of full performance; knowledge that weaker party will be unable to
  receive substantial benefits; knowledge that weaker party cannot reasonably protect his interests "by reason of physical
  or mental infirmities, ignorance, illiteracy or inability to understand the language of the agreement." Illus 3 (Spanish-
  literate buyer, $900 + $250 freezer that cost seller $350: court "may ... limit B's recovery to a reasonable sum").
- cmt e (unconscionable terms): a term may be unconscionable whether or not the contract as a whole is; some terms are
  never enforced regardless of context (unreasonably large liquidated damages; limits on a debtor's right to redeem
  collateral); others depend on context. **Illus 5 restates Williams** (13 purchases, $1,200, $164 owed, $514 stereo,
  ~800-word fine-print paragraph): "It may be determined that either the quoted clause or the contract as a whole was
  unconscionable when made." Illus 6: distant-forum clause with no reason but inconvenience is unconscionable. Illus 7:
  finding unconscionability "on its face, without regard to context, is error."
- cmt f (law and fact): determination "made by the court in the light of all the material facts"; under § 2–302 "as a
  matter of law," with the evidentiary opportunity; "Incidental findings of fact are made by the court rather than by a
  jury, but are accorded the usual weight given to such findings of fact in appellate review."
- cmt g (remedies): "simplest application" is denial of specific performance when the whole contract was unconscionable
  when made; if "entirely executory, denial of money damages may also be appropriate"; "the policy is not penal: unless the
  parties can be restored to their pre-contract positions, the offending party will ordinarily be awarded at least the
  reasonable value of performance rendered by him"; where only a term is unconscionable, "the appropriate remedy is
  ordinarily to deny effect to the unconscionable term."

**1.8 RLCC (2019 TD) § 5.**
- (a) unconscionable contract or term "is unenforceable, to the extent stated in § 9."
- (b) unconscionable "if at the time the contract is made it is: (1) substantively unconscionable, namely fundamentally
  unfair or unreasonably one-sided, **and** (2) procedurally unconscionable, because it results in unfair surprise or
  results from the absence of meaningful choice on the part of the consumer." Sliding scale in black letter: "a greater
  degree of one of the elements ... means that a lesser degree of the other element is sufficient."
- (c) nonexhaustive list of substantively unconscionable effects: (1) unreasonably excluding/limiting the business's
  liability or the consumer's remedies for death or personal injury, or for loss caused by the business's intentional or
  negligent act; (2) unreasonably expanding the consumer's liability or the business's remedies or enforcement powers;
  (3) unreasonably limiting the consumer's ability to pursue or express a complaint or seek reasonable redress.
- (d) "the court should afford the parties a reasonable opportunity to present evidence as to its commercial setting,
  purpose, and effect" ("should," not § 2–302(2)'s "shall").
- cmt 1: both prongs; examine "expected effects as they appear at the time that the contract was made"; procedural
  required "except in rare circumstances."
- cmt 2 (sliding scale): "In general, both the substantive and the procedural prongs are necessary ... But they need not be
  present in the same degree"; "in appropriate circumstances a high degree of substantive unconscionability is sufficient
  to find that a standard contract term, ordinarily not one of the core-deal terms ..., is unconscionable."
- cmt 4(b): cross-collateral clauses, waiver-of-defense clauses, debt-collection clauses may be substantively
  unconscionable. Illus 11: a retail installment-sales grant of a nonpossessory security interest in household goods other
  than those acquired in the transaction is unfair under the FTC Credit Practices Rule (16 C.F.R. § 444.2) "and also
  substantively unconscionable." Illus 15: the same clause in long boilerplate presented at closing to consumers of limited
  financial sophistication is procedurally unconscionable.
- cmt 4(c): (c)(3) "does not address the possible interaction ... with federal law, and specifically, it does not address
  its possible preemption under the Federal Arbitration Act." Illus 7 (distant forum / filing fee exceeding remedy) and 8
  (class waiver for low-value claims) apply "be it a public court or a private arbitration tribunal."
- cmt 6(a): standard, non-negotiable form "without more, constitutes the lowest quantum of procedural unconscionability
  and would have to be matched with a high degree of substantive unconscionability."
- cmt 7(b): conspicuous disclosure (ALL CAPS, separate assent) is "not ... a safe harbor."
- cmt 8 (unconscionable price): "can be applied to scrutinize the contract price, but ... with extra care"; excessively high
  price "substantially in excess of the price at which similar products are obtainable in similar consumer transactions
  by like consumers, or bearing no reasonable relationship to the cost" may be substantively unconscionable; procedural
  test "may be more difficult to satisfy" for price; surprise from complex, contingent, or deferred price; literacy and
  numeracy. Illus 19 (cookware $375 vs $125), Illus 20 (135% loan).
- cmt 10 and § 9(a): court should (1) refuse to enforce the contract, (2) enforce the remainder without the term, or (3)
  limit its application; § 9(b) lets the court replace a severed term with a reasonable term, a minimal-correction term,
  or (bad-faith placement) a deterrent term.
- cmt 12: shield vs. sword; cmt 13: consistent with § 208 and § 2–302 "as applied by courts"; "long been understood as being
  comprised of a procedural prong and a substantive prong."

**1.9 Contrast to teach.** Neither § 2–302 nor § 208 states a two-prong requirement or a sliding scale in its text. The
two-prong formulation comes from Leff (1967) and from case law (Williams's "absence of meaningful choice ... together
with ... unreasonably favorable" terms); the RLCC puts both prongs and the sliding scale into black letter. § 208 cmt c
leaves theoretical room for substantive-only unconscionability; RLCC cmt 1 and cmt 2 allow a high degree of substantive
unconscionability to carry a non-core standard term in "rare" or "appropriate" circumstances.

## 2. Section introduction and two views (C&M 679–682)

**2.1** McKinnon v. Benedict (C&M 679, cross-ref to 641): inadequacy "so gross as to be unconscionable and a bar to the
plaintiff's invocation of the extraordinary equitable powers." The UCC (early 1960s) made the doctrine "a defense in its
own right," "no longer limited to actions in equity." Comment 1 excerpt.

**2.2 Campbell Soup Co. v. Wentz (3d Cir. 1948)** (C&M 680): carrot grower, $30/ton cap vs $90 market; buyer's form; clause
barring sale of rejected carrots without Campbell's permission ("carrying a good joke too far"). Court refused specific
performance; "not suggesting that the contract is illegal"; "too hard a bargain to entitle the plaintiff to relief in a
court of conscience." It is the § 208 Illus 1 source. Commercial (business-to-business) case; doctrine has since made its
mark "primarily ... in the area of consumer contracts." State codes extend unconscionability to all contracts (Cal. Civ.
Code § 1670.5; Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 17.45(5)).

**2.3 Llewellyn** (C&M 680–681): lawyers "draft to the edge of the possible"; "the court kicks it over." No jury (681).

**2.4 Leff's distinction, via Eisenberg** (C&M 681–682): procedural = "fault or unfairness in the bargaining process";
substantive = "fault or unfairness in the bargaining outcome—that is, unfairness of terms." Effect was to "domesticate"
the doctrine; Eisenberg (95 Harv. L. Rev. 741 (1982)): the distinction "is too rigid," norms track market deviations from
perfect competition; cites § 208 cmt c. Epstein (18 J.L. & Econ. 293 (1975)): unconscionability legitimate only to police
process defects (duress, fraud, undue influence) and incompetence; the "substantive dimension ... serves only to undercut
the private right of contract."

## 3. Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co., 350 F.2d 445 (D.C. Cir. 1965) (C&M 683–688)

**3.1 Facts.** Printed form "lease" with title retained until total payments equal stated value; repossession on default.
The pro rata clause: payments "credited pro rata on all outstanding leases, bills and accounts." Effect ("rather obscure
provision"): keeps a balance due on every item until all are paid, so each new purchase is secured by everything
previously bought (C&M 683). Two appellants: Thorne (Daveno etc., $391.10, May 12, 1962) and Williams (stereo $514.95,
April 17, 1962; $164 balance before; purchases over years totaled $1,800, payments $1,400 (fn 1)); store sought to replevy
all items since 1957/1958. Court of General Sessions for store; D.C. Court of Appeals affirmed.

**3.2 D.C. Court of Appeals (quoted, C&M 684).** Store knew of $218 monthly stipend and seven children; "We cannot condemn too
strongly appellee's conduct"; but found "no ground upon which this court can declare the contracts in question contrary to
public policy"; invited Congress to legislate.

**3.3 Skelly Wright's majority — holdings.**
- Power: "We do not agree that the court lacked the power to refuse enforcement to contracts found to be unconscionable"
  (C&M 684). Common-law basis (other jurisdictions; Scott v. United States). Question of first impression in D.C. (685).
- UCC § 2–302, enacted by Congress for D.C. **after** these contracts, is not controlling but is "persuasive authority for
  following the rationale of the cases, from which the section is explicitly derived" (685). Fn 10 (Danaher): D.C. Code
  § 28–2–302 effective January 1, 1965.
- Rule: "where the element of unconscionability is present at the time a contract is made, the contract should not be
  enforced" (685).
- Definition: "Unconscionability has generally been recognized to include an absence of meaningful choice on the part of
  one of the parties together with contract terms which are unreasonably favorable to the other party" (685).
- Meaningful choice: "can only be determined by consideration of all the circumstances surrounding the transaction"; often
  "negated by a gross inequality of bargaining power"; "manner in which the contract was entered is also relevant": "Did
  each party ..., considering his obvious education or lack of it, have a reasonable opportunity to understand the terms
  of the contract, or were the important terms hidden in a maze of fine print and minimized by deceptive sales
  practices?" (685). Fn 4: "a one-sided bargain is itself evidence of the inequality of the bargaining parties."
- Assent: ordinarily a signer without full knowledge assumes the risk; but "when a party of little bargaining power, and
  hence little real choice, signs a commercially unreasonable contract with little or no knowledge of its terms, it is
  hardly likely that his consent, or even an objective manifestation of his consent, was ever given to all the terms";
  then "the court should consider whether the terms of the contract are so unfair that enforcement should be withheld"
  (686).
- Measuring point and substantive test: "the primary concern must be with the terms of the contract considered in light of
  the circumstances existing when the contract was made"; "in the light of the general commercial background and the
  commercial needs of the particular trade or case"; Corbin: "so extreme as to appear unconscionable according to the mores
  and business practices of the time and place" — "the test to be applied in those cases where no meaningful choice was
  exercised" (686).
- Disposition: "no findings were made on the possible unconscionability of the contracts"; "the record is not sufficient for
  our deciding the issue as a matter of law"; "the cases must be remanded to the trial court for further proceedings"
  (686). **The D.C. Circuit did not find the clause or the contracts unconscionable.** It reversed the premise that the
  court lacked power and remanded for findings.

**3.4 Danaher dissent (C&M 686–687).** The lower court "made no finding that there had actually been sharp practice";
"appellant seems to have known precisely where she stood"; public-policy questions (luxury vs necessity; oversight of
relief spending; relief clients "may well need credit"; sellers' "pricing policies" reflect risk); possible remedy in the
"Loan Shark" law; urges "a cautious approach ... since the law for so long has allowed parties such great latitude";
"thousands upon thousands of installment credit transactions"; would affirm. Fn 10: D.C. § 2–302 not effective until
1/1/1965.

**3.5 Notes after Williams.**
- Note 1 (687–688): What did the court hold; what on remand? Allocation: if payments had been applied to each item's
  original debt, she "would evidently have paid in full for about a dozen of the 16 items"; could the store be compelled to
  reallocate as "a plausible reading of the contract?" Cross-collateral ("dragnet") clause: seller repossesses one item for
  failure to pay for another; deficiency judgment if resale yields less; surplus must be accounted for but is unlikely on
  second-hand consumer goods; so what function does repossession serve (leverage)? Would explaining the clauses have
  changed the holding? Terms vs process vs characteristics of the buyer.
- Note 2 (688–689): store's credit-dependent clientele, in-home collectors timed to benefit checks.
- Note 3 (689): legal aid would concede the stereo; what stirred them was the store's attempt "to scoop up all that it had
  ever sold." Precedent vs settlement; lawyer's duty.
- Note 4 (689–690) Remedies: Frostifresh v. Reynoso: freezer, $32 paid of $348; trial court's $316 judgment reversed;
  judgment instead for "net cost ..., plus a reasonable profit, in addition to trucking and service charges necessarily
  incurred and reasonable finance charges." Critiques (Narral; Kripke). Uniform Consumer Credit Code compares the credit
  price with the price at which similar property is "readily obtainable in credit transactions by like consumers"; gross
  disparity is a factor.
- Note 5 (690): rent-to-own; Green v. Continental Rentals (N.J.): "rent" was interest in disguise, unconscionable under the
  N.J. Consumer Fraud Act; TILA excludes rent-to-own (Ortiz).
- Note 6 (690): FTC D.C. study (high markups, modest net profit); Speidel: price over 100% of average prima facie
  unconscionable; Patterson v. Walker-Thomas (discovery of cost/markup).
- Note 7 (690–691): payday lending ($15 per $100 per two weeks = 390% APR); 10 U.S.C. § 987 36% cap for military.

**3.6 Paternalism (C&M 691–692).** Leff: unconscionability and incapacity both stereotype parties; "the benevolent have a
tendency to colonize." Should the focus stay on the transaction rather than the party? Shiffrin: non-paternalist basis — the
community may decline to lend its enforcement to exploitative promises ("self-regarding" reasons); judges "focus far more
on the conduct of the stronger party than on the needs of the weaker party."

## 4. Price unconscionability: Stoll v. Xiong, 241 P.3d 301 (Okla. Civ. App. 2010) (C&M 692–698)

**4.1 Framing (692):** may a price alone make a contract unconscionable; must a court fix intrinsic value or reasonable profit?

**4.2 Facts.** Buyers: Xiong (three years' school in Laos; reads a "couple" English words) and Yang (no schooling in Laos;
six months adult school); their interpreter (Xiong's sister, Yer Lee) explained the land price but did not understand the
litter paragraph. 60 acres at $2,000/acre + $10,000 road = $130,000 (typed "$120,000" [sic]). Paragraph 10: buyers must build
a 43' × 80' concrete-floor litter shed, deposit all litter at the end of each growing cycle; "Seller shall have all rights to
the litter for a period of 30 years [from] the date of closing"; seller empties the shed. Stoll says buyers had a preliminary
version in November 2004 and time to get it explained. Stoll sued for specific performance and an injunction; buyers raised
unconscionability, merger into deed, fraud, etc. Cross motions for summary judgment; trial court: clause unconscionable,
"a prime example"; SJ for buyers on Stoll's petition.

**4.3 Stoll's argument rejected.** He argued the court erred "by considering the fairness of the contract" and anything
"other than fraud, duress, undue influence, mistake, or illegality" (695). Court applies Barnes v. Helfenbein (Okla. 1976):
"no person in his senses ... would make ... and which no fair and honest man would accept"; unconscionability "meaningful
only within the context of otherwise defined factors of onerous inequality, deception and oppression"; "directly related to
fraud and deceit"; basic test at "the time of the making of the contract," "so one-sided as to oppress or unfairly surprise";
"absence of meaningful choice ... together with contractual terms which are unreasonably favorable" (696).

**4.4 Law for the court; UCC by analogy (696).** "The question of unconscionability is one of law for the Court to decide."
Oklahoma's § 2–302 governs sales of goods; via § 208 cmt a, the court uses it by analogy for a land sale.

**4.5 The price computation (697).** About 20 tons per house per de-caking; ≤$12/ton before 2008 (Stoll) or $25 (Yang's
account of Stoll); five flocks a year, six houses: $7,200–$15,000 a year from de-caking alone; 30 years at $12 = $216,000,
"roughly an additional $3,325.12 more per acre" over the $2,000 stated. "Effectively, Stoll either made himself a partner in
their business for no consideration or he would receive almost double to way over double the purchase price."

**4.6 Deed-cancellation analogy and combined holding (697).** Gross inadequacy plus a grantor "feeble-minded and unable to
understand" raises a presumption of fraud; "Under such circumstances, there is no assent to terms." Here "a nearly reverse
situation": the consideration actually to be paid far exceeds that stated; the contract is "onerous to one side ... while
solely benefitting the other, and the parties to be surcharged ... were, due to language and education, unable to understand
the nature of the contract." Stoll valued his land at $2,000/acre vs $1,200 nearby. "The actual price Buyers will pay ...
is so gross as to shock the conscience." Court agrees no fair person would propose and no rational person would accept it.
Judgment affirmed (698). **The holding rests on both the hidden, conscience-shocking price and the buyers' inability to
understand; it is not a price-alone holding.** Remedy: the clause was not enforced and judgment entered for buyers on Stoll's
claims; no reformation or reasonable-profit award (Note 3 asks how the court took Stoll's reasonable profit into account).

**4.7 Notes and problems (698–699).** Note 1: would duress, misrepresentation, incapacity, undue influence fit? Note 2: Hmong
farmers' history; compare Vokes. Note 3: remedy; "What if Stoll had used the revenue from chicken litter when setting his
price for the land?" Problem 1 (Nez Perce): $2.97/acre vs found market value $4 held not unconscionable by the Commission;
"What result?" Problem 2 (Shaffer): discovery of a law firm's profit margins to prove unconscionable fees.

**4.8 Price regulation in crisis (699–700).** N.Y. Gen. Bus. Law § 396-r: "unconscionably excessive price" for vital goods
during "abnormal disruption of the market" tied to a declared state of emergency; seller rebuts a prima facie case by showing
the increase preserved its prior profit margin or reflected added costs; remedies: injunction, civil penalty up to $25,000
per violation or treble gross receipts, whichever is greater, and restitution. People ex rel. James v. Quality King (2022):
statute constitutional, enforcement upheld (Lysol 12-pack $60 → $100). Questions: pure price unconscionability, or does the
emergency create procedural unconscionability?

## 5. Franchises (C&M 700–702)

Courts less willing where bargaining positions are equal: Continental Airlines v. Goodyear (9th Cir. 1987) ("cannot be
invoked by so sophisticated a party ... in reference to a contract so laboriously negotiated"); U.S. Welding v. Battelle
(D. Idaho 2010). But Gianni Sport v. Gantos (Mich. App. 1986) upheld a finding that a cancellation clause imposed by "big
sharks" on small manufacturers was unconscionable. Shell Oil v. Marinello (N.J. 1973): trial court found the 10-day
termination provision unconscionable; N.J. Supreme Court agreed Shell "had no legal right to terminate ... except for good
cause." Zapatha v. Dairy Mart (Mass. 1980): 90-day no-cause termination not unconscionable — no unfair surprise (not
obscure, not in fine print, pointed out, buyer declined to consult a lawyer; contrast Williams) and not oppressive (Dairy
Mart had to repurchase saleable inventory; OCR reads "at 0% of its retail value," likely "90%"; "no potential for forfeiture
or loss of investment"). Note: Automobile Dealers' Day in Court Act; PMPA; FTC franchise disclosure rule.

## 6. Arbitration clauses (C&M 703–706)

- FAA framework: Circuit City v. Adams (2001): employment arbitration agreements generally enforceable under the FAA "save
  for upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract"; unconscionability "is one such
  ground." FAA § 2 (sel.): "valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the
  revocation of any contract."
- Armendariz (Cal.) (703): statutory claims arbitrable if minimum requirements met (neutral arbitrator, adequate discovery,
  written decision, cost limits); only employees bound to arbitrate; several provisions "so one-sided as to 'shock the
  conscience'"; damages limit compounds it. (Casebook cites "24 Cal.4th 83 (2004)"; date as printed.)
- Oblix v. Winiecki (7th Cir. 2004) (704): one-sided employee-only clause enforced; Easterbrook: arbitration agreements
  "must be enforced unless the states would refuse to enforce all off-the-shelf package deals"; "no state can apply to
  arbitration (when governed by the FAA) any novel rule."
- Note 1 (704–705): Pinedo; Villa Milano; Pichly; Baker; Shubin (hurry/pressure, no chance to modify → procedural
  unconscionability established); Samaniego (non-English readers refused translation; arbitration rules not provided; 11
  dense pages; clause unconscionable; Concepcion "does not change our analysis").
- Note 2 Severability (705): Cal. Civ. Code § 1670.5(a) (modeled on § 2–302; applies to all contracts): refuse whole, sever,
  or limit. Graham v. Scissor-Tail: union-of-one-party arbitrator fails "minimum levels of integrity"; court severed that
  provision and remanded for selection of a suitable arbitrator. Bolter: Utah forum provision severed, rest enforced. Cross-
  ref Ch. 7, pp. 872–874.
- Note 3 Geese and Ganders (705–706): Eisenberg & Miller (11% of 2,800 firm contracts arbitrate); Sanga replication (19%
  overall; 41% of employment agreements).
- Prasad v. Pinnacle (N.D. Cal. 2018) begins on 706 (facts only within the assigned pages; it continues on 707).

## 7. Anderson v. Binance, 2026 WL 538293 (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 26, 2026) (Supp. 94–101)

- **What the case decides.** It is an assent (contract-formation) and modification case. It does not decide
  unconscionability. "The dispositive question in this case is whether Plaintiffs assented to the 2019 Terms of Use" (96).
- **FAA's role.** "While federal policy generally favors arbitration, the obligation to arbitrate nevertheless remains a
  creature of contract ... 'a party cannot be required to submit to arbitration any dispute which he has not agreed so to
  submit'" (96, Louis Dreyfus, quoting AT&T Techs.). "The Court must first decide if there is an agreement to arbitrate."
  State law governs formation (Specht; Perry v. Thomas: state law applies "if that law arose to govern issues concerning
  the validity, revocability, and enforceability of contracts generally") (97). The excerpt does not cite FAA § 2 by name.
- **Facts.** 2017 Terms (clickwrap) had no arbitration, choice-of-law, or class waiver, but had a change-of-terms clause
  (amend by announcing on the website, effective immediately; continued use = acceptance) (95). Feb. 20, 2019 Terms (posted;
  browsewrap) added SIAC arbitration seated in Singapore and Singapore law; warned of a class waiver that no provision
  actually contained (95–96).
- **Choice of law.** Applying the Singapore clause to decide formation puts "the cart before the horse" (Berkson;
  Schnabel). Interest analysis → California, Nevada, Texas law, substantively similar (97–98).
- **Notice.** Passive assent turns on "whether a reasonably prudent offeree would be on notice" (98); reasonable notice may
  be actual or constructive. No constructive notice: no individual notice, no record evidence the clause was "announced,"
  no indication where to look; "[p]arties to a contract have no obligation to check the terms on a periodic basis" (Douglas);
  otherwise drafters could bind users "daily, or even hourly" (Stover) (99). Needleman distinguished (individual statement
  insert with opt-out to a user who agreed to online communications) (98–99). Elided holding: all plaintiffs had actual
  notice by June 8, 2020 (99).
- **Retroactivity.** Even with notice, the 2019 Terms do not apply to claims accrued before Feb. 20, 2019. Peleg (Cal. App.
  2012): a unilateral-modification clause expressly reaching known or accrued unfiled claims is illusory; one silent on
  the point is "impliedly restricted by the covenant [of good faith and fair dealing] so that changes do not apply to such
  claims" (100). Binance's clause is silent → cannot reach claims accrued or known before Feb. 20, 2019. Motion to compel
  arbitration DENIED (100).
- fn 2 (100–101): motion to compel is decided on a standard similar to summary judgment; reasonable inferences for the
  non-movant. fn 1: Zhao pardoned (Oct. 2025).
- **Teaching links.** Shows the FAA's "save upon such grounds" structure from the other side: the FAA does not supply
  assent; ordinary state contract law (formation, notice, implied covenant, illusoriness) decides whether an arbitration
  clause exists and what claims it reaches. Connects to unconscionability only by contrast (a court reaches
  unconscionability only after finding an agreement) and through RLCC § 5(c)(3)/cmt 4(c).

## 8. Ranked teaching points to check against the Atlas

1. Williams held only that a court has power to refuse enforcement of an unconscionable contract and remanded for findings;
   it did not find the clause unconscionable.
2. Williams test: absence of meaningful choice **together with** unreasonably favorable terms; measured at the time of
   contracting; Corbin test applies where no meaningful choice was exercised.
3. Who decides: the court, as a matter of law; no jury; § 2–302(2) evidentiary opportunity on commercial setting, purpose,
   effect; § 208 cmt f incidental findings of fact by the court.
4. Remedies: refuse the whole (Comment 2: if permeated), sever the clause, or limit its application; § 208 cmt g not penal,
   denial of specific performance simplest; Frostifresh reasonable-profit measure; Graham/Bolter severance.
5. Both elements: case law and RLCC require both; RLCC sliding scale; § 208 cmt c theoretical substantive-only; UCC/§ 208
   text does not state the two-prong test.
6. Stoll: combined substantive (hidden price far exceeding stated, ~$216,000) and procedural (language/education); law for the
   court on summary judgment; § 2–302 by analogy; affirmed for buyers.
7. Price unconscionability: inadequacy alone insufficient (§ 208 cmt c; Nez Perce problem); RLCC cmt 8 extra care; § 396-r.
8. Anderson: not an unconscionability holding; FAA favors arbitration but assent is a state-law contract question; no
   constructive notice; implied-covenant limit on retroactivity; motion denied.
9. Danaher: legislative-deference, credit-access, no finding of sharp practice.
