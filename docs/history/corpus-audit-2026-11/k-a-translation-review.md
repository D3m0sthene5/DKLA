## CT-CB-0002 · `hawkins 3.0`

**Atlas** If McGee promised a good or perfect hand, damages measured the value to Hawkins of that hand against the actual hand, with incidental consequences fairly within the parties’ contemplation.

**≈** If McGee promised a good or perfect hand, "damages" were the value to Hawkins of that hand minus the value of the hand he actually got. He could also recover incidental losses fairly within the "parties’ contemplation," meaning ones both sides could have foreseen.

**Confirmed.** The jury could find that McGee promised a good hand. The value difference is correct. For an incidental loss, imagining it was possible sets the bar too low and leaves the time of assessment unclear. Suppose an extra trip to a specialist was conceivable when they agreed, but neither party contemplated it or had reason to expect it would probably follow a breach. Mere possibility would qualify the trip under the gloss. [Hawkins v. McGee, 84 N.H. 114, 117–18 (1929), discussion of incidental losses and application to the hand](https://law.justia.com/cases/new-hampshire/supreme-court/1929/84-nh-114.html).

**Replace** “meaning ones both sides could have foreseen” with “meaning losses that, when they made the contract, both sides knew or should have known would probably follow from a breach”.

## CT-CB-0004 · `bayliner 1.1`

**Atlas** His personal speed requirement differed from ordinary trade fitness and from what he conveyed at sale.

**≈** Crow personally needed a boat that could go about thirty miles per hour, but that is not what boats of this kind ordinarily must do, and he had not made it clear to the dealer. That matters because the warranty claims turned on ordinary standards for such boats or on what the seller knew or said.

**Confirmed.** Crow described his own trips. The Virginia Supreme Court held that his evidence failed to establish a general speed requirement or that he had conveyed his minimum to Atherton, the dealer’s sales representative. Suppose a later buyer supplies credible trade evidence that boats in this class must reach 30 mph. The holding leaves that showing open. The gloss tells the student the court has already settled the issue against that buyer. [Bayliner Marine Corp. v. Crow, No. 980392, slip op. 8–10, 11–12 (Va. Jan. 8, 1999)](https://www.vacourts.gov/static/opinions/opnscvwp/1980392.pdf#page=9).

**Replace the first sentence with** “Crow needed about thirty miles per hour. He did not show that this speed was ordinarily required of offshore fishing boats or that he had made clear to the dealer that anything slower was unacceptable.”

## CT-CB-0004 · `bayliner 3.0`

**Atlas** For merchantability under § 2-314, Crow supplied no trade standard or evidence that a substantial segment of buyers regarded boats with this capability as unacceptable.

**≈** To prove a breach of "merchantability" under § 2-314, meaning the boat was not fit for ordinary use, Crow needed proof of a trade standard. He supplied none, and no evidence that a substantial segment of buyers regarded boats with this speed capability as unacceptable.

**Confirmed on the definition only.** This ≈ already reports a failure of proof, and its trade-standard requirement tracks the court’s express language. The error is defining merchantability solely by ordinary use. A boat could do the ordinary work of offshore fishing yet be so slow that a significant share of buyers would object under the trade standard. On those facts § 8.2-314(2)(a) remains a ground for breach even if (2)(c) is satisfied. Crow supplied no such trade evidence. [Bayliner, slip op. 8–10, statutory tests on p. 9 and evidence of actual fishing on p. 10](https://www.vacourts.gov/static/opinions/opnscvwp/1980392.pdf#page=9).

**Replace** “meaning the boat was not fit for ordinary use” with “which required the boat to pass without objection in its trade and be fit for ordinary use”.

## CT-CB-0004 · `bayliner 3.1`

**Atlas** Section 2-315’s particular-purpose warranty required reason to know Crow’s special purpose and reliance on seller judgment.

**≈** Section 2-315’s particular-purpose warranty required that the seller had reason to know Crow’s special purpose for the boat. It also required that Crow relied on the seller's "judgment," or skill, to pick suitable goods.

**Confirmed.** Suppose the seller knows about the 30-mph minimum. Crow privately relies on the seller’s judgment but gives the seller every reason to think he chose the model himself. Both requirements in the gloss are met. The separate requirement that the seller have reason to know of Crow’s reliance fails. In the actual appeal Crow lost at the earlier step of proving he conveyed his minimum speed. K-A’s proposed edit restores the seller’s perspective; retain the original’s express requirement that Crow actually relied, too. [Va. Code § 8.2-315](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title8.2/part3/section8.2-315/) · [Medcom, Inc. v. C. Arthur Weaver Co., 232 Va. 80, 84–85 (1986), three elements](https://law.justia.com/cases/virginia/supreme-court/1986/830540-1.html) · [Bayliner, slip op. 10–12](https://www.vacourts.gov/static/opinions/opnscvwp/1980392.pdf#page=11).

**Replace the ≈ with** “For § 2-315’s particular-purpose warranty, Crow had to rely on the seller’s skill or judgment to choose a suitable boat. At the time of contracting, the seller had to have reason to know both Crow’s particular purpose and that he was relying on the seller.”
