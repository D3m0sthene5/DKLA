# Moulton v. Kershaw
59 Wis. 316, 18 N.W. 172 (Wis. 1884) | Contracts

## Decision and posture

The Wisconsin Supreme Court held that the salt dealers' letter solicited orders rather than making an offer that bound them upon the buyer's response. It reversed the order overruling their general demurrer and remanded for further proceedings according to law. Taylor wrote the opinion; the complete report contains no separate judicial writing or judicial footnote. The report does not identify a numerical voting division. This was a decision on the sufficiency of the complaint's alleged contract, not a trial determining whether the buyer actually suffered the $800 damages he demanded. (59 Wis. at 316–319, 322–323.)

## Facts alleged and the parties' arguments

Moulton alleged that Kershaw and Son dealt in salt in Milwaukee, including Michigan Salt Association salt, while he dealt in La Crosse and customarily bought large quantities, a practice known to the defendants. Their September 19, 1882 letter described an opportunity arising from a rupture in the salt trade. They were authorized to offer Michigan fine salt in carloads of eighty to ninety-five barrels, delivered in his city for eighty-five cents a barrel, with shipment only over the Chicago and North Western railroad. They described the price as a bargain compared with generally unchanged prices and expressed pleasure at receiving his order. The complaint alleged that the defendants acted on their own responsibility rather than as agents for the association. (Id. at 316–318.)

Moulton received the letter September 20 and telegraphed an order for 2,000 barrels on the stated terms, requesting an answer. He alleged that the defendants received the telegram that day and that this was a reasonable quantity which they could reasonably expect in light of his business. On September 21, they notified him that they were withdrawing their supposed offer. He demanded delivery, offered payment on the stated terms, and alleged readiness to receive and pay for the salt. Their refusal allegedly caused $800 in damages. The circuit court overruled their general demurrer, and they appealed. (Id. at 317–318.)

The buyer construed the letter as an offer to furnish any reasonable quantity, no smaller than a carload, that the sellers could reasonably anticipate he would order. The sellers construed it as a business communication stating their prices and seeking patronage. The court treated the correspondence as the only communications on which the asserted contract could rest. Buyer’s counsel did not contend that recovery was possible unless those writings, properly construed, themselves created the binding agreement. (Id. at 319–320.)

## Why the letter was a solicitation

The court rejected the proposed reasonable-quantity construction because the supposed commitment was not expressed in the writings. Whether a particular order was reasonably anticipated would require examination of the buyer's business and the sellers' business in each case. That added factual qualification would make the existence of the obligation depend on evidence outside the correspondence. The opinion also invoked the then-applicable statute of frauds for sales of personal property exceeding $50, under which the contract had to be in writing; it would not add a new contractual commitment by parol evidence. This is the court's historical statutory and interpretive analysis, not a statement of present Uniform Commercial Code requirements. (Id. at 319–320.)

The alternative literal reading—an offer to fill any quantity the buyer selected, provided it was at least one carload—would create a commitment the buyer's counsel themselves did not insist upon. The court found it more reasonable to read the letter as notice that the sellers could supply salt at the stated prices and an invitation to deal with them. Preliminary negotiations should not be converted into an agreement the parties did not express. (Id. at 320–321.)

The wording supported that conclusion. Although the letter used the word “offer,” it did not say that the defendants offered to sell to this buyer a particular quantity, any quantity he might select, or a reasonable quantity. Its general language, price comparison, and request for an order resembled an advertisement or circular directed to participants in the salt trade. The decisive issue was the meaning of the actual letter, not the presence or absence of a single magic word. Because that communication was not an offer, the telegraphed order did not by itself perfect the asserted contract. The next day's notice therefore did not constitute revocation of an offer already accepted on these allegations. (Id. at 319, 321–323.)

## Express reservation concerning open quantities

The court expressly declined to hold that a seller could never make a binding offer leaving quantity for the buyer to specify. If the letter had promised to sell all the Michigan fine salt the buyer ordered on the stated terms, a timely order could bind the sellers to a reasonable amount, and possibly any amount, or expose them to damages. That was a hypothetical illustration distinguishing an expressed commitment from the actual circular, not an adjudication of the precise limits of every open-quantity contract. (Id. at 321.)

The opinion illustrated the distinction through a cited grape-sale case. There, the seller actually offered to deliver as many grapes from the year's crop as the purchaser wished to take; the buyer selected 1,900 pounds before withdrawal. The offer expressly delegated quantity selection, while the crop itself bounded the seller's maximum undertaking. The salt letter contained neither that promise nor that inventory limitation. The Wisconsin court placed its own decision on the language before it rather than declaring all price quotations incapable of being offers. (Id. at 321–322.)

## Result, assigned account, and source scope

The complaint did not show a contract, so the demurrer should have been sustained. The Supreme Court reversed the circuit-court order and remanded for further proceedings; it did not award damages or report a final damages judgment for either party. (Id. at 322–323.)
