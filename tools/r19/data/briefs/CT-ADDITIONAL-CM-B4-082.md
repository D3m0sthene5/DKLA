# In re TJX Companies Retail Security Breach Litigation
564 F.3d 489 (1st Cir. 2009) | Contracts

## Decision and contractual holding
The First Circuit affirmed dismissal of issuing banks' contract claims arising from a large payment-card data breach. Massachusetts beneficiary doctrine permitted the direct contracting parties to exclude third-party enforcement, and the merchant agreement and card-network regulations consistently did so. The court nevertheless revived a Massachusetts unfair-practices theory and vacated an unauthorized transfer to state court. Judge Boudin wrote for Boudin, Lipez, and Howard, with no separate opinions. The preserved report identifies March 30, 2009 as the decision date and May 5, 2009 as the amendment on rehearing in part; this brief describes that published amended text. (489, 491, 499, 501–502.)

## The breach and payment relationships
In January 2007, TJX, a Massachusetts discount-store operator, announced that hackers had stolen millions of customers' credit- and debit-card records. Banks that issued the cards allegedly suffered reimbursement costs from fraudulent use, monitoring expenses, and cancellation and replacement costs. Fifth Third, an Ohio processing bank and its parent, processed TJX transactions, transmitted payment information, and authorized transactions following verification with issuing banks. Visa and MasterCard operated the networks and imposed security requirements through their contracts with participating banks; Fifth Third's merchant contract likewise imposed requirements on TJX. (491–492.)

AmeriFirst filed suit in May 2007 and sought a class of issuing banks. SELCO, an Oregon credit union, participated in a second action against Fifth Third, consolidated with the first. Plaintiffs alleged failures to observe measures such as firewalls, data protection, encryption, and access tracking, with breaches from July 2005 onward discovered and disclosed later. These were complaint-stage allegations, not trial findings about the extent of either defendant's security failures. (491–492 & n. 1.)

## District proceedings, appeal, and jurisdiction
The district court dismissed negligence and contract claims but sustained negligent misrepresentation and a Chapter 93A theory resting on it. Negligence per se was dismissed and not at issue on appeal. Plaintiffs sought to add conversion and additional unfair-practices facts. The court denied amendment, finally denied class status, and ordered transfer to Massachusetts Superior Court, describing its previous rulings as open to reconsideration there. Defendants appealed the surviving claims and transfer; plaintiffs cross-appealed dismissed claims and amendment rulings and disputed the implications for certification. (492–493.)

The federal jurisdictional basis had been CAFA's minimal diversity for class actions. The court recognized provisional jurisdiction to decide merits questions bearing on whether a certifiable class existed. Defendants were concretely harmed by having to continue litigating the surviving claims, rather than merely concerned about possible future preclusion. Although denial of a dismissal motion ordinarily is not immediately appealable, the district court's purported transfer ended its proceedings and could not defeat review when the court lacked transfer authority. Its without-prejudice terminology was not an invitation to cure the complaint and refile in the same court. (492–494.)

## Express exclusion of beneficiary enforcement
Massachusetts followed Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 302, including its qualification that promisor and promisee may agree otherwise. The TJX–Fifth Third contract specifically limited benefit and enforcement to the bank and merchant and denied both to any third party. Issuing banks were not parties to that contract or to the relevant network contracts, so they needed beneficiary status and could not establish it against this exclusion. (499.)

Plaintiffs argued that Visa and MasterCard regulations superseded the merchant clause because the regulations prevailed in a conflict. There was no conflict. MasterCard reserved the sole right to interpret and enforce its regulations; Visa expressly denied third-party-beneficiary status and third-party rights or claims. The provisions therefore reinforced, rather than displaced, the merchant agreement's restriction. The court affirmed dismissal without announcing that every security obligation can be enforced by every participant economically affected by a payment network. (499.)

## Negligence, misrepresentation, and amendment
The economic-loss doctrine barred the simple negligence theory because plaintiffs alleged purely economic injury without personal injury or physical property damage. A property interest in electronic data did not establish the missing physical destruction; neither policy objections nor a request to certify clear state law justified changing the result. (498–499.)

Misrepresentation was more provisional. The complaint expressly alleged misrepresentation, but plaintiffs' developed argument largely treated participation in credit-card transactions as an implied representation of compliance with all detailed security requirements. The court considered that implication too loose to support Massachusetts negligent-misrepresentation liability and contrasted cases with a closer connection between words, conduct, and the represented fact. Note 2 addresses an asserted website listing but explains why it did not clearly supply an affirmative representation and was not the theory defended on appeal. (494–495 & n. 2.)

Nevertheless, the court stopped short of requiring dismissal on the complaint. A facially pleaded claim could be tested more appropriately through summary judgment once the actual evidentiary theory became clear. It therefore sustained both the negligent-misrepresentation claim and its derivative Chapter 93A theory, expressly leaving defendants free to seek summary judgment. This was not a finding that transaction processing itself constituted actionable misrepresentation. (495–496; 501–502.)

Conversion was proposed belatedly despite no newly discovered facts. The court left debatable whether Massachusetts conversion was confined to tangible property and whether customer data stolen by hackers could fit appropriation by the store. It affirmed denial of amendment under abuse-of-discretion review because the claim could have been raised earlier, rather than adopting a categorical ban on conversion of intangible property. The same timing reasoning supported denial of the proposed additional Chapter 93A allegations. (499–500 & n. 7.)

## Independent unfairness theory under Chapter 93A
The district court had rejected plaintiffs' alternative Chapter 93A theory based on FTC unfairness standards, treating consent decrees as insufficient authority. The appellate court disagreed. General FTC criteria and a body of complaints and decrees could inform whether alleged protracted, reckless security failures and delayed disclosure were unfair and highly injurious. Consent decrees were ordinarily instructive, not conclusive proof of either facts or liability. Deliberate wrongdoing or personal enrichment was not invariably necessary; systematic recklessness might suffice. The court revived the theory on the allegations rather than determining liability. The separate Gramm-Leach-Bliley theory had been abandoned. (495–498 & nn. 3–5.)

At the pleading stage, TJX's requests for payments through Fifth Third could establish a sufficiently close business relationship with issuing banks. Fifth Third's alleged Massachusetts offices and communications with TJX's Massachusetts servers also prevented rejecting the claim then on the statutory geographic limitation. Those conclusions left fact-dependent defenses available after discovery. (497–498.)

The court cautioned that using federal standards in a state claim did not automatically supply embedded federal-question jurisdiction. Whether the federal issue was sufficiently substantial, and whether Massachusetts treated the standards as binding rather than informative, would require proper briefing if that alternative jurisdictional route became necessary. No independent federal-question holding was made. (498 & n. 6.)

## Certification, transfer, and exact judgment
Plaintiffs did not appeal the denial of certification for the misrepresentation claim or its derivative Chapter 93A theory. That denial had depended principally on individual reliance. The revived unfairness theory focused on defendants' conduct and might present different commonality and predominance questions, though individual causation, class definition, and representational conflicts could still matter. The court left certification of that theory to the district court in the first instance and declined either side's request to settle it preemptively. It did not certify a class itself. (500–501.)

Binding circuit precedent foreclosed the purported transfer to state court; no specialized state transfer statute was invoked. If federal jurisdiction ultimately failed, the ordinary response was dismissal rather than this transfer. The transfer's accompanying without-prejudice gloss fell with the vacated order; the appellate court did not authorize a federal court to decide merits and then erase the ordinary rules of preclusion. (501.)

The court affirmed dismissal of negligence and contract, denial of amendment, and sustaining misrepresentation and its Chapter 93A derivative, without prejudice to defendants' summary-judgment motions. It vacated dismissal of the Chapter 93A unfairness theory and vacated the transfer and no-prejudice ruling, remanding for further proceedings. Each party bore its own appellate costs. No damages or security-breach liability were finally adjudicated. (501–502.)
