# Finley v. United States

**Citation and court:** 490 U.S. 545 (1989), Supreme Court of the United States, No. 87–1973, decided May 22, 1989. Justice Scalia wrote for Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices White, O'Connor, and Kennedy. Justice Blackmun dissented separately; Justice Stevens dissented, joined by Brennan and Marshall. The result was 5–4.

**Assigned reading and complete source:** CivPro, physical pages 203–204, printed 361–364, entire Finley account and retained Stevens quotations; ensuing supplemental-jurisdiction commentary and four notes on physical 205 were read as context. Linked editorial notes j and k on physical 219 identify original alterations and emphasis. All four original scans were checked. The complete 36-page official report, 545–580, was read before drafting: Scalia's opinion at 546–556 and all six notes, Blackmun's complete dissent at 556–558 with no judicial notes, and Stevens's complete dissent at 558–580 and all thirty-seven notes. There are no appendices.

**Facts and chronology.** On November 11, 1983, a twin-engine plane carrying Finley's husband and two children struck electrical transmission lines while approaching a San Diego airfield; no one survived. Finley first sued in state court, claiming the utility had negligently positioned and inadequately illuminated the lines and the city had negligently maintained runway lights that were inoperative that night. She later learned that the Federal Aviation Administration was responsible for the runway lights and brought a federal action against the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act. That complaint alleged negligent operation and maintenance of the lights and negligent air-traffic-control functions. These were allegations supporting the tort claims, not liability findings made by the Supreme Court. 490 U.S. at 546–547.

Almost a year later, she sought to add the city and utility to the federal action. There was no independent federal jurisdictional basis for her claims against them. The district court nevertheless permitted amendment, relying on Gibbs, a shared nucleus of operative facts, and the efficiency of trying the accident claims together. It certified an interlocutory appeal under § 1292(b). The Ninth Circuit summarily reversed under its precedent categorically rejecting pendent-party jurisdiction in FTCA actions. The Supreme Court took the case to resolve a circuit conflict. Id. at 546–547.

**Issue and holding.** Did the FTCA's grant of jurisdiction over civil actions on claims against the United States also permit Finley to join related state-law claims against additional defendants without independent federal jurisdiction? The Court held **no under the statute then governing**. It assumed, without deciding, that Article III could encompass the additional claims; the defect was lack of statutory authorization. It expressly preserved the Gibbs line permitting pendent claims between parties already properly before the court. Id. at 548–549, 556.

**Constitutional capacity is not statutory power.** A lower federal court requires both constitutional capacity and a congressional jurisdictional grant. Gibbs had permitted sufficiently related federal and state claims to constitute one constitutional case, but the majority declined to transfer that approach automatically to new parties. Finley's city and utility were not named in any independently cognizable federal claim. Their factual connection with the claim against the United States did not itself establish Congress's authorization to adjudicate their liability. Id. at 547–549 & nn.1–2.

**Precedents and posture.** Scalia read Zahn, Aldinger, and Kroger as establishing that a grant concerning particular parties did not automatically extend to claims involving different parties. Aldinger required attention to Congress's treatment of the proposed additional defendant; Kroger required attention to both statutory text and the posture in which the claim appeared. The majority distinguished adding a new defendant from a counterclaim between parties already before the court. Its note 3 recognized Monell's intervening change to the municipal-liability premise discussed in Aldinger. Id. at 549–551 & nn.2–3.

The Court acknowledged narrow ancillary situations involving assets already under a court's control or proceedings necessary to make its judgment effective. The fraudulent-transfer counterclaim in Dewey supplied an example of this different necessity: a judgment could be useless unless transferred assets were recovered. Finley's independent accident claims instead had factual overlap, not that relation to the court's ability to protect its judgment or controlled property. Convenience alone could not bridge the jurisdictional gap. Id. at 551–552 & n.4.

**Text, exclusivity, and joinder rules.** Section 1346(b) referred to civil actions on claims against the United States. The majority read that language as describing the permissible defendants, rather than merely requiring one United States claim somewhere within a larger lawsuit. Congress had not instead referred to actions that included such a claim. The fact that Finley had to use federal court for her FTCA claim did not change the grant's scope, even though it made consolidated litigation impossible. The majority also invoked Sherwood's exclusion of private defendants under the Tucker Act. Id. at 552–553.

The dissent's reliance on Rules 14 and 20 did not persuade Scalia: procedural permission to implead or join parties could not independently extend subject-matter jurisdiction, as Rule 82 made explicit. The Court was not saying that adding an unsupported claim destroyed jurisdiction over the existing FTCA claim. It was saying that jurisdiction did not reach the new claim. Id. at 552–553 & nn.5–6.

**The 1948 revision.** Finley argued that replacing jurisdiction over any claim against the United States with jurisdiction over civil actions on such claims broadened the grant. The Court treated the change as a stylistic accommodation to the Federal Rules' unified civil-action terminology. It found no clearly expressed substantive expansion in the comprehensive recodification. In contrast, Congress had used explicit language when addressing related unfair-competition claims in § 1338(b). The majority wanted a stable interpretive rule against which Congress could legislate; Congress remained free to change the result. Id. at 553–556.

**Blackmun's dissent.** Blackmun thought the majority had asked the wrong question. Aldinger called for an inquiry into whether Congress excluded the proposed additional party, rather than a demand for an express affirmative grant covering every pendent party. Municipalities had been treated as substantively excluded from § 1983 when Aldinger was decided. No corresponding exclusion of private tort defendants appeared in the FTCA. Mere omission of a party from a jurisdictional grant could not itself show the relevant exclusion, because such omission was the premise of every pendent-party problem. Id. at 556–557.

Blackmun also distinguished Sherwood's specific Court of Claims history from tort jurisdiction. Federal exclusivity made the district court the only forum capable of hearing the whole constitutional case, strengthening the case for permitting jurisdiction. He would allow the combined proceeding rather than create a categorical statutory obstacle. Id. at 557–558.

**Stevens's dissent.** Stevens, Brennan, and Marshall likewise read Aldinger to require congressional negation of otherwise available pendent jurisdiction. Stevens explained constitutional case jurisdiction, distinguished it from personal jurisdiction, and argued that related claims and parties could be handled within a case properly placed before the court. Historical ancillary decisions, the Federal Rules, Gibbs, and subsequent lower-court practice supplied his framework. He discussed Judge Friendly's decisions in detail as evidence that related additional parties were a familiar part of this doctrine. Id. at 558–571 & nn.1–24.

Stevens disputed the majority's analogy to diversity cases. Zahn's amount requirement and Kroger's complete-diversity requirement represented specific congressional limitations; they did not establish an equivalent barrier in a case placed exclusively in federal court. Splitting the FTCA controversy could impair the practical value of Congress's federal forum. His notes considered compulsory counterclaims, distinct jurisdictional contexts, and the historical treatment of contribution claims. Id. at 571–578 & nn.25–36.

Different jury rights, administrative-claim requirements, defenses, or damage rules could weigh against exercising jurisdiction or require separate trials. Stevens treated those difficulties as matters of discretion and management, not categorical absence of power. He also challenged narrow sovereign-immunity construction through Yellow Cab and the distinction between a separate contribution action and third-party practice. His final note collected broader reasoning supporting generous construction once governmental consent was given. These were dissenting arguments, not the Court's controlling rule. Id. at 570–572 & nn.24–25; 578–580 & n.37.

**Exact disposition and later assigned context.** The Supreme Court **affirmed the Ninth Circuit's judgment** rejecting the addition of the city and utility claims to this FTCA suit. Id. at 556. It did not adjudicate negligence, reverse a damages verdict, or decide that Article III forbade the combined controversy.

The assignment then identifies Congress's 1990 response through § 1367. The archived [statutory text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/1367) confirms an express grant covering sufficiently related claims involving additional parties, subject to statutory exceptions, diversity limitations, and grounds for declining exercise. That later enactment supplies the bridge the Finley majority would not infer. It must not be read backward into the 1989 judgment or treated as eliminating every jurisdictional limit. The following assignment notes discuss counterclaims, impleader, and amount requirements as further statutory questions. This brief verifies the historical opinion, complete assigned account, and that bounded statutory comparison; it does not certify all later decisions or current FTCA litigation consequences.
