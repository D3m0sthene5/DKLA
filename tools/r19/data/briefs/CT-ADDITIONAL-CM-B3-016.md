# Gianni Sport Ltd. v. Gantos, Inc.
151 Mich. App. 598, 391 N.W.2d 760 (1986) | Contracts

## Court, issue, and result
The Michigan Court of Appeals decided this commercial-sales appeal on April 28, 1986. The per curiam panel consisted of Presiding Judge D. F. Walsh, Judge Hood, and Circuit Judge K. N. Hansen sitting by assignment. It affirmed in all respects the trial court's $27,290 judgment for the clothing manufacturer, including refusal to enforce the retailer's unilateral cancellation clause. It also rejected the manufacturer's cross-appeal seeking additional damages on other transactions. (598–599, 603–604.)

## Facts and procedural history
Gianni Sport was a New York manufacturer and distributor of women's clothing; Gantos was a retailer headquartered in Grand Rapids. They had dealt with each other for more than two years. On June 10, 1980, Gantos ordered holiday clothing for delivery on October 10. The back of its purchase order reserved a right, by notice, to terminate all or part of the order for goods not yet shipped, or goods not timely delivered for any reason. Gantos canceled in late September. The trial court found that Gianni subsequently agreed to a fifty-percent price reduction to induce Gantos to accept the goods anyway, but invalidated that concession because the unconscionable cancellation clause had made it necessary. (599–600, 602.)

Following a bench trial in Kent Circuit Court, Gianni obtained $27,290. Gantos appealed the unconscionability determination. Gianni cross-appealed findings concerning a price reduction on a separate shipment arriving two weeks late and a $2,000 advertising allowance. Thus the appeal involved both the enforceability of the cancellation power and distinct disputed concessions; the court did not treat every price reduction in the parties' dealings as invalid. (599–600, 603–604.)

## Rules and arguments
Michigan's enactment of UCC section 2-302 required an opportunity to present evidence about a disputed term's commercial setting, purpose, and effect. The relevant inquiry examined one-sidedness against the trade's commercial background and needs at formation. The official comment emphasized preventing oppression and unfair surprise, while preserving reasonable risk allocation despite superior bargaining power. Although the opinion described unconscionability as a question of law, it expressly applied the then-stated clearly-erroneous review standard to the trial court's determination. (600–601.)

The court drew on Michigan decisions asking about the parties' economic strength, bargaining power, alternatives, and the challenged term's substantive reasonableness. It made reasonableness the primary consideration: unequal power alone would not invalidate a reasonable provision. Gantos argued that two experienced merchants familiar with the fashion industry had merely allocated risks through a standard clause. The court evaluated that argument against what this particular cancellation power did to custom, seasonal production. (601–602.)

## Reasoning
The holiday order represented twenty to twenty-two percent of Gianni's business in 1980, while Gantos's total sales were approximately twenty times Gianni's. Those differences mattered, but did not alone determine the result. The decisive commercial problem was that Gianni manufactured these goods especially for Gantos, in an industry where styles changed rapidly. Last-minute cancellation did not simply return durable stock to a shelf awaiting another buyer. It left the small manufacturer absorbing the loss or bargaining with the same retailer to accept completed goods at a deep discount. The fifty-percent concession showed how the cancellation power operated in this setting. (600–602.)

Mr. Gianni testified that he had not read the clause and would not have done business with Gantos had he read it. The court did not transform that testimony into a general excuse for failing to read contracts. It also examined negotiability and trade power. There was no evidence this provision could be negotiated, even though Mr. Gantos acknowledged that some manufacturers wanted to negotiate it. The trial court credited the retailer's buyer's explanation that buyers controlled the industry and could impose these provisions on small independent manufacturers. The appellate court held that the trial court's assessment of the clause's substantive unfairness was not clearly erroneous. (602–603.)

Cardinal Stone did not require enforcement here. There, the court had characterized the parties as experienced businessmen who knowingly accepted a cancellation risk, without indications of unequal power or lack of alternatives and without analyzing substantive reasonableness. Johnson v. Mobil Oil was also inapposite: it concerned an illiterate dealer and a consequential-damages limitation, did not involve the UCC as that court had treated it, and involved more extreme disparities. The Gianni court neither made consumer status indispensable nor imported Johnson's affirmative-assent requirement wholesale into a merchant transaction. (602–603.)

## Cross-appeal and exact disposition
On the separate late shipment and advertising allowance, Gianni conceded that testimony supported the trial court's findings but argued that the evidence overall favored its position. Under Michigan Court Rule 2.613(C), findings could be set aside only if clearly erroneous, with regard for the trial judge's opportunity to evaluate witnesses. The court found no clear error in crediting Gantos's witnesses. It therefore affirmed the judgment in all respects, rather than awarding the additional damages or remanding for another trial. (603–604.)
