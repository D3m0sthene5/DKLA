Capron v. Van Noorden
Citation
Capron v. Van Noorden, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 126 (1804). Supreme Court of the United States, February Term
1804. The report does not identify an opinion author or give precise argued and decided dates. No separate
opinion is reported. Casebook pp. 29-30 (repository PDF p. 45). Full judicial opinion. The linked report
includes counsel’s argument and the Court’s disposition rather than a signed explanatory opinion.
Parties
George Capron was the plaintiff in the federal Circuit Court for North Carolina and the plaintiff in error in
the Supreme Court, the historical designation for the party seeking reversal through a writ of error.
Hadrianus Van Noorden was the defendant below and the defendant in error. Capron sought reversal of the
judgment that had been entered against him; Van Noorden did not appear in the Supreme Court.
Material Facts
Capron brought an action of trespass on the case, a historical civil form of action for a claimed wrong. The
report does not describe the underlying transaction or injury. The proceedings described Van Noorden as
formerly of Pitt County but did not allege that Capron was an alien, a citizen of any state, or resident
anywhere. These omissions left the record without the citizenship allegations that Capron later argued were
necessary to establish the federal court’s jurisdiction. 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) at 126.
Procedural History
A verdict was returned for Van Noorden, and the Circuit Court entered judgment accordingly. Capron
obtained a writ of error, the procedure by which he asked the Supreme Court to review errors shown by the
record. He assigned as error the failure of the record to establish that the parties fell within the Circuit
Court’s limited jurisdiction and the court’s decision to proceed to a final judgment rather than dismiss.
Although Van Noorden did not appear in the Supreme Court, the citation notifying him of the review
proceeding had been properly served. The judgment was reversed. Id. at 126-27.
Issue
Could the plaintiff obtain reversal for a jurisdictional defect in the record even though he had chosen the
federal court and had himself initiated the proceedings that omitted the necessary allegations?
Rule and Holding
The reported disposition permits the plaintiff to obtain reversal when the record fails to establish the federal
Circuit Court’s jurisdiction, notwithstanding that he selected that court. The report records a reversal on the
jurisdictional challenge; it does not supply an independently written judicial statement of a broader rule.
Subject-matter jurisdiction means a court’s legal authority to adjudicate the category of dispute before it. Id.
at 126-27.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Circuit Court’s judgment. The report does not state that Capron prevailed
on his underlying claim, award him damages, or explain further proceedings. Id. at 127.

Reasoning
The explanation printed in the casebook is Harper’s argument for Capron, not a separately identified opinion
of the Court. Harper framed the question as whether a plaintiff could complain of the lack of jurisdiction in
the court to which he had chosen to resort. He distinguished errors attributable to a party’s process or delay
from errors of the court itself. He argued that a party could challenge the latter even when an aspect of the
error had benefited that party. Id. at 126-27; casebook pp. 29-30.
Harper then characterized the failure to assure jurisdiction as the Circuit Court’s own error. In his account,
the court was responsible for determining that it had jurisdiction, and the parties’ consent could not supply it.
His conclusion was that Capron could invoke that error despite having filed the action. The Court reversed
after this argument, but the report gives no judicial explanation that permits every step of counsel’s
reasoning to be attributed to the Court as its own words. Id. at 126-27; casebook p. 30.
Separate Opinions
No concurrence or dissent appears in the report. Its failure to identify separate opinions does not provide a
recorded vote count.
Notes
Narrow reading — interpretation. A plaintiff who lost in this federal trial court could attack a judgment
when the record did not establish the citizenship basis for the court’s jurisdiction. The decision concerns
what the jurisdictional record showed, not a factual finding that Capron and Van Noorden were actually
citizens of the same state.
Broad reading — interpretation. The reversal supports treating a federal court’s limited subject-matter
jurisdiction as a constraint the parties cannot create merely by choosing to litigate there. That broader
formulation draws on the relationship between the issue presented, Harper’s argument, and the disposition; it
is not a quotation from an authored judicial opinion.
The casebook uses this short report to distinguish the court’s authority over the kind of action from its
authority over a particular defendant. Reversal of a defense judgment for lack of an established jurisdictional
basis does not resolve the merits of the plaintiff’s claim. The underlying facts, exact decision date, and an
author’s identity have been left unelaborated because neither source confirms them.
