CT-CB-0074 | Mills v. Wyman
Mills v. Wyman
20 Mass. (3 Pick.) 207 (1825)
Court, author, and reading scope
The Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts considered exceptions to a nonsuit in an action
on a father's promise to reimburse the person who had cared for his adult son. Chief Justice
Parker delivered the opinion. The assigned excerpt is at printed pages 74–75. The complete
opinion adds substantial discussion of revived debts, formal instruments, and a statutory
family-support theory omitted from that excerpt. Reporter pinpoints below refer to
Massachusetts Reports, volume 20, also cited as 3 Pickering.
Parties and material facts
Daniel Mills supplied shelter and care to Levi Wyman, the twenty-five-year-old son of
defendant Seth Wyman. Levi had long since left his father's household and was returning
from a voyage when he became ill among strangers. The care was supplied without a request
from Seth. The reported opinion describes Mills as caring for Levi until his death. After the
services and expenses had already been incurred, Seth wrote promising reimbursement and
subsequently refused to pay. The claim was against the father on that later promise, rather
than an action against the son or the son's estate for the value of necessaries. 20 Mass. at
208–09; casebook 74–75.
The separation between those people and transactions is decisive. Mills incurred expense,
but he did not incur it in response to the father's promise: the promise came afterward. The
father felt gratitude, but the son, an independent adult, had received the care. The father had
neither initially engaged Mills nor been shown to bear an existing legal responsibility for this
particular expense. A promise can therefore be morally serious and still lack the legal basis
the court requires. Id. at 209–12.
The casebook's subsequent notes discuss historical questions about the underlying events.
This brief reports the facts on which Parker's opinion proceeds; it does not resolve the
editors' historical questions or convert their discussion into findings by the deciding court.
Casebook 75, note 2.
Procedural history
Mills sued to recover compensation for the services on the father's promise. The Court of
Common Pleas directed a nonsuit because the family relationship and subsequent promise
supplied no sufficient consideration. Mills took exceptions. The Supreme Judicial Court
sustained the nonsuit and directed entry of judgment, including costs, for the defendant. It
did not conduct a damages valuation or order reimbursement on another theory. 20 Mass. at
207–08, 212.

Issues
First, can a father's moral obligation or gratitude for care already provided to his adult son
support his subsequent express promise to reimburse the caregiver, absent a prior request or
established legal obligation? Second, do the recognized cases enforcing new promises to pay
old debts support that result? Third, did the Massachusetts statute requiring certain relatives
to support impoverished family members establish the necessary obligation in this record?
Id. at 209–12.
Rules and holdings
The court held the promise unenforceable. Moral obligation, understood broadly as a
conscientious reason to pay, does not itself furnish consideration. The precedents sustaining
a new promise after an earlier obligation has become legally unenforceable depend on a
valuable consideration or obligation in the underlying transaction. They do not establish that
any past kindness, followed by an express promise of payment, becomes a legally enforceable
contract. Id. at 209–11.
The court also rejected the statutory support argument. Liability under that statute depended
on facts and adjudicative prerequisites that had not been established: the relative's legally
relevant settlement and likelihood of becoming a public charge, the proposed obligor's ability
to provide support, and the required judgment after an opportunity to be heard. Kinship
alone did not substitute for those conditions. Id. at 212.
Actual judgment
The nonsuit directed by the Court of Common Pleas was upheld. Judgment was to be entered
for Seth Wyman, with costs. The result left Mills without recovery on the father's promise in
this action. The court's disapproval of the father's conduct did not become a monetary award
or an equitable exception. Id.
Reasoning
The timing and object of the alleged exchange
Parker first identifies what is missing: the services were not bestowed at the father's request,
and the adult son was no longer under his care. The later writing could acknowledge
gratitude, but it could not show that the earlier care had been rendered as the price of that
writing. Nor did the father's relationship to Levi make Levi's independent expenses the
father's debts. A later undertaking to pay another adult's expense therefore needed a legal
foundation beyond the emotional reason for making it. Id. at 208–09, 211.
This reasoning does not require a finding that Mills's services lacked value. Their humane
importance is expressly recognized. The difficulty concerns whether their value supports this
defendant's promise under the applicable doctrine. Treating every valuable past service as
consideration for any later promisor would bypass both the exchange relationship and the
identification of the person whose obligation is being enforced. Id. at 209–11.

Why revived-debt precedents do not control
Mills invoked the proposition that moral obligation can support an express promise. Parker
examines the usual illustrations: a debt barred by limitations, an obligation incurred during
infancy, and a debt discharged in bankruptcy. In those settings there was an original
transaction involving a quid pro quo. The subsequent promise removes or waives a legal
impediment to enforcing an obligation with that antecedent basis. The moral description does
not mean that consideration was unnecessary throughout the entire history of the
transaction. Id. at 209–10.
The distinction is between reviving an obligation grounded in an earlier exchange and
creating an obligation from gratitude where none had existed for this defendant. The father's
promise falls in the latter category. This is the court's account of those historical exceptions,
not an assertion that all nineteenth-century revival rules remain unchanged by modern
statutes. Id. at 209–11; casebook 75–76, note 3.
Legal enforceability does not follow every moral duty
Parker accepts that breaking a deliberate promise can be morally wrongful. He nevertheless
reasons that society leaves many family and benevolent duties to conscience rather than
judicial coercion. If moral obligation alone established a legal duty, it would be difficult to
explain why the duty required a later express promise at all: the reason for payment would
already exist. Examples of affluent children and needy parents expose the breadth of the
proposed principle. Id. at 210–11.
The court therefore tolerates an unattractive individual result to preserve the boundary of a
general rule. Its criticism of Seth is unusually direct, but that criticism explains the tension
rather than resolving it in Mills's favor. The opinion distinguishes a duty of imperfect legal
obligation from a duty that is unimportant to morality; those are different judgments. Id.
A writing does not supply the missing consideration
The father's promise was written, voluntary, and apparently deliberate. Parker still treats it
as unsupported. He separately explains why instruments under seal and certain negotiable
commercial instruments do not refute the general rule: those historically recognized formal
or mercantile categories have their own legal treatment. The existence of some exceptions
for formal instruments does not make an ordinary reimbursement letter equivalent to them.
Id. at 210–12.
The family-support statute required an established legal duty
The complete opinion ends with an alternative route advanced by Mills. A statute imposed
support obligations on lineal relatives in defined circumstances involving poor relations likely
to become chargeable to a town. Parker does not deny the statute's possible force. He rejects
its application because the record does not establish its triggering facts or required
adjudication. This is a separate ground for rejecting a proposed source of obligation and
should not disappear into the general consideration discussion. Id. at 212.

Separate opinions and participation
No separate opinion appears in the report read for this brief. Parker says that all members of
the court agree that the nonsuit was proper. The report does not supply a basis for inventing
an individual vote count or assigning unreported reasons to other judges. Id.
Study analysis and limits
The useful sequence is to identify the service recipient, the defendant promisor, the time of
each act, and any prior request or obligation. That prevents the shorthand “past
consideration” from obscuring why the father is different from the adult son whose needs
were met. A promise made before future care is provided, with that care requested in
exchange, would pose a materially different formation problem; it is not the transaction the
court decided.
Mills also supplies the restrictive position against which Webb's later material-benefit
reasoning can be studied. Webb involves a promise by the person whose life was saved and
who repeatedly paid the injured rescuer; Mills involves a father's promise concerning
benefits given to an independent adult son. Those distinctions may assist comparison, but
one should not attribute a complete reconciliation of the two decisions to Parker, who wrote
more than a century earlier. The comparison is analytical, not part of this holding.
