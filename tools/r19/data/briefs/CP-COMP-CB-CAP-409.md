# Bass v. Hoagland

**172 F.2d 205 (5th Cir. 1949).** No. 12394; January 18, 1949; rehearing denied February 28, 1949. Sibley wrote the majority opinion on a panel with McCord and Hutcheson; Hutcheson dissented. Assigned source: CivPro.pdf physical page 531, printed pages 1043–1044, quoting Bass within Coulas and then discussing it in Note 1.

## Facts and procedural posture

Hoagland sued in a Texas federal court to enforce a Kansas federal personal-injury judgment against Bass. The Kansas judgment purportedly entered December 13, 1943 awarded $7,810.70 plus costs; the enforcement complaint alleged $9,177.53 in principal and interest. The Texas judgment appealed here was $9,833.49. These amounts describe different steps, not inconsistent descriptions of one award. 206–207.

Bass had appeared in Kansas through counsel and answered the merits, denying liability and alleging contributory negligence. Hoagland demanded a jury, and Bass had not consented to withdrawing that demand. Counsel later withdrew, leaving the answer and appearance in place. The Kansas judgment recited notice of counsel’s withdrawal and evidence at trial, but Bass alleged he knew of neither the withdrawal nor the judgment, received no advance notice of an application for default judgment, and that no evidence actually was heard. He also alleged fraudulent concealment until the time for ordinary relief had passed. The judgment was filed April 5, 1944. 207–208.

Hoagland moved for judgment on the pleadings under Rule 12(c), expressly admitting Bass’s defenses as true solely for that motion while arguing they were an impermissible collateral attack. Consequently, the appellate account treats those allegations as assumed facts, not established findings. The report gives November 17 and November 19, 1946 in different passages for service of the enforcement action; the brief does not resolve that internal date discrepancy. 207–208.

## Issues and governing distinctions

Could the enforcement court enter judgment on the pleadings despite allegations that the earlier judgment denied due process? Did the existing answer become a default merely because counsel withdrew and neither counsel nor Bass attended trial?

Ordinarily, a judgment of a court with personal and subject-matter jurisdiction cannot be collaterally attacked for procedural or substantive error. Its recitals presumptively speak the truth. Most of Bass’s defenses therefore were insufficient. But a constitutionally void judgment can be resisted when enforcement is sought. Both courts were federal, so the constitutional Full Faith and Credit Clause did not directly govern; federal constitutional and procedural law controlled. 208–209.

## Majority reasoning and holding

An answer denying liability kept the case contested. Counsel’s withdrawal did not withdraw that answer, the appearance, or the jury demand. Under the majority’s reading of Rule 55(a), “otherwise defend” included preliminary defensive motions that avoid default without yet answering; it did not require an answering defendant to attend trial merely to keep the complaint from becoming confessed. Hoagland could proceed in Bass’s absence, but had to prove his case. 209–210.

The majority also emphasized that Hoagland’s jury demand protected Bass unless properly withdrawn with his consent. No attendance or acquiescence supplied a waiver on the assumed facts. If the court nonetheless proposed to proceed by default, the then-applicable Rule 55(b)(2) required three days’ notice, served on Bass after counsel’s withdrawal. The combination of an extant merits answer, unwithdrawn jury demand, alleged absence of evidence, and lack of notice presented a constitutional challenge sufficient to defeat enforcement on the pleadings. The opinion analogized Hovey, Windsor, and Wetmore, concerning judgments entered without a meaningful opportunity to defend. It did not find that every procedural mistake permits collateral attack. 209–210.

## Exact disposition and limits

**Reversed and remanded for further proceedings in the Texas enforcement action.** The truth of Bass’s answer had to be tried. If the Kansas judgment proved void, the majority anticipated that the Kansas court might set it aside and try the underlying merits. The Texas court could choose to delay if a direct Kansas challenge were entertained; the appellate court expressly gave no such direction. It neither finally adjudicated the alleged fraud/no-evidence facts nor itself ordered a new Kansas personal-injury trial. 210–211.

## Dissent, notes, and source reconciliation

Hutcheson viewed the alleged defects as procedural errors within established jurisdiction. He read “otherwise defend” to permit default for failure to attend after counsel withdrew with notice, and reasoned that default eliminated any jury entitlement. He rejected the majority’s analogies and would enforce the judgment. His three notes cite collateral-attack authorities, identify the majority’s precedents, and reproduce a Kansas journal entry captioned **Bailey**, awarding **$15,475**. That note concerns the companion record; those names and amount must not replace Hoagland’s $7,810.70. All three dissent notes and the majority’s single starred statutory cross-reference were read. 208 n.*, 211–212 nn.1–3.

The assigned note compresses the outcome as a holding that the judgment “was void.” The actual appellate mandate required a factual inquiry into voidness under allegations admitted only for the motion. Coulas’s affirmed judgment after actual proof is a distinct case. The recovered report also includes Bass v. Bailey on page 212; that companion decision is distinguished. Three-day notice and contemporary Rule 60 discussion are historical, not statements of current deadlines.
