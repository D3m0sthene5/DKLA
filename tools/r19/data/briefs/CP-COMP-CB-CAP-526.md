# Jennings v. Stephens

**574 U.S. 271 (2015) — Supreme Court of the United States — January 14, 2015.** Justice Scalia wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan. Justice Thomas dissented, joined by Justices Kennedy and Alito. The judgment was 6–3. The assigned Civil Procedure account is Note 3, printed pages 1247–1248, physical PDF page 630.

## Crime, punishment proceeding, and habeas theories

Robert Mitchell Jennings entered an adult bookstore to commit robbery in July 1988. Officer Elston Howard happened to be there to arrest the clerk. Jennings shot Howard four times, robbed the store, and escaped; Howard died. Jennings had a juvenile adjudication and prior aggravated-robbery convictions and had been released from prison two months earlier. A jury convicted him of capital murder. At punishment, the State introduced his violent criminal history; his lawyer presented only the prison chaplain, who described improvement and said Jennings was not incorrigible. Counsel's closing acknowledged that he could not quarrel with a death sentence but requested mercy. The jury found deliberate conduct and a continuing threat to society, and the court sentenced Jennings to death. Texas courts affirmed and denied postconviction relief. 574 U.S. at 273–274.

Jennings then sought federal habeas relief for ineffective assistance at sentencing on three relevant theories. First, counsel had failed to present mitigating evidence of a disadvantaged childhood: his mother had been raped, was seventeen when he was born, and raised him in poverty. Second, counsel had failed to investigate and present mental-impairment evidence. Prior case files contained a report suggesting low intellectual functioning and mild organic brain dysfunction, although it also suggested malingering. Counsel acknowledged not reviewing those files. Third, the closing argument allegedly communicated resignation to, or approval of, the death sentence. Id. at 274–275.

The parties called the first two theories the *Wiggins* theories and the closing-argument theory the *Spisak* theory. These labels organized the litigation; Jennings had not actually cited *Spisak* when advancing the third theory. The Supreme Court did not adjudicate the truth of the mitigating evidence or whether any of these theories ultimately established constitutionally ineffective assistance. Id. at 274–275.

## District judgment and the appeal

The District Court granted relief on the two *Wiggins* theories and rejected the *Spisak* theory. Its conditional order required the State to release Jennings unless, within 120 days, it provided a new sentencing hearing or resentenced him to imprisonment as allowed by Texas law at the time of his crime. The corresponding judgment contained similar language. Id. at 275–276.

The State appealed the two successful theories. Jennings defended the judgment on those grounds and also on the rejected closing-argument theory. The Fifth Circuit reversed the habeas grant on the *Wiggins* grounds and rendered judgment for the State. It declined to consider the *Spisak* theory, reasoning that it lacked jurisdiction because Jennings had neither timely appealed nor obtained a certificate of appealability. The Supreme Court reviewed whether defending on that rejected theory required either step. Id. at 275.

## The judgment-based cross-appeal rule

An appellee may support an existing judgment with any ground in the record, including an attack on the lower court's reasoning. Without a cross-appeal, however, the appellee cannot seek to enlarge his rights under the decree or lessen the opponent's rights. The controlling inquiry therefore compares the relief sought on appeal with the judgment, rather than treating every unfavorable finding or rejected theory as something independently requiring appeal. Id. at 276.

Under this judgment, Jennings was entitled to release, resentencing, or commutation within a fixed period, at the State's option. The State could retain him pending resentencing or commute the sentence. Success on the *Spisak* theory would provide the same relief and impose no greater obligation. Accordingly, that theory defended the existing judgment instead of enlarging it. Id. at 276–278.

The State argued that each constitutional ground implicitly entitled Jennings to a new proceeding free of that particular error. Adding another ground would therefore add another restraint on the State at resentencing. The majority rejected incorporating the opinion's reasoning as unstated conditions in the judgment. Courts ordinarily reduce decisions to judgments precisely to define enforceable rights. Inferring a list of conditions would transform conditional habeas relief into broader federal supervision of state trial proceedings. The Court distinguished a hypothetical judgment or appellate request **expressly imposing conditions on the details of retrial**; that was not this case. Id. at 277–278.

## Preclusion, distinct liabilities, and limits on alternative grounds

Thomas argued that changing the judgment's issue-preclusive consequences expanded rights. The majority responded that alternative-ground affirmance normally changes which issue supplies a judgment's preclusive basis. If that change itself required a cross-appeal, the ordinary rule allowing alternative grounds would largely disappear. The majority separated such future effects from the relief granted by this judgment. Id. at 278.

The State also relied on tax decisions involving multiple discrete liabilities or refund claims. The Court distinguished them: adding another tax liability or defense could enlarge or reduce the Commissioner's rights even if one possible combination of successful arguments produced the same total amount. Jennings, by contrast, sought the same indivisible sentencing relief whether he prevailed on one or all three theories. Id. at 280–282.

The holding does not permit every rejected habeas theory without a cross-appeal. A prisoner who won only resentencing would need to cross-appeal to obtain a new trial. Even a prisoner who won a retrial would need to cross-appeal to establish that the State constitutionally could not punish the conduct at all. Alternative arguments must also have support in the record. Id. at 279.

The majority disputed predictions of excessive frivolous defenses. Successful applicants have an incentive to defend their existing victory effectively; alternative grounds need consideration only if the initially successful ground fails. A separate certificate process would itself consume resources. The majority treated any broader reform as Congress's responsibility. Id. at 278–280.

## Certificate of appealability

Section 2253(c) requires a certificate when an appeal is taken in covered habeas proceedings. The Court held that defending an already appealed judgment on an alternative ground is not taking an appeal. The certificate requirement therefore did not apply to this defense. The Court **reserved whether a genuine cross-appeal in a case already before the appellate court requires a certificate**; its holding did not need to resolve that question. Id. at 282–283.

## Thomas's dissent

Thomas, Kennedy, and Alito agreed that the operative question was whether the asserted cross-point was substantively a cross-appeal, but construed the rights under a conditional habeas order differently. Their account of habeas history emphasized its original procedural role and the modern conditional remedy's purpose: giving a State time to cure the specific constitutional violation found. In their view, that purpose defined the prisoner's enforceable right even when the order's text referred generally to resentencing. Id. at 283–288.

The dissent relied on the relationship between an identified defect and equitable relief, including the treatment of a conditional order in *Pitchess v. Davis*. A new constitutional violation at resentencing would not ordinarily permit release under the old order, while repetition of the adjudicated violation would. Adding the *Spisak* violation thus added a condition and another basis for demanding release, changing the judgment's substance. Thomas would require a certificate because the cross-point was a cross-appeal. Id. at 286–289.

Thomas also objected that the majority understated rights arising from ordinary judgments beyond their expressly ordered remedies, including merger and preclusion. Finally, he stressed AEDPA's screening function and the difference between a certificate application decided by one judge and merits review by three. He thought the majority invited scattershot alternative arguments and increased appellate burdens. These were dissenting criticisms, not limits adopted by the Court. Id. at 289–292.

## Exact judgment and assignment

The Court **reversed the Fifth Circuit and remanded for consideration of Jennings's Spisak theory**. It did not hold that the closing argument was ineffective, reinstate habeas relief on the *Wiggins* theories, or order unconditional release. Id. at 283.
