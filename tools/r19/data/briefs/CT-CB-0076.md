# Webb v. McGowin

27 Ala. App. 82, 168 So. 196 (1935); certiorari denied, 232 Ala. 374, 168 So. 199 (1936)

## Court, author, and source scope
Presiding Judge Bricken wrote for the Alabama Court of Appeals; Judge Samford supplied a short concurrence. The assigned casebook dates that opinion to 1935 and also prints part of Justice Foster's later Alabama Supreme Court opinion denying certiorari. Both judicial texts were read. The original report identifies the appellate decision as November 12, 1935, and records rehearing denied on February 18, 1936. The Supreme Court's separate certiorari opinion is dated May 14, 1936. The rehearing date is a reporter notation, not a separately reproduced merits opinion.

## Parties and material facts
Joe Webb sued the executors of J. Greeley McGowin's estate to recover installments allegedly due under McGowin's promise of lifetime support. On August 3, 1925, Webb was working for the W. T. Smith Lumber Company and clearing an upper mill floor. The ordinary method was to drop waste material to the ground. While preparing to drop a pine block weighing about seventy-five pounds, Webb saw McGowin directly below its expected path. The pleaded account stated that releasing it would probably kill or seriously injure McGowin. 27 Ala. App. at 82–85; casebook 76–77.

Webb could have remained safe by releasing the block. To protect McGowin, he instead retained hold and fell with it, diverting its path. McGowin escaped injury, but Webb suffered severe injuries to his right leg, right heel, and right arm and was permanently disabled. Both the original report and the assigned excerpt describe him as unable to do physical or mental labor. That is the reported pleaded account, not an independent finding of cognitive impairment after an evidentiary trial. Id.

On September 1, 1925, McGowin allegedly agreed to pay Webb fifteen dollars every two weeks for the remainder of Webb's life, in recognition of both the rescue and Webb's injuries. Payments continued for more than eight years until McGowin died on January 1, 1934; they continued afterward through January 27, 1934, and then stopped. Webb sought the unpaid installments accruing after that date through commencement of suit. The pleaded duration was Webb's life, so McGowin's earlier death was not the contractual endpoint. Id. at 84–85.

## Procedural history
Webb filed an assumpsit action, the historical form used here to enforce the payment undertaking, and amended his complaint. The trial court sustained demurrers. Webb then took a nonsuit because of that ruling and appealed. The objections included failure to state a cause of action, absence of consideration, absence of a prior agreement to pay before the rescue, and the Statute of Frauds. The Court of Appeals reversed and remanded. Its reasoning tests the legal sufficiency of the alleged facts; it is not a report of a completed evidentiary trial establishing those allegations. Id. at 82–86.

The Alabama Supreme Court subsequently denied certiorari in an opinion by Justice Foster. That court expressly stated that denial of certiorari ordinarily does not necessarily endorse every principle below, then explained why it agreed with the material-benefit reasoning in this particular case. 232 Ala. at 375; 168 So. at 199.

## Issues
Can a person's later express promise to compensate another for saving his life and suffering permanent injury in the rescue be enforceable although there was no preceding bargain or legal obligation to pay? Does the material benefit to the promisor distinguish the promise from one resting only on conscientious gratitude? Do the pleadings defeat the undertaking under the Statute of Frauds? 27 Ala. App. at 85–86.

## Rules and holdings
The Court of Appeals held that the complaint stated an enforceable undertaking. A moral obligation associated with an actual material benefit to the promisor can support a subsequent express promise of payment even without an original duty to pay. Saving a person's life or protecting his body from serious harm counts as a material benefit. The court also treats the subsequent undertaking as ratification of the services and the payments as inconsistent with characterizing the services as gratuitous. Id.

The court rejected the Statute of Frauds objection in a distinct, brief paragraph. It did not undertake an extended statutory analysis in the reported opinion. The brief should therefore report that resolution without supplying an elaborate rationale and attributing it to Bricken. Id., opinion pt. 6.

## Actual judgments
The Court of Appeals reversed the ruling that had sustained the demurrer and remanded for further proceedings. It did not itself enter a quantified judgment for all future lifetime payments. The Supreme Court later denied the writ of certiorari, leaving that appellate disposition intact while expressly approving the material-and-substantial-benefit principle it discussed. 27 Ala. App. at 86; 232 Ala. at 375.

## Reasoning of the Court of Appeals
### Preserving life is a material benefit
The court rejects a division under which saving property has pecuniary value but saving the owner has only sentimental value. It compares the rescue to a physician administering an antidote in an emergency, the care of an escaped animal, and the preservation of property. In those examples, an express subsequent commitment to compensate for a valuable service can have legal force. The personal character of the benefit makes it more significant rather than less legally cognizable. 27 Ala. App. at 85.

Bricken reinforces the point through institutions that already value bodily integrity: payment for medical services, personal-injury damages, and life and accident insurance. The argument does not require pricing McGowin's life at a market value or awarding Webb that entire value. It establishes that the rescue supplied the kind of substantial benefit capable of supporting McGowin's specified promise. Id. at 85–86.

### The subsequent promise has a ratifying function
Some precedents permit a new promise only where an earlier legal or equitable obligation has become unenforceable. Bricken recognizes that position and states a qualification for material benefits conferred on the promisor. On this reasoning, a later express promise affirms or ratifies the completed service and carries a presumption of a prior request. The court uses that doctrinal device to overcome the absence of an actual pre-rescue request. Id. at 86.

A careful account should not convert that presumption into a factual assertion that McGowin called to Webb or negotiated compensation before the fall. No such exchange appears in the pleaded events. Ratification is part of the court's legal explanation of why the later promise can bind despite that chronology. Id.

### The benefit and the rescuer's injury reinforce the undertaking
The pleaded transaction contains both a substantial benefit to McGowin and a severe loss to Webb. McGowin's commitment expressly recognized the injuries as well as the service. Bricken treats those circumstances together as supporting the obligation rather than characterizing the fifteen-dollar installments as detached gifts unrelated to any previous act. Years of performance further support the characterization of an acknowledged compensatory arrangement. Id. at 85–86.

The court says the services were not gratuitous because McGowin agreed to pay and Webb accepted payment. That reasoning looks backward from the subsequent relationship; it does not establish that Webb had expected a reward while diverting the block. The difference matters when comparing decisions that classify emergency rescue as presumptively gratuitous. Id. at 86.

### The identity and timing of the beneficiary matter
The court distinguishes decisions involving improvements to land while it still belonged to the United States, followed by promises from later purchasers. Those later purchasers were not owners when the services were rendered and therefore were not the direct beneficiaries in the relevant sense. Here the person promising payment was the person actually protected by Webb's act. This explains part of the boundary of the material-benefit principle; it is not a rule that all subsequent gratitude by any person is sufficient. Id., discussion following pt. 6.

## Samford's concurrence
Samford agrees with the result while acknowledging uncertainty under the strict formulations of the common-law rule. He invokes the idea that law should not be separated from justice when the governing law is doubtful. His concurrence is more openly equitable in emphasis than Bricken's multiple doctrinal routes. It does not announce a separate damages measure or eliminate the need for the pleaded rescue and subsequent undertaking. Id., Samford concurrence.

## Foster's later certiorari opinion
The Supreme Court begins with an important qualification about its own action: a denied writ is not automatically approval of all reasoning below. It nevertheless chooses to speak because the application of the principle is significant. Foster agrees that an actual material and substantial benefit to the promisor's person may be recognized through an executed payment or an executory promise. The rescuer's injury strengthens that conclusion. 232 Ala. at 375; 168 So. at 199.

Foster refers to earlier public-payment cases but cautions that they do not authorize compensation merely for courtesy or refined ethical obligations. His focus remains an actual substantial claim arising from benefits and injuries. Anderson, C.J., Gardner, and Bouldin concurred in that later opinion. This is subsequent review by another court, not an additional concurrence in Bricken's original opinion. Id.

## Study analysis and limits
Webb belongs beside Mills and Harrington because all involve completed services followed by later promises. In Mills the defendant father had not received the adult son's care himself. In Webb the later promisor was the direct beneficiary of the rescue. Harrington, however, also involved a direct lifesaving benefit and a later promise, yet reached the opposite result under its consideration analysis. Direct benefit is therefore an important distinction from Mills, but it does not by itself reconcile Webb with every contrary rescue case.

The case should also be separated from an ordinary bargain and from reliance on a promise. The rescue preceded the promise, so Webb did not fall because he relied on promised installments. The opinion's route is a subsequent promise recognizing a material benefit, supported by ratification reasoning. A modern analysis may ask about later restatement formulations, but those formulations should not replace the actual reasoning or be described as provisions applied by this court.
