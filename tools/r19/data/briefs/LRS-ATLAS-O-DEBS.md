# In re Debs
158 U.S. 564 (1895) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and procedural posture

On May 27, 1895, the Supreme Court denied the original petition for habeas corpus brought by Eugene Debs and other prisoners confined for disobeying a federal injunction during the 1894 railway boycott. Justice Brewer delivered the Court's opinion; the report contains no dissent or separate opinion. The disposition was denial of habeas, leaving the contempt imprisonment in force, rather than affirmance of a criminal conviction. An earlier application for a writ of error had been denied because the contempt order was not a final judgment or decree. The habeas question concerned the Circuit Court's jurisdiction to issue and enforce its injunction; once jurisdiction was established, its factual finding of disobedience could not be reexamined in this proceeding. (158 U.S. 564, 572–573, 599–600.)

The Court sustained federal intervention to protect interstate commerce and the mails, and recognized equity's power to restrain the alleged continuing obstruction. It expressly declined to examine the Sherman Act ground principally used below. The decision's broad executive and sovereign-power language belongs to a case in which Congress had already exercised its commerce and postal powers through extensive legislation. It did not adjudicate a general power to enjoin every labor dispute or resolve the underlying workers' grievances. (Id. 577–583, 598–600.)

## Facts, allegations, and chronology

A dispute between the Pullman Palace Car Company and its employees arose in May 1894, leading many employees to stop working. American Railway Union officers organized a boycott of Pullman cars and encouraged railway employees to refuse work on trains using those cars. On July 2, District Attorney Thomas Milchrist, acting under Attorney General Richard Olney's direction, filed a verified bill for the United States in the federal Circuit Court for the Northern District of Illinois. The defendants included Debs, Howard, Rogers, Keliher, and others; the bill identified twenty-two railroads affected by the conduct. (Id. 565–570.)

The bill alleged that the railroads carried interstate passengers and freight, operated as post roads under federal law, and transported the mails under contract; some also had federal military-transport obligations, and two were managed by federally appointed receivers. The alleged disruption extended beyond a private contract dispute: blocked railway and stockyard operations interrupted food, fuel, and other necessities reaching Chicago and communities elsewhere. The government's allegations included threats and intimidation preventing replacement employment, forcible interference with switches and signals, removed rails and spikes, derailed or wrecked trains, and plans to extend paralysis throughout the railway system. These were the allegations supporting equitable jurisdiction, not findings that Debs personally committed every enumerated act of violence. (Id. 566–570.)

The court issued an injunction against interference with the named railroads' interstate business and mail transportation, damage to their property and operational equipment, and participation in the obstructive combination. Its detailed terms also prohibited inducing failures of duty by threats, intimidation, persuasion, force, or violence, and directing or assisting others to perform forbidden acts. Service and notice provisions covered named defendants and other persons who acquired knowledge of the order. Thus the actual order had extensive restraints; the Court's later characterization of its purpose cannot replace the text with a narrower imaginary decree. The petitioners before the Supreme Court had been served. (Id. 570–572.)

Contempt informations followed on July 17 and August 1. After hearing, the Circuit Court found the petitioners guilty on December 14 and imposed county-jail terms ranging from three to six months. They applied to the Supreme Court for writs of error and habeas on January 14, 1895. The writ of error was denied January 17; the Court heard the original habeas application in March and denied it in May. The report describes the contempt proceedings at 64 F. 724, while the underlying injunction and any criminal proceedings remained distinct stages. (158 U.S. 572–573.)

## Petitioners' objections and the questions decided

Counsel Lyman Trumbull argued that the object was a lawful adjustment of Pullman employees' grievances, with effects on commerce incidental to a lawful refusal to work. Government did not own the railroads, and traditional equity did not authorize it to protect someone else's private property or to enforce criminal law. If criminal statutes had been violated, an indictment and jury trial were the proper route. In his view, transforming the same conduct into an injunction violation evaded the constitutional jury guarantee. He also contested the bill's sufficiency and its characterization of the stockyard company's powers. (Id. 573–577.)

Brewer organized the Court's inquiry around two questions: whether the national government's relation to interstate commerce and mail transportation authorized prevention of their forcible obstruction, and whether equity could assist performance of that governmental duty. The Court treated authority as involving duty as well as power. Its answer depended on the nature of the protected federal interests and the alleged sustained obstruction, rather than the parties' general status as employers or organized workers. (Id. 577.)

## Federal authority and the choice of judicial process

Within its enumerated powers, the United States acts directly on people throughout its territory and has the attributes of sovereignty needed to perform its functions. The Court quoted McCulloch, Lane County, and Siebold to reject dependence on state cooperation. Concurrent state law could continue to operate where consistent, but could not defeat authorized federal functions. The constitutional commerce and postal grants therefore supplied a sphere of national responsibility. (Id. 578–579.)

Congress had made those powers operative. Brewer reviewed legislation authorizing interstate rail transport, regulating livestock carriage and disease, creating and empowering the Interstate Commerce Commission, providing arbitration between railways and employees, and requiring automatic couplers, alongside the extensive postal system. These enactments demonstrated actual federal assumption of the subjects, rather than merely dormant constitutional power. If States could not obstruct interstate commerce, a voluntary association of private individuals could not possess a superior right to do so. (Id. 579–581.)

Criminal punishment was not the only available protection. A local community committed to obstruction might make criminal prosecution ineffective, leaving national obligations at its mercy. The Court stated that federal strength, including executive force when necessary, could remove obstruction. But availability of force did not exclude a peaceful judicial determination or preventive injunction. Drawing on nuisance cases, it treated resort to courts as a legitimate alternative to police or military action, not an unconstitutional transfer of military authority to a chancellor. These observations supported judicial jurisdiction; the validity of a particular troop deployment was not the judgment presented by the habeas petition. (Id. 581–583, 596–598.)

## Government's interest and equitable jurisdiction

The United States had a proprietary interest in the mails sufficient to answer the objection that equity required property. The Court nevertheless refused to rest solely on that interest. A government's duty to protect public rights within its constitutional responsibility could permit suit without a financial stake. San Jacinto and Bell Telephone supported this distinction: government could not use its power merely to advance one person's private dispute, but an obligation owed to the public could supply the necessary interest. The claim here concerned keeping federally regulated highways of interstate commerce open. (Id. 583–586.)

The Court traced traditional equitable prevention of public nuisances through cases about streets, rivers, canals, wharves, and bridges. It recognized that courts commonly exercised this jurisdiction cautiously and could refuse relief when ordinary remedies adequately addressed the problem. The jurisdiction's existence differed from the necessity of exercising it in a particular case. The nationwide consequences, continuing threats, and forcible interference alleged in this bill supplied the exigency warranting equitable intervention. (Id. 586–593.)

Private railway ownership and new transportation technology did not disable the commerce power. Congressional control over navigable waterways and over railways arose from the same grant, rather than ownership of the land or water. Constitutional provisions retained their meaning while applying to changing modes of business and transport. The Court accordingly treated railways as artificial highways subject to the same preventive principle, not as an exempt category because earlier nuisance cases involved rivers. (Id. 589–592.)

## Criminal-law objection, jury trial, and contempt

The Court agreed with the general proposition that equity does not possess criminal jurisdiction and cannot issue an injunction merely because someone threatens a crime. An independently cognizable interference with property or protected rights supplied the basis for equitable relief here. That the conduct also might be criminal did not destroy civil jurisdiction. Civil compensation or preventive relief could coexist with criminal prosecution. (Id. 593–594.)

Likewise, punishment for disobeying the injunction concerned the court's authority and the rights it had adjudicated; it was not a substitute criminal trial for derailing trains or assaulting railway employees. A contempt sanction would provide no defense to a later prosecution for those acts. The Court relied on the historic summary power of courts to determine disobedience and enforce their orders, concluding that submitting contempt to a separate jury or tribunal would impair that function. Its jury discussion concerns this historical contempt framework and must not be presented as a complete statement of modern contempt procedure. (Id. 594–596, 599–600.)

Petitioners' argument that civil orders could not suppress a mob questioned the remedy's expediency rather than jurisdiction: an obstruction did not cease to be judicially cognizable when committed by many people. Brewer quoted a defendant's Strike Commission testimony that removal of union leaders and restrictions on their communications had broken the strike. He regarded compliance by most participants as demonstrating that the events were not a rebellion beyond ordinary legal process. The testimony was evidence of the injunction's practical effect, not a legal declaration that every communication by a union official is prohibited. (Id. 596–598.)

The Court expressly stated that workers' right to stop working was not challenged and characterized the bill's purpose as restraining forcible obstruction of commerce and the mails. It rejected sympathy with Pullman employees as legal justification for violence or obstructive action. That characterization should be read alongside the actual injunction's broad wording and the petitioners' objection that contempt bypassed the jury trial otherwise needed for criminal liability. (Id. 570–572, 597–599.)

## Exact holding and limits of the assigned account

The Circuit Court possessed jurisdiction over the government's protective suit and authority under Revised Statutes § 725 to punish disobedience of its lawful injunction. Habeas therefore could not be used to review the factual contempt determination. The Court denied the petition. It neither accepted nor rejected the lower court's construction of the July 2, 1890 antitrust statute, expressly choosing the broader federal-interest and equity grounds instead. No separate writings appear in the complete judicial report. (Id. 599–600.)
