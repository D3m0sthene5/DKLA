# Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh

**547 U.S. 677 (2006) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 15, 2006.** Justice Ginsburg wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Stevens, Scalia, and Thomas. Justice Breyer dissented, joined by Justices Kennedy, Souter, and Alito. The judgment was 5–4. The assigned account is CivPro, physical PDF page 199, printed page 352, Note 1.

## Facts and the dispute

The Federal Employees Health Benefits Act (FEHBA) authorized the Office of Personnel Management (OPM) to negotiate health-insurance contracts for federal employees. The nationwide Blue Cross Blue Shield plan operated through regional carriers, including Empire in New York. Federal and employee contributions funded the program through a special Treasury fund. The Government supplied approximately 75 percent of premiums, but the immediate reimbursement dispute was between Empire and an enrollee's estate. (683–685.)

The plan's brochure required an enrollee who recovered money for an injury from another source to reimburse medical benefits paid for that injury. The contract required reasonable recovery efforts by the carrier, and recovered sums were credited to the Treasury fund. The brochure also imposed duties to inform the carrier and provided mechanisms such as liens and assignments. These arrangements explain the federal financial interest without turning every resulting collection action into an action by the United States. (684–685 & n.1.)

Joseph McVeigh was injured in 1997 and died in 2001. Empire paid $157,309 for his medical care. Denise Finn McVeigh, his administratrix, brought state tort claims on behalf of herself, the decedent, and their minor child against third parties. Those claims settled for $3,175,000; $100,000 was placed in escrow. Empire had notice of the tort proceeding but did not participate. It then sought the entire $157,309 reimbursement without an offset for the estate's attorney's fees or litigation costs. (683, 687–688.)

## Procedure and the competing positions

Empire sued the estate in federal district court, alleging breach of the reimbursement obligation. The estate moved to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The district court granted that motion, and a divided Second Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court affirmed the dismissal; it did not adjudicate the estate's repayment liability or determine the amount properly recoverable. (688–689, 701.)

Empire argued that federal common law governed this federal-program contract dispute, that FEHBA's preemption provision displaced state law, and that the case presented an important federal question under the doctrine illustrated by Grable. The estate maintained that Empire was pursuing a contract-based collection claim tied to a state tort recovery and that Congress had not supplied federal jurisdiction over it. (689–690.)

## Rules and holding

Section 1331 covers claims created by federal law and the narrower class of state-law claims that necessarily present a substantial federal issue appropriate for resolution in federal court. Federal involvement in a program, a federal financial interest, or a federal contract does not by itself establish that a private carrier's reimbursement suit belongs in federal court. This suit satisfied neither route. (689–690, 699–701.)

The Court did not hold that federal common law can never support federal-question jurisdiction. It held that Empire had not established a federal common-law basis for this particular claim. Clearfield involved the Government's own rights and an independently available jurisdictional basis. Subsequent decisions showed that federal contracts do not invariably require a uniform federal rule; Boyle required an identifiable, significant conflict between a federal interest and state law. Empire identified no sufficient specific conflict governing reimbursement from this state tort settlement. (691–693.)

## Why the majority rejected federal jurisdiction

The reimbursement right depended upon the enrollee's recovery from third parties. State tort law determined that recovery, and questions such as allocation of litigation costs were closely connected to it. Congress's intent concerning the relevant rights and remedies mattered more than the general observation that OPM negotiated the insurance contract. The Court acknowledged the federal program and Treasury's financial interest, but FEHBA did not create an express statutory reimbursement right for carriers or expressly give them federal jurisdiction over collection suits. (693–696.)

FEHBA's express jurisdictional provision, §8912, concerns a civil action or claim against the United States founded on the Act. Regulations channel certain challenges to benefits denials to actions against OPM after administrative review. That express framework was different from Empire's carrier-versus-estate contract claim. The assigned account's statement that the statute gives the insurer a cause of action against the United States should therefore be read with this correction: §8912 is a jurisdictional provision, not an express creation of the carrier's asserted collection cause of action. (686–687, 696; assigned printed 352.)

The majority also declined to settle the full reach of §8902(m)(1), which makes contract terms concerning coverage or benefits preempt state or local law relating to health insurance or plans. Congress had removed the former inconsistency limitation in 1998, so the clause could displace both consistent and inconsistent state law on covered matters. (685–686.) The provision could be read narrowly as concerning coverage and benefit payments, or more broadly as extending to reimbursement after benefits were paid. The Court expressly did not choose between those readings. Even the broader interpretation did not make this provision a sweeping grant of federal jurisdiction over the carrier's suit. Its unusual reliance on contract terms to preempt state law warranted a modest reading. Thus the jurisdictional decision should not be converted into a categorical holding that the reimbursement terms could never preempt state law on the merits. (697–699.)

Finally, Grable concerned a federal agency's action and a comparatively pure legal question about statutory notice that could control other cases. Empire's claim instead arose from a particular state tort settlement and was fact-bound. The estate disputed matters including alleged overcharges, duplicative charges, and treatment unrelated to the injury; those were allegations, not Supreme Court factual findings. Questions about sharing the costs of obtaining the recovery did not supply the necessary substantial federal issue. State courts could consider any federal questions that actually arose. (699–701.)

## Breyer's dissent

Breyer emphasized the program's pervasive federal character, national administration, federal funding, and the Treasury's receipt of reimbursements. Uniform interpretation would protect consistent benefits and repayment obligations across States. He considered the United States the real party in interest in substance, even though Empire was the named plaintiff. (702–710.)

The dissent would apply federal common law and recognize §1331 jurisdiction. It rejected the majority's negative inference from §8912, explaining that provision's role in permitting larger claims against the Government in district court. It also distinguished the decision whether federal law supplies the governing rule from the later choice whether federal law should borrow state rules. Borrowing state law would not eliminate federal jurisdiction if the governing source of law remained federal common law. The competing positions therefore concerned the proper source of authority and forum, not merely whether a court should use one nationally uniform contract interpretation. (710–714.)

## Judgment and significance

The Supreme Court **affirmed the Second Circuit's judgment**, leaving the dismissal for lack of federal subject-matter jurisdiction in place. (701.) The case illustrates the limits of federal-program involvement, contract-based preemption, and substantial-federal-question reasoning as jurisdictional grounds. It does not decide the ultimate repayment obligation or all possible preemption issues. This brief describes the historical decision; it does not certify later-law treatment.
