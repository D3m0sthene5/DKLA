Original report: [U.S. Reports scan](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep522/usrep522479/usrep522479.pdf).

## Parties and Material Facts

The NCUA and AT&T Family Federal Credit Union were petitioners in the consolidated matters. Credit Union National Association intervened on the agency’s side in the District Court and was also a petitioner in the Supreme Court. Id. at 485,487. Five commercial banks and the American Bankers Association were respondents. Section 109 of the Federal Credit Union Act limited federal credit-union membership to groups having a common bond of occupation or association, or groups within a defined geographic community. 522 U.S. at 483-484. Before 1982, the agency required one occupational common bond across an occupational credit union. In 1982 it changed course, allowing multiple unrelated employer groups, each with its own occupational bond, to join the same federal credit union. AT&T Family Federal Credit Union expanded dramatically; only about 35% of its roughly 110,000 members were AT&T employees or affiliates, while other members worked for unrelated companies. Id. at 484-485.

The banks alleged competitive injury because the expanded credit union could serve people who otherwise might become bank customers. They challenged NCUA charter amendments under APA §702 and argued that §109 unambiguously required a common occupational bond across the credit union as a whole. Id. at 485-488.

## Procedural History

The District Court initially dismissed for lack of prudential standing, reasoning that Congress had not enacted §109 to protect banks. The D.C. Circuit reversed, relying on financial-industry competitor-standing cases. On remand the District Court found the statutory language ambiguous at Chevron step one. It did not proceed to step two because the banks had not seriously argued that the agency’s construction was unreasonable. Thus the reported procedural history should not be read as a separately reasoned step-two reasonableness decision. Id. at 487. The D.C. Circuit again reversed, holding at Chevron step one that Congress unambiguously required a single common bond. The Supreme Court granted review and affirmed. Id. at 485-488, 503.

## Issues

Were commercial-bank competitors within the APA zone of interests protected or regulated by §109 even if Congress did not specifically intend to benefit banks? On the merits, did §109 unambiguously require one common occupational bond across all members, making NCUA's multiple-group interpretation unlawful under then-governing Chevron step one?

## Rule and Holding

Under the APA zone-of-interests test as applied in the case, a plaintiff need not show that Congress specifically intended to benefit it. The court identifies interests arguably protected or regulated by the statutory provision and asks whether the plaintiff's affected interest is among them. 522 U.S. at 488-499.

The banks satisfied that test. Section 109 limited who could become credit-union members and thereby limited the markets federal credit unions could serve. Competitor banks had an interest in those market limits, even if protecting banks was not Congress's subjective purpose. Id. at 492-499.

On the merits, the Court held §109 unambiguous: the same common bond of occupation must unite the members of an occupationally defined federal credit union. NCUA's interpretation failed at Chevron step one. Id. at 499-503.

## Judgment

The Court affirmed the D.C. Circuit. Respondents had standing under the APA, and NCUA's multiple-unrelated-employer interpretation was contrary to the statute. 522 U.S. at 503.

## Reasoning

### Zone of interests is not an intended-beneficiary test

Thomas relied on Data Processing, Arnold Tours, Investment Company Institute, and Clarke. Those cases allowed competitors of regulated financial institutions to challenge agency relaxation of statutory limits. The relevant question was whether the competitors' interest bore the required relationship to interests protected or regulated by the statute, not whether legislative history showed a purpose to enrich or protect those competitors. Id. at 488-495.

### Why §109 implicated competitive interests

Federal credit unions generally serve members. Limiting membership therefore limits the potential customer market. The connection between the membership restriction and market scope was direct. NCUA's interpretation enlarged the customer base available to federal credit unions and affected the banks' competitive interest. That interest was at least arguably within §109's zone. Id. at 492-495.

### Rejection of the specific-intent argument

Petitioners argued Congress enacted the FCUA to promote credit unions, not shield banks. The majority treated that as the wrong level of inquiry. Zone-of-interests doctrine did not require proof that Congress specifically intended to benefit the plaintiff class. The banks' motives could be commercial; standing did not depend on altruism. Id. at 495-499.

### Statutory merits

The phrase "groups having a common bond of occupation" could not sensibly mean that each unrelated employer group needed only the bond already inherent in being a group. That reading made "common bond" largely surplusage. The parallel geographic clause also supported a unitary limitation: NCUA did not claim it could combine unlimited unrelated geographic groups. Finally, a reading permitting unlimited employer groups would drain "shall be limited" of practical force. Id. at 499-503.

### Separate Opinion — Justice O'Connor, joined by Stevens, Souter, and Breyer

The dissent accepted the banks’ competitive injury for Article III purposes but treated the zone-of-interests requirement as an additional limitation. It argued that the majority effectively equated a concrete injury from statutory noncompliance with an interest in enforcing the statute. Its agency-reporter hypothetical and Air Courier comparison illustrated why consequential economic harm does not alone establish that the relevant statutory provision arguably protects the injured interest. Bennett, in the dissent’s reading, required a protected economic interest rather than merely an economic consequence. Id. at 503–509.

O’Connor did not require that Congress specifically name or favor these particular banks. She read Data Processing, Arnold Tours, Investment Company Institute and Clarke as cases involving anti-competition restrictions that protected competitor interests generally. Section 109, by contrast, governed each credit union’s internal cooperative membership: a group unable to join one credit union could join another or form its own. In the dissent’s view, that differed from excluding all national banks from a product or geographic market. Id. at 509–515.

She also read the Depression-era history as encouraging credit for people banks had neglected, with a common bond promoting informed lending, repayment incentives and financial stability. She required some indication of a protected interest beyond incidental benefit from enforcement. Competitors’ incentives to suppress credit-union growth could conflict with the statutory aim of supporting cohesive credit unions. Id. at 515–518. These are dissenting statutory interpretations, not findings adopted by the Court.

The dissent’s final section stressed the limited judicial role underlying the then-described prudential standing requirement. It would have vacated the Court of Appeals’ judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss, rather than decided the merits in the agency’s favor. Id. at 518–519. The dissent has no judicial footnotes.

## Doctrinal Significance and Historical Note

The case is important for the historical APA zone-of-interests test and for how competitor injury can connect to statutory market restrictions. Its merits discussion used Chevron step one because that was the governing framework in 1998; the brief reports the case historically and does not treat Chevron as current doctrine. The standing analysis should also be read with later cases that reformulated the zone-of-interests inquiry as a question of whether the plaintiff falls within the class Congress authorized to sue.

## Additional reasoning and footnote limits

The Article III competitive injury was not disputed. The question was the additional statutory relationship under APA §702. In the majority’s formulation, an affected interest must still be arguably among the interests protected by the provision; footnote 7 expressly rejects the idea that every plaintiff with an interest in enforcement automatically qualifies. Footnote 8 explains why commercial rather than charitable motives do not disqualify the banks. Footnote 9 notes that no separate claim of congressional preclusion of review had been made. Id. at 487–489,493–494,499.

The majority distinguished Air Courier because the postal statutes protected public service and postal revenue, not the employees’ interest in greater employment opportunities. The banks here asserted an interest in the express restriction of a credit union’s customer market. The opinion’s discussion of financial-industry precedents supplies that statutory connection; competitive injury alone is not the complete test. Id. at 488–499.

Footnote 6 uses the credit unions’ cooperative history to support the standing discussion’s characterization of limited markets. Scalia did not join that footnote; it therefore had four Justices rather than the five joining the remainder. Footnote 10, in the separate merits analysis, says that the statutory text resolves the issue without consulting the murky legislative history. The two footnotes address different questions and should not be conflated. Id. at 493n 6,503n 10. The textbook’s bracketed reference to Scalia is editorial; Scalia wrote no separate opinion in this report.

The article “a” and the plural “groups” did not, standing alone, conclusively settle the merits. The Court relied additionally on avoiding surplusage, reading the parallel occupational and geographic limits consistently, and preserving the practical force of “shall be limited.” It rejected the agency’s proposed distinction between the participial “having” and prepositional “within.” The February 29 wedding-anniversary example is a grammatical hypothetical, not a case fact. Id. at 500–503. This is the Court’s interpretation of the then-existing statute under the then-governing Chevron framework; it is not a statement about current permissible credit-union membership or present judicial deference doctrine.

## Assigned textbook discussion

The principal excerpt occupies 1250–1258. The editors omit most of the majority’s detailed precedent discussion, all of its statutory-merits section, and the end of the dissent, including the proposed vacatur and dismissal. Those omissions are supplied above from the complete original report.

The one complete editorial note runs 1258–1259 and precedes the separate heading “F. Universal Relief.” It compares Thompson v. North American Stainless, Match-E-Be-Nash-She-Wish Band v. Patchak and Twin Rivers Paper v. SEC. In the editors’ account, Thompson’s harmed fiancé was a means of retaliation against the employee, rather than an accidental victim outside Title VII’s protected relationship; Patchak’s neighboring land-use interests had a foreseeable connection to land acquisition under the Indian Reorganization Act; and Twin Rivers rejected a paper company’s attempt to turn fortuitous economic harm from electronic reporting into a statutory interest. The common question is the connection between the injured interest and the statute’s purposes, with differences in the statutes and causes of action. These are later editorial comparisons, not holdings in the 1998 NCUA case, and this brief does not independently certify the complete opinions in those other cases.
