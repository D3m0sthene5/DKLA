# Pan American Fire & Casualty Co. v. Revere

**Civil Procedure · CP-086**  
188 F. Supp. 474 (E.D. La. 1960) · September 30, 1960 · District Judge Wright

## Accident, policy, and requested relief

A tractor-trailer collided head-on with a school bus near Covington, Louisiana, on February 3, 1960. The bus driver and three children died; twenty-three others were injured. A second collision between vehicles following the bus injured John Wells. The tractor's insurer, Pan American, faced three filed suits and numerous additional claims against a $100,000 policy limit. It sought interpleader against all potential claimants, injunctions restricting other proceedings, and adjudication of the competing demands in one case. It deposited a bond; the complete opinion's first footnote specifies an actual bond of **$100,400**, with the additional $400 presumably covering costs. 188 F. Supp. at 476 & n.1.

Pan American disclaimed an interest in the deposited proceeds while denying liability. Counsel explained that abandoning a claim to the fund did not mean conceding the insured's negligence, which could expose the insured to liability beyond insurance. At least one claimant challenged interpleader's availability. This opinion decided that threshold motion and the machinery for resolving claims, rather than fault or damages from the accident.

## Jurisdiction and the two forms of interpleader

The insurer invoked both Rule 22 and statutory interpleader under 28 U.S.C. §§ 1335, 1397, and 2361. Pan American was a Texas citizen with its principal place of business there; Wells was a Wisconsin citizen, and the other claimants were Louisiana residents. The fund exceeded both the ordinary diversity amount then applicable and statutory interpleader's $500 minimum. Complete diversity between insurer and claimants satisfied the ordinary jurisdictional basis for Rule 22; diversity between Wells and the Louisiana claimants also supplied statutory interpleader's required claimant diversity. The court therefore did not need to resolve competing approaches to an interested stakeholder's citizenship. 188 F. Supp. at 476–77 & nn.4–8.

Traditional “strict” interpleader involved a disinterested stakeholder; a bill “in the nature of interpleader” could involve a stakeholder contesting claims. The insurer's denial of liability complicated its characterization, but modern Rule 22 and the statute recognized both types. An older demand for an additional equitable ground when the stakeholder was interested had lost its foundation. Exposure to multiple litigation over one inadequate fund supplied the relevant equitable justification. It would not suffice simply to identify several unrelated suits or claims that did not exceed this insurer's limited obligation. 188 F. Supp. at 477–80 & n.21.

## Liability, adversity, and fault objections

A fixed policy limit did not defeat Rule 22's concern with possible multiple liability. The possibility of excessive payment, rather than an immediate certainty, was sufficient; an insurer confronted by claims exceeding its coverage qualified under that standard. Claimants need not demand identical amounts or have claims from the same legal source. Their competition for an insufficient fund made them adverse: reducing one person's recovery could increase what remained for another. 188 F. Supp. at 480–81 & nn.24–29.

The court also rejected unclean hands. Louisiana's characterization of an insurer as a joint tortfeasor with its insured served prescription rules and did not establish actual blame by this insurer for the accident. The insured tortfeasor might be unable to invoke equitable protection from its own wrong, and its unlimited personal liability supplied a separate problem, but neither point defeated the blameless insurer's claim. 188 F. Supp. at 482; see also 480 n.21.

Unliquidated tort claims were not excluded. Although tort damages could be uncertain, four deaths and numerous serious injuries supported anticipating demands greater than $100,000. Louisiana's direct-action law also answered the objection that the insurer faced no relevant exposure until judgments against its insured existed. The provisions' references to claims that might be asserted allowed relief before each claim matured into a judgment. 188 F. Supp. at 482–83 & nn.28, 36–38.

## Jury rights, injunctions, and procedural limits

Equitable interpleader did not require denying a jury for legal issues of negligence and damages. The court could provide a jury trial of the claims and retain the equitable task of allocating an inadequate fund among successful claimants. Rule 42(b)'s separate-issue authority supported that arrangement. 188 F. Supp. at 483 & n.45.

For pending state litigation, § 2283's exception for injunctions expressly authorized by Congress applied to statutory interpleader through § 2361. That express authority did not extend to Rule 22 suits as such. The court discussed whether the separate “necessary in aid of jurisdiction” exception could support a Rule 22 injunction, acknowledged doubt, and favored that conclusion; its actual decree rested on statutory interpleader. 188 F. Supp. at 483–85 & nn.46–53.

The venue and service rules then applicable made this distinction decisive. Under Rule 22 alone, the Wisconsin claimant prevented reliance on a common state of residence for all defendants, leaving the insurer's Texas domicile as the proper venue, with service ordinarily confined to the forum state. Statutory interpleader instead permitted venue where a claimant resided and nationwide service. Absent Wells's waiver and appearance or presence in Louisiana, the suit was maintainable there only under the statute. These are the court's **1960 procedural rules**, not statements that later venue and service provisions remain identical. 188 F. Supp. at 485–86 & nn.54–64.

## Exact decree and source scope

The court **denied the motion to dismiss and granted interpleader**, keeping Pan American in the suit because it remained contractually obliged to resist claims. It directed injunctions against pending or new accident-related suits against the insurer **or its insured**, required claims by answer within thirty days after notice, provided for a joint jury trial upon timely demand, and reserved allocation if aggregate verdicts exceeded available insurance. It did not determine negligence or award compensation. Wright was the sole judge; no separate writing is reported. 188 F. Supp. at 486.
