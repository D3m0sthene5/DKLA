# Bains LLC v. ARCO Products Co.
405 F.3d 764 (9th Cir. 2005) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Judge Kleinfeld wrote for Judges Gould and Tallman. ARCO appealed post-trial rulings sustaining a special verdict awarding Flying B $50,000 for breach of contract, $1 nominal damages for racial discrimination under 42 U.S.C. § 1981, and $5 million punitive damages for discrimination, together with fee and cost awards. The Ninth Circuit affirmed all challenged matters except the punitive amount, vacated that amount, and remanded for selection within $300,000 to $450,000. Each side bore its appellate costs. The opinion describes a new trial unless plaintiff accepts a remittitur as the procedural alternative to the excessive verdict; it did not itself enter a fixed $450,000 award. There is no separate writing. 405 F.3d at 769, 777–778.

## Facts and evidentiary posture
A 1999 petroleum-pipeline rupture disrupted fuel shipments to Seattle for about two years. ARCO hired trucking companies to bridge the interruption. Paul, Gary, and Deep Bains, American citizens born in Punjab, owned the company doing business as Flying B. They had grown a gas-station business and purchased a tanker and permits before the pipeline failure. They contracted with ARCO in March 2000, began hauling in June, and added three trucks and drivers in August. ARCO terminated the work October 30, after approximately 600 loads and 130,000 miles. Id. at 766–767.

The court recounted contested trial evidence favorably to the successful plaintiff, as footnote 1 and the opinion expressly explain. Seattle terminal leadman Bill Davis repeatedly subjected Sikh drivers and owners to ethnic and religious insults. He delayed their paperwork, imposed slower pumps and extra checks, required them to remain in rain while others sheltered, and made them clean other drivers' spills. With payment of about $460 per load, unnecessary delay reduced earning capacity. Drivers threatened to quit; even the two non-Sikh Flying B drivers experienced hostility because of their association with the company. Id. at 767–768.

The owners complained repeatedly to terminal manager Al Lawrence. Evidence supported a conclusion that he excused Davis and failed to stop the conduct. ARCO then terminated Flying B without contractual thirty-day notice, forcing layoffs and sales of trucks. Central dispatch manager Tim Reichert, who had formal termination authority, testified that he did not know the owners' ethnicity and relied on safety reports and excess trucking capacity. Lawrence claimed safety violations, but acknowledged that he had not documented them as they occurred. Davis admitted some use of discriminatory language, disputed the full account, and claimed to have stopped after a conversation with Lawrence. A witness heard Davis boast of having expelled the Sikh company. These competing accounts mattered to the jury's permissible inferences rather than being undisputed admissions of every discriminatory act. Id. at 768–769.

## Verdict, arguments, and standards
Flying B's economist estimated $576,000 in profits lost after termination through the pipeline's repair, but did not quantify profits lost through discriminatory slowdowns during performance. The jury awarded $50,000 on the state contract claim, $1 on § 1981, and $5 million punitive damages. The district court denied judgment as a matter of law, a new trial, and reduction of the punitive award. ARCO argued that a corporation lacked racial identity, nominal damages disproved economic injury, legitimate termination defeated liability, and employee-level conduct could not support corporate punitive responsibility. It also challenged the amount as unconstitutional. Id. at 768–770, 772–775.

The court reviewed denial of renewed judgment as a matter of law independently, denial of a new trial for abuse of discretion, and constitutional excessiveness independently. Its substantial-evidence inquiry viewed the evidence favorably to the verdict, asking whether only a contrary conclusion was reasonably possible. The opinion contains fifty footnotes, including the statutory text defining § 1981's coverage of contractual performance as well as formation and termination. Id. at 769–770, 773–775; nn.3, 5, 10, 25–26, 31.

## Corporate discrimination and nominal damages
Prior circuit decisions allowed corporations with an imputed racial identity to pursue discrimination claims. Flying B was wholly Sikh-owned, and even non-Sikh drivers were targeted because of the company's identity. Its claim that discrimination impaired its contract therefore supported § 1981 standing. This was the corporation's claim, not an award for owners' or employees' personal emotional distress; the jury had been instructed accordingly. Id. at 770–771 & nn.6–12.

The jury's answers could fairly be harmonized. It could find economic injury from deliberately reduced loads without enough evidence to assign a reasonably certain amount. The economist's termination-profit calculation did not quantify the slowdowns. Alternatively, the jury could place quantified termination harm under the contract count to avoid duplication while recording discrimination separately. The appellate court offered permissible reconciling explanations, not a definitive finding about which explanation the jury used. Nominal damages did not establish that discrimination caused no actual economic harm. Id. at 771–772.

Section 1981 covered performance-related discrimination throughout the relationship. Even a termination based entirely on legitimate safety grounds would not excuse racially imposed delays that reduced profits earlier. The mixed-motive argument did not need resolution because the verdict supported harm independently of the termination decision. The challenged instruction properly confined the legitimate-termination defense to that decision instead of extending it to every discriminatory form of adverse treatment. Id. at 772–773.

## Corporate responsibility for punitive damages
Agency principles limited vicarious liability, and an employer's genuine preventive and corrective efforts could support a good-faith defense. ARCO challenged evidentiary sufficiency, rather than the vicarious-liability instructions. Evidence permitted treating Davis as a supervisor controlling daily operations and carriers. Even if he were not, Lawrence unquestionably held managerial authority over safety and terminal access. He could be found to have supported Davis rather than protecting Flying B after repeated complaints. A manager's backing of a subordinate's discriminatory conduct could create responsibility even if that manager's own motivation was loyalty or avoidance of internal conflict. A written policy was insufficient when not enforced. Id. at 773–774 & nn.18–28.

## Constitutional limit on amount
Under BMW and State Farm, the court considered reprehensibility, relation to harm, and comparable authorized sanctions. Repeated intentional ethnic harassment of a financially vulnerable company was highly reprehensible, and ARCO did not ameliorate it. Nonetheless the harm was economic and did not threaten physical safety. Punitive liability was appropriate, but the amount required separate scrutiny. Id. at 774–775 & nn.29–38.

On these intertwined facts, the court used the $50,000 contract award in evaluating harm, instead of treating the sole dollar on the discrimination count as the exclusive denominator. Flying B's potential-harm argument did not justify a larger award: the wrongful conduct succeeded, and individual drivers retained their own remedies for personal harm. The court considered $50,000 substantial, making this an ordinary case for the single-digit guidance rather than an exceptional tiny-compensation case. A hundredfold $5 million award far exceeded the resulting maximum of $450,000. Id. at 775–776 & nn.39–44.

The $300,000 Title VII cap supplied a comparable benchmark even though it did not legally cap § 1981 awards. ARCO's wealth could help calibrate deterrence but could not rescue a constitutionally excessive amount. Considering both ratio and comparative sanctions, the court directed the district judge, who knew the trial better, to choose a punitive award between $300,000 and $450,000. That range was case-specific, not a universal § 1981 ceiling or a statutory minimum. Id. at 776–778 & nn.45–50.

## Significance and source scope
The punitive award rested on the independent statutory discrimination wrong in the contractual relationship; this case does not authorize punitive damages merely for ordinary breach. It also distinguishes nominal recovery from absence of injury, preserves corporate claims based on imputed racial identity, and treats liability for punishment separately from constitutional limits on amount.
