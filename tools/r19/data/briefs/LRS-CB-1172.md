## Citation and Court

Federal Election Commission v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11 (1998). Argued January 14, 1998; decided June 1, 1998. Justice Breyer wrote for Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg. Justice Scalia dissented, joined by Justices O'Connor and Thomas. Full opinion: [Justia reproduction](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/524/11/). Original report: [U.S. Reports scan](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep524/usrep524011/usrep524011.pdf).

## Parties and Material Facts

The FEC was petitioner. Respondents were voters whose political views often opposed those of AIPAC, the American Israel Public Affairs Committee as named in the original opinion. The assigned excerpt supplies the bracketed spelling “American Israeli Public Affairs Committee”; that editorial spelling is not adopted as a finding about the organization’s legal identity. They filed an administrative complaint asserting that AIPAC was a "political committee" under FECA and therefore had to disclose contributions and expenditures. The FEC found that AIPAC's communications did not fall within FECA's membership-communications exception, but dismissed because it applied a "major purpose" test: AIPAC was principally an issue-oriented lobbying organization rather than an organization whose major purpose was candidate nomination or election. 524 U.S. at 14-18. The voters alleged informational injury: if AIPAC was a political committee, FECA entitled the public to contributor and expenditure information useful in evaluating candidates and political support.

## Procedural History

Respondents sought review under FECA's provision for a party "aggrieved" by dismissal of an administrative complaint. The District Court granted the FEC summary judgment. The D.C. Circuit, ultimately sitting en banc, rejected the FEC's major-purpose interpretation. The Supreme Court held that respondents had standing but vacated and remanded rather than finally deciding AIPAC's status, because the FEC still had to determine whether the communications were statutory "expenditures." Id. at 18-19, 26-29.

## Issues

Did denial of statutorily required campaign-finance information constitute injury in fact? Did the fact that many voters shared the injury make it a generalized grievance? Were causation and redressability satisfied where further agency proceedings were required? Should the lower court have decided the major-purpose issue before the FEC resolved the expenditure question?

## Rule and Holding

A concrete informational injury can satisfy Article III when a statute requires disclosure and the plaintiff is denied the information. A harm does not become nonjusticiable merely because many people share it; the problem with generalized grievances is abstraction, not numerosity. 524 U.S. at 20-25. Respondents' injury was fairly traceable to the FEC's dismissal and redressable because a favorable judgment could require lawful reconsideration. They did not have to prove that disclosure was certain after remand. Id. at 25. The Court vacated the merits judgment so the FEC could first address whether AIPAC's communications were expenditures. Id. at 26-29.

## Judgment

The D.C. Circuit's judgment was vacated and the case remanded. The Supreme Court did not itself order AIPAC to register or disclose. Id. at 29.

## Reasoning

### Broad statutory access to review

FECA allowed "any person" to file a complaint and an "aggrieved" party to obtain judicial review. The majority associated that language with broad access and observed that respondents' asserted informational interest was the kind FECA's disclosure regime addressed. Id. at 19-20.

### Informational injury versus abstract law enforcement

The voters were not asserting only a desire to see the FEC obey FECA. They sought information that, on their legal theory, the statute required AIPAC to make public. The information bore directly on voting and campaign influence. That concrete denial distinguished the case from taxpayer or citizen suits alleging only a generally shared interest in lawful government. Id. at 20-24.

### Widely shared injury

The majority expressly rejected the proposition that widespread injury is necessarily nonjusticiable. A large number of people can suffer the same concrete harm. The constitutional inquiry asks whether the plaintiff has actually suffered the relevant injury, not whether the plaintiff is unique. Id. at 24-25.

### Causation and redressability

The FEC's legal determination prevented the disclosure regime from applying. Judicial invalidation of that determination could reopen the administrative process and lead to disclosure. Article III did not require certainty that respondents would ultimately receive every requested record. Id. at 25.

### Remand on the merits

The Commission's major-purpose theory mattered only if AIPAC had qualifying expenditures. The FEC had previously found its membership-communications exception inapplicable because many AIPAC supporters lacked sufficient control over its policies to qualify as members. After that decision, Chamber of Commerce v. FEC invalidated the membership regulations. The FEC then proposed new membership rules; these were proposed rules, not a completed final rulemaking. The changed regulatory framework could bring these communications within the exception, removing them from the qualifying expenditure total and making the major-purpose question moot. Id. at 27–29.

The Court declined to decide whether Buckley’s major-purpose limitation applied only to independent expenditures or more broadly. It directed agency consideration of the communications exception first, both for the FEC’s expertise and because the communicative and associational context could inform the constitutional and statutory analysis if the exception proved unavailable. The Court did not finally decide political-committee status, the major-purpose test’s scope, or the ultimate First Amendment questions. Id. at 26–29.

### Dissent — Justice Scalia, joined by O'Connor and Thomas

Scalia made separate statutory and constitutional objections. FECA permits any person to complain administratively, but grants review only to a party aggrieved. In his view, equating that narrower language with every voter wanting public information drained the limitation of meaning. Respondents sought enforcement against a third party that would eventually produce disclosure, rather than delivery of information already held by an agency under a direct disclosure duty such as FOIA or FACA. Constitutional avoidance also favored a narrower reading. Id. at 29–32.

He read Richardson as rejecting the same informational grievance, including its voter-based allegation, rather than as confined to the taxpayer-standing nexus. He disputed the majority’s distinction between abstract and concrete harm: widely shared bodily injuries or interference with individual voting rights remain particularized because each person suffers a differentiated injury, while unavailable public information affects the electorate in an undifferentiated way. He argued that judicial direction of executive enforcement at any voter’s request would transfer the President’s Take Care responsibility to the courts. These are dissenting positions, not the Court’s holding. Id. at 32–37. His sole judicial asterisk on 33 explains why Richardson could not satisfy Flast’s taxing-and-spending nexus; the counsel/amicus asterisk on 13 lists participants.

## Doctrinal Significance

Akins is the important counterpoint to Lujan: widespread injury can be concrete, and statutory disclosure rights can generate informational injury. The case does not say every statutory violation creates standing. The plaintiff still needs a concrete interest of the kind the disclosure requirement protects, causation, and redressability.

## Additional statutory and factual limits

The historical statute generally treated more than $1,000 in qualifying contributions or expenditures during a calendar year as a political-committee threshold, and required covered committees to disclose donors giving more than $200 per year along with other receipt and spending information. These historical figures are facts of this opinion, not a statement of present campaign-finance law. The General Counsel’s estimates for 1983 and 1988 were investigative conclusions; the Court did not enter a final finding that AIPAC was a covered political committee. The FEC separately declined further action on its corporate-expenditure allegation in the exercise of enforcement discretion. Id. at 14–18.

The majority distinguished Heckler v. Chaney because FECA expressly provides review of this category of dismissal. Akins supplies no blanket rule that every agency nonenforcement decision is reviewable under the APA. Its causation and redressability analysis permits correction of a harmful legal premise despite uncertainty about how the agency will lawfully exercise discretion on remand. Id. at 25–26.
