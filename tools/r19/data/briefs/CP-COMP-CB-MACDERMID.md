# MacDermid, Inc. v. Deiter

**702 F.3d 725 (2d Cir. 2012), decided December 26, 2012.** Civil Procedure — remote use of a forum computer, statutory long-arm jurisdiction, and due process. Judge Barrington D. Parker wrote for Judges Newman, Parker, and Raggi; there was no separate opinion. Assigned: CivPro PDF pages 127–128, printed pages 204–207, Note 6. Reporter pinpoints below were checked against the preserved full report and its page markers.

## What Deiter allegedly did

MacDermid was a specialty chemical company based in Waterbury, Connecticut. Its Canadian subsidiary employed Jackie Deiter as an account manager from May 2008 until April 2011. She lived and worked in Canada. The relevant computer system, however, was centralized in Connecticut: MacDermid stored confidential and proprietary electronic information on Waterbury servers, and employees of the Canadian subsidiary could retrieve it only by accessing those servers. Employees were informed that the email system and protected data were housed there. Deiter had agreed in writing to safeguard the information and was not authorized to transfer it to a personal email account. 702 F.3d at 727.

After learning that she would be terminated effective April 7, 2011, Deiter allegedly forwarded confidential files from her company email account to her personal account shortly before termination. To obtain and send those files, she necessarily accessed the Waterbury servers. MacDermid sued in Connecticut federal court, alleging unauthorized computer access and misuse and misappropriation of trade secrets under Connecticut law. Subject-matter jurisdiction rested on diversity; personal jurisdiction required Connecticut's long-arm statute and compliance with due process. Id.

The district court dismissed under Rule 12(b)(2), reasoning that Deiter had used only computers in Canada: essentially, she had sent email from one Canadian computer to another. MacDermid appealed. The question was whether her remote operation of the Connecticut servers counted as using a computer located in Connecticut and, if so, whether asserting jurisdiction over her was constitutional. Id. at 727–729.

## The procedural standard matters

The court reviewed dismissal de novo. MacDermid bore the burden of establishing jurisdiction, but the district court had decided the motion without an evidentiary hearing. Deiter submitted no affidavit testimony with her motion. At that stage, MacDermid needed a prima facie jurisdictional showing; its uncontroverted complaint allegations and opposing affidavit were assumed true. The appellate decision therefore did not resolve contested facts at a trial or find that every alleged misappropriation had actually occurred. Id. at 727–728.

This posture supplied the factual premises for both parts of the analysis. In particular, the court had to accept the assertions that Deiter accessed the Connecticut servers and knew that the email system and confidential information were located there. The ruling is consequently not a general conclusion that sending email through an unknown server always subjects its sender to that server's jurisdiction.

## The statute reaches remote use, not just local keyboards

Connecticut General Statutes § 52-59b(a)(5) authorized jurisdiction over a nonresident individual who used a computer or computer network located in the state. The incorporated definition of a computer included equipment that automatically performs operations on data and communicates the results, as well as connected equipment enabling storage, retrieval, and transmission. A server meeting those functions was itself a computer. Thus, the physical location of Deiter's keyboard and personal computer did not exhaust the location of the computers she used. Id. at 728–729.

The statutory language located the computer or network in Connecticut, not necessarily the person operating it. On the accepted facts, Deiter's remote access caused Connecticut servers to retrieve and communicate protected files. That was use of a Connecticut computer under subsection (a)(5). Footnote 1 explained that it was unnecessary to decide whether the plural servers also constituted a qualifying network: each already satisfied the computer definition. Id. at 729 & n.1.

The structure and purpose of the legislation reinforced that reading. The computer provision formed part of a scheme addressing unauthorized operations, such as copying data, erasing it, or causing malfunction. Limiting subsection (a)(5) to users physically present in Connecticut would make it largely redundant, because the statute's existing subsection (a)(2) already reached an in-state tort. The additional provision sensibly addressed remote conduct not necessarily covered by the other branches. Id. at 729.

The court also explained why the out-of-state-injury provision did not straightforwardly describe Deiter's alleged conduct: she was not alleged to have regularly done business in Connecticut or derived revenue from what she did. Nevertheless, footnote 2 reserved MacDermid's alternative jurisdictional arguments under subsections (a)(2) and (a)(3), because subsection (a)(5) sufficed. That reservation should not be rewritten as a comprehensive final holding against every alternative theory. Id. at 729 & n.2.

Deiter invoked a separate definition of computer use in the computer-crimes provisions. The court gave two responses. The long-arm statute did not incorporate that particular definition. Even if it did, her alleged conduct caused the servers to perform computer operations and therefore met it. Her attempt to distinguish merely sending email from using the remote computer failed under either approach. Id. at 729.

## Knowing access supplied the constitutional connection

Statutory authorization did not complete the analysis. The court separately considered minimum contacts and reasonableness under the Fourteenth Amendment. Although the district court had not reached that issue, the appellate court could address it because it had been pressed or passed upon below. Id. at 729–730.

The accepted allegations established purposeful conduct tied to Connecticut. Deiter knew the company centralized its email system and confidential information in Waterbury. She retrieved and transmitted the protected files through those servers, and the email transfer itself constituted the alleged tortious conduct. Her connection was therefore not merely the happenstance that an Internet communication traversed an unidentified machine in another state. She knowingly used a particular forum's computer infrastructure in carrying out the conduct underlying the suit. Id. at 730.

The court also noted that the alleged misconduct was directed toward a Connecticut corporation. That fact complemented the knowing server use; it did not substitute for it. Physical presence was unnecessary because a defendant can purposefully engage in forum activity remotely. The court expressly acknowledged that most Internet users may not know their servers' locations, making the pleaded knowledge important to this case. Id.

The reasonableness inquiry considered the burden on Deiter, Connecticut's interests, MacDermid's interest in relief, efficient resolution, and substantive social policies. Travel from Canada imposed a burden, but travel alone did not make jurisdiction unreasonable. MacDermid was based in Connecticut, most corporate witnesses were there, and the state had an interest in interpreting and enforcing its own laws. Efficiency and policies opposing computer-based theft also favored adjudicating in the state from which the files were allegedly taken. Those considerations supported jurisdiction once purposeful contacts had been established. Id. at 730–731.

## Result and the limits of the assigned account

The court held that § 52-59b(a)(5) authorized jurisdiction on the accepted facts and that exercising it satisfied due process. It **reversed the dismissal and remanded for further proceedings**. It did not award damages or adjudicate Deiter liable for computer misuse or trade-secret theft. Id. at 731.

The textbook reproduces the statutory and constitutional reasoning at length. Its transition describes certain record facts as undisputed; the complete opinion explains why MacDermid's assertions were accepted on a written, pretrial motion without a contrary defense affidavit. The assignment's questions about an employee lacking knowledge of server location or a claim not involving an intentional tort remain hypotheticals. The actual decision rested on knowing access and a direct connection between that access and the alleged wrong. Neither statutory server location alone nor a generic Internet connection was held sufficient in every case.
