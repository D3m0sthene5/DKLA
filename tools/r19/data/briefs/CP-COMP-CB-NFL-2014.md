# In re National Football League Players’ Concussion Injury Litigation — preliminary approval denied

**961 F. Supp. 2d 708 (E.D. Pa. 2014) · January 14, 2014 · Civil Procedure**

District Judge Anita B. Brody denied, without prejudice, the motion for preliminary settlement approval and class certification. She required the parties to provide their supporting financial analyses through the Special Master. The decision concerns the original capped settlement, not the later uncapped settlement or the appellate judgment. The assigned account appears in the NFL litigation note, CivPro physical PDF 446–448, printed 866–869.

## The allegations and settlement process

Beginning in July 2011, retired players sued the NFL and NFL Properties LLC, alleging that those entities failed to protect them against chronic risks from concussive and subconcussive head injuries and concealed those risks. More than 4,500 former players had filed substantially similar cases. The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation consolidated the cases for pretrial proceedings in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania. As transferee judge, Brody had authority over coordinated pretrial matters, including settlement proceedings. These were allegations supporting litigation, not findings of liability in this opinion. Note 1 distinguishes the Riddell defendants, who were also sued but did not participate in the proposed settlement. (710 & n.1.)

Brody directed mediation before retired federal judge Layn Phillips in July 2013. The parties consulted medical, actuarial, and economic experts about disease incidence and funding. They signed a principal-terms agreement in August. In December, Brody appointed Perry Golkin under Rule 53 to help assess the financial arrangements. Plaintiffs filed the proposed class complaint and settlement motion on January 6, 2014. The NFL parties did not oppose the motion. That agreement among litigants, however, did not discharge the court’s responsibility to absent members. (710–711.)

## What the proposed class would receive and surrender

The proposed nationwide class included retired NFL players, authorized representatives of deceased or legally incapacitated players, and derivative claimants with claims arising from their relationship to a player. The NFL’s records indicated more than 20,000 class members. One subclass consisted of players without qualifying diagnoses before the proposed approval order; the other covered those already diagnosed and certain representatives of deceased players, including players with a postmortem CTE diagnosis. The qualifying conditions included specified levels of dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, ALS, and death with CTE. These were proposed definitions, not an already certified class. (711.)

The NFL parties would pay $760 million over twenty years into three programs: $75 million for baseline neurological assessment and some associated medical benefits, $675 million for monetary awards, and $10 million for education and injury prevention. Maximum individual monetary awards varied by diagnosis: $1.5 million for early dementia, $3 million for moderate dementia, $3.5 million for Alzheimer’s or Parkinson’s disease, $5 million for ALS, and $4 million for death with CTE. Age, seasons played, and other offsets could reduce awards. Note 3 adds a provision for up to $37.5 million of further NFL funding if a shortfall occurred; the proposed fund nevertheless remained capped. (711–712 & n.3.)

Notice expenses of up to $4 million and proposed attorneys’ fees and costs of up to $112.5 million were additional to the three funds. Administration also consumed resources: the monetary fund would pay claim-administration expenses and part of the Special Master’s compensation and expenses. The settlement contemplated releasing claims against the NFL parties and others, dismissing related actions, and covenants not to sue. Recipients of monetary awards would additionally have to dismiss or refrain from cognitive-injury litigation against the NCAA and other collegiate, amateur, or youth organizations. The adequacy inquiry therefore concerned benefits exchanged for substantial legal rights, not the announced dollar amount alone. (712.)

## The court’s obligation at preliminary approval

Rule 23(e), as applied in this 2014 decision, permitted settlement approval only upon a finding of fairness, reasonableness, and adequacy. Ordinarily the court first conducted a preliminary evaluation; if the proposal passed that threshold, class notice and a formal fairness hearing followed. Preliminary approval used a less demanding inquiry than final approval, asking whether obvious deficiencies or preferential treatment existed and whether the proposal was within a possible approval range. It still required comparison between the value offered and the members’ expected recovery. (713–714.)

The court explained why this scrutiny could not be a formality. Class judgments can preclude absent members from relitigating their claims. Once representatives and defendants agree, the usual adversarial process may no longer expose weaknesses affecting absentees. The judge consequently must guard against subordinating their interests to those of representatives or counsel. Settlement negotiated before certification called for especially careful scrutiny. Detecting a conspicuous deficiency before notice also avoided making costly notice and hearings futile. (713–715.)

Note 4 separately preserves the certification question: a settlement class must satisfy Rule 23(a) and an appropriate Rule 23(b) category. Because another deficiency warranted denying preliminary approval, Brody did not decide whether to conditionally certify this proposed class. Note 5 identifies the nine Girsh considerations for final approval, including litigation risks, likely duration and expense, class reaction, discovery, defendants’ financial ability, and the settlement’s reasonableness against possible recovery. Their inclusion explains the later inquiry; this opinion did not conduct final approval under those factors. (713 n.4, 714 n.5.)

## Why the available record was inadequate

Brody found no indication that negotiations lacked good faith or occurred other than at arm’s length. The problem was evidentiary and structural: she could not tell whether all eligible retired players would receive benefits before the fund ran out. The monetary fund had a 65-year lifespan, roughly 20,000 potential members, and substantial maximum payments for qualifying diagnoses. Even a hypothetical ten-percent diagnosis rate raised serious questions about whether the capped fund could meet its obligations. This was a concern requiring evidence, not a judicial actuarial finding that exactly ten percent would become ill or that depletion was certain. (715.)

The parties’ submissions asserted that experts had modeled incidence rates and that the funding was adequate. Phillips’s declaration relied on those analyses, and counsel expressed confidence in the aggregate amount. But the actual analyses had not been supplied to the court. Their conclusions could not substitute for documentation allowing Brody and the Special Master to test the proposal. The parties bore responsibility for supplementing the record; agreement and assurances were insufficient to show a possible fair settlement on the record before her. (715–716.)

Note 6 adds concerns about the assessment program’s adequacy and releasing claims against amateur football organizations. Thus, even resolving the monetary-fund projections would not necessarily resolve every outstanding issue. Brody neither found collusion nor held that an uncapped fund was the only legally permissible settlement structure. She held that this proposed arrangement and evidentiary submission had not earned preliminary approval. (715 n.6, 716.)

## Disposition and assigned-source distinction

The motion was denied without prejudice, and the parties were to submit the financial documentation through the Special Master as a first step toward possible approval. No class was conditionally certified, no settlement received final approval, and no merits judgment against the NFL was entered. (716.)
