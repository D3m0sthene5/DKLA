# Northern Mariana Islands v. Canadian Imperial Bank of Commerce — final federal disposition

**717 F.3d 266 (2d Cir. 2013) — May 15, 2013.** Per curiam opinion of Circuit Judges Cabranes, Straub, and Hall. Assigned source: CivPro physical page 610, printed page 1205, Note 3. This is the returned federal appeal following certification at 693 F.3d 274 and the state answer at 21 N.Y.3d 55.

## What returned to the federal court

The Commonwealth appealed Judge Lewis A. Kaplan’s denial of a turnover order under Rule 69 and New York CPLR §5225(b). It sought the Millards’ Cayman Islands accounts held by a CIBC subsidiary. It relied on CIBC’s 92 percent ownership of FirstCaribbean, operational oversight, and overlapping personnel to show that the parent could practically direct delivery of the money. The district court concluded that practical control did not establish the parent’s actual possession or custody. FirstCaribbean itself had not been served. (267–268.)

Because New York law was unsettled, the Second Circuit had certified whether §5225(b) allowed an order to an entity lacking actual possession or custody when a subsidiary might possess the assets, and, conditionally, which factual considerations should determine permissibility. The New York Court of Appeals accepted and answered the first question negatively. It did not reach the conditional second question. Its answer required the garnishee banking entity itself to have actual rather than merely constructive possession or custody. (267–268.)

## Applying the answer

The state court’s decision settled the dispositive legal question. The Commonwealth’s argument addressed practical ability to control the subsidiary, but the governing statute omitted “control” while other CPLR provisions expressly included it. That omission was intentional, and constructive possession through a subsidiary did not satisfy §5225(b). The state answer therefore confirmed the district court’s construction and its conclusion that the Commonwealth had not carried its burden to establish CIBC’s possession or custody of the accounts. (268.)

The appellate court did not hold that 92 percent ownership can never create any relevant legal relationship between companies. It held that the evidence offered for practical control did not meet this turnover statute’s actual-possession-or-custody requirement. Nor did the result adjudicate the validity of the tax judgments or declare the debt paid. It resolved this attempted enforcement order against this garnishee under §5225(b).

## Exact disposition and the interim injunction

The Second Circuit **affirmed the district court’s denial of the turnover motion**. It also **vacated the injunction that had preserved the accounts during the appeal**, and directed that the mandate issue forthwith. The injunction had prevented further dissolution or movement of the accounts while the court dealt with the unsettled state question. Once the turnover denial was affirmed, that appellate preservation measure was lifted. The injunction’s vacatur was a distinct operative step; it should not disappear into a shorthand description that the bank merely prevailed. (268.)

The complete reported opinion has no judicial footnotes, separate writings, or appendices. Its references to the district and state opinions explain its reliance on the resolved state-law question; they do not incorporate a different judgment into this federal mandate.

The textbook’s account correctly describes the result but compresses three separate decisions into one paragraph. The certification judgment deferred the merits and preserved interim protection; the state judgment supplied the authoritative statutory answer; this judgment applied that answer, affirmed denial of turnover, vacated the injunction, and immediately returned the mandate. Those procedural stages are independently identified here, rather than counted as three versions of one opinion.
