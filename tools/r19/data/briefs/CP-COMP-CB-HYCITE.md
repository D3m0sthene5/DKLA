# Hy Cite Corp. v. badbusinessbureau.com, L.L.C.
297 F. Supp. 2d 1154 (W.D. Wis. 2004) | Civil Procedure

## Court, question, and result

Chief Judge Crabb decided No. 03-C-0421-C on January 8, 2004. Hy Cite, a Wisconsin company whose products appeared in an online consumer-complaint forum, alleged unfair competition, false advertising, disparagement, and trademark infringement. The defendant challenged personal jurisdiction. The court found neither general jurisdiction nor specific jurisdiction consistent with due process, granted the defendant's motion, and directed entry of judgment for it and closure of the case. It did not determine whether the consumer complaints were false, whether infringement occurred, or whether the website operator was liable for publishing them. Crabb wrote the sole opinion, without judicial footnotes or an appendix. (1156–1167.)

The court declined to make website interactivity a substitute for ordinary minimum-contacts analysis. Actual forum-directed conduct and a connection to the controversy mattered more than a site's potential to conduct transactions everywhere. That distinction organizes both the assigned account and the full decision.

## The complaint forum and actual Wisconsin contacts

Hy Cite was incorporated in Wisconsin and based in Madison. It sold cookware, dinnerware, glassware, and related goods under its Royal Prestige mark. The defendant was a limited liability company organized in St. Kitts/Nevis, with no Wisconsin assets, offices, or employees. Its Rip-Off Report website displayed consumer submissions about businesses: at least 61,000 complaints overall and thirty to forty concerning Hy Cite's products. Companies could respond, with a $25 charge for posting more than four rebuttals. No Wisconsin company had purchased a rebuttal. (1156.)

Hy Cite emailed the operator in June 2003 to ask about resolving the complaints. The operator responded concerning its Corporate Customer Advocacy Program and quoted an initial $30,000 fee plus a later $20,000 payment. Hy Cite declined to enroll; no other Wisconsin company had enrolled either. The contacts arose from Hy Cite's inquiry, not an unsolicited approach by the defendant. The quoted price was not a completed Wisconsin transaction. (1156, 1164.)

The site also offered advertising, linked to a book for purchase, requested donations, recruited volunteer reporters, and offered to contact complainants about possible class actions. No Wisconsin business had bought advertising; one Wisconsin resident had bought the book; Wisconsin donations were not established; and the operator had organized no Wisconsin class action. The court distinguished actual contacts from unexercised capabilities available to any Internet user. (1157, 1161, 1163–1164.)

## Jurisdictional framework and the state-law question left open

The party asserting jurisdiction had to make a prima facie showing unless an evidentiary hearing occurred. The court treated the jurisdictional facts as such only for deciding the motion, accepting well-pleaded allegations subject to the opposing affidavits and resolving relevant factual conflicts favorably to Hy Cite. This was not a trial finding establishing all the complaint's substantive accusations. (1156–1157.)

Federal subject-matter jurisdiction existed under §§ 1331 and 1367, but that did not itself supply personal jurisdiction. The relevant federal statutes did not authorize nationwide service. Nor did the absence of jurisdiction in every state offer an alternative: the operator admitted it could be sued in Arizona, where it had a distribution center. Ordinary state-based personal-jurisdiction analysis therefore applied. (1157.)

The parties mostly assumed Wisconsin's long-arm statute extended to the constitutional limit. Crabb warned that this might be incorrect because Wisconsin decisions examined statutory and constitutional requirements separately. She did not settle § 801.05's application: Hy Cite's failure under due process made that question unnecessary. The decision should not be described as holding the Wisconsin statute coextensive with the Constitution. (1157.)

The opinion distinguished the more demanding general-jurisdiction standard then applied from specific jurisdiction over controversies arising from or relating to forum contacts. Purposeful conduct, rather than random or fortuitous connections, had to justify requiring the defendant to defend in the forum. The general-jurisdiction formulations below are the historical standards this 2004 court applied. (1157–1158.)

## Why Crabb declined a special Internet test

The court reviewed early decisions inferring jurisdiction from broadly accessible online advertising and explained their danger: any operator might become suable everywhere without specifically aiming at a state. It then considered Zippo's spectrum of commercial, interactive, and passive websites. Although influential, that classification could not reliably answer the whole jurisdiction question. A passive site might intentionally target harm in a state, while an interactive commercial site might not target that state at all. Specific jurisdiction still required a connection to the claim; general jurisdiction still required sufficiently substantial forum contacts. (1158–1160.)

Crabb also questioned the need for a medium-specific test. Flexible minimum-contacts principles already existed to accommodate changing technology, and the Supreme Court had rejected mechanical tests. She declined Zippo as a replacement for those principles, while retaining interactivity as potentially relevant evidence of purposeful availment. Repeated transactions could support jurisdiction when accompanied by targeting of forum residents, rather than mere accessibility regardless of location. The decision did not declare interactivity irrelevant or Internet businesses categorically beyond jurisdiction. (1160–1161.)

## General jurisdiction failed

Hy Cite identified no Wisconsin office, substantial forum business, or agents spending significant time there. Nearly all the alleged website contacts were possibilities rather than actual Wisconsin dealings. The single book sale and communications with Hy Cite came nowhere near the continuous and systematic general-business contacts required for jurisdiction over any controversy. (1161–1162.)

The court observed that substantial targeted sales might present a different record, but one sale did not. Hy Cite's undeveloped stream-of-commerce argument supplied no alternative; under the circuit precedent applied here, that theory concerned specific rather than general jurisdiction. Treating mere global accessibility as sufficient would subject an operator to every suit everywhere, contrary to the continuing significance of territorial boundaries. (1162.)

## Specific jurisdiction required both purposeful conduct and a related claim

Hy Cite asserted both ordinary purposeful availment and intentional forum-directed harm. Under the first theory, it relied on the site's several commercial possibilities, its Wisconsin consumers, the one book sale, and the advocacy-program communications. No evidence showed Wisconsin donations, advertising purchases, or class-action activity. The defendant sent no unsolicited Wisconsin emails or mailings and did not advertise its site in Wisconsin. Hy Cite initiated the advocacy discussions and no sale followed. Consumer submissions also did not establish that the operator targeted Wisconsin or obtained its benefits and privileges through those submissions. (1163–1164.)

The book sale was a real transaction, but the claims did not arise from it. The court did not hold a single contract could never establish jurisdiction; it assumed that possibility and examined the required nexus. Hy Cite sued over website statements and trademark references, not breach or fraud concerning the book. That both the book sale and disputed statements appeared on the same website was too tenuous under the claim-connection standard the court applied. (1164–1165.)

## The intentional-effects theory also failed

Applying Calder and circuit authority, the court looked for an intentional tort expressly aimed at Wisconsin, harm principally suffered there, and knowledge of those forum effects. The effects approach did not abolish the requirement of purposeful minimum contacts or automatically authorize suit in any plaintiff's home state. (1165.)

The record did not show that the operator wrote the consumer complaints. Consumers used Hy Cite's names and made the allegedly defamatory statements. Even allowing an inference that the operator created some headings, putting a company's trademark on a website did not itself show targeting of the company's state. Hy Cite also alleged metatags using its marks but did not explain their operation or jurisdictional significance. Crabb treated that undeveloped argument as waived, rather than deciding that metatags could never be relevant. (1165–1166.)

The location of injury was independently deficient. Hy Cite asked the court to infer Wisconsin suffered the brunt simply because its headquarters were there. Crabb declined: a corporation's principal office does not necessarily identify where most sales occur or where reputation is most harmed. She distinguished the Indianapolis Colts case, where a locally based team's mark, fans, and revenues supplied an evident forum concentration. Hy Cite had not shown an equivalent Wisconsin connection. With both specific-jurisdiction routes unproved, the court did not reach the remaining reasonableness factors. (1166–1167.)

## Exact judgment and assigned boundaries

The operative order granted the defendant's jurisdiction motion, directed judgment in its favor, and closed the case. An opening sentence's reference to the plaintiff's motion is inconsistent with the actual posture and closing order; the defendant was the movant. The merits remained unadjudicated. (1156, 1167.)
