# Garlock Sealing Technologies, LLC v. NAK Sealing Technologies Corp.
148 Cal. App. 4th 937, 56 Cal. Rptr. 3d 177 (2007) | Contracts

## Court, judgment, and procedural stage
The California Court of Appeal, Third District, decided this appeal on March 21, 2007. Justice Cantil-Sakauye wrote the opinion, with Acting Presiding Justice Raye and Justice Butz concurring. The report reproduces the opinion as modified when rehearing was denied on April 17. It contains no separate writing. The court affirmed the damages judgment for breach of merchantability and the manufacturer's obligation to reimburse its agent's defense costs. It reversed only the denial of Garlock's implied contractual indemnity claim and remanded for reconsideration. It expressly declined to award that indemnity itself or decide its ultimate equitable merits. (937, 941, 974–975.)

## Supply chain, specifications, and the hidden abrasive
Garlock designed and sold industrial seals. Taiwan manufacturer Mao Shun, later named NAK, supplied seals through Sunrise Trading, its American intermediary. Garlock resold them to Rockwell for gear reducers used in conveyor systems. Rockwell previously had heat-related failures with nitrile-rubber seals and wanted a material compatible with synthetic lubricant. A radial lip seal made from viton was appropriate for that ordinary application. The dispute concerned brown viton seals, not the earlier nitrile failures. (941–942 & nn. 1–3.)

Garlock supplied physical-property and dimensional specifications known as RA-37 and requested the manufacturer's standard brown viton compound. It did not prescribe the compound's formula or a particular iron-oxide pigment. Mao Shun furnished sample seals and a material slab. Garlock checked dimensions, configuration, and standard ASTM physical properties; the samples complied. It did not functionally test them in an operating gearbox, although it had facilities to do so. Its engineer explained that functional testing normally applied to a newly configured component using a new compound, whereas these seals used a standard configuration and familiar compound. Garlock had no reason to suspect abrasive material. Mao Shun formulated the compound itself using pigment from a reputable supplier; its purchasing specifications did not address particle size, and it neither sifted nor tested for particle size. (942–943.)

The brown seals ultimately produced almost a thirty-percent leakage rate, sometimes failing after only 150 operating hours. Investigators found unusual grooves in hardened steel shafts. Even a gearbox submerged in oil developed grooves, eliminating inadequate lubrication as the explanation. Specialized laboratory analysis identified hard, sharp-edged magnetite particles in the brown pigment capable of abrading the shafts. Green viton replacement seals reduced leakage to one or two percent and eliminated grooving. While the cause was being investigated, the brown seals remained in use because no alternative material was available and Rockwell still needed to supply customers. Initial reports of a small number of brown-seal leaks did not establish prior knowledge of the pigment defect. (944–946.)

Rockwell sued Garlock in federal court in August 2001. Garlock's attempt to bring Mao Shun into that case failed for lack of personal jurisdiction in South Carolina. After discovery and expert exchanges, Garlock settled for $2.2 million cash, $150,000 in refunds and discounts, and further potential discounts of $530,000, a stated settlement value of $2.88 million. Garlock then sued Mao Shun and Sunrise in October 2002. After a bench trial, it obtained $1,870,424 in lost profits, consequential and incidental damages, plus interest and costs, against Mao Shun. The court denied Garlock's indemnity claim, held that Sunrise was Mao Shun's agent, ordered Garlock to take nothing against Sunrise, and required Mao Shun to reimburse Sunrise's costs, expenses, and fees defending the suit. Both Garlock and Mao Shun appealed. (946–947.)

## Merchantability survives compatible specifications and reasonable inspection
Mao Shun first argued that complying with the buyer's specifications displaced merchantability. The court rejected its characterization of the trial findings: the trial court had identified an express warranty by sample, not expressly called RA-37 an express warranty. Even assuming the specifications created another express warranty, the warranties were reasonably consistent and cumulative under California UCC § 2317. Garlock specified standard material and dimensions while leaving chemical formulation to the manufacturer. It did not knowingly demand a particular flawed pigment or recipe. A decision concerning wire made to the buyer's prescribed chemical analysis therefore did not control. The court did not hold that every buyer specification preserves every implied warranty; note 7 confines its conclusion to a standard component whose ingredients and potential ingredient flaws were unknown to the buyer. (948–950 & nn. 6–7.)

Mao Shun next relied on § 2316(3)(b), which removes an implied warranty for defects that a precontract examination ought, in the circumstances, to have revealed. The trial court found the abrasiveness latent and not reasonably discoverable through the applicable testing practices. Mao Shun called that a legal error, but its actual argument attacked the evidence supporting a factual finding. On substantial-evidence review it had to fairly summarize all material evidence and show the absence of support, rather than select favorable statements or ask the appellate court to reweigh conflicts. Its incomplete and misleading summary forfeited the challenge; the court also rejected it on the merits. (950–952 & nn. 8–9.)

Standard ASTM testing had been done, the explanation for omitting functional testing was reasonable, and the extensive later investigation corroborated that the abrasive problem was unexpected. Evidence about practices learned after the failures did not establish that Garlock should have performed those tests beforehand. Nor did describing the product as custom-manufactured establish that it used a new compound or nonstandard design. The inspection consequently did not exclude merchantability for this defect. The rule turns on what examination ought to reveal under the circumstances, not merely whether a buyer examined samples or whether any more elaborate test conceivably could detect a flaw. (951–952.)

The breach finding likewise stood. Rockwell used the seals within ordinary operating parameters, even though they were made for its particular gearboxes. A seal that abraded shafts and prematurely lost oil failed its ordinary purpose. The high leakage rate, laboratory findings, and successful replacement seals supplied substantial evidence. Mao Shun again relied on isolated favorable evidence without confronting the trial court's contrary findings. (953–954 & nn. 10–11.)

## Consequential damages and the limits of appellate review
Providing replacement seals did not eliminate consequential loss from damaged gear reducers. Under § 2715(2)(a), the seller needed reason to know at contracting of requirements and exposure to loss; actual foresight of this precise defect was unnecessary. A longtime seal manufacturer could reasonably anticipate that abrasive seals incorporated in machinery would damage shafts and require replacement. The disparity between a roughly one-dollar seal and a four-hundred-dollar gearbox did not make that consequence unforeseeable. Garlock's additional express obligations to Rockwell did not sever responsibility because merchantability alone would have supported its liability for defective seals. The court upheld the finding under subdivision (2)(a), reserving the alternative property-injury theory under subdivision (2)(b) and the proposed superseding-cause analysis. (955–958.)

The court also upheld the $1,678,267 consequential component within the total $1,870,424 judgment. The trial court used accounting evidence to identify the portion of Rockwell's probable recoverable losses sufficiently proved and attributable to the seal failure. It applied a 73.79-percent share to its valuation of Garlock's actual settlement payment as $2,274,383, including $74,383 in valued discounts. This calculation was not simply an award of the entire $2.88-million nominal settlement package. The court found the expert's analysis and supporting documentation sufficient. (947, 959–960.)

Mao Shun did not connect testimony about earlier purchases to any particular damages awarded, so its four-year limitations objection failed to establish error. The court did not decide Garlock's alternative discovery-accrual contention. Continued use while no replacement material existed did not require a mitigation reduction on the evidence viewed favorably to the judgment. A statistical-sample challenge first raised in the reply brief was not considered because no good reason excused its timing. (960–961 & nn. 12–13.)

## Agency and the independent indemnity issue
Substantial evidence supported Sunrise's actual agency. Its 1981 written agreement established representation of Mao Shun, responsibility for finding buyers and facilitating communications, and manufacturer responsibility for product quality. The 1986 shipping and compensation changes accommodated ocean freight, customs, and administrative efficiency; evidence supported continuity of agency despite use of Sunrise order forms and a markup. Sunrise paid Mao Shun after receiving Garlock's payment and referred warranty issues back to the manufacturer. Whether an intermediary acts primarily for the manufacturer's benefit or buys for its own account depends on the relationship's facts. Mao Shun's selective evidentiary presentation forfeited its challenge, which the record also defeated on the merits. Because actual agency sufficed, the court reserved the alternative ostensible-agency issue. (962–967 & n. 14.)

Garlock's own indemnity appeal presented a different question. The trial court had required proof that Mao Shun negligently breached its contractual duties and rejected indemnity because negligence was not proved. Reviewing the doctrine's history, the appellate court explained that earlier cases involving negligent performance did not make negligence indispensable. Implied contractual indemnity derives from contractual responsibilities and breach foreseeably causing the indemnitee's third-party loss, with loss apportioned according to the contractual equities. Breach of merchantability could supply that basis even without proof of unreasonable care. The court reversed the negligence prerequisite but left the trial court to determine the claim and any equitable allocation or denial on remand. Garlock's settlement reduced the manufacturer's exposure to Rockwell, which could not recover twice, but note 17 explicitly reserved the ultimate equitable outcome. (967–975 & nn. 15–17.)

## Assigned comparison and reading
The assigned latent-defect problem occupies C&M physical pages 322–323, printed pages 629–630. It simplifies the supply chain by placing the defective equipment with Garlock and assumes unmerchantability uncontested. The actual equipment belonged to Rockwell, and Mao Shun actively challenged breach and the survival of the warranty. The inspection point is accurately illustrated, but those problem assumptions do not replace the actual litigation findings or separate indemnity remand.
