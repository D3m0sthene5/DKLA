# Swift Canadian Co. v. Banet
224 F.2d 36 (3d Cir. June 23, 1955), rehearing denied August 3, 1955 | Contracts

## Facts and contractual allocation
Swift, a Canadian seller, contracted with the Philadelphia partners doing business as Keystone Wool Pullers to sell lamb pelts at $3.80 each in United States funds, F.O.B. Toronto. The contract also gave routing instructions through Buffalo and the Pennsylvania Railroad to a Philadelphia freight station. It excused liability for governmental orders or acts and provided that, when pelts were sold F.O.B. the seller's plant, title and risk passed upon loading onto railroad cars there. Some pelts were loaded in Toronto and delivered to Philadelphia. Around March 12, 1952, Swift announced readiness to deliver the remainder aboard cars in Toronto. Stricter federal import regulations prevented Keystone from importing these pelts into the United States. Keystone refused delivery; the remaining goods were never loaded or shipped. Swift subsequently sold them in the Toronto market. (37–39.)

## Procedure and arguments
Both sides moved for summary judgment on stipulated facts. The district court entered judgment for the buyers. Swift appealed, seeking judgment in its favor or, alternatively, a trial. Keystone's position combined the Philadelphia shipping directions with the governmental-acts provision: because the new regulations prevented importation, the contemplated shipment could not be completed and the buyer should be excused. Swift maintained that it had promised delivery in Toronto, which remained possible, and that the buyer bore the consequences of import restrictions beyond that delivery point. (37–38.)

## Issue and holding
The controlling question was what performance this particular agreement required of Swift. The Third Circuit held that Swift's obligation ended with delivery F.O.B. Toronto, rather than delivery at a Philadelphia destination. The Philadelphia routing directions served the buyer's convenience and did not transform the shipment contract into a promise to accomplish United States importation. Keystone's inability to import the goods did not excuse acceptance and payment under this allocation. Swift was entitled to the difference between the contract price and the stipulated resale price. (38–39.)

## Reasons
The buyer could have changed the destination without violating the agreement. It could, for example, have directed shipment to New York rather than Philadelphia, and Swift could not have insisted that Philadelphia was the only permissible destination. That flexibility showed that the destination instructions were directions for shipment, not an undertaking by Swift to deliver successfully into that market. The court cited sales authorities and the UCC's treatment of buyer-supplied shipping directions to support this construction. (38.)

The express risk provision referred to F.O.B. the seller's plant, whereas the operative price term said F.O.B. Toronto. The court acknowledged the textual difference but treated the risk provision as evidence of the parties' expressed meaning of F.O.B.; it found no relevant difference between the named Toronto delivery point and the plant formulation here. F.O.B. ordinarily carried a presumption that property passed upon delivery aboard the carrier. That meaning was rebuttable, rather than an inflexible rule for every contract, but nothing in this agreement displaced it. Swift was to forward the bill of lading and a draft through a Philadelphia bank after shipment. Its promised performance would then be complete. (38 & n. 1.)

The buyer received both the risk of loss and the possibility of gain from market changes after that point. United States import restrictions disappointed its expectation of a profitable transaction, but did not prevent the seller's promised Canadian delivery. So far as the record showed, other destinations remained available. The governmental-acts clause therefore did not reallocate the import obstacle to Swift simply because the buyer's intended market became unavailable. The analysis depends on the agreed delivery point and the particular clause; the opinion does not decide what a destination contract or a Canadian export prohibition would require. (38.)

Swift did not have to load the pelts after Keystone's refusal merely to preserve its claim. A party ready to perform need not undertake a futile physical tender after the other side announces that it will reject performance. The seller could assert the rights it would have had upon loading. This was a readiness-to-perform case, not a finding that the undelivered pelts had actually been loaded or that title had factually transferred before refusal. (37.)

## Governing law and judgment
The court described the controversy as one of local sales law, with no federal question. Pennsylvania and Ontario had materially identical statutory provisions governing this point. The dispute concerned their application to this agreement, rather than disagreement over the general sales rule. Because the contract price, resale price, and manner of resale were undisputed, no factual issue required trial. The court reversed and instructed the district court to enter judgment for Swift for the contract-price/resale-price difference. Goodrich wrote for the panel of Goodrich, Hastie, and District Judge Miller; no separate opinion or dissent is reported. (38–39.)
