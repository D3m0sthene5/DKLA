# Neumiller Farms, Inc. v. Cornett
368 So. 2d 272 (Ala. 1979) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and outcome
The Alabama Supreme Court decided this case on March 9, 1979. Justice Shores wrote; Chief Justice Torbert and Justices Maddox, Jones, and Beatty concurred, without a separate opinion. Potato broker Neumiller appealed a $17,500 jury verdict for farmers Jonah Cornett and Ralph Moore. The court affirmed, finding evidence supporting wrongful rejection and an award within the damages recoverable under Alabama’s enactment of UCC section 2-708(2). Its factual account describes what the jury could have found, not independently undisputed findings resolving every conflict. (272, 274, 277.)

## Contract, falling market, and contradictory satisfaction claims
The parties signed a contract on March 3, 1976 for twelve loads of United States Grade No. 1 chipping potatoes, to be delivered in July and August. Each load contained 430 hundredweight; the agreed price was $4.25 per hundredweight, or $1,827.50 a load. The potatoes also had to chip to the buyer’s satisfaction. The sellers’ crop yielded twenty to twenty-four loads. Neumiller accepted three without objection while the market price was $4.25, but later rejected additional loads after the market fell to $2.00, claiming that they would not chip satisfactorily. (274.)

An agricultural-extension expert tested samples and found the potatoes suitable in all respects. Following a demand for performance, Neumiller agreed to try another load. The sellers obtained that load from grower Hartline. Although Neumiller’s agent had recently bought potatoes from Hartline’s fields at $2.00, he expressed dissatisfaction when the sellers offered potatoes from those same fields at the contract price. The jury could credit that comparison over testimony asserting the sellers’ potatoes were diseased and unsuitable. When the sellers offered to obtain the remaining nine loads from other growers, the agent rejected any further performance and referred to being able to buy potatoes for $2.00. (274.)

## Good faith limits the satisfaction condition
The buyer did not claim an inadequate tender procedure; it claimed the goods failed the satisfaction requirement. The court recognized the statutory right to reject nonconforming goods, but required dissatisfaction to be genuine good-faith dissatisfaction rather than a device to escape an unfavorable bargain. Neumiller dealt professionally in farm products and was therefore a merchant. Under the applicable statutory definition, its good faith included both factual honesty and observance of reasonable commercial fair-dealing standards. For a merchant purchasing fungible goods, an objective standard could test whether the dissatisfaction claim met that requirement. (274–275.)

The evidence that the potatoes were suitable and the buyer treated the same grower’s goods differently at different prices allowed the jury to reject Neumiller’s subjective assertion. Rejection based on dissatisfaction not made in good faith was ineffective and breached the contract. The satisfaction clause therefore did not authorize an arbitrary exit when the market changed. This was not a portrait or personal-taste transaction in which the court adjudicated the buyer’s aesthetic preference. (274–275.)

## Why market damages were inadequate on this record
Neumiller argued that section 2-708(1) limited damages to the contract-minus-market difference: $2.25 per hundredweight for the nine rejected loads, subject to statutory adjustments. The court disagreed. It interpreted the market formula as presupposing not only an available market but an obligation on the seller to enter it to avoid the breach’s consequences. Where that obligation was absent and the formula would not restore the performance position, subsection (2)’s profit-based measure could apply. (275–276.)

Some market remained, but at final rejection the sellers had seventeen to twenty-one loads still in the fields, with only about four loads sold despite reasonable efforts in several regional markets. The court concluded the jury could find those efforts reasonable and in good faith. Requiring the farmers to prioritize Neumiller’s nine loads in that limited market over their unallocated crop would sacrifice a substantive opportunity for the breaching buyer’s benefit. The farmers were not required to do so. The court expressly distinguished an inventory consisting only of the contract goods, or a failure to make reasonable efforts to sell the whole inventory, which would have implicated avoidable consequences differently. Its holding did not rest on the absence of any market or a general permission never to mitigate. (274, 276.)

## Profit, incurred costs, and the supported verdict
Subsection (2) allowed profit including reasonable overhead, incidental damages, an allowance for reasonably incurred costs, and appropriate credits. The court distinguished relatively stable overhead from variable costs tied to the particular contract. Already-incurred variable costs could be recoverable, but costs avoidable through diligent, reasonable efforts were not reasonably incurred and could not be recovered. The court found evidence that the farmers had substantially performed and incurred substantially all performance expenses, supporting the jury’s award within this measure. It did not decide the implications of resale or payment credits under subsection (2), expressly finding those unnecessary on these facts. (276–277.)

The nine loads’ contract value was $16,447.50. The opinion reports the sellers’ further claimed $1,480.20 interest and $62 freight, and their asserted total of $17,989.78. The printed components actually sum to $17,989.70, an eight-cent reporting discrepancy; neither sum should replace the affirmed $17,500 verdict. The court sustained that verdict as within recoverable damages, rather than ordering the claimed total paid or describing the entire contract price as pure profit. (276–277.)
