# General Electric Co. v. Environmental Protection Agency

**Course:** Legislation, Regulation & the State  
**Citation:** 290 F.3d 377 (D.C. Cir. 2002)  
**Court and date:** United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, May 17, 2002  
**Opinion:** Chief Judge Douglas H. Ginsburg for the unanimous panel of Ginsburg, Randolph, and Tatel; no separate writing appears.  
**Assigned source:** LRS November pages 1–165 scan, PDF pages 56–61, printed pages 631–636, beginning at the GE caption and ending before Notes.  
**Brief ID:** LRS-MAX-GE

## Case orientation

EPA called its PCB risk-assessment document guidance, but its commands bound applicants and EPA to specified methods. It was therefore a legislative rule requiring the statutory and APA procedures that EPA conceded it had not followed. The court granted GE's petition and vacated the document. It also found the challenge ripe and the action final, without requiring GE first to submit an application and suffer denial.

## Facts and regulatory setting

The Toxic Substances Control Act restricted manufacture, processing, distribution, and use of PCBs unless EPA found the activity would not create an unreasonable risk of injury to health or the environment. EPA regulations supplied general options for cleaning up and disposing of PCB remediation waste and disposing of PCB bulk product waste. Under 40 C.F.R. §§ 761.61(c) and 761.62(c), an applicant could seek approval of another method if it would not pose that unreasonable risk. The regulations did not prescribe how to perform the necessary risk assessment. (Opinion, background; first starred note.)

EPA's Guidance Document addressed that gap. It directed applicants toward two methods. One calculated cancer and non-cancer risks separately, using an EPA-recognized cancer potency factor and considering specified non-cancer endpoints. The other used a total toxicity factor of 4.0 (mg/kg/day)⁻¹ for cancer and non-cancer effects together. EPA represented the latter as a default it would accept without further justification, saving applicants time and expense. The factor was not described in the opinion as a judicially established scientific truth.

GE challenged the document as a legislative rule issued without required public procedure and alternatively as unsupported by substantial evidence. EPA answered that the document was flexible policy, subject to revision as scientific knowledge developed. It also contested ripeness and the court's direct-review jurisdiction.

## Posture, questions, and holdings

GE petitioned for direct review under TSCA § 19(a), 15 U.S.C. § 2618(a). The court first considered ripeness, then whether the document was a rule within that review provision, and finally whether its promulgation complied with law. It held the challenge ripe, concluded the document was a legislative rule within the statutory review provision, and vacated it for procedural noncompliance. It did not decide GE's alternative substantial-evidence attack on the scientific content. (Opinion, ripeness, jurisdiction, and conclusion.)

## Ripeness and finality

The governing ripeness inquiry considered fitness for decision and hardship from withholding review. Whether the document facially bound applicants and the agency was largely a legal question turning on its text. The court did not need a developed dispute about application to a particular cleanup site to resolve that question.

The document marked the consummation of EPA's decisionmaking and determined rights and obligations. The possibility of later revisions did not make its present operative commands nonfinal. Otherwise, routinely updated scientific rules might never become reviewable. EPA's expectation of further non-cancer research therefore did not defeat finality.

EPA also relied on an earlier Fifth Circuit remand concerning PCB regulations. The court explained that the Fifth Circuit had not held the regulations nonfinal or GE's challenge unripe: EPA had agreed to remand for further assessment, and that was the relief GE sought there. The second starred note reproduces that earlier court's limited explanation. An agreed remand for reassessment was not a jurisdictional holding barring present review.

The court did not require an additional showing of hardship. The TSCA's sixty-day deadline for review of a promulgated rule demonstrated a congressional preference for immediate review. Once GE established fitness, delaying review served no statutory purpose. This reasoning does not abolish the hardship component for every agency challenge; it rests on the particular review scheme. (Opinion, ripeness discussion.)

## Legislative rule or policy statement

GE urged a broad APA definition of “rule” for TSCA appellate jurisdiction; EPA argued direct appellate review reached only legislative rules. The court reserved which interpretation was generally correct because this document was legislative under either approach. It did not decide that every guidance document could immediately be reviewed in the court of appeals.

The controlling inquiry was binding effect. Relevant formulations asked whether an announcement imposed rights and obligations or left genuine decisionmaking discretion, and considered its characterization, publication, and actual binding effects. An agency label and absence from the Federal Register were not dispositive. A document could have practical binding force through mandatory text before particular applications proved its effect, or through binding administration. The opinion focused on the text here. (Opinion, legislative-rule discussion.)

Applicants had to address both cancer and non-cancer endpoints. Those declining the total toxicity factor had to address specified effects, including neurotoxicity, reproductive and developmental toxicity, immune suppression, liver damage, skin irritation, and endocrine disruption. Choosing between two mandatory methods was still a restriction; it did not create freedom to submit a third method or omit the specified endpoints.

The document allowed case-by-case treatment of unusual components and unconventional scenarios. That qualification did not eliminate the binding commands applicable in ordinary cases. An exception provision did not by itself turn the standard requirements into tentative policy.

EPA was also bound not to question use of the default total toxicity factor within the document's framework. Its supplemental litigation brief asserted that policy could change at any time, but the document gave applicants no such qualification. The unqualified assurance reasonably created a norm on which applicants could rely. Acceptance of the factor was a further agency obligation, even if EPA had already used the same number in generic 1998 regulations. It was not a judicial conclusion that every plan containing 4.0 had to receive approval regardless of all other requirements. (Opinion, binding-effect application.)

## Application evidence and the scientific-data distinction

EPA said it had approved applications using both listed methods, a claim GE disputed. The court considered that dispute immaterial: even if both methods were practically available, applicants remained confined to the prescribed approaches and endpoints. EPA did not claim to have accepted a third method, allowed omission of required endpoints, or rejected use of the default factor. Its application arguments thus did not rebut the facial binding effect.

The document also differed from informational risk data previously considered in *Chemical Manufacturers Association*. Such data constrained no one until applied in a particular rule. This guidance directly governed the method of an application and the agency's treatment of it. Providing scientific information and imposing a binding assessment method were different actions. The court did not hold that every scientific value or every agency publication necessarily required notice and comment.

## Procedure, judgment, and limits

Once classification and jurisdiction were resolved, EPA conceded the decisive procedural failure. It had not published a proposed rule, afforded interested parties an opportunity to comment, or held the informal hearing required by the TSCA provisions at issue. The requirements arose from both TSCA and APA § 553; this opinion should not be read to make an oral informal hearing universally necessary under the APA alone. (Opinion, final merits paragraphs.)

The unanimous court granted the petition and vacated the Guidance Document. It expressly did not reach GE's alternative claim that the document lacked substantial-evidence support. Vacatur therefore did not judicially establish that the default factor was scientifically incorrect, nor did it invalidate all underlying PCB cleanup and disposal regulations.

## Exam use

Analyze the actual words and operative commitments of a guidance document rather than stopping at its label. A choice between two compulsory pathways can be binding; promises creating a dependable default can bind the agency as well as constrain applicants. Keep classification, finality, statutory appellate jurisdiction, and procedural validity distinct even when the same textual commands bear on all four. State whether the court actually resolved the scientific merits or instead stopped at a conceded procedural defect.
