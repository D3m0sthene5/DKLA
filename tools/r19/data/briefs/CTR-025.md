# Lucy v. Zehmer

**Course:** Contracts
**ID:** CTR-025

196 Va. 493, 84 S.E.2d 516 (1954). Supreme Court of Appeals of Virginia. Buchanan, J., for the Court; no separate opinion recorded. Record No. 4272; decided November 22, 1954.
Appeal from the Circuit Court of Dinwiddie County.

## Parties

Complainants / appellants: W. O. Lucy, a lumberman and farmer who sought to buy the Ferguson Farm, and J. C.
Lucy, his brother, to whom W. O. Lucy transferred a one-half interest in the asserted purchase. Defendants / appellees:
A. H. Zehmer, owner of the Ferguson Farm and operator of a restaurant, filling station, and motor court in McKenney,
Virginia, and Ida S. Zehmer, his wife and a co-signer of the sale memorandum.

## Procedural History

The Lucys filed a bill in equity in the Circuit Court of Dinwiddie County seeking specific performance of a written
agreement for sale of the Ferguson Farm. The Zehmers' answer admitted that Lucy offered $50,000 cash and that both
Zehmers signed the writing. Their defenses characterized the exchange as a jest, attributed legal significance to the
parties' drinking, described Lucy as taking the memorandum from the counter without a formal delivery, and asked
equity to withhold specific performance. The parties presented the evidence through depositions.

The circuit court entered a decree holding that the Lucys had failed to establish a right to specific performance and
dismissed the bill. The Lucys appealed. The Supreme Court of Appeals of Virginia independently assessed the legal meaning of
the parties' communicated words and acts while evaluating the evidentiary record supporting the equitable remedy. It
reversed and remanded with instructions to enter a decree requiring the Zehmers to perform the contract.

## Material Facts

The Ferguson Farm was a tract of approximately 471.6 acres in Dinwiddie County. Lucy had known Zehmer for fifteen
to twenty years, had been familiar with the farm for about ten years, and had tried to purchase it before. According to
Lucy, Zehmer had orally accepted a $20,000 offer seven or eight years earlier and then declined to carry out that sale.
By December 1952, Zehmer had owned the farm for more than ten years after purchasing it for $11,000 and had
received numerous inquiries about selling it.

On Saturday evening, December 20, 1952, Lucy visited Zehmer's restaurant. He asked whether Zehmer had sold the
farm and said he believed Zehmer would decline $50,000 for it. Zehmer answered that he would take that amount and
questioned whether Lucy could supply $50,000 cash. The men discussed the proposed sale for approximately thirty to
forty minutes. Their discussion covered Lucy's ability to pay, the property included in the sale, and examination of title.
Both men drank whiskey during the evening.

Zehmer first wrote on the back of a restaurant guest check, in substance, that he agreed to sell the Ferguson Farm to W.
O. Lucy for $50,000 complete. Lucy objected to the singular wording because Ida Zehmer also needed to sign. Zehmer
prepared a second memorandum: 'We hereby agree to sell to W. O. Lucy the Ferguson Farm complete for $50,000.00,
title satisfactory to buyer.' A. H. Zehmer and Ida Zehmer signed it. The writing incorporated the subject property, the
price, the sellers, the buyer, and the title condition.

The witnesses gave different accounts of delivery and the moment Zehmer announced his claimed joke. Lucy testified
that Zehmer handed him the signed paper and declined a $5 binder because the signed agreement was sufficient.
Zehmer testified that he laid the paper on the counter, Lucy picked it up and pocketed it, and Zehmer then rejected the
$5 and described the exchange as liquor talking. Both Zehmers testified that A. H. Zehmer privately told Ida that the
writing was a joke before she signed; their account placed that statement in a whisper outside Lucy's hearing. On either
account, every communication Lucy received through signing and possession conveyed a completed sale agreement.

The next day, Lucy arranged for his brother to take a half interest and supply half the price. On Monday he retained an
attorney to examine title. The attorney reported favorably on December 31. On January 2, 1953, Lucy wrote Zehmer
that title was satisfactory, he was ready to pay the $50,000 in cash, and he requested a closing date. Zehmer replied by
letter mailed January 13 that he had never agreed or intended to sell.

## Question Presented

Did the Zehmers' words and conduct - extended negotiations over price and terms, preparation and revision of a
complete written memorandum, both spouses' signatures, and transfer of the writing to Lucy - objectively manifest
assent to sell the farm, thereby creating a contract enforceable by specific performance, when the Zehmers asserted an
inward intention to jest and drinking accompanied the exchange?

## Holding

Yes. The communicated words and acts carried the reasonable meaning of a serious offer and acceptance. Contractual
intention therefore corresponded to that objective meaning, regardless of the Zehmers' privately held characterization of
the exchange as a joke. The record also established that each signer understood the nature and consequences of the
instrument. The contract was complete, the price was fair by Zehmer's own account, and established equitable
principles entitled the Lucys to specific performance. The decree was reversed and the cause remanded for entry of an
enforcement decree.

## Reasoning

Objective assent: Contract law assigns a party the intention reasonably expressed by that party's words and conduct.
Mutual assent concerns manifestations communicated between the parties. When words and acts carry one reasonable
meaning, a party's unexpressed personal meaning becomes legally immaterial unless the other party knows of that
special meaning. This rule protects the reliability of exchanges by allowing each party to organize conduct around the
meaning the other party deliberately conveys.

Application to the negotiations: The exchange displayed the structure of a land-sale transaction. The discussion lasted
forty minutes; the parties debated the buyer's resources; they identified what property the word 'complete' covered;
Lucy requested a title-examination condition; Zehmer produced a written agreement; Lucy identified the need for both
spouses' signatures; Zehmer revised the paper to use 'we'; and Ida read and signed the final writing. Those acts
objectively communicated deliberation, agreement, and completion.

The claimed joke: A jest affects assent through its communicated context. Here, Zehmer's alleged statement to his
wife was designed to remain outside Lucy's hearing. The first claimed communication to Lucy that the matter was a
joke followed execution of the writing and Lucy's act of taking possession and offering a binder. The manifestations
available to Lucy before formation therefore conveyed a sale.

Lucy's understanding: Lucy actually treated the writing as a purchase and had objective grounds for doing so. His
immediate arrangements with J. C. Lucy, retention of a title attorney, acceptance of the title, stated readiness to pay the cash price,
and request for closing corroborated his understanding. These later acts confirmed the meaning he reasonably assigned
to the completed transaction at the restaurant.

Intoxication: Intoxication supplies a basis for avoiding a transaction when it deprives a party of the ability to
comprehend the transaction's nature and consequences and the legal prerequisites for avoidance are established. The
detailed bargaining, drafting, revision, signing, and testimony showed that Zehmer retained comprehension. Defense
counsel conceded at oral argument that the evidence established capacity to contract.

Specific performance: Specific performance rests in sound judicial discretion governed by settled equitable principles.
The memorandum identified the farm and $50,000 price and contained the title condition. Both sellers signed. Zehmer
called $50,000 a good price; the evidence showed drinking at a level consistent with comprehension; and the
transaction involved parties dealing at arm's length. These circumstances supported enforcement of the promised
conveyance.

Exam rule: Identify the words and acts communicated to the promisee, place them in their transactional setting, and
assign them the meaning a reasonable person in the promisee's position would understand. Then address special
defenses such as incapacity and equitable-remedy limits separately. The objective meaning conveyed to the promisee
controls over a secret reservation about legal commitment.
