Citation
Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc., 517 U.S. 370 (1996). Supreme Court of the United States; argued
January 8, 1996; decided April 23, 1996. Justice Souter delivered the opinion for a unanimous Court. Casebook
pp. 1084–1088; full judicial opinion: Full judicial opinion. The detailed historical inquiry and distinctions
among earlier patent decisions are drawn from the full opinion; the casebook abbreviates those portions. This
brief addresses the judge–jury allocation decided in 1996 and does not purport to resolve later questions about
standards of appellate review.
Parties
Markman owned a patent for an inventory-control system used by dry-cleaning businesses and was the
infringement plaintiff and Supreme Court petitioner. He sued Westview Instruments, which supplied the
accused system, and Althon Enterprises, a dry-cleaning operator using it. The opinion refers to the defendants
collectively as Westview. They were respondents defending a judgment as a matter of law entered in their
favor after a jury verdict for Markman. 517 U.S. at 374–376.
Material Facts
Markman’s patent described a system that monitored the status, location, and movement of clothing through a
dry-cleaning establishment. A keyboard and data processor generated transaction records, including bar codes
read by optical detectors as employees logged the clothing’s progress. Westview’s system also used a
keyboard, processor, bar-coded tickets, and optical readers, but Westview maintained that it tracked invoices,
charges, and receivables rather than the physical articles of clothing. Id. at 374–375.
The dispute turned in part on the patent’s use of the word inventory. Patent claims are provisions within the
patent document that define the protected invention’s scope; the word claim here does not mean a cause of
action in a complaint. Independent claim 1 referred to maintaining an inventory total and detecting improper
additions and deletions. Markman’s witness testified about the meaning of the language. The meaning of
inventory determined whether the accused device’s inability to track garments placed it outside the patent. Id.
at 373–375.
The district judge construed the patent to require tracking physical clothing as well as cash inventory. On that
construction, a system that only tracked receivables and transaction totals lacked the specified capability. The
Supreme Court’s question was who had authority to make that construction, including the treatment of
technical terminology explained through testimony. Id. at 375–376.
Procedural History
A jury in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania heard the evidence and found infringement of independent claim
1 and dependent claim 10. The district court nevertheless granted Westview’s deferred motion for judgment as
a matter of law. One ground was its construction of inventory and the accused system’s inability to track
clothing through the cleaning process or report garments’ location and status. Id. at 375 & n.2.
Markman appealed, arguing that the judge had improperly replaced the construction the jury presumably
adopted. The Federal Circuit affirmed, holding claim interpretation to be the court’s responsibility and
rejecting the Seventh Amendment objection. The Supreme Court granted review of that allocation and
affirmed. The result left the judgment favoring Westview in place. Id. at 376, 391.

Issue
Although a patent-infringement action of this kind carries a right to a jury, must the jury also construe disputed
terms of art in the patent claims when expert testimony bears on their meaning? Or may the judge determine
the meaning of inventory as part of construing the patent document? Id. at 372, 376–378.
Rule and Holding
Construction of the patent, including technical terms within its claims, is the exclusive responsibility of the
court. The Seventh Amendment jury right attached to the infringement action does not make every subsidiary
issue a jury question. Allocation of a particular issue requires examination of the historical practice or
appropriate analogies and, where those do not supply a decisive answer, precedent, institutional competence,
and relevant statutory policies. Id. at 376–391.
Those considerations supported judicial construction of inventory. The Court retained the distinction between
interpreting the scope of the patent and deciding whether the accused product infringed under the governing
construction. Its holding did not remove every factual question in a patent case from the jury. Id. at 384–390.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the Federal Circuit. The district judge, rather than the jury, was authorized to
interpret inventory, and Markman’s jury-allocation objection did not disturb the judgment for Westview. The
Supreme Court did not reinstate the infringement verdict or order a new jury determination of the patent’s
meaning. Id. at 391.
Reasoning
The first historical question was straightforward: patent-infringement actions seeking legal relief descended
from actions tried at law, and their jury character was undisputed. The Court then asked a distinct question
about the subsidiary task of claim construction. Protecting the substance of the jury right does not require
assigning every step in a legal action to the jury. Some matters, including legal interpretation, may belong to
the judge without undermining the jury’s proper responsibility for the ultimate dispute. Id. at 376–378.
The exact modern practice lacked an eighteenth-century counterpart. Modern claims did not acquire their later
statutory role until the nineteenth century. Earlier disputes focused on specifications describing inventions,
often addressing novelty or whether the description enabled a skilled person to reproduce the invention. The
Court therefore examined specification construction as the closest historical practice, rather than treating the
absence of modern claims in 1791 as automatically favoring either decisionmaker. Id. at 378–380.
The surviving cases did not establish that juries construed disputed patent terms. A jury’s resolution of novelty
or enablement did not prove that it independently supplied the meaning of every word in the document. Judges
ordinarily construed written instruments in other litigation, and the more informative early patent reports
likewise showed judges interpreting specifications. The Court acknowledged the limited historical record
instead of claiming a direct and unbroken eighteenth-century rule for modern claim construction. Id. at
380–383.
Markman’s narrower argument about terms of art also lacked a sufficient historical foundation. He relied on
nineteenth-century statements suggesting that technical words could go to a jury. The Court concluded that
those later statements did not establish a controlling practice at the founding. It expressly left open whether its
treatment of technical terms in patents should extend to other kinds of written instruments. Id. at 383–384 &
n.9.
Existing precedent reinforced the allocation to the judge. Winans v. Denmead had separated judicial
construction of the patent from the jury’s factual determination of infringement. Markman relied on Bischoff v.

Wethered and Tucker v. Spalding, where expert testimony helped the jury compare inventions or identify their
physical characteristics. Souter distinguished using evidence to understand the accused things from assigning
the meaning of the patent document itself to the jury. Those cases preserved, rather than repudiated, judicial
construction of the written instrument. Id. at 384–388.
The need for expert testimony did not by itself transform interpretation into a jury function. Judges may receive
evidence explaining technical language and then evaluate a proposed meaning against the patent as a whole.
The Court considered the judge’s experience in interpreting legal documents particularly relevant: a plausible
trade meaning still had to fit the structure, specification, and claims of the particular instrument. Treating any
evidentiary foundation as dispositive would bypass that integrative interpretive task. Id. at 387–390.
Markman stressed that conflicting expert testimony could require credibility judgments, an area traditionally
associated with juries. The Court recognized that possibility. It nevertheless expected most such judgments to
be embedded in the broader evaluation of the patent’s internal coherence, rather than turn solely on demeanor.
In its assessment, the decisionmaker responsible for the document as a whole was better positioned to decide
whether an expert’s proposed definition made sense within it. This was a functional reason for the particular
allocation, not a finding that juries are generally incapable of evaluating technical evidence. Id. at 388–390.
Uniformity supplied another reason. A patent establishes rights that affect both its owner and other inventors or
businesses deciding what they may lawfully make. Inconsistent constructions of the same patent create
uncertainty about those boundaries. Judicial treatment permits precedential reasoning and appellate supervision
to promote consistent interpretation. Issue preclusion alone was incomplete protection because new,
independent defendants would not necessarily be bound by an earlier construction. The Court said judicial
allocation would promote certainty, while expressly declining to promise complete uniformity. Id. at 390–391.
These considerations supported assigning the meaning of inventory to the judge even though the infringement
case as a whole was triable to a jury. The Court did not resolve the question merely by labeling the task legal at
the outset. It used history, analogies, precedent, and institutional considerations to explain why the task
belonged on the judicial side of the trial. Id. at 377–391.
Separate Opinions
Justice Souter wrote for a unanimous Court. No separate concurrence, dissent, or nonparticipation is reported.
The conflicting positions discussed in the opinion concern the parties’ arguments and historical authorities;
they do not represent separate Supreme Court votes in this case. Id. at 370, 391.
