# Porter & Dietsch, Inc. v. Federal Trade Commission

**605 F.2d 294 (7th Cir. 1979) — August 8, 1979, amended on denial of rehearing and rehearing en banc October 16, 1979.** Judge Tone wrote the opinion with Judge Pell and District Judge Kirkland, sitting by designation. Assigned source: CivPro physical page 667, printed page 1324, Note 2. No separate judicial writing or numerical vote is reported.

## Parties, advertisements, and the FTC proceeding

Porter & Dietsch packaged and sold X-11 weight-reduction tablets; Fraser was its president and sole shareholder. Its advertising agency and responsible account executive were additional respondents. Pay’n Save, a retail drug chain, participated in a cooperative advertising program, publishing supplied advertisements under its own name. Unlike the other respondents, it had no demonstrated knowledge that the claims were false or unsubstantiated. (297.)

An FTC administrative law judge found that the advertisements promised weight loss without restricting accustomed food intake, conveyed a scientifically supported expectation that substantially all users would lose significant weight, and described a unique ingredient. They also omitted that testimonial results were atypical, a restricted-calorie diet was necessary, and consumers with specified diseases should use the tablets only under medical direction. The ALJ found violations of §§5 and 12 of the FTC Act. The Commission adopted the findings and remedy with minor modifications. Petitioners sought circuit review on tribunal composition, procedural fairness, preclusion, evidence, and remedy. (297–298.)

## Participation and procedural fairness

Commissioner Dole was absent from oral argument while on leave but remained a commissioner; Chairman Pertschuk joined the Commission afterward. The court upheld both members’ participation. The recorded argument and record permitted absent or newly appointed members to decide the case; the FTC could decide without oral argument and was not subject to the different statutory requirement petitioners invoked from a communications case. Litigants were entitled to impartial decision makers, not a guaranteed unchanged tribunal composition. Concerns about hypothetical presidential packing did not require excluding later appointees. (298–299.)

The court also rejected a claim that confusion about the role of phenylpropanolamine, the tablets’ principal active ingredient, prevented effective cross-examination. The central question was the truthfulness of the advertisements, not whether the ingredient had any appetite-suppressing effect in isolation. Petitioners actually cross-examined the experts and submitted scientific material; the asserted procedural injury was not shown. (299.)

## Assigned issue: administrative findings did not compel preclusion

Petitioners relied on an earlier FTC case and two Postal Service cases about Hungrex, assumed for this argument to be identical to X-11. The earlier FTC proceeding concerned different issues. The Postal Service cases raised a similar safety issue, but similarity did not require preclusion. (299–300, n.2.)

The court recognized that adjudicative administrative findings can have issue-preclusive effect and that the asserting party need not invariably have participated in the first proceeding. It nevertheless applied an exception for a clear and convincing need to redetermine an issue because of its effect on the public or persons absent from the original case. The FTC sought to protect a broad public from health risks and false advertising in a field of increasing medical and pharmacological knowledge. Under those circumstances, the government could litigate against a new respondent. This was an exception applied to this setting, not a categorical declaration that administrative findings never bind government agencies. (300.)

Footnote 3 supplies a different reason another Postal Service proceeding did not preclude the FTC: that complaint against Porter & Dietsch itself was dismissed without prejudice by stipulation. It also rejects portraying the record as harassment through repeated agency prosecutions. The reasons for rejecting preclusion should therefore remain distinct—different issues, the public-interest exception, and a nonpreclusive dismissal—not collapsed into one universal rule.

## Substantial evidence supported the deceptive-advertising findings

The court reviewed the whole record for substantial evidence, giving appropriate weight to FTC advertising expertise. Large promises about eating normally and automatic substantial losses were not cured by qualifications buried in small print. The package insert itself prescribed a strict eating program, and the record established that weight reduction required restricted caloric intake. The advertised product was the tablets, not an adequately disclosed broader reducing plan. (300–301, nn.1, 4.)

Scientific studies supported, at most, appetite suppression for some users alongside strict diet control; they did not substantiate the represented automatic significant losses for substantially all users. The Commission did not need to decide that scientific testing was the only conceivable reasonable basis for every product claim: these advertisers specifically represented a scientific basis they lacked. A later FDA panel vote could not retrospectively support earlier claims and did not establish their actual breadth. (302–303, nn.5–7.)

The ingredient was not unique to this product. A possible technical sense in which it was distinctive did not cure the consumer impression of a newly discovered, uniquely effective ingredient. Exceptional testimonials misleadingly conveyed typical results. The health warning on packaging came too late for mail-order purchasers who had already paid, and statements suggesting universal safety were misleading. The court sustained the omissions findings, including the need to disclose dieting and specified health risks. These medical conclusions describe the historical record, not present product-safety guidance. (303–304, nn.8–10.)

## Remedies: broad authority with tailored limits

The FTC had broad remedial discretion, but its order needed a reasonable relationship to the unlawful practices. False or misleading commercial speech did not receive the protection claimed by petitioners. Broad coverage of other foods, drugs, cosmetics, and devices was justified for Porter & Dietsch and Fraser because of past violations, ease of introducing new products, and their demonstrated approach to advertising. “Fencing in” could reach related future misconduct rather than only repeat the exact historical violation. (304–306, nn.11–13.)

The court sustained prohibitions on unsupported efficacy claims, unrepresentative testimonials, and misleading uniqueness representations. The scientific-substantiation provision was at the extreme boundary the court would sustain, but these respondents’ conduct justified it. The Commission still bore the burden of proving a violation; the wording did not make truth an affirmative defense respondents had to prove after any representation. The requirement to disclose that dieting was necessary also survived. Different treatment in older adjudications did not itself invalidate this order or create an industry-wide regulatory standard. (305–308, nn.14–17.)

The health-warning provision required modification. As written, it reached all products, although the demonstrated health risk came from PPA or similar compounds. The court limited its coverage accordingly and made the warning identify high blood pressure, heart disease, diabetes, and thyroid disease rather than an unspecified risk to “some users.” The advertising agency and executive waived distinct objections to their order’s paragraph by not raising them; their knowing involvement supplied additional support. (308, n.18.)

## Retailer liability and exact disposition

Pay’n Save could be held liable despite lack of knowledge. Section 12 prohibited disseminating false advertisements without adding a mental-state requirement or excluding unwitting retailers. Congress expressly created specified exemptions elsewhere, including in the criminal provision; enforcement discretion did not create a statutory liability defense. Pay’n Save’s participation was not de minimis, and the Commission acted for public rather than private interests. Footnote 19 clarifies that its §5 liability arose through §12(b), not independently under §5. (308–309.)

Culpability nevertheless affected the remedy. The record did not show a substantial danger that Pay’n Save would deceptively advertise unrelated diet products, so broad fencing-in relief was unjustified for it. The court limited its order to advertising Porter & Dietsch products. (309–310.)

The FTC order was **enforced as modified**, with the narrowed health warning and retailer product coverage.
