1. Hamer v. Sidway
124 N.Y. 538, 27 N.E. 256 (N.Y. 1891). The New York Court of Appeals decided the case on April 14, 1891. Judge
Parker wrote for a unanimous court.
Parties and procedural history
Louisa W. Hamer was the plaintiff and appellant. She held the claim through assignments from William E. Story II, the
nephew and promisee, and from his wife. Franklin Sidway, the executor of William E. Story Sr.’s estate, was the
defendant and respondent.
The trial court entered judgment for Hamer. The General Term reversed. The Court of Appeals reversed the General
Term and reinstated the trial court’s judgment. Because the General Term’s order did not state that its reversal
rested on the facts, the Court of Appeals treated the trial court’s findings as controlling.
Material facts
On March 20, 1869, the uncle promised to pay his nephew $5,000 if the nephew refrained from drinking liquor, using
tobacco, swearing, and playing cards or billiards for money until age twenty-one. The nephew agreed and fully
performed. The agreement therefore required nearly six years of forbearance.
On January 31, 1875, the nephew wrote that he had turned twenty-one and had performed the agreement. On
February 6, 1875, the uncle answered that the nephew ‘shall have $5,000 as I promised you,’ that the money was
certain, that the uncle would not interfere with it until the nephew could care for it, and that the nephew could
consider the money on interest. The nephew consented to leave the money with the uncle on those terms.
Questions presented
The principal question was whether the nephew’s requested forbearance from lawful conduct supplied consideration
even though the conduct may have benefited the nephew and no material benefit to the uncle was shown. The court
also had to address the one-year Statute of Frauds and the statute of limitations.
Holdings
 •  The nephew’s forbearance supplied consideration because he restricted his lawful freedom of action at his uncle’s
 request and on the faith of the promise.
•  The oral agreement fell within the one-year Statute of Frauds because its terms required performance for more
 than one year. The defense was unavailable because it was not pleaded, and the court also treated the uncle’s
later letter and statements as a waiver.
•  An action based solely on the 1869 contract would have been time-barred. The 1875 correspondence and the
 nephew’s assent instead created a trust relationship, which produced a different limitations result.
The exam rule from Hamer
A promisee’s requested surrender of a legal right can be consideration even when the promised conduct improves
the promisee’s health or welfare. The court does not measure consideration by deciding whether the promisee
made a wise choice or whether the promisor received a measurable economic gain.

Hamer: reasoning, limits, and modern translation
Why the forbearance counted
The executor argued that abstinence benefited the nephew and that the uncle received no benefit. The court rejected
that framing because it would require courts to litigate whether promised conduct was good for the promisee in every
case. The legally relevant fact was that the nephew had a lawful choice and surrendered it for years in response to
the uncle’s promise.
The court used the nineteenth-century language of legal detriment. It explained that the waiver of a legal right at
another person’s request is sufficient consideration. The court also stated that, even if benefit to the uncle were
relevant, the record did not permit a finding that the uncle received no legal benefit. A brief should therefore avoid
stating as fact that the uncle received no benefit.
The Statute of Frauds point
The opinion expressly states that the 1869 oral agreement was within the one-year provision. That point is easy to
omit because the case is taught for consideration. The court gave procedural and waiver reasons for refusing the
defense. It did not hold that the agreement fell outside the statute.
The limitations and trust point
The court accepted that the original contract claim would have been barred by limitations. It then asked whether the
1875 exchange left the parties as debtor and creditor or made the uncle a trustee. The uncle identified money in the
bank, said it was intended for the nephew, promised not to interfere with it, offered interest, and received the
nephew’s assent. The court held that those circumstances created a trust.
The opinion does not announce a general rule about the date on which every claim against a trustee accrues. The
careful statement is that the trust characterization changed the limitations result in this case.
Modern Restatement translation
The facts fit Restatement (Second) § 71 because the uncle sought the abstention in exchange for his promise and the
nephew gave the abstention in exchange. Section 79 explains why no additional showing of benefit, detriment,
equivalent value, or ‘mutuality of obligation’ is required once consideration exists. This Restatement formulation is a
modern translation; it is not language used by the 1891 court.
What Hamer does not establish
 •  The case does not make every condition attached to a gift into consideration. The requested conduct must
 function as the agreed price of the promise.
•  The case does not hold that consideration must harm the promisee in an economic or practical sense. The
 relevant surrender was a legal freedom.
•  The case does not establish that an oral agreement within a statute of frauds is automatically enforceable after
 performance. Its treatment of waiver and pleading arose under the law and procedure before that court.
