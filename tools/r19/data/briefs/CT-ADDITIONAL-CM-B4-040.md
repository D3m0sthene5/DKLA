# Leaf v. International Galleries
[1950] 2 K.B. 86 (C.A.) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and disposition
The English Court of Appeal heard the case on February 28 and March 1, 1950. Denning L.J., Jenkins L.J., and Evershed M.R. each delivered reasons, and all agreed to dismiss the buyer’s appeal from Westminster County Court. The appeal concerned rescission and repayment, not an award of damages: a late attempt to add a damages claim had been refused below, and no such claim was before the appellate court. The judges agreed that this particular demand for rescission came too late while expressing different views about the broader availability of rescission after a sale had been performed. ([1950] 2 K.B. at 86–89, 91–95.)

## The painting, discovery, and proceedings
On March 8, 1944, Ernest Louis Leaf bought the specific painting Salisbury Cathedral from International Galleries for £85. The sellers represented it as a work by John Constable. Five years later, when Leaf tried to sell it, he learned that it was not a Constable. He returned it to the gallery and sought repayment; the gallery retained it for investigation and continued to maintain the attribution. Leaf sued to rescind the sale and recover his price. (Id. at 86–87.)

The county court found that the picture was not by Constable and that the sellers had made an innocent misrepresentation. It nevertheless gave judgment for the gallery, relying on the view that an executed contract could not be rescinded for innocent misrepresentation. At the beginning of the hearing, the judge had suggested damages and offered an opportunity to amend, which Leaf declined. At the end he sought that amendment, but the judge refused it as too late. The existing action and appeal therefore did not determine what damages, if any, he could recover for breach of a contractual condition or warranty. (Id. at 87, 89.)

## The buyer’s argument
Leaf argued that an innocent misrepresentation should support rescission even after delivery if the parties could be restored to their original positions. The painting could be returned and the price repaid; the county court had accepted that restoration was possible. Counsel relied on judicial criticism of an absolute executed-contract bar and argued that time should run from discovery of the incorrect attribution, since the buyer had no obligation to obtain an expert examination upon purchase. He asserted an election between a damages remedy under the Sale of Goods Act 1893 and rescission. The respondents’ counsel were not called on. (Id. at 87–88.)

## Denning L.J.: identity, contractual remedies, and acceptance
Denning distinguished the particular subject matter from its authorship. Both parties agreed to buy and sell this specific painting of Salisbury Cathedral. Their belief that Constable painted it concerned quality, even though that belief was important or fundamental in one sense. It did not prevent a contract from existing. The promised authorship could instead be a contractual condition or warranty. Denning assumed in the buyer’s favor that it was a condition, which would initially permit rejection for breach. (Id. at 89.)

The Sale of Goods Act supplied the limiting rule. Under section 11(1)(c), a buyer who had accepted the goods could no longer treat breach of the condition as grounds for rejecting them and repudiating the sale, but could claim damages. Section 35 included retention beyond a reasonable time without notice of rejection among the forms of deemed acceptance. Leaf had taken the picture home, apparently displayed it there, and kept it for five years without rejecting it. That exceeded a reasonable time. His contractual remedy at that point was damages, which he had not presented in this action. The report reproduces the relevant statutory language in its notes. (Id. at 88, 90.)

Denning rejected the proposition that execution always precluded rescission for innocent misrepresentation, citing the contrary treatment of that proposition in other authorities. But he did not need to resolve every implication of those authorities here. In his reasoning, innocent misrepresentation was less potent than breach of a contractual condition; it could not preserve a rescission right after the stronger right to reject for breach of condition had expired. The buyer had an opportunity to investigate within the first days after purchase and had allowed five years to pass. He could not reopen the transaction through this rescission action. (Id. at 90–91.)

## Jenkins L.J.: assumed availability, finality, and damages
Jenkins agreed with dismissal but expressly left open how far innocent misrepresentation could support rescission of an executed sale of chattels. Decisions involving conveyances and leases did not necessarily establish a universal bar for goods delivered without such instruments. Assuming rescission could remain available after delivery, it was still inappropriate on these facts. (Id. at 91–92.)

The attribution was important and induced the purchase. Leaf could have rescinded before delivery, and Jenkins left open whether discovery and rejection within a reasonable time after delivery would also have sufficed. Keeping the painting for five years without seeking further evidence was different. Although the county court had found no laches, the bargain could not remain open indefinitely to later discovery of an incorrect attribution. A buyer had to verify or disprove the representation within a reasonable time or stand by it. Moreover, the representation amounted to a warranty, providing a damages remedy that Leaf had elected not to include when first invited to amend. Jenkins therefore refused equitable rescission without settling its availability in other completed sales. (Id. at 92–93.)

## Evershed M.R.: agreement and expressly noncontrolling observations
Evershed agreed with the grounds for dismissal given by his colleagues. He did not decide the general validity of the rule barring rescission of an executed contract. He added observations for future consideration, rather than a separate categorical holding: artistic attributions could generate changing expert opinions, costly disputes, and uncertainty years after a sale. Even restoration of the same object might raise disputes about wear, fashion, and market value. (Id. at 93–95.)

Leaf’s assertion that he had bought a Constable but never received one required qualification. He had received the specific painting for which he contracted, although its quality and value differed from those represented. Where the representation was contractually binding, damages could give compensation; where it was not, Evershed saw substantial reasons to consider delivery and acceptance the end of the transaction. His observations about the longstanding doctrine and Parliament’s opportunity to change it did not amount to a decision that the doctrine was correct in every respect. He expressly rested dismissal on the grounds already stated by his colleagues. (Id. at 93–95.)

## Assigned account and source scope
The assigned account is on C&M Textbook 1051–1313, physical PDF page 84, printed page 1092. It focuses on the quality-versus-substance distinction and asks about warranty damages. The complete report supplies the separate reason rescission failed after five years, the absence of a damages claim on appeal, and the judges’ differing positions on executed contracts. The result neither awards warranty damages nor establishes a unanimous categorical bar against rescinding every completed sale.
