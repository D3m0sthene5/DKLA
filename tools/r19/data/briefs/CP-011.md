# Hicks v. United States
368 F.2d 626 (4th Cir. 1966) | Civil Procedure

## Patient's treatment and death

The administrator of Carol Greitens's estate sued the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act for negligence by the doctor at the Little Creek Naval Amphibious Base dispensary in Virginia. Greitens, twenty-five and a diabetic since thirteen, was entitled to care as a Navy enlisted man's wife. Her diabetes was controlled. At about 4 a.m. on August 25, 1963, her husband brought her to the dispensary with severe abdominal pain and repeated vomiting beginning roughly an hour earlier. A corpsman obtained records and a history, measured vital signs, and called the doctor, who arrived after fifteen or twenty minutes. The doctor questioned Greitens, felt her abdomen, listened to bowel sounds, diagnosed gastroenteritis, prescribed drugs, and sent her home with instructions to return after eight hours. The examination lasted about ten minutes. 368 F.2d at 628–629.

After further vomiting, she took the drugs and lay down. Around noon, she drank water, immediately vomited, collapsed unconscious, and was returned to the dispensary. Resuscitation failed, and she was pronounced dead at 12:48 p.m. The autopsy showed a high intestinal obstruction caused by small-intestine loops internally herniating through an abnormal congenital peritoneal opening. Strangulation produced a massive hemorrhagic infarction. The appellate claim was not that every erroneous diagnosis constitutes malpractice, but that inadequate diagnostic investigation allowed a lethal, treatable condition to go unrecognized. Id. at 629–630.

## Trial ruling and appellate questions

Following the nonjury proceedings, the District Court dismissed the complaint, finding insufficient proof either of negligence or of causation. On appeal, the administrator argued that prevailing professional practice required additional diagnostic inquiry and immediate hospitalization. The government defended the doctor's judgment and asserted that any claim of successful surgery was speculative.

The Fourth Circuit had to assess both the substantive standard and the proper scope of reviewing the trial judge's conclusions. Sobeloff wrote for a panel with Bryan and District Judge Hemphill; no separate opinion appears. The court reversed and remanded for determination of damages, thereby resolving liability rather than merely ordering a new trial on negligence. Id. at 633.

## Standard of care and inadequate investigation

The court applied Virginia's contemporary standard: a physician must use the skill and diligence of an ordinarily prudent practitioner in the relevant field and community or similar communities, rather than guarantee a correct result or exercise the profession's highest conceivable care. A mistaken diagnosis reached with ordinary care does not alone create liability. Id. at 629.

Here, gastroenteritis and high obstruction had similar symptoms, but the latter was nearly always lethal without prompt surgery. Both plaintiff's local general-practitioner experts, the government expert, and the doctor recognized asking about diarrhea and performing a rectal examination as accepted procedures for differentiating the conditions. The doctor omitted those steps. The greater statistical likelihood of gastroenteritis did not excuse skipping recognized diagnostic precautions against the lethal alternative. An adequate investigation could have alerted him to the need for observation and immediate surgery if obstruction was confirmed. Id. at 629–630 & n.1.

The government expert's general characterization of the doctor's performance as average judgment rested on an assumption that the diagnosis was tentative. The doctor's own testimony contradicted that assumption: he had ruled out obstruction at the beginning without differentiating tests, recorded gastroenteritis without qualification, and told the patient not to return for eight hours despite continuing pain. He explained the return instruction by possible diabetic complications, rather than ongoing investigation of obstruction. Even the government expert acknowledged that persistent abdominal pain over several hours could call for surgery. Sending her home made the insufficiently investigated diagnosis functionally final. Id. at 631–632.

The expert's conclusory favorable description did not bind either court. Experts supplied the professional standard; the court determined whether the undisputed conduct met it. The panel concluded that the omitted examination and diagnostic steps established negligence as a matter of law. Id. at 632.

## The opinion's treatment of Rule 52

Hicks distinguishes the trial judge's findings of evidentiary facts from the ultimate application of a negligence standard to undisputed facts. It emphasizes deference when the judge reconciles conflicting testimony or evaluates demeanor, but views those rationales as having limited significance where the experts agree on the basic facts and accepted diagnostic steps. It therefore characterizes negligence on this record as a legal conclusion freely reviewable on appeal and declines to apply the clearly-erroneous standard to that conclusion. Id. at 630–631.

That is the Fourth Circuit's 1966 reasoning, rather than a universal present-day exemption from Rule 52. The casebook's editorial note quotes Anderson v. City of Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564, 574–575 (1985), which requires deference to factual findings even when based on documents, physical evidence, or inferences and explains that the factfinder's role itself supports deference. The note expressly asks whether Anderson undermines Hicks's rationale. It should be kept distinct from Hicks's own judicial text. The procedural lesson includes scrutinizing whether the proposition being reviewed is an evidentiary fact, a legal standard, or its application; undisputed evidence alone should not be treated as an automatic license for fresh appellate factfinding.

## Causation omitted from much of the assigned excerpt

The complete opinion's Part II rejects the government's causation defense. Both plaintiff's experts testified categorically that prompt surgery would have saved Greitens, and the government expert did not contradict them. The court therefore did not face a record containing only a small, unquantified survival chance unsupported by testimony. Id. at 632.

The panel nevertheless explained that a negligent defendant who destroys a substantial possibility of survival cannot require proof to absolute certainty of what would have happened if the omitted intervention had occurred. Footnote 2 discusses a negligent failure to diagnose a penetrating wound and the distinction between reasonable causation proof and certainty. The opinion analogizes to Gardner's duty to attempt rescue of a missing seaman where a reasonable possibility of rescue existed; footnote 3 adds other maritime examples involving omitted precautions. These analogies explain why uncertainty produced by the breach does not necessarily defeat recovery. Id. at 632–633 & nn.2–3.

On the actual record, the doctor inadequately examined Greitens, misdiagnosed her, and sent her home rather than hospitalizing her. The uncontradicted survival testimony connected prompt surgery to survival. The negligence therefore deprived her of the available opportunity for recovery and proximately caused her death. The judgment was reversed and the case remanded solely for determining damages. Id. at 633.
