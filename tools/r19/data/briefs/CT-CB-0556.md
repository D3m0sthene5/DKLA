# Greenfield v. Philles Records, Inc.
98 N.Y.2d 562, 780 N.E.2d 166 (N.Y. 2002) | Contracts | Unconditional ownership, new uses, and contractual royalties

## The recording agreement and later licensing

Veronica Bennett, later Ronnie Greenfield, her sister Estelle Bennett, and their cousin Nedra Talley formed the Ronettes in the early 1960s. In 1963 they met producer Phil Spector and signed a five-year personal-services recording agreement with his company, Philles Records. They undertook to record exclusively for Philles, which acquired ownership of the resulting recordings and promised compensation under a royalty schedule. The group executed the agreement without counsel and received a collective advance of approximately $15,000. They recorded dozens of songs, including the million-selling “Be My Baby,” but disbanded in 1967. Philles eventually went out of business. Apart from the advance, the artists received no royalties. The exclusivity undertaking did not prohibit earning income from concerts or television and movie appearances or selling reproduction rights to those performances. (98 N.Y.2d at 566–568 & n.1.)

Greenfield married Spector in 1968. Their 1974 California divorce settlement included mutual general releases purporting to resolve past and future claims between them and between Greenfield and Spector's companies. Later, renewed interest in 1960s music and new technologies made the old recordings valuable again. Defendants licensed them for synchronization with film and television, including “Be My Baby” in *Dirty Dancing* in 1987. They also issued licenses to third parties for domestic production and distribution of audio recordings and sold compilation albums. They earned substantial compensation but paid none of the artists royalties. (Id. at 567.)

The artists sued in 1987, arguing that the agreement did not authorize synchronization or domestic third-party licensing and demanding compilation royalties. Defendants initially denied that a contract existed but stipulated in 1992 that an unexecuted copy would determine the parties' rights. They then maintained that its ownership grant authorized any use, subject to whatever royalties the agreement required. Following extensive pretrial proceedings, Supreme Court awarded the artists approximately $3 million in damages and interest. The Appellate Division affirmed and permitted unjust-enrichment recovery at a 50% industry-standard royalty rate for synchronization and third-party licensing. The Court of Appeals granted leave. Whether compilation uses were authorized and the compensation awarded for them were not raised on this appeal. (Id. at 567–568 & n.2.)

## What the two-page agreement transferred

The introductory language contemplated making phonograph records, tape recordings, and similar devices, excluding radio-broadcast transcriptions. The operative clause made all recordings, their reproductions, and the embodied performances entirely Philles' property, free of the artists' claims. It expressly allowed reproductions by methods then known or subsequently developed, sale under chosen labels, or a decision to refrain from exploitation. The artists conceded that this language transferred unconditional ownership of the masters. They nevertheless argued that silence about synchronization and third-party domestic licensing reserved those uses or created ambiguity allowing evidence of industry understandings. Defendants argued that full ownership carried those rights unless the agreement expressly limited them. (Id. at 568–570 & n.3.)

Judge Graffeo applied New York's rule that a complete, clear, unambiguous writing is enforced according to its terms. The writing supplies the best evidence of contractual intent; extrinsic evidence becomes admissible to resolve ambiguity, a legal question for the court. Language with a definite, precise meaning and no reasonable basis for competing interpretations is unambiguous. Courts may not rewrite such language to achieve their own view of fairness. The court situated this controversy in common-law contract interpretation: all these masters predated the extension of federal statutory copyright protection to sound recordings in 1971. Its holding concerned this transfer and did not announce a comprehensive rule for contemporary sound-recording copyrights. (Id. at 569–570 & n.4.)

## Full ownership did not depend on listing every later use

Silence concerning a particular use did not make this unconditional ownership transfer ambiguous. In *Pushman*, an artist who sold a painting without reserving reproduction rights could not prevent the purchaser from reproducing it. In *Burnett*, a comprehensive assignment of a play's rights permitted a television spinoff of *Casablanca* because the assignors had not retained the relevant rights. The court also relied on *Boosey & Hawkes*: broad licensing language covering a musical composition in *Fantasia* encompassed later videocassette and laser-disc distribution, placing the responsibility for negotiating an exception on the party seeking it. These decisions did not mean that every license transfers every right. A contract conveying less than full ownership or enumerating only specified rights can leave other rights with the grantor. That distinction explained *Warner Bros. v. Columbia Broadcasting*, whose grant concerned only certain aspects of *The Maltese Falcon*. (Id. at 570–573 & n.5.)

Reading the Ronettes agreement as a whole, the introductory reference to similar devices did not narrow the operative ownership clause. It was consistent with reproduction through current and future technologies. The royalty schedule likewise established compensation rights rather than restrictions on the owner's ability to exploit the recordings. The agreement therefore authorized synchronization with visual media and domestic third-party distribution in audio formats. An unconditional transfer could not be turned into a limited grant merely because the document did not individually name these markets. (Id. at 572.)

The artists invoked *Thomas v. Gusto Records*, a Sixth Circuit decision applying New York law. The Court of Appeals distinguished its royalty-entitlement question from the ownership-use question here and expressly rejected its suggestion that silence about a royalty category creates ambiguity justifying outside evidence. The court also directed that *Caldwell v. ABKCO*, which relied on *Thomas* to presume rights not specifically granted remained with the artist, was not to be followed. Thus these cases are not unqualified supporting precedents for the artists' proposed reservation rule. The court acknowledged that its holding prevented the Ronettes from sharing in synchronization profits under this agreement, but sympathy could not displace the written bargain. (Id. at 572–573.)

## Royalties and the California release required different treatment

Authorization to use the recordings did not eliminate contractual compensation. Defendants conceded that the domestic-sales royalty schedule covered records, compact discs, and other audio reproductions sold by entities holding Philles' domestic distribution licenses. The court remanded to recalculate royalties for all such sales under the contractual schedule. It rejected using the industry-standard 50% figure to replace the agreed compensation. The result therefore was neither complete dismissal of the artists' action nor affirmance of the entire approximately $3 million award. (Id. at 573–574.)

Defendants separately contended that Greenfield's divorce release barred her participation. Because the release was executed and the divorce finalized in California, the court applied California law to that instrument. It expressly contrasted New York's four-corners approach with California's preliminary consideration of credible evidence relevant to a meaning the language could reasonably bear, citing *Pacific Gas & Electric*. Supreme Court had found from the extrinsic evidence that compensation under the 1963 recording agreement was not an intended subject of the release. The Appellate Division affirmed that finding, which the record supported. The Court of Appeals left it undisturbed, allowing Greenfield to share in damages properly assessed on remand. The distinct treatment followed the different governing law for the release, rather than an exception to the New York rule used for the recording contract. (Id. at 574.)

## Disposition and source scope

On October 17, 2002, the Court of Appeals modified the Appellate Division's order without costs, remitted the case to Supreme Court for proceedings consistent with its opinion, and otherwise affirmed. Graffeo wrote for all five participating judges; Levine, Ciparick, Wesley, and Rosenblatt concurred. Chief Judge Kaye and Judge Smith took no part. There was no separate opinion. (Id. at 574.)
