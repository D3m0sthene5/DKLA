# Berube v. Fashion Centre, Ltd.

771 P.2d 1033 (Utah 1989) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B2-010

## Employment, policy, and repeated examinations

Fashion Centre hired Shirley Berube as a sales clerk in its Ogden store in April 1979 and promoted her to assistant manager in 1981. Her reviews ranged from good to superior, and a manager told her she could eventually manage a store. Although she acknowledged that employment had no specified duration, she understood the company's representations and disciplinary policy to limit its grounds for discharge. The policy promised warnings and an opportunity to improve performance except for listed grounds, including dishonesty, falsification of records, gross misconduct, and refusal to take or failure to pass a polygraph. The precise obligation derived from that policy was a central remand question, not an adjudicated lifetime-employment guarantee. 771 P.2d at 1035–1036, 1047–1048 & nn.14–15, 1050.

An apparent inventory shortage exceeding three percent prompted an investigation in fall 1981. Whether a shortage actually existed or was recovered remained unclear. Fashion Centre furnished fifteen questions to Western States Polygraph. Berube's first examination indicated apparent deception only when she denied knowing for certain who had stolen from the store. The examiner's post-test interview explained the reaction as suspicion of others who had threatened to quit rather than take the test, rather than concealment of definite information. Western States sent both the result and that explanation to the employer. An expert affidavit later criticized the questions and examination as below professional standards; the appellate court did not adjudicate negligence on that evidence. Id. at 1036 & nn.1–2.

Without explaining why or saying she had failed, Fashion Centre required a second examination by another company. She showed no deception and passed. A pretest interview disclosed occasional rounding of manual class-count figures when she believed employees had erred; those counts were distinct from the company's perpetual inventory system. The employer nevertheless required a third examination, again without explaining the reason. Berube believed she had passed the prior tests and became distressed. On the scheduled day she sought postponement, offered a doctor's statement, and was told she must take the examination or lose her job. After working her shift, she said she could not take it and was directed to sign termination papers the next day. Her renewed willingness to take the examination did not avert discharge; suggested reapplication produced no response. Id. at 1036–1037 & n.3.

## Procedure and the divided court

Berube sued in October 1983 for defamation, wrongful discharge, emotional distress, and breach of employment contract. The court allowed amendment to add Western States but refused a claim under Utah Code § 34-37-16(2). It granted summary judgment to Western States on all claims and to Fashion Centre except on defamation and wrongful discharge. A jury then found Fashion Centre not liable. On appeal, Berube challenged the statutory ruling, the polygraph-related negligence dismissal, and refusal of instructions on exceptions to at-will employment. Id. at 1037, 1039–1040.

Justice Durham wrote the lead opinion, joined throughout by Stewart. Howe, joined by Chief Justice Hall, concurred in Parts I–III but only in the results in Parts IV–V, relying on the written policy manual. Zimmerman joined Parts I–IV and V.A, but not the lead opinion's remaining discussion; he supported a retrial on the implied contractual term. All five therefore agreed in the relevant affirmances and the remand, with different employment rationales. Only Durham and Stewart supported a remand on a broader implied-in-law good-faith covenant. The governing remand was limited to an implied-in-fact contract theory. Id. at 1049–1053.

## Statutory and negligence affirmances

Section 34-37-16 concerned surreptitious deception-detection examinations. Berube contended that its prohibition on terminating someone for refusal to submit covered every such examination, relying on a sponsor's statements and legislative counsel's interpretation. The employer read the subsection within the statute's narrower context. The court agreed with the employer: the language protected refusal to consent or cooperate with proposed surreptitious examinations, but did not reach the nonsurreptitious polygraphs here. An expansive prohibition on polygraph screening would ordinarily have been stated directly rather than embedded in a provision concerning a subset of examinations. The court expressly reserved whether employment polygraphs were generally sound or lawful under other theories. Id. at 1037–1039 & nn.4–6.

The negligence ruling rested on Berube's signed consent releasing Fashion Centre and Western States from claims related to the first polygraph. The trial court had relied on proximate cause, reasoning that refusal of the third test caused discharge. The Supreme Court instead affirmed on the release, whose scope and validity Berube did not challenge on appeal. Earlier claims of duress and post-signature insertion of the employer's name had not been pursued there. The court recognized that releases could be attacked on ordinary contract grounds, including unconscionability; it did not declare every employment waiver invulnerable. Nor did it resolve the proximate-cause issue. Id. at 1039–1040 & nn.7–8.

## Contract theory supporting retrial

Although Berube had not objected to specific instructions drafted to conform to the trial court's ruling, she had renewed her challenge to their overall statement of employment law. In the interests of justice under Rule 51, the court considered that unusual attempt to change the law. Durham's historical discussion treated at-will employment as a rebuttable construction of unspecified-duration arrangements, not a rule overriding proven contractual intent. Promises limiting dismissal could arise from an employment manual and surrounding circumstances. Separate consideration and symmetrical termination rights were not necessary conditions for enforcing a proved limitation; Zimmerman expressly agreed on those points. Id. at 1040–1041, 1044–1045, 1051–1052.

Durham found the manual, favorable reviews, advancement, and representations sufficient prima facie to support contractual limits on discharge. The policy's investigatory purpose also mattered: repeated cooperation on one shortage might satisfy the contemplated obligation, rather than permit an unlimited succession of tests followed by discharge at the employee's eventual refusal. Durham rejected Berube's literal argument that the singular expression “a polygraph” permitted only one examination during an entire career. The question was reasonable use in these circumstances, not an absolute one-test limit. Id. at 1047–1049.

Howe and Hall confined the analysis to the employer's manual as part of the employment agreement. A fact finder should decide whether discharge without warning violated that agreement or whether Berube was properly discharged for unreasonable refusal or failure of a polygraph, grounds permitting discharge without advance warning. Zimmerman likewise reasoned that a jury could find an implied-in-fact term prohibiting discharge for a reasonable refusal. Interpreting the policy as permitting unlimited examinations followed by discharge would effectively restore at-will power and could conflict with the agreement's overall meaning. Taking the evidence favorably to Berube, a jury could find breach by demanding a third test despite no unexplained indication of deception in the first two. These were permissible jury findings on remand, not final appellate findings of breach. Id. at 1050, 1052–1053.

## Proposals that did not control this remand

Durham and Stewart also favored a contract-based good-faith covenant responsive to the employment setting and reasonable expectations, while cautioning that ordinary termination without cause would not invariably breach it. Durham viewed the repeated-test sequence as prima facie unreasonable under that broader covenant. Zimmerman declined to recognize that cause of action: the proposed duty lacked predictable limits and would allow fact finders to impose obligations afterward on almost any employer. Howe and Hall found it unnecessary to go beyond the manual. Consequently the lead opinion's good-faith discussion did not authorize trial on that independent theory. Id. at 1046–1047 & nn.11–13, 1049–1052.

The lead opinion also discussed a substantial-public-policy exception but acknowledged that it did not apply to Berube's case. Zimmerman agreed that an exception should be recognized, while reserving its precise content and preferring a contract remedy absent an independent tort. The lead opinion's broader survey and tort discussion should therefore not be treated as a unanimous resolution of a public-policy claim in this litigation. Id. at 1042–1044 & n.10, 1050–1051.

## Exact result and reading scope

The court **affirmed the statutory and negligence rulings, reversed in part, and remanded for retrial on breach of an implied employment-contract term**. Fashion Centre could contest existence of the term, show an express disavowal, or establish that the third examination was essential and reasonable. If Berube proved breach, general and foreseeable consequential contract damages could be awarded on the proof; no damages amount or final liability award was entered. Id. at 1035, 1039–1040, 1049–1050, 1053. Rehearing was denied April 28, 1989.
