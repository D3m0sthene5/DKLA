# McCarthy v. Tobin
429 Mass. 84, 706 N.E.2d 629 (1999), March 2, 1999 | Contracts

## Court, parties, and litigation
Justice Abrams wrote for the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, with Chief Justice Wilkins and Justices Lynch, Greaney, Fried, Marshall, and Ireland also sitting. The published opinion contains no separate writing or numerical vote. John McCarthy sought specific performance and damages against Ann Tobin, claiming that their signed offer-to-purchase form bound her to sell real estate. Robert and Juliann DiMinico intervened because they later contracted to buy the same property. (84–86 & n. 1.)

The motion judge granted summary judgment to Tobin and partial summary judgment to the interveners, declaring that Tobin had no obligation to sell to McCarthy. The Appeals Court vacated that judgment and directed judgment for McCarthy. On the interveners’ application, the Supreme Judicial Court granted further appellate review. Its account treats the transaction’s material facts as undisputed, while preserving an immaterial disagreement about a later delivery conversation. (84–86 & n. 2.)

## Offer, formal agreement, and missed deadline
On August 9, 1995, McCarthy signed a standard Greater Boston Real Estate Board offer-to-purchase form. Tobin accepted it on August 11. The form described the property, price, deposits, limited title requirements, and closing time and place. It required execution of the standard purchase-and-sale agreement by 5 p.m. on August 16 and said that document, once executed, would be the parties’ agreement. A typed provision made the offer subject to a purchase-and-sale agreement satisfactory to both. The form also made time essential and prominently warned that it created binding obligations. (85.)

Tobin’s lawyer undertook to prepare the formal agreement, but faxed the first draft only after 5 p.m. on August 16. McCarthy’s lawyer proposed revisions on August 21 concerning title, preclosing casualty risk, indemnification for title-insurance purposes, and working building systems. The lawyers discussed them the next day without discussing a deadline extension or an objection to the elapsed date. A revised draft arrived August 23. On Friday, August 25, McCarthy’s lawyer said the agreement was acceptable and would be signed and delivered Monday. McCarthy signed it Saturday, August 26; Tobin accepted the DiMinicos’ competing offer that day. (85–86.)

McCarthy delivered the executed agreement and deposit to Tobin’s broker Monday, August 28. The next day her lawyer rejected it as late and reported acceptance of the competing offer. Tobin and the DiMinicos later executed their agreement, and ultimately closed. The court notes a dispute about whether Tobin’s lawyer had agreed to Monday delivery or expected signing Friday; that dispute did not alter the consequences of waiver. (86 & n. 2, 89 nn. 6–7.)

## Existing contract versus agreement to negotiate
The controlling formation question was the parties’ manifested intention. Contemplating a later formal writing can indicate incomplete negotiations, but it need not do so when material terms have already been agreed. A contemplated final writing may memorialize an existing bargain, particularly where customary norms or agreed procedures resolve subsidiary uncertainties. Here the property and price were fixed, and the requested changes were ministerial and nonessential rather than evidence of an unfinished material bargain. (86–87.)

The printed warning reinforced that intention, but did not operate alone. The operative language said that McCarthy offered to buy and that Tobin accepted; it specified payment and the property and limited the offer’s duration. The interveners’ alternative reading—that the parties promised only to negotiate in good faith—did not account for that language. Acceptance bound Tobin to sell and McCarthy to buy. Note 3 advised parties who intend no preliminary binding obligation to say so clearly rather than rely on this form. (87–88 & n. 3.)

## Condition subsequent and waiver
Formation did not make the formal-document requirement meaningless. The court held it unambiguous and enforceable, classifying the August 16 deadline as a condition subsequent: absent timely execution, the obligations already created would be extinguished. A time-essential deadline would ordinarily be enforced, but a condition can be waived. Note 4 acknowledged potential contradiction between the typed and preprinted language while explaining that either construction produced the same result because waiver occurred. (88 & nn. 4–5.)

The lawyer’s conduct, attributable to Tobin as principal, established waiver. He assumed responsibility for drafting but supplied the first draft too late for timely execution. He then continued substantive communications and revisions without invoking the deadline, and his only express communication concerning execution implied that a later date would suffice. Those acts were incompatible with insisting that all obligations had already ended August 16. Once waived, time was no longer essential, and McCarthy’s later tender was reasonable and timely. Whether the lawyer contemplated August 25 or August 28 did not present a material issue. Although waiver ordinarily is factual, undisputed facts made it a legal question here, permitting summary judgment for McCarthy. (88–89 & nn. 5–6.)

## Specific performance and exact judgment
Specific performance ordinarily lies within judicial discretion, but is commonly granted for land because money may not adequately replace that unique interest. The court held specific relief appropriate here. The DiMinicos’ later purchase did not extinguish McCarthy’s right: they had actual notice of his claim and assumed the risk of an adverse outcome. Note 7 confirms that they had closed and held legal title subject to an equitable obligation to convey to McCarthy upon payment of the price he and Tobin had agreed. (89–90 & n. 7.)

The court vacated the judgment and remanded to Superior Court for entry of judgment on McCarthy’s specific-performance claim. This was not merely permission to try formation again and did not quantify money damages. McCarthy separates three issues that an informal preliminary document can combine: present assent, an enforceable later-document condition, and conduct waiving that condition. In the casebook’s prevention-and-waiver discussion, the seller’s late drafting matters through the actual holding of waiver, not a ruling that every formal-document condition is automatically excused. (88–90.)
