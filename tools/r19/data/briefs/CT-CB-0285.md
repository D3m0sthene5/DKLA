Citation, court, and assigned scope
The Second Circuit decided Bayway on June 8, 2000. Judge Jacobs wrote for Chief Judge
Winter and Judge Calabresi. The assigned excerpt at printed pages 285–289 concentrates on
the additional-term materiality inquiry. The full opinion also resolves admission of reply
evidence and identifies arguments and a separate transaction that were outside the appeal.

Parties and requested relief
Bayway, a petroleum seller, sought reimbursement of $464,035.12 in federal excise taxes
from its buyer, Oxygenated Marketing and Trading A.G. (OMT). The government imposed the
tax on Bayway because OMT lacked the relevant tax registration. Bayway asserted that the
parties' contract shifted the economic burden to OMT. The distinction between tax liability to
the government and contractual reimbursement between the parties explains why Bayway
paid the tax before suing. Opinion ¶¶ 1–2, 12–14.
Material facts and the exchanged forms
The transaction involved 60,000 barrels of methyl tertiary butyl ether, a gasoline blendstock.
On February 12, 1998, OMT faxed a confirmation describing its purchase and stating that the
confirmation constituted the entire contract. It asked that discrepancies or omissions be
raised within two working days. The litigation treated this document as the offer. Bayway
responded the next day with its own confirmation, which stated the commercial particulars
and incorporated its March 1994 general terms by reference. Those general terms were not
attached to the fax. Id. ¶¶ 2–10.
The incorporated Tax Clause required the buyer to reimburse the seller for enumerated
federal, state, and local taxes connected to the product or its value, excluding taxes on
income. OMT made no objection and accepted delivery on March 22. Bayway subsequently
learned that OMT lacked the registration that would avoid the federal excise tax. It paid
$464,035.12 and demanded reimbursement under the clause. OMT refused, claiming it had
never agreed to bear that tax. Id. ¶¶ 8–14.
The parties had not previously followed a contrary course of dealing. In litigation OMT's
executives described the clause as an unwelcome surprise, but OMT supplied no evidence
that a reasonable petroleum merchant would have been surprised. Bayway later produced
industry affidavits and standard forms from five petroleum firms showing that buyers
ordinarily bore the taxes on their purchases. Id. ¶¶ 34–42.

Procedural history and scope of review
The Southern District of New York granted Bayway summary judgment. It held that
incorporation by reference was effective notwithstanding the missing attachment, because
OMT could have obtained the terms on request, and that OMT had not established material
alteration. OMT appealed the materiality determination and the use of trade-practice
evidence submitted with Bayway's reply. The Second Circuit reviewed summary judgment de
novo but the decision to receive the reply evidence for abuse of discretion. Id. ¶¶ 14–17,
50–57.
Two qualifications in the full opinion limit what the appeal decided. OMT first argued on
appeal that a different broker's fax had already accepted its offer and that its integration
language was an advance objection under § 2-207(2)(c). The court declined those
unpreserved arguments. Also, a separate transaction with Bayway's parent Tosco produced a
ruling that Tosco lacked a claimed right of offset; Tosco did not appeal. The affirmance of
Bayway's reimbursement judgment did not reverse OMT's success on that distinct offset
dispute. Id. nn.1–2.
Issues
Who bears the burden of showing material alteration when an additional term appears in a
merchant's acceptance under UCC § 2-207(2)? Did the Tax Clause materially alter this
contract because of its character, the buyer's surprise, or the resulting hardship? Could the
court consider trade-practice affidavits offered for the first time with Bayway's reply to
OMT's summary-judgment opposition?
Rules and holdings
The party opposing inclusion bears the burden of proving material alteration under §
2-207(2)(b). Between merchants, the statute presumptively incorporates additional terms
unless one of the specified exceptions applies. Placing the materiality burden on the
objecting party implements that statutory default. Id. ¶ 29.
OMT did not establish that the Tax Clause was material per se or raise a genuine factual
issue of qualifying surprise or hardship on this record. Subjective surprise did not establish
objective surprise where the term reflected unrebutted industry practice. The court expressly
declined to decide whether hardship is an independent basis of material alteration, because
OMT's showing would fail even on that assumption. Id. ¶¶ 31–48.
Receiving Bayway's responsive trade-practice evidence was within the district court's
discretion. OMT first presented its material-alteration theory in opposition, anticipated the
relevance of industry evidence, requested no sur-reply, and identified no contrary evidence it
could have produced. Id. ¶¶ 50–57.
Actual judgment
The Second Circuit affirmed the judgment for Bayway, leaving OMT contractually
responsible for the $464,035.12 tax reimbursement. It did not remand for a jury to decide
materiality, decide the late broker-fax or advance-objection arguments, or revisit Tosco's
unsuccessful offset claim. Id. ¶ 58 & nn.1–2.

Reasoning
Formation and additional-term inclusion were separate inquiries
For purposes of the appeal, Bayway's fax was an effective acceptance under § 2-207(1), not
an acceptance expressly conditioned on assent to new terms. The existence of the sales
contract therefore did not depend on a later agreement to the Tax Clause. Section 2-207(2)
supplied the separate inquiry into which additional terms entered that contract. Both parties
were merchants, and the preserved exception was material alteration. Id. ¶¶ 19–29.
This procedural framing matters because both forms used apparently assertive contract
language. The court did not hold that every integration clause is ineffective as an objection or
limitation. OMT's particular advance-objection theory was outside the preserved appeal.
Treating the result as a categorical ruling about integration clauses would turn a forfeited
issue into a merits holding.
The objecting party bore materiality, while the proponent still bore proof of a trade
usage
If neither side introduced materiality evidence, the statutory merchant rule would include the
added term. The court reasoned that the party seeking an exception must carry the burden of
showing why the presumptive result should not follow. This allocation was a substantive New
York-law question in the diversity case. Id. ¶ 29.
Footnote 4 keeps a related evidentiary point separate. Bayway ordinarily had to prove a trade
usage on which it relied; placing the materiality burden on OMT did not compel OMT to
disprove every imaginable industry practice. Here Bayway did supply affirmative evidence,
and the appellate court reviewed that evidence along with the rest of the record. Id. ¶ 36 &
n.4.
This transaction-specific tax obligation was not an open-ended indemnity
The court rejected OMT's attempt to classify the clause as inherently material simply by
calling it a broad indemnity. The duty concerned taxes on an identified sale, rather than a
prolonged, unpredictable allocation of unrelated liabilities. The opinion contrasted examples
of more fundamental alterations, including its account of New York arbitration law at the
time, but did not announce a universal rule that all tax clauses are immaterial. Id. ¶¶ 31–32.
In a further alternative observation, the court noted that industry practice could rebut a per
se characterization in some circumstances under the New York authorities it cited. That
reinforces the importance of the record rather than eliminating materiality analysis. Id. n.3.
Surprise required an objective commercial component
OMT's executives sufficiently demonstrated their personal surprise. What they failed to
provide was evidence that the clause lay outside what a reasonable merchant in this trade
should anticipate. No contrary prior dealing or trade evidence supported that conclusion. A
party cannot create a trial merely by describing itself as astonished. Id. ¶¶ 34–35.
Bayway's evidence positively reinforced the absence of objective surprise. Three comparison
contracts allocated taxes like the challenged clause. Chevron's form incorporated the tax into
the price instead of separately itemizing it; that did not shift the burden away from the buyer.

Texaco's silence did not contradict the otherwise unrebutted affidavits. The buyer's ability to
obtain the registration that avoided the tax supplied a practical reason for this allocation. Id.
¶¶ 36–42.
A large loss did not establish the asserted kind of hardship
OMT argued that it operated on thin margins and could not afford the loss. Even assuming
hardship independently qualified, the court regarded this liability as limited to a particular
sale, commercially ordinary, and avoidable through the buyer's registration. It contrasted
that obligation with open-ended or prolonged risk shifting. Id. ¶¶ 44–48.
The reservation is important: Bayway did not adopt the categorical position that hardship can
never matter under § 2-207. It held that the asserted hardship was insufficient here and
therefore found it unnecessary to decide the broader question.
Reply evidence answered a newly raised defense without unfairly foreclosing a
response
OMT's answer had denied incorporation generally. Its opposition papers first made the
material-alteration argument and specifically criticized Bayway for lacking industry evidence.
Bayway's reply then addressed precisely that issue. The court saw no unfairness in
permitting a movant to answer new material rather than forcing it to anticipate every
defense in its opening submission. Id. ¶¶ 50–54.
OMT neither sought leave to respond nor identified evidence it would have offered. Those
omissions supported the discretionary evidentiary ruling. The result is not a blanket
authorization for any new reply evidence without an opportunity to answer; it rests on the
sequence and available opportunities in this case. Id. ¶¶ 55–57.
Separate opinions
The reported decision contains a single opinion by Judge Jacobs and no separate concurrence
or dissent.
Study use and limits
Identify the subsection before evaluating fairness. Bayway assumes formation under
subsection (1), applies the merchant inclusion rule in subsection (2), and resolves the
particular materiality exception. It does not use subsection (3)'s terms-by-conduct formula.
The case also demonstrates how burdens of proof, preservation, and an unrebutted
evidentiary record can determine a supposedly fact-sensitive issue on summary judgment. Its
references to tax and arbitration law report the 2000 decision rather than a comprehensive
current-law update.
