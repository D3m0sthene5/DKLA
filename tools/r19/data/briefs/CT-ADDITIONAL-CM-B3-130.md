# O’Day v. McDonnell Douglas Helicopter Company
191 Ariz. 535, 959 P.2d 792 (1998) | Contracts

## Decision and certified questions
Justice Martone wrote the Arizona Supreme Court’s en banc answer to federal certified questions about after-acquired employee misconduct. Chief Justice Zlaket, Vice Chief Justice Jones, Justice Feldman, and designated Judge Toci concurred; retired Justice Moeller did not participate. The court distinguished contractual recovery of discharge-related wages and benefits from tortious wrongful-discharge liability. It answered the questions rather than entering a final damages judgment or itself deciding every issue in the federal action. Citations below refer to the opinion’s original numbered paragraphs and official slip pages. (¶1; slip 1, 18–19.)

## Certified facts and prior proceedings
McDonnell Douglas denied Dennis O’Day promotion to lead engineer on June 8, 1990. About a month later it laid him off during a general workforce reduction. O’Day was forty-six and had worked there fourteen years; he believed age motivated the adverse decisions. After exhausting EEOC remedies, he sued under the federal ADEA and Arizona Civil Rights Act and also pleaded breach of contract and wrongful discharge in violation of public policy. (¶¶2–3.)

The night after the promotion denial, O’Day searched his supervisor’s office, took documents he thought useful to a discrimination claim, copied them, and returned the originals. McDonnell Douglas learned of that misconduct only after discovery in the suit began. The district court initially granted summary judgment for the employer on all counts under an after-acquired-evidence defense. The Ninth Circuit affirmed establishment of that defense but reversed dismissal under McKennon’s remedial principles. It remanded for trial while reserving how the defense affected the state contract and tort claims. Those reserved questions prompted this certification. These facts supply the decision’s setting; the Arizona court was not independently trying the alleged discriminatory motive. (¶¶4–6.)

## Parties’ arguments and the statutory comparison
O’Day argued that misconduct during employment differed from fraudulent procurement of a job, that its materiality to the agreement mattered, and that later-discovered misconduct should affect remedies rather than liability. On the tort claim he argued against the proposed discovery-date cutoff and limits on reputation, emotional-distress, punitive, and other damages. He also invoked Arizona constitutional provisions restricting limitations on injury damages. McDonnell Douglas urged ordinary contract principles excusing its later performance after an employee’s prior material breach. On tort, it conceded public-policy concerns prevented a complete bar but sought limits extending to punitive and emotional-distress damages. (¶¶9–12.)

The court explained McKennon’s balancing in statutory discrimination cases: sufficiently serious after-acquired evidence ordinarily precluded reinstatement and front pay while allowing back pay until discovery. It also emphasized that McKennon did not require proof the employer would have discovered the wrongdoing independently of discrimination and the ensuing litigation. That historical statutory discussion was a comparison for the distinct common-law questions before the Arizona court. (¶¶7–8.)

## Contract claim: earlier material breach can excuse remaining duties
The court followed traditional contract doctrine, including Restatement §237’s condition that an earlier uncured material failure can discharge the other party’s remaining performance. An employer’s ignorance of the employee’s misconduct did not eliminate this defense when the employer could show that it would have discharged the employee had it known. The court rejected the need to revise contract doctrine merely to duplicate protections provided by statutory and tort claims. (¶¶13–16.)

The holding was specifically a defense to contractual wages and benefits lost because of discharge. It did not excuse duties already earned, ordinarily allowing wages and benefits for services performed through termination. Equitable principles such as quantum meruit and unjust enrichment generally prevented recovery of wages previously paid merely because past wrongdoing was discovered. Likewise, an employer’s prior knowledge coupled with a decision to ignore the misconduct would defeat its later attempted legal-excuse defense. (¶¶16–17.)

Chronology mattered in O’Day’s case. His promotion denial preceded his improper search and copying. Accordingly, after-acquired evidence of that later misconduct did not bar a contract claim arising from the earlier refusal to promote: there the employer’s alleged breach came first. The court did not treat all claims arising out of the employment relationship as automatically extinguished. (¶15.)

## Tort claim: liability survives, but remedies account for misconduct
The court rejected both treating all after-acquired evidence as irrelevant and allowing it to erase tort liability. Ignoring serious misconduct would conflict with the employer’s right to discharge; a complete defense would defeat the tort’s purpose of assigning consequences to the employer’s wrongful conduct. The remedies-only approach attributed significance to both parties’ conduct. (¶¶18–20.)

If the employer established that it would have fired the employee for the misconduct, reinstatement and its functional equivalent, front pay, were unavailable. Lost past earnings remained recoverable from discharge until discovery. Other compensatory damages caused by the employer’s wrongful conduct, including diminished earning capacity, and punitive damages remained available when supported by the evidence. The court therefore refused the employer’s proposed automatic bar to emotional-distress and punitive recovery. Availability was not a finding that O’Day had proved those injuries or deserved a particular award. (¶¶20, 25; answers B, slip 18–19.)

The court distinguished specific future wages from this employer from general diminished earning capacity. Loss of the former followed from the lawful basis for discharge and the unavailability of reinstatement. The latter could reflect lasting harm to the employee’s ability to work in appropriate occupations, beyond the particular job, and remained recoverable if proved. Labels could not convert foreclosed front pay into an otherwise identical specific-earnings claim. (¶¶26–28.)

## Policy concerns and constitutional objection
The court acknowledged fears that employers would search records for minor wrongdoing, save known misconduct for later use, or discourage employees with imperfect records from complaining. Continuing compensatory and punitive exposure reduced the incentive to abuse the defense. An employer already found liable also had to persuade the factfinder it actually would have fired this employee; discovery sanctions, perjury consequences, and lawyers’ professional duties supplied further safeguards. The court did not adopt Lewis’s separate clear-and-convincing standard or erase the would-have-fired requirement. (¶¶21–24.)

The constitutional objection failed because the cited provisions addressed enacted or statutory damage limits, while this decision imposed no such statutory cap. More fundamentally, the employee retained tort damages legally attributable to the employer, including proven loss of earning capacity. Inability to recover specific future wages from that employer followed from the employee’s own discharge-justifying conduct. Both constitutional footnotes quote the relevant provisions. (¶29; slip 18 & nn.1–2.)

## Exact answers and source scope
The court answered the contract question by recognizing the defense to discharge-related lost wages and benefits upon proof the employer would have fired the employee. It answered the tort question by preserving liability and other supported damages while limiting reinstatement, front pay, and specific lost earnings as described above. It did not reverse or affirm an Arizona trial judgment, determine a final damages amount, or decide the federal statutory counts. (Answers A–B, slip 18–19.)
