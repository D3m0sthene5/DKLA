# Monge v. Beebe Rubber Company
114 N.H. 130, 316 A.2d 549 (1974) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

The New Hampshire Supreme Court decided this employment-contract action on February 28, 1974. Justice Lampron wrote the majority opinion. Justice Griffith did not sit, Justice Grimes dissented, and the remaining participating justices concurred. A jury had awarded Olga Monge $2,500; the employer challenged the refusal to set aside that verdict or enter judgment notwithstanding the verdict, together with evidentiary and substantive rulings. The court sustained the evidentiary basis for wrongful termination but required a new trial unless Monge accepted a $1,083.80 reduction of the verdict. Thus, it did not simply affirm the entire $2,500 award. (114 N.H. at 130, 133–134.)

## Employment and contested discharge

Monge, formerly a teacher in Costa Rica, came to the United States in 1964 and New Hampshire in 1965. She attended college five evenings a week to qualify to teach locally and financed that education by working the factory night shift beginning at 11 p.m. Beebe hired her in September 1968 under an oral agreement for an indefinite period, initially paying $1.84 per hour on a conversion machine. She alleged that she was told good work would bring better jobs and pay. The factory was a union shop; she joined the union and became subject to the collective agreement's seniority and other rules. (Id. at 130–131.)

After approximately three months, she sought a press-machine opening. According to her testimony, her foreman said she would have to be nice to obtain it. She received the $2.79-per-hour job, but refused his subsequent invitation to go out because she was married with three children. Three weeks later, the press stopped operating and she was moved to a $1.99-per-hour degreaser. She testified that her overtime alone disappeared and that requests for additional earnings led to floor sweeping, washroom cleaning, and ridicule. These events were disputed evidence relevant to motive, rather than judicial findings that every reassignment necessarily lacked a business justification. (Id. at 131.)

On July 23, 1969, a shortage of boxes led to an argument over making boxes while maintaining production. After she consulted the union steward and refused an order to return to her machine, the foreman fired her at 2 a.m. A union complaint secured reinstatement with a warning. She subsequently reported illness, entered the hospital, and returned on August 4. She said the personnel manager appeared unusually late at night and questioned her return. After two and a half hours she became unconscious and was taken to the hospital, remaining hospitalized through August 8. Company records then recorded three unnotified absences on August 11–13. The manager's August 13 letter treated those absences as a voluntary quit. (Id. at 131–132.)

Both Monge and the foreman supplied evidence that she had called on August 10 to report continuing illness. There was also evidence that, during an earlier home visit concerning disturbing telephone calls, the manager acknowledged the foreman's use of his position to force attentions upon female employees and asked her not to cause trouble. This allowed the jury to view the purported voluntary quit as an employer-imposed termination connected with the earlier hostility. (Id. at 132–134.)

## Rule and majority reasoning

The court acknowledged the traditional presumption that employment for an indefinite period was terminable at will. It nevertheless treated common-law employment doctrine as responsive to changing social and economic conditions, drawing an analogy to its earlier modification of landlord-tenant rules and recognizing that union activity had changed workplace relations without eliminating the courts' responsibility. Its stated balance involved the employer's interest in managing the business, the employee's interest in continued employment, and the public's interest in a proper accommodation. (Id. at 132–133.)

The resulting historical holding was that an employer's termination of at-will employment motivated by bad faith or malice, or based on retaliation, constituted a breach of the employment contract. The majority viewed that restriction as protecting some employment stability while preserving the employer's ordinary ability to discharge workers for efficient and profitable operation. It cited Frampton and Petermann, together with scholarship, in support of this change. The opinion's broad formulation should be understood as the rule announced in this 1974 decision, rather than a certification of present New Hampshire law or an assurance that every adverse employment decision establishes liability. (Id. at 133.)

Applying the usual respect for a jury's resolution of conflicting testimony, the majority held that the evidence supported an inference of malicious dismissal. The foreman's overtures, the earlier abrupt firing, apparent manipulation of work assignments, and the personnel manager's apparent participation could reasonably connect the August 13 letter to retaliation. The letter's characterization of the separation as voluntary did not prevent the jury from finding an actual discharge. The court sustained that inference; it did not independently retry the disputed events. (Id. at 133–134.)

## Damages and exact disposition

The jury's award exceeded the contract loss supported by the court's calculation. Twenty weeks of lost work at average weekly pay of $70.81 accounted for $1,416.20. The remaining $1,083.80 was attributed to mental suffering, ordinarily unrecoverable in a contract action. The court additionally found no evidentiary basis connecting that suffering to discharge: the marital difficulties and troubling telephone calls preceded termination, and no medical testimony was introduced. The case was remanded for a new trial unless Monge consented to the specified reduction, which would leave $1,416.20. (Id. at 134.)

## Grimes's dissent

Grimes disputed both the factual inference and the new contract rule. In his account, there was only one invitation, eight months before termination; uncontradicted evidence tied the press shutdown and lost overtime to work shortages and Monge's low seniority. The other work was the only work available. He also pointed to assistance from the manager and foreman, the company's established attendance rule, the absence of a grievance challenging the final separation, and an unappealed unemployment determination describing a voluntary quit. These are the dissent's evaluation of the record, not additional majority holdings. He concluded that a retaliatory discharge could not reasonably be found. (Id. at 134–135.)

Independently, he considered the majority's contract rule unsupported by the cited cases. Frampton involved a tort claim for workers' compensation retaliation prohibited by statute; Petermann protected the public policy against perjury. Neither, he argued, established the majority's general contract restriction, and Monge already had union protections. He would preserve the traditional ability of either party to end indefinite employment regardless of motive and would not permit this liability finding to stand. (Id. at 135–136.)
