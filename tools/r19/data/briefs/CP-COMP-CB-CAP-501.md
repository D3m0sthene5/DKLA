# Brown Shoe Co., Inc. v. United States

**370 U.S. 294 (1962) | Supreme Court of the United States | June 25, 1962**  
**Civil Procedure - finality of a divestiture decree; antitrust merger review**  
**Assigned:** CivPro PDF 621, printed 1228–1229, own-caption account and Note 1. The separate Copar Pumice account begins in Note 2.

## Facts and the competing positions

Brown Shoe was a substantial shoe manufacturer that also sold shoes through stores it owned or controlled. The opinion describes it as the country's third-largest shoe seller by dollar volume and fourth-largest manufacturer, accounting for approximately 4% of domestic shoe production. Its retail network included company stores, franchise stores, and outlets operated through its Wohl subsidiary. Kinney was a national retail chain, with approximately 400 stores by trial, and also manufactured some shoes. Although Kinney represented a small percentage of national retail shoe sales, its stores were significant purchasers from independent manufacturers. Before the merger it bought approximately 20% of its shoes from its own plants and none from Brown. By 1957, Brown supplied 7.9% of Kinney's purchases. These relationships made the transaction more than an aggregation of manufacturing shares: it could remove an important independent retail buyer from the market available to other manufacturers. (297–304, 331–334.)

The Government sued in November 1955 to prevent Brown's acquisition of Kinney through an exchange of stock. It alleged a violation of § 7 of the Clayton Act, as amended in 1950: the acquisition might substantially lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly. The district court denied preliminary relief, allowing the merger to go forward on May 1, 1956, subject to an agreement to maintain separate books and preserve the identity of Kinney's assets. The case therefore concerned an accomplished merger when it reached final adjudication. (296–298.)

The Government attacked both the combination of manufacturers and the combination of retailers, as well as the vertical integration of Brown's manufacturing with Kinney's retail outlets. The district court rejected the manufacturing-competition theory, which the Government did not appeal. It nevertheless found the merger unlawful because it threatened competition through vertical foreclosure and increased retail concentration. Its decree permanently prohibited Brown from maintaining an interest in Kinney and required complete divestiture, but directed Brown to submit a detailed implementation plan within 90 days. Brown appealed before that plan had been formulated. (304–308, 335.)

Brown disputed the substantive findings. It argued that its generally medium-priced shoes and Kinney's generally lower-priced shoes served different markets, that local retail markets had been drawn too narrowly, and that modest shares within a vigorous national industry could not establish the requisite threat to competition. Both Brown and the Government wanted the Supreme Court to hear the appeal immediately. Their agreement did not determine jurisdiction: the Court had to examine appealability for itself. (304–306, 326–328, 336–342.)

## Review posture and the finality issue

The Expediting Act then provided a direct appeal to the Supreme Court from a final district-court judgment in a Government civil antitrust action. It did not supply a general route for interlocutory appeals to the courts of appeals. The question was whether an unconditional decision requiring full divestiture was final although the particular divestiture plan remained undecided. The Court held that it was and proceeded to affirm the antitrust judgment. Chief Justice Warren wrote the Court's opinion; Justice Clark concurred separately. Justice Harlan dissented on jurisdiction but, once the Court exercised jurisdiction, concurred in affirmance on the merits. Justices Frankfurter and White did not participate. Thus six participating Justices accepted jurisdiction, and all seven participating Justices supported affirmance of the antitrust judgment, although Harlan would first have dismissed the appeal as premature and did not join the Court's merits reasoning. (304–311, 346, 355–357, 374.)

## Why the Court treated this decree as final

The Court began with the policy against piecemeal appellate review, but explained that finality requires a practical assessment of what the district court has actually decided. Three features mattered here.

First, the district court had disposed of the entire complaint and granted the Government's requested substantive relief. Brown had to divest all of Kinney; the permanent prohibition and underlying obligation were settled. The remaining plan addressed implementation of that obligation. Brown's present objections went to the legality of condemning the merger, rather than to the appropriateness of complete divestiture if the merger was unlawful. Review of those objections would settle the basic issue, rather than require the Court to reconsider it after the plan was prepared. The Court characterized the remaining task as subordinate to the already determined substantive issues. (306–308.)

Second, delaying review could obstruct the remedy itself. Divestiture entails extended negotiations with potential buyers and financial institutions, and its feasible form can depend on changing market conditions. Negotiating those arrangements while the entire underlying duty remains subject to appellate invalidation makes implementation harder. Waiting until a plan was complete might also produce an appeal so late that the previously feasible plan would need revision. Deciding the merger's legality first could stabilize the premises on which a workable disposition was negotiated. (309.)

Third, the Supreme Court had repeatedly decided comparable Government antitrust appeals before implementation of divestiture was complete. The Court acknowledged that a jurisdictional issue not discussed in earlier opinions is not conclusively resolved by those opinions. Nevertheless, the long and consistent practice supported the Court's reading of finality in this particular setting. Its reasoning did not establish that every outstanding remedial issue is immaterial or that the parties' preference can create appellate jurisdiction. It concerned a completed determination of the violation and unconditional basic remedy, with implementation still to follow. (309–311.)

## Harlan's jurisdictional dissent

Harlan thought the Court understated what remained to be decided. A divestiture plan can determine substantial rights and obligations, including the precise manner in which stock is transferred and the effectiveness of the relief. Such questions are not equivalent to merely carrying out a fully specified decree. They could generate another appeal requiring the Supreme Court to scrutinize the remedy. The Expediting Act's final-judgment requirement was intended to produce one appeal after adjudication was complete, rather than separate appeals on liability and relief. (357–360.)

He also rejected analogies to the collateral-order doctrine or exceptional orders permitting immediate execution. Brown did not face an independently final, separable ruling or the sort of irreparable harm that could justify such treatment; it had already obtained permission to consummate the merger while the litigation continued. General concerns about delay and effective judicial administration did not authorize rewriting the statute. Previous appeals decided without considering jurisdiction did not settle the issue. (360–364.)

Harlan agreed that direct Supreme Court review of voluminous antitrust trial records was an inefficient arrangement. He proposed legislation returning initial review to the courts of appeals, which could also hear qualifying injunction appeals. But he distinguished a reason for Congress to amend the law from a reason for the Court to disregard the law as it stood. (364–365.)

## The merger rule and the Court's merits reasoning

Section 7 asks whether the acquisition's effect may substantially lessen competition in a line of commerce within a section of the country. The Court read the 1950 amendments as addressing anticompetitive tendencies before they hardened into monopolies. Their reach included vertical and other acquisitions, not only combinations of direct competitors. The inquiry concerned reasonable probabilities, assessed in the particular industry's economic setting; neither proof of certain future monopoly nor a remote possibility of harm supplied the governing test. The legislative history supported preserving decentralized markets and viable independent businesses, while leaving room for combinations that helped small firms compete or preserved a failing firm. No fixed percentage conclusively resolved every merger. (311–323.)

**Vertical integration.** The Court identified men's, women's, and children's shoes as relevant product lines. It explained that recognized submarkets within a broader market can be identified through practical considerations such as distinctive uses, customers, prices, production facilities, and industry recognition. Brown's and Kinney's price differences did not eliminate their competitive relationship: small price divisions should not arbitrarily divide goods that competed for customers. A still finer age subdivision of children's shoes would not materially change the comparative showing on this record. The relevant geographic market for the vertical analysis was national. (323–328.)

The Court then assessed the manufacturers' access to retail customers. A vertical acquisition can foreclose other manufacturers from an outlet for their goods, particularly where the acquired retailer increasingly purchases the acquirer's production. Foreclosed share mattered, but it was not an exclusive formula. The nature and purpose of the transaction, the relationship's permanence, industry history, and trends toward integration also informed whether the threat was substantial. Brown's history of retail acquisitions, evidence of directing its outlets toward its own shoes, Kinney's importance to independent suppliers, and the beginning substitution of Brown shoes for outside purchases supported the finding. The Court found no countervailing competitive, economic, or social advantage sufficient to offset that threatened foreclosure. Present competition did not immunize an acquisition that accelerated a harmful trend. (328–334.)

**Horizontal retail competition.** The Court separately upheld findings concerning retail stores in cities of at least 10,000 inhabitants and their immediately contiguous areas where Brown and Kinney both operated. Those boundaries were based on where customers realistically shopped, including movement between downtown and suburban stores, rather than merely on political boundaries. Franchise stores could be considered part of Brown's controlled network because Brown exercised substantial operational influence; note 66 discusses the relationship's controls, including the ability to terminate franchise agreements on 30 days' notice. (335–339.)

Local evidence and sales tables supported the finding beyond the places from which particular witnesses came. The Court recognized imperfections in translating pair-sales statistics to dollar sales and in drawing local boundaries, but concluded that the figures adequately depicted the competitive structure. Note 69 expressly examines statistical limitations rather than treating every figure as exact. The merger produced substantial combined shares in numerous cities. The appendices document the local markets separately for women's, children's, and men's shoes and compare geographic measures. In Dodge City, for example, the original tables show 57.7% of women's shoe sales and 49% of children's shoe sales, figures illustrating why small national shares did not dispose of local competition. (340–344; Appendices A–D, 347–354.)

The Court did not hold that injury to an individual competitor alone proves an antitrust violation. It explained, however, that Congress valued maintaining competition through viable independent local businesses even when larger integrated chains might achieve cost efficiencies. Nor did it equate internal growth with acquisition: note 72 distinguishes adding new capacity and opportunities from absorbing an existing competitor and eliminating an independent purchasing or selling choice. The Government had proved the statutory probability of substantially lessened competition in the markets at issue. (344–346.)

## The separate merits opinions

Clark accepted jurisdiction but criticized a procedure that had consumed years while supposedly expediting review. He preferred a single national market for ordinary shoes over the Court's product subdivisions and city-by-city retail analysis. Nonetheless, Brown's integration history, the importance of Kinney's stores, and the likely displacement of independent manufacturers' sales supported affirmance. His starred note at 355 explains why deciding an alternative monopoly theory was unnecessary once the competition ground sufficed. (355–357.)

Harlan likewise found the vertical evidence sufficient, while refusing to endorse the horizontal analysis or the Court's broader industry conclusions. Kinney's small percentage of all national retail sales understated its importance to the independent manufacturers actually competing to supply it. Brown's intended integration and the transaction's probable future effects supplied the stronger basis. He cautioned against arbitrary numerical thresholds and against a market definition tailored to establish illegality. His note 9 disputed the claim that the available statistics established an oligopolistic industry trend: the data used varying bases, and some major producers' shares had declined. Affirmance could rest on this merger's demonstrated circumstances without accepting that broader inference. (365–374.)

## Judgment and assigned-material clarification

The Supreme Court **affirmed** the district court's judgment, sustaining the requirement of complete divestiture and the prohibition on Brown's continuing interest in Kinney. It did not itself devise or approve a specific implementation plan. (346, 374.) The Civil Procedure lesson is that an unconditional determination of liability and the basic remedy may be final despite an outstanding implementation task, but the dispute over whether that task remains genuinely substantive is central. The decision is not a free-standing permission to hear any inconveniently delayed appeal.

The assignment's Harlan quotation is from the actual discussion at **364–365**, rather than its malformed printed pinpoint “365–82.” Its statement that the Expediting Act was amended in **1993** is also incorrect. The archived public law shows that Congress changed the appeal route in **1974**: Pub. L. 93-528, § 5, 88 Stat. 1709, approved December 21, 1974, provided court-of-appeals review and retained a specified direct-review procedure for matters of general public importance. Section 7 supplied the transition rule. This is a verified later historical development, not a certification of all current appellate law.
