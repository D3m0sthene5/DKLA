# Gibson v. Cranage
39 Mich. 49 (1878) | Decided June 11, 1878 | Contracts

## Portrait commission and the satisfaction term
Jefferson Gibson solicited Thomas Cranage’s order for an enlarged portrait of Cranage’s deceased daughter, using a small photograph. Gibson described the bargain as a picture the father would like and recognize as a good picture of his child. Cranage testified that the photograph was to be sent away for finishing and that he need neither take nor pay for the result unless it was satisfactory to him in every particular. There was little dispute about the agreement. The seller’s own account thus tied the payment obligation to the customer’s personal satisfaction, while the father’s version made the right to reject especially explicit. 39 Mich. at 49.

When Gibson showed the completed portrait, Cranage disliked it and refused acceptance. Gibson could not clearly ascertain the objections and sent the picture back to the artist for changes. The following day Cranage sent a letter invoking the original terms, rejecting that picture or another similar one, and countermanding the order. Further correspondence did not materially change the case. After the altered portrait returned, Cranage declined to inspect it. He ultimately viewed it during trial and found the same objections. Id. at 49–50. The opinion distinguishes Gibson from the artist to whom he sent the picture; it does not establish that Gibson personally painted it.

## Action and position on review
Gibson brought assumpsit for the contract price and sought review by writ of error from Bay County after losing below. The report records counsel’s contention that a seller should receive a reasonable time to replace defective merchandise before rescission. The Supreme Court did not analyze the jury charge in detail because it regarded the charge as at least as favorable to Gibson as the actual agreement warranted. Id. at 49–50. The question therefore concerned the effect of the express satisfaction bargain on Gibson’s claimed right to payment, not a judicial determination that the artist’s work was objectively poor.

## Rule and reasoning
The parties had deliberately assigned the decision to Cranage. Even an excellent portrait that artists or other observers would approve could fail to satisfy the bereaved father who ordered it. His inability to articulate the defects clearly did not transfer the contractual choice to an objective observer. Where the customer expressly reserves the right to reject or decline payment for a portrait unsatisfactory to him, and the seller accepts those terms, the customer may insist on that bargain. Id. at 50–51.

Gibson’s own evidence showed that the important satisfaction requirement had not been fulfilled. The court therefore rejected the idea that the picture’s possible excellence, or a judgment that Cranage ought to have liked it, supplied an entitlement to the price. It emphasized that enforcing a deliberately made agreement free of fraud or mistake and violating no public policy imposed no legally objectionable hardship. Id. at 50. That framing is narrower than a general license for dishonest dissatisfaction. The opinion applies the terms of this personal portrait commission; it does not formulate the later casebook’s distinctions among all commercial satisfaction clauses.

Cranage had actually examined the first version and rejected it, and the later examination during trial confirmed that his dissatisfaction persisted. The report does not resolve the editorial hypothetical of a buyer who refuses ever to look at the work. Nor does it adjudicate a restitution claim concerning permanent improvements retained by a customer. The property and personal taste involved here help explain why a third party’s assessment could not replace Cranage’s agreed judgment.

## Judgment and significance
The Supreme Court affirmed with costs. Marston wrote the opinion, and the report states that the other justices concurred. There is no separate writing or judicial footnote in the complete archived report. Id. at 51.

Gibson is useful for separating a conditional price claim from an assessment of workmanship: a seller may produce objectively commendable work yet fail the event chosen to make the price payable. The court did not award Cranage damages against Gibson or decide every question of mutual obligation suggested by its parenthetical doubt about whether the arrangement could be called a contract.
