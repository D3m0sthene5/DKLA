# Dickinson v. Zurko
527 U.S. 150 (1999) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and assigned setting

The Supreme Court held that the Federal Circuit must review Patent and Trademark Office fact findings within the Administrative Procedure Act’s framework, rather than use the appellate standard for district-court fact findings merely because the specialized patent courts had allegedly followed that practice before the APA. Justice Breyer wrote for Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Thomas. Chief Justice Rehnquist dissented with Justices Kennedy and Ginsburg. The Court reversed the Federal Circuit’s en banc judgment and remanded. It did not decide whether the APA’s substantial-evidence or arbitrary-and-capricious formulation specifically governed the PTO finding at issue. (151–152, 158, 165.)

The assignment is editorial note 4 on printed page 921, physical PDF page 141 of the November first volume. Its selected quotations contrast substantial evidence with clear error. The full opinion supplies an important qualification to the editorial description: the Court required the APA framework while leaving the choice between particular APA standards unresolved. Reporter page numbers below refer to the complete U.S. Reports opinion, including its appendix and dissent.

## Facts and procedural history

The respondents sought a patent on a method of improving computer security. A PTO examiner found the proposed method obvious in light of prior art and denied the application under 35 U.S.C. § 103. The Board of Patent Appeals and Interferences upheld that decision. On direct appeal, a Federal Circuit panel treated what the prior art taught as a factual question and concluded that the PTO’s finding was clearly erroneous. The Federal Circuit then heard the case en banc to settle an existing dispute over the proper review standard and endorsed that form of review at 142 F.3d 1447 (1998). The Commissioner, represented by the Solicitor General, sought Supreme Court review. The Court addressed the standard of review rather than resolving the disputed computer-security technology or independently deciding patentability. (153–154.)

The parties agreed that the PTO was an agency subject to the APA, that the disputed determination was factual, and that it constituted agency action. Section 706 therefore ordinarily supplied the governing review framework. The claimed departure rested on § 559, which preserves additional requirements recognized by law. In the Federal Circuit’s account, its predecessor, the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals, had used the more demanding judicial clear-error standard before the APA’s enactment in 1946; that established practice was an additional requirement preserved by the savings provision. (154.)

## Standards and the claimed historical exception

Section 706 directs courts to set aside agency findings that are arbitrary, capricious, or an abuse of discretion, and, in the specified on-record settings, findings unsupported by substantial evidence. It also requires review of the whole record or the cited portions. By contrast, Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 52(a) governs appellate review of a district judge’s findings and permits reversal for clear error. Both approaches defer to a factfinder, but the court-to-court formulation traditionally permits somewhat closer scrutiny than the court-to-agency formulations. The Commissioner favored ordinary APA review; the Federal Circuit and respondents favored the preserved, stricter approach. (152–154.)

The majority required clear evidence that the asserted additional requirement had been recognized by law. A mere possibility or even a bare preponderance was insufficient. It drew support from the statutory language and the APA’s express requirement that later legislation departing from the Act do so expressly. Although grandfathered common-law rules differ from later statutes, both exceptions threatened the uniformity Congress sought in a previously diverse field. Allowing an ambiguous historical practice to create an exemption would undermine that purpose. The majority examined the eighty-nine pre-APA CCPA decisions identified by respondents and amici and found no established stricter standard. (154–155.)

## Majority’s historical analysis

Twenty-three of the historical cases used phrases such as “clear case of error” or “clearly wrong”; the rest generally used “manifest error.” Those formulations sounded compatible with clear-error review but did not prove it. Before the APA, courts had not consistently assigned today’s meanings to these expressions. Some used clear-error language when reviewing agencies, while others used substantial-evidence language when reviewing trial courts. The Court traced how Congress’s increasing use of substantial-evidence language gradually encouraged standardized terminology. The patent statutes themselves supplied no particular review formulation. None of the eighty-nine opinions actually used the precise terms “clear error” or “clearly erroneous,” and “manifest error” was not a settled modern term of art. (155–158.)

The majority also examined Morgan v. Daniels, 153 U.S. 120 (1894), which the Federal Circuit considered the historical foundation. Morgan concerned competing inventors’ priority and a reviewing court’s reversal of the Patent Office without additional testimony. Although language about thorough conviction and clear mistake could resemble modern clear-error review, Morgan expressly distinguished review of an executive department from ordinary review of a trial judge. Its reasoning required substantial deference to the specialized administrative tribunal and treated the ordinary appellate approach as too strict. The majority therefore refused to extract isolated modern-sounding phrases from their historical explanation. (158–160.)

The CCPA opinions likewise emphasized agency expertise, technical complexity, and agreement among multiple agency tribunals as reasons for deference. Nearly half invoked expertise or complexity; more than three-fourths invoked agreement within the agency. These explanations resembled the traditional reasons for agency deference, rather than the reasons for respecting trial-court findings, such as a judge’s direct evaluation of witnesses. The appendix organized all eighty-nine cases by whether they discussed technical expertise, intra-agency agreement, both, or neither. The majority concluded that the corpus did not establish the stricter judicial standard as an additional requirement preserved by § 559. (160–161; appendix 165–170.)

## Policy objections and the practical difference between standards

The Federal Circuit argued that changing a settled practice would disrupt the patent bench and bar. The majority answered that the Supreme Court had never settled the issue and that recognizing an ambiguous exemption could itself destabilize uniform APA review elsewhere. It also regarded the practical gap between the standards as modest. Substantial-evidence review asks whether a reasonable mind could accept the record as adequate; clear-error review asks whether the reviewing judge has a definite and firm conviction of error. The former is somewhat more deferential, but the Court found no other acknowledged example in which choosing between them would have changed the result. This did not mean that the standards were identical or that APA review was perfunctory. The Act required meaningful review, including review of reasoning, and did not authorize rubber-stamping. Expertise, intra-agency review, and the particular record might matter more to outcomes than verbal formulations alone. (161–164.)

The Federal Circuit’s own specialized patent experience remained relevant: it helped judges understand the basis of PTO findings under the APA framework. Agency reasoning could still be tested for arbitrariness or, when bound up with record-based factual conclusions, substantial evidentiary support. The new standard did not make factual reasoning unreviewable. (163–164.)

A second objection concerned applicants’ alternative routes. Under § 141 they could appeal directly to the Federal Circuit; under § 145 they could first obtain district-court review and then appeal. The second route might yield appellate clear-error review of the district court and appear more attractive. The majority explained that § 145 allowed new evidence, making the district judge a factfinder whose findings appropriately received court-to-court review. It acknowledged a possible anomaly where the district judge merely reviewed the existing PTO findings, but left the Federal Circuit room to harmonize related review standards. This was not a comprehensive ruling on every feature of the alternative statutory proceedings. (164.)

Finally, the majority rejected the claim that stricter review uniquely promoted better PTO records and clearer patent boundaries. The Federal Circuit and amici had not convincingly shown why patent denials required more exacting factual review than other agencies’ decisions. Congress had already provided the standard, and the claimed exception lacked adequate support. (165.)

## Dissent

Rehnquist read the APA as a floor preserving more rigorous preexisting requirements, rather than as a uniform ceiling displacing them. He considered the patent bench and bar’s longstanding understanding persuasive and would defer to the unanimous en banc specialized court’s plausible reading of its own historical precedents. His footnote observed that the PTO appeared to have acquiesced for almost fifty years, first arguing for ordinary APA factfinding review before the Federal Circuit in 1995. That was a dissenting historical argument, not the majority’s finding of a binding preserved requirement. (170–171 & sole judicial note.)

He also rejected the majority’s heightened clarity requirement. The statute said requirements recognized by law, without an added clarity qualifier. The express-statement rule for later statutes, in his view, did not justify imposing a comparable condition on antecedent common-law requirements. Congress could preserve rigorous old rules while preventing future implied repeal of the APA’s minimum protections. He would affirm for the Federal Circuit’s reasons. (171–172.)

## Disposition, boundaries, and source scope

The judgment was reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with APA review. The Court neither granted a patent nor finally upheld the examiner’s obviousness finding. It expressly reserved whether substantial evidence or arbitrary-and-capricious review specifically applied to PTO factfinding. The assigned note illustrates why the review formulation matters, while the full decision explains why historical practice must be established by more than an ambiguous label before it can displace an otherwise applicable statutory framework. (158, 165.)
