# D & G Stout, Inc. v. Bacardi Imports, Inc. — trial on remand
805 F. Supp. 1434 (N.D. Ind. 1992) | Contracts

## Why this decision is a separate stage
After the Seventh Circuit reversed summary judgment for Bacardi, District Judge Miller conducted a nonjury trial and made findings under Rule52(a). His October21,1992 memorandum applied Indiana promissory-estoppel law to facts materially different from the version assumed for summary judgment. The appellate decision remained controlling as to its interpretation of Indiana law, but did not establish the contested facts for trial. This brief covers that later judgment rather than treating the earlier remand as a damages award. (805 F. Supp. at 1437,1443–1444.)

## Assurances during a distributor's business crisis
General Liquors, later D & G Stout, distributed liquor in northern Indiana. In1987 Brown-Foreman and Beam withdrew important product lines on customary thirty-day notice under at-will arrangements, without fault by General. General's president David Stout examined mergers, sales, expansion, downsizing, and closure. He concluded that General could remain profitable if it retained Bacardi, Hiram Walker, and Canandaguia and closed its Fort Wayne facility. He obtained assurances from the latter two suppliers and sought Bacardi's assurance. Bacardi accounted for thirty percent of General's sales and twenty-three percent of its profit. (Id. at 1437–1439.)

At a July9 meeting, Bacardi was interviewing replacements for its departing southern Indiana distributor and assessing General's viability in the north. Stout said General needed Bacardi to remain in business. Bacardi's marketing vice president William Tovell assured him that General would remain a distributor if it continued meeting sales expectations and market conditions did not change. Regional manager Ned Russo reaffirmed that commitment during subsequent calls. The trial court found a conditional promise, not the unconditional emphatic commitment assumed on appeal. (Id. at 1438–1439,1441,1444–1445.)

Meanwhile, National Wine & Spirits wanted to purchase General. Its July offer included assets it did not want, such as the Fort Wayne warehouse, because General's ability and inclination to remain in business gave General bargaining strength. Stout seriously considered the offer but decided to continue operating, a decision the court placed around July21, before a July23 call on which the plaintiffs relied heavily. The court therefore found no reliance on representations first made during that later call. (Id. at 1440–1441 & n.1.)

Contrary to the earlier summary-judgment assumptions, Bacardi did not know about the National negotiations before their rejection. Stout may have assumed Russo knew through the industry grapevine, but did not communicate the negotiations in terms Russo understood. Contemporaneous memoranda and the earlier deposition supported that finding despite generally credible witnesses recalling conversations five years later. (Id. at 1441–1442,1444.)

Bacardi changed strategy and selected Olinger as its statewide distributor. Its concerns included exaggerated, unconfirmed reports of General's sales-force losses and its perception that Olinger wanted a statewide appointment. Bacardi announced the selection July29 and informed General July30. It asked General to continue distributing through year-end, but Stout believed impending losses of personnel made that impracticable. He reopened negotiations with National, which now held the stronger bargaining position. The resulting August asset sale was less favorable than National's July offer. (Id. at 1441–1443.)

## A conditional promise could support promissory estoppel
The court applied Indiana's five elements: a promise, an expectation of reliance, induced reasonable reliance, reliance of a definite and substantial nature, and injustice avoidable only by enforcement. Bacardi's conditions did not by themselves eliminate the promise. A promise supporting section90 need not satisfy every requirement of a contractual offer, although its conditions remain important to the other elements. (Id. at 1444–1445.)

Bacardi argued that it could not reasonably expect General to reject a purchase offer it did not know existed. The court distinguished the exact opportunity forgone from the general nature and extent of reliance. Bacardi knew General's survival depended on its line and was asked for a commitment for that reason. It reasonably should have expected General to continue operating and thereby forgo alternatives requiring departure from the distribution business. It need not know which available purchase offer General would reject. The objective foreseeability inquiry did not excuse actual reliance, which the court separately found. (Id. at 1445–1447.)

Footnote3 acknowledged differences between the Restatement's wording and Indiana courts' summaries about expectation and reasonable reliance. The court found Indiana decisions examining reasonableness on both sides, without deciding scholarly disagreements about which inquiry should predominate. Footnote4 also limited the foreseeable type of reliance: remaining in business was reasonably expected, but purchasing additional assets in anticipation of expansion would not have been. Thus the judgment did not impose liability for any imaginable action following an assurance. (Id. at 1445–1446 & nn.3–4.)

## Why reliance was reasonable despite at-will status
General had not assumed Bacardi would remain before seeking the assurance. The other suppliers' departures and unsettled market motivated its inquiry. Once Bacardi expressed a current commitment, however, those uncertainties did not require General to disregard the promise as meaningless. Unlike the other departing suppliers, Bacardi had supplied a reassuring commitment directed at General's continued operation. (Id. at 1447–1448.)

Neither the relevant sales performance nor market conditions changed between July9 and July23. Reports of personnel losses were greatly exaggerated and unverified; the loss of exclusivity on one line was accompanied by acquisition of another profitable line. A prospective distributor's preference for a statewide appointment was not a new market condition, since Bacardi had previously rejected a similar proposal. What changed was Bacardi's assessment of trends and its business plan. General was not required to anticipate that reversal. (Id. at 1448–1449.)

The promise was not perpetual, and the court declined to fix an outer limit for reasonable reliance. In the context of Bacardi's southern-distributor search, General reasonably understood its own northern appointment not to be under immediate reconsideration. The decision to announce a new arrangement three-and-a-half weeks later contradicted that short-term assurance. (Id. at 1449.)

Bacardi's proposed continuation through December31 and arguments about reasonable termination notice did not answer the claim. General sought costs of reliance on the additional assurance, not damages for breach of the underlying at-will distributorship. It had already rejected National's firm offer when Bacardi announced termination. Nor did the possibility of a later condition justifying termination erase reliance reasonable when undertaken. The opinion's statement about causation not being an element addressed a requirement to prove loss from nonperformance of the promise; it still expressly required that the promise induce the actual forbearance. (Id. at 1449–1450.)

National had made an offer General could accept, so rejecting it was definite and substantial forbearance rather than an abstract lost possibility. The industry's longstanding informal, handshake relationships supported the equitable conclusion that enforcement was necessary. Market consolidation did not establish that commercial assurances between these longtime parties had ceased to carry their ordinary meaning. (Id. at 1450–1451.)

## Reliance damages were limited rather than equivalent to every claimed loss
The court awarded the difference between the rejected July offer and the August asset-sale proceeds for covered assets: $250,000 on inventory, $5,000 on receivables, $36,000 on South Bend real estate, and $103,050 on furniture, merchandise, automobiles, and delivery equipment after accounting for later sales. These items totaled $394,050. This compensated the proven deterioration in General's sale position, not profits from a guaranteed distributorship term. (Id. at 1443,1452.)

Fort Wayne warehouse expenses were excluded. General had planned to close and sell that facility before the assurance and would have faced its carrying costs even if Bacardi had remained. Making Bacardi pay those costs would put General in a better position than performance, contrary to the remedial limit applied under section90. The individuals David and Georgia Stout claimed only warehouse losses and recovered nothing. Evidence concerning losses after they bought the warehouse from the corporation was also stricken. (Id. at 1443,1451–1452.)

Prejudgment interest was available only on losses ascertainable by mathematical comparison without valuation judgment. Inventory, receivables, and South Bend real-estate losses totaled $291,000. The equipment-related $103,050 required weighing testimony and fair-market-value judgments, so received no interest. Eight percent simple interest for five years on $291,000 produced $116,400. (Id. at 1452–1453.)

## Exact judgment and source scope
The court directed judgment for D & G Stout against Bacardi for $510,450: $394,050 reliance damages plus $116,400 prejudgment interest. It directed judgment for Bacardi on David and Georgia Stout's individual claims and assessed costs against Bacardi. This was the post-trial result, distinct from the earlier appellate reversal and remand. (Id. at 1453.)
