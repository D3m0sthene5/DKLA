# Erickson v. Pardus

**551 U.S. 89 (2007) · Supreme Court of the United States · June 4, 2007 · Civil Procedure**

The Court vacated the dismissal of a prisoner's hepatitis-treatment claim because his allegations gave adequate notice of the harm allegedly caused by withdrawing treatment. This was a decision about the sufficiency of those allegations, with the defendants' other grounds for dismissal expressly left open. The assigned account appears in CivPro physical page 340, printed pages 646–647, including its three attached questions. The Supplement reproduces the underlying complaint at physical pages 303–312, printed pages 443–451.

William Erickson was incarcerated at the Limon Correctional Facility in Colorado. Officials diagnosed hepatitis C and approved a yearlong treatment course after he completed required classes and otherwise satisfied the treatment protocol. The course involved weekly injections. Shortly after treatment began, a syringe went missing. Officials found a modified syringe in a communal trash can, rejected Erickson's denial that he had taken it, and found him guilty of possessing drug paraphernalia. They inferred that he intended to use illegal drugs and removed him from treatment. They explained that successful treatment depended on remaining drug and alcohol free. Under the protocol described to the Court, renewed treatment could require a year's wait followed by six months of classes. These were allegations and procedural facts described for pleading review, not findings that Erickson stole the syringe or that stopping treatment was medically justified. **90–91.**

Erickson sued prison officials Barry Pardus and Dr. Anita Bloor under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. His medical claim alleged that Bloor removed him from prescribed treatment, endangered his life, and refused treatment he still needed. Grievances attached to the complaint alleged continued liver damage. Later filings, made before the district court's judgment, alleged irreversible liver damage, possible death, deaths of other inmates from hepatitis C, and imminent danger. He sought damages and an injunction requiring treatment under the Department's protocol. **91–92.**

The supplied complaint makes the litigation concrete. Its typed form identifies three claims: deliberate indifference to medical needs, a procedural due process claim based on the treatment protocol, and a challenge to medical copayments allegedly leaving insufficient money for hygiene supplies. It requests declaratory and injunctive relief, litigation expenses, nominal damages of one dollar against each defendant, and punitive relief tied to copayments. Erickson signed the declaration on January 22, 2005. The reproduced complaint refers to exhibits and grievances but does not reproduce those attachments. Its allegations about the protocol, finances, medical necessity, and wrongful accusation must therefore be treated as assertions rather than established facts. **Supplement physical 303–312; complaint numbered pages 1–8.**

The defendants moved to dismiss. A magistrate judge recommended dismissal of the treatment claim for failure to allege substantial harm caused by the interruption. The district court agreed, and the Tenth Circuit affirmed. Although it recognized that serious delay and future harm can implicate the Eighth Amendment, the appellate court required allegations of harm attributable to stopping treatment beyond the hepatitis itself. It characterized the allegations as conclusory and did not reach the subjective component of deliberate indifference—whether Bloor acted with a sufficiently culpable state of mind. **92–93.**

The Supreme Court held that this assessment departed from Rule 8(a)(2). The rule requires a short and plain statement showing entitlement to relief and giving fair notice of the claim and its grounds. The Court quoted the recently decided *Twombly* for that requirement and for accepting factual allegations as true at the motion-to-dismiss stage. The alleged combination was sufficient: prescribed medication had been withdrawn soon after a yearlong course began; Erickson remained in need of treatment; officials refused it; and their refusal endangered his life. The Court said that this alone satisfied Rule 8(a)(2), with the attachments and subsequent filings providing additional support. The error was demanding further harm allegations before allowing this medical claim to proceed. **93–94.**

Liberal construction of a pro se complaint reinforced that conclusion. Erickson had proceeded without counsel throughout the litigation, and his pleading was entitled to less stringent treatment than formal pleadings prepared by lawyers. The Court cited the then-existing Rule 8(f), requiring construction to do substantial justice. It did not announce an exemption of all pro se lawsuits from *Twombly*, remove substantive Eighth Amendment requirements, or decide that every conclusory assertion must be accepted as a sufficient claim. Its assessment concerned the concrete treatment-withdrawal allegations before it. The assigned questions explore whether *Erickson* and *Twombly* can be reconciled, empirical dismissal trends, and the relation between access to counsel and apparent merit. Those questions and cited academic accounts are editorial material, not additional holdings. **94; assigned physical 340, printed 646–647.**

The exact judgment matters. The Court granted certiorari and leave to proceed in forma pauperis, **vacated the Tenth Circuit's judgment, and remanded**. It expressly reserved whether the complaint was sufficient in every respect and the proper application of the controlling principles to the facts, because the defendants had raised multiple dismissal arguments. The textbook's shorthand that the Court “reversed” should be understood in light of the actual vacatur and remand. Nothing in the opinion awarded damages, ordered immediate medical treatment, or adjudicated the distinct copayment and procedural due process claims. **94–95.**

The disposition was 7–2. Justice Scalia would have denied certiorari without a written explanation. Justice Thomas dissented. He maintained that the Eighth Amendment historically concerned injuries relating to the criminal sentence itself. Even under the Court's broader precedents, he would require actual serious injury rather than exposure to a risk of injury and would affirm. His objection therefore addressed the constitutional theory beneath the majority's pleading ruling, rather than supplying another requirement adopted by the Court. **95.**

On June 7, 2007, the same Tenth Circuit panel issued a separate remand order. It held that the medical claim adequately pleaded both objective and subjective deliberate-indifference components and vacated the corresponding portions of the district court's March 13, 2006 order. It reinstated its earlier disposition of the procedural due process and hygiene claims, which the Supreme Court had not addressed. That later order helps identify the limited effect of the Supreme Court judgment; it does not turn the 2006 appellate judgment into the Supreme Court's holding. **No. 06-1114, June 7, 2007 order, pages 1–3.**
