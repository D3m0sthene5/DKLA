# People v. Smith
393 Mich. 432, 225 N.W.2d 165 (1975) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, decision, and assigned scope
The Michigan Supreme Court decided this case on January 21, 1975. Chief Justice Thomas Giles Kavanagh wrote the opinion, joined by Thomas Matthew Kavanagh, Swainson, Williams, and Mary S. Coleman. Levin and John W. Fitzgerald did not sit. The participating justices therefore agreed in the published disposition; the court did not publish a dissent. The assigned Class 5 excerpt appears at PDF pages 10-12 and concerns the scope of a weapons statute. This is the Michigan rifle case, not one of the many unrelated decisions captioned People v. Smith.

## Facts and the charge
At a preliminary examination, Detroit police officer Ward testified that he and his partner observed a Ford Econoline van making several erratic U-turns. They stopped the van. Approaching it, Ward saw through the right window what he believed was a rifle stock. He opened the door and removed an M-1 rifle from beneath the second seat. Smith, a passenger, was sitting on the third seat with his feet up. Officers also found a cartridge belt and ammunition clips on the front seat between two other defendants, Gaut and Turner. (393 Mich. at 434.)

Those were the facts presented in preliminary-examination testimony. They were not a jury's findings that Smith possessed the rifle, knew it was there, or intended to use it unlawfully. Smith and three other occupants were charged under Michigan's carrying-concealed-weapons statute, MCLA 750.227. After the examination, the defendants were bound over for trial. (Id.)

The distinction between the charge and the rifle's physical danger is the heart of the case. A military rifle plainly could cause serious harm. The legal question was whether the specific offense chosen by the prosecution covered it.

## Procedure and the parties' positions
Smith moved to quash the information, the formal charging document. He advanced two grounds: an M-1 rifle was not among the dangerous weapons covered by section 227, and the preliminary-examination evidence did not establish probable cause that he knowingly carried the rifle. The trial court denied relief; the Michigan Court of Appeals affirmed. Smith obtained an interlocutory appeal to the Michigan Supreme Court. The appeal therefore occurred before a final trial judgment of guilt. (Id. at 434-435.)

The lower courts concentrated on the weapon's dangerousness. The trial court reasoned that an M-1's military character brought it within the statute. The Court of Appeals noted that the 43-inch rifle was heavy and could also be used as a club. The Supreme Court thought that reasoning answered the wrong question. Whether the rifle was dangerous in ordinary speech did not determine whether the legislature's particular list included it. (Id. at 435-436.)

## Statutory framework and issue
Section 227's first clause prohibited carrying a dagger, dirk, stiletto, or other dangerous weapon, except hunting knives adapted and carried as such, concealed on the person or concealed or otherwise in an occupied vehicle, subject to specified location exceptions. A separate clause addressed pistols carried without the required license. A companion statutory definition treated a pistol as a firearm thirty inches or less in length, or a firearm whose construction and appearance concealed its character as a firearm. (Id. at 435-437.)

The question was whether the residual phrase other dangerous weapon, or the separate reference to pistols, made carrying the long M-1 rifle an offense under section 227. Smith also framed his first question by referring to legislative intent and the Michigan Constitution. The Supreme Court resolved the case through statutory construction; it did not announce a separate constitutional invalidation of the weapons law.

## Holding and disposition
Section 227 did not cover the M-1 rifle. The general phrase following dagger, dirk, and stiletto was limited to weapons of the same class, and the rifle did not fit the separate statutory definition of pistol. The Court reversed the lower courts and directed that the information be quashed. It expressly declined to decide Smith's second question concerning probable cause and knowledge because the statutory ruling disposed of the charge. (Id. at 436-438.)

This was a meaningful victory for Smith against the pending prosecution under section 227. It was not an appellate acquittal following a trial, and it did not establish that all rifle transportation was lawful or that prosecution under a different applicable law would be impossible.

## Reasoning: identify the genus before reading the catchall
The Court applied ejusdem generis. When general language follows specifically named subjects, courts ordinarily understand it to include other things of the same kind, class, character, or nature. Here, the named daggers, dirks, and stilettos were stabbing weapons. The residual phrase thus covered comparable stabbing weapons, rather than every object capable of inflicting harm. The hunting-knife exception also belonged within that statutory setting. (Id. at 436.)

The selection of the category mattered. If dangerousness alone were the genus, the specific list would do little work because rifles, clubs, and many other objects are dangerous. The Court instead treated the enumeration as a legislative indication of the kinds of weapons reached by that clause. It emphasized that recognizing a limit on the statutory class did not deny the M-1's danger. This prevents the analysis from becoming an argument about whether guns are less dangerous than knives. (Id. at 435-436.)

The Court then separately tested the pistol clause. The rifle was longer than the statutory thirty-inch limit and was not brought into that category merely by calling it a firearm. Reading each clause distinctly avoided using a general assessment of danger to erase a legislatively supplied definition. (Id. at 436-437.)

## Reasoning: the wider scheme explains the narrower offense
Another provision, MCLA 750.226, prohibited going armed with a pistol or other firearm, or listed weapons, with intent to use the weapon unlawfully against another person. The Court regarded the rifle as unquestionably a firearm within that provision. The legislature had therefore addressed long guns; interpreting section 227 narrowly did not mean that it had overlooked them entirely. (Id. at 437.)

The two provisions imposed different requirements. Section 226 required unlawful intent. Reading section 227 to cover rifles would permit a serious felony charge without proving that additional intent and could sweep in ordinary hunters transporting rifles. That the chosen reading made some prosecutions harder did not authorize courts to shift conduct into the easier-to-prove offense. The statutory scheme, rather than prosecutorial convenience, supplied the governing distinction. (Id. at 437-438.)

The Court also acknowledged other restrictions. Its footnote cited a law governing transportation of firearms in vehicles, generally requiring an affected long gun to be taken down, cased, or carried in the trunk, and a provision separately regulating specified weapons such as machine guns and silencers. These observations qualify the holding without adding an alternative conviction: they explain why excluding rifles from this particular charge did not legalize every way of carrying them. (Id. at 438 n.2.)

Finally, the Court placed any decision to strengthen long-gun regulation with the legislature. It did not decide that the existing scheme was optimal. The question was which restrictions the legislature had already enacted and under which of them Smith had been charged. (Id. at 438.)

## Material restored from the full opinion
The complete opinion identifies the interlocutory posture, the second probable-cause question that the court left unanswered, the precise direction to quash the information, and the participating judges. Those details are compressed or missing in the assigned excerpt. They keep the brief from converting an interpretive ruling on a pending charge into a factual determination about Smith's possession or a verdict after trial. In class, the case pairs naturally with McBoyle: both turn on whether a residual phrase preserves the category suggested by a specific list.
