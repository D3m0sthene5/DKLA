# K & G Construction Co. v. Harris
223 Md. 305, 164 A.2d 451 (1960) | Contracts

## Excavation subcontract and the damaged house
K & G owned and served as general contractor for a housing subdivision. Harris and Brooks undertook excavation and earthmoving. They promised workmanlike performance according to best practices, timely progress, and property-damage liability insurance. Monthly requisitions were subject to ten-percent retention, and payments were barred until insurance requirements were met. The contract also authorized substitution and recovery of loss for delay and required notice for claims involving services or materials supplied by the contractor to the subcontractor. 223 Md. at 307–09.

On August 9, 1958, the subcontractor’s bulldozer operator drove too close to a house while grading, collapsing a wall and causing $3,400 damage. The subcontractor had insurance but it and the carrier denied liability and refused compensation or repair. K & G otherwise was generally satisfied with the work and had paid earlier requisitions. It withheld the progress payment due August 10 because the damage remained uncompensated. The subcontractor continued working until September 12, then stopped, offering by registered letters to return only upon payment. Later requests to finish were refused for the same reason. Another excavation contractor completed the job for $450 above the contract price. Id. at 309–10.

## Separate damage claim and the judgment under review
K & G sued in negligence for the bulldozer damage and in contract for the $450 completion overrun. Harris and Brooks counterclaimed for $1,484.50 of unpaid work and $1,340 anticipated profit, totaling $2,824.50. By agreement, a jury tried the negligence count and awarded K & G $3,400; that judgment was paid. The trial judge, sitting without a jury, then resolved the contract claims on stipulated facts and awarded the subcontractor the full $2,824.50. K & G appealed that latter judgment, using a case stated under Maryland Rule 826 g. Id. at 307, 310–11.

The central question was which party had first committed a breach justifying the other’s nonperformance. Harris and Brooks treated the August 10 nonpayment as releasing them from further work. K & G argued that the negligent performance was a prior material breach permitting withholding and that the later work stoppage was another subcontractor breach. Id. at 311–12. The already-paid negligence award was not reopened on appeal.

## Dependent performances and progress payments
The court distinguished independent promises, which require performance despite the other party’s breach and leave a separate damages claim, from mutually dependent performances. Dependent promises may call for one act before another or simultaneous exchange. The modern presumption favors dependence where possible, but the parties’ intent, the entire agreement, circumstances, and nature of the transaction remain controlling. Id. at 312–14.

Here workmanlike performance preceded and conditioned the contractor’s monthly payment undertaking. A progress-payment schedule did not by itself separate payment from the quality of the work. Requiring a contractor to keep funding badly performed work and pursue only a later action could expose it to insolvency while the project continued. Construing this agreement by its purpose and circumstances, the court held the performances mutually dependent. Id. at 314.

The bulldozer damage breached the promise of workmanlike performance. Its materiality was especially clear because $3,400 damage exceeded twice the installment due August 10. K & G was therefore justified in withholding that installment. When a withholding is justified by the other party’s performance, the unpaid contractor cannot treat it as a breach authorizing abandonment. Id. at 314–15.

## Continued work did not eliminate the prior breach
Allowing Harris and Brooks to work after the accident showed that K & G treated the breach as partial rather than ending the entire relationship. It did not make the mutually dependent payment obligation unconditional. Because K & G was not in default in withholding the installment, the subcontractor’s September 12 cessation was wrongful and triggered liability for the stipulated $450 increased completion cost. Id. at 315–16.

The court separately rejected two defenses. The provision requiring written notice concerned services rendered or materials furnished by K & G to the subcontractor; a claim for negligent damage was neither. Supplying insurance also did not license negligent workmanship or bar a direct claim against the subcontractor, particularly when the subcontractor and insurer denied liability. Id. at 316.

## Judgment, report qualifications, and participation
The Court of Appeals reversed the judgment against K & G and entered judgment in its favor against Harris and Brooks for $450, with costs payable by the appellees. Prescott delivered the court’s opinion; the case was argued before Brune, Henderson, Hammond, Prescott, and Horney. No separate writing or dissent is reported. Id. at 307, 316.

The report’s four judicial notes were read. Footnote 3 warns that the stipulated case does not disclose whether compensation was a lump sum, by volume, by day, or otherwise; this brief does not invent a payment formula beyond the reported progress arrangement. Footnote 2 notes ambiguity in the requisition provision. The casebook retains that note, renumbered 1, while omitting other full-source discussion. Its bracketed factual account was compared with the actual stipulated statement. The decision concerns a material uncured workmanship breach and justified withholding, rather than every small defect or unrelated tort claim. No current citator review is represented.
