# Decatur v. Paulding
39 U.S. (14 Pet.) 497 (1840) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and procedural posture

On March 3, 1840, the Supreme Court affirmed the Circuit Court for the District of Columbia’s refusal to issue a peremptory mandamus compelling the Secretary of the Navy to pay Susan Decatur an additional pension. Chief Justice Taney’s opinion treated the disputed payment as an executive duty involving judgment rather than a ministerial act enforceable through that writ. It expressly withheld an opinion on whether Decatur’s interpretation of the pension legislation was correct. McLean, Baldwin, and Catron supplied separate explanations for leaving the refusal undisturbed; their differences make a single undifferentiated account of the Court’s reasoning misleading. The report does not provide a formal numerical vote. (513–518, 523; Baldwin’s relocated writing, expanded report PDF 19–35.)

## Facts and competing claims

Congress enacted two provisions on March 3, 1837. A general statute allowed the widow of an officer who died in naval service half the officer’s monthly pay, calculated under the pay laws in force on January 1, 1835, beginning at the officer’s death. Upon the widow’s death or remarriage, the benefit passed to the officer’s children. A same-day resolution specifically granted Susan Decatur, widow of Commodore Stephen Decatur, a five-year pension beginning June 30, 1834, together with arrearages of a post captain’s half-pay from her husband’s death to that date. The arrearages were to be vested in the Secretary of the Treasury in trust for her; the pension would cease upon her death or marriage. Both provisions drew upon the navy pension fund, whose trustee was the Secretary of the Navy. (513–514.)

Decatur asked Secretary Mahlon Dickerson to pay her under both provisions. Attorney General Butler advised that she could elect either benefit but could not receive both. Dickerson offered that choice. She accepted payment under the general statute while protesting the denial of the additional benefit and expressly intending to preserve her claim to the balance. In the fall of 1838, after James Paulding succeeded Dickerson, she asked him to reconsider. He declined. Decatur then sought mandamus; the circuit court issued a rule to show cause, considered Paulding’s return, and refused the peremptory writ. She brought a writ of error to the Supreme Court. (514.)

Decatur argued that Congress’s specific direction to pay a named beneficiary fixed an enforceable duty. She treated the differently framed grants as cumulative and maintained that receiving one under protest did not surrender the other. The government disputed both the availability of judicial coercion and her statutory entitlement. It argued that the legislation provided alternatives, challenged her inclusion of rations and interest, and warned that applying the same interpretation to other pensioners could exhaust the fund. These competing statutory positions were litigated, but Taney’s opinion did not resolve them. (497–513, 517, 518–520.)

## Controlling distinction and reasoning

Taney began from *Kendall*: the District of Columbia circuit court possessed authority to command a federal officer to perform a ministerial act. Decatur therefore could not be rejected simply because the defendant headed an executive department. The question was whether the particular duty she sought to compel was ministerial. Heads of departments ordinarily exercise judgment in administering statutes and resolutions. Their ability to consult the Attorney General confirmed that legal interpretation formed part of those executive responsibilities. (514–515.)

Taney nevertheless distinguished an officer’s administrative interpretation from a court’s interpretation in a proper lawsuit. A court considering a statutory question within its jurisdiction would not be bound by the department head’s construction and could pronounce it wrong. That did not give the circuit court appellate authority over the Secretary’s executive decisions or authorize mandamus to direct how he exercised judgment. The opinion thus addressed the availability and scope of this remedy, rather than a universal obligation to accept agency interpretations. (515–516.)

The particular demand required Paulding to consider whether to reopen his predecessor’s decision, whether the general statute and individual resolution granted cumulative benefits, what half-pay included, and how the asserted entitlement affected competing demands upon the pension fund. Those were matters of judgment rather than mechanical execution of an already settled command. Judicial direction of such administration could create conflicting rules and impair the discharge of executive responsibilities. *Kendall* involved a specifically prescribed credit that left the officer no comparable judgment to exercise. Here, the circuit court could not assume jurisdiction over the Secretary’s action in this respect through mandamus. The Court affirmed the refusal and explicitly reserved the merits of Decatur’s construction of the legislation. (515–517, 523.)

## Separate writings

McLean agreed that mandamus should be refused but rejected the proposition that this pension direction left executive discretion. A statutory instruction to pay a named person a fixed half-pay pension was imperative and ministerial. He stated that Story agreed with that view. McLean instead concluded that Congress did not authorize two pensions: receipt under the general act had satisfied the benefit, and he also expressed the impression that the special resolution alone governed Decatur’s entitlement. Story’s reported agreement concerns the ministerial character of the duty; the report does not expressly attribute every part of McLean’s statutory reasoning to him. (517–518.)

Baldwin’s much longer writing, relocated to an appendix in the ordinary report and inserted after McLean in the expanded reprint, also opposed cumulative benefits. He applied the principle that express provision for a particular case excludes it from another statute’s general provision. He did not treat Decatur’s receipt of the general pension under protest as necessarily relinquishing her ability to elect the special benefit. More fundamentally, Baldwin disputed Taney’s jurisdictional analysis. The circuit court’s power to hear and determine a mandamus application was distinct from whether the writ should issue on the merits. An erroneous classification of an act as ministerial would be an erroneous exercise of jurisdiction, not necessarily an act wholly outside jurisdiction. If the legislation established the claimed entitlement, determining dates and amounts and directing payment would be ministerial duties. (Expanded report PDF 19–28.)

Baldwin separately maintained that the Supreme Court lacked statutory appellate jurisdiction to revise this mandamus proceeding. He compared mandamus with other summary prerogative writs, argued that the order was not the final judgment contemplated by the Judiciary Act, and questioned precedents that had assumed such review without adequate argument. He would not expand appellate power through asserted necessity; Congress had to supply it. His agreement that the refusal should remain undisturbed therefore did not mean agreement that the Supreme Court properly reviewed and affirmed it on Taney’s ground. (Expanded report PDF 28–35.)

Catron emphasized a broader conflict between judicial coercion and executive administration of public funds. He regarded this proceeding as an attempt to make the local circuit court adjudicate a disputed government obligation and force payment contrary to the executive’s decision. Questions about double pensions, rations, interest, and fund sufficiency illustrated the danger of replacing the established administrative system with litigation. He concluded that the circuit court lacked jurisdiction over this subject and that its refusal should be affirmed. That extensive institutional position is Catron’s separate reasoning. (518–523.)
