# Poitra v. School District No. 1 in the County of Denver

**311 F.R.D. 659 (D. Colo. 2015)** — December 21, 2015; No. 14-cv-00887-WYD-CBS; Document 82. Magistrate Judge Craig B. Shaffer. Civil Procedure: supplemental witness disclosures and exclusion under Rule 37(c)(1). Assigned: CivPro.pdf physical page 501, printed pages 979–980, Note 7.

## Facts and posture

Michael Poitra sued Denver's school district and supervisor Anita Marchant after his 2013 termination, alleging employment discrimination and other violations. This discovery order did not decide the discrimination merits. Note 4 records a separate September 2015 summary-judgment order: retaliation, due-process, and tortious-interference claims were dismissed, while race, gender, and age discrimination claims remained for trial. 662 & n.4.

Discovery closed January 5, 2015. Poitra did not identify employee Rebecca Ortega in his initial disclosures or any written supplement before the May 5 final pretrial conference. His April 30 proposed pretrial order included her among forty-one witnesses. The district objected to seven undisclosed witnesses and raised the issue at the conference. The magistrate judge declined to rule without a written motion. A May 8 order set trial for January 11, 2016, but the district waited until November 16 to move for sanctions. By the December 16 hearing, Poitra had removed the other three people named in that motion; the dispute concerned Ortega alone. 661–663.

Poitra argued that references to Ortega during his and Marchant's depositions supplied sufficient notice under Rule 26(e)'s exception for information otherwise made known during discovery. He also pointed to the final pretrial order and the separate requirement to disclose trial witnesses at least thirty days before trial. The district sought both exclusion and its motion expenses. Although the motion's caption invoked Rule 37(a)(3)(A), counsel conceded that Rule 37(c)(1) was the appropriate basis. 661 & n.1, 664–665, 668 n.10.

## Issue, rules, and holding

**Did mentioning Ortega during depositions satisfy supplementation when those references did not adequately disclose her intended supporting role and testimony?** No. Information otherwise made known must be the functional equivalent of a proper disclosure, provided in time for meaningful discovery and trial preparation. These references were insufficient. The court excluded Ortega from substantive or merits testimony, preserved possible impeachment testimony, and denied the district's requested attorney's fees and costs. 666–671 & n.13.

Rule 26(a)(1)(A)(i) required identifying individuals likely to have discoverable information that the party might use to support its claims or defenses, along with the subjects of their information, except witnesses used solely for impeachment. It did not require disclosing every person who might know something. Rule 26(e)(1)(A) imposed a timely duty to correct incomplete or inaccurate disclosures unless equivalent information had otherwise been communicated. Rule 26(g)'s reasonable-inquiry certification reinforced the need for usable, deliberate disclosure. 663–668.

Under Rule 37(c)(1), undisclosed testimony generally could not be used unless the omission was substantially justified or harmless. The court retained discretion to select other appropriate sanctions. The Woodworker's factors considered prejudice or surprise, ability to cure, trial disruption, and bad faith or willfulness. The opinion quotes the fourth factor as the **moving party's** bad faith, but its application expressly examines whether **Poitra's** nondisclosure was willful; that wording inconsistency should not be silently attributed to a corrected quotation. 668–670.

## Why the deposition references were insufficient

Poitra's testimony mentioned Ortega's dissatisfaction with her job, fear of losing it, dislike of Marchant, and earlier workplace allegations. Marchant made one limited reference to Ortega's harassment complaints and their investigation. These accounts did not adequately match the subsequently proposed testimony about observations of Poitra, disciplinary practices, Marchant, Montgomery, and Worden. Knowing that Ortega existed was different from knowing that Poitra intended to rely on her as a supporting witness with specified information. 664–667.

The court rejected an interpretation requiring a risk-averse opponent to investigate every passing name despite deposition limits and proportionality. It distinguished Llewellyn, where deposition questioning concerned a litigation document created by an accountant and made the witness's relevant damages information clear. Thus, Poitra does not abolish the discovery-based exception; it requires adequate content in the particular circumstances. 666–668 & n.9.

The thirty-day trial-witness requirement also did not replace initial disclosure and supplementation. Note 10 emphasizes that Rule 26(a)(3) applies **in addition to** the earlier requirements. Counsel learned about Ortega around the December 2014 deposition and interviewed her near the discovery cutoff, yet formally designated her only months later. The court found no substantial justification. Notes 3 and 11 discuss counsel's use of a broader Colorado disclosure formulation and the historical transition in the federal rule. 662 n.3, 668–669 & nn.10–11.

## Why exclusion survived the defendant's delay

The omission prejudiced preparation and efficient discovery. An eleventh-hour deposition would leave little time to assimilate testimony, and reopening discovery or postponing trial would impose its own expense and disruption. No evidence established that Poitra's failure was willful or in bad faith, but lack of willfulness alone did not establish harmlessness. Note 12 also suggests that other witnesses could cover much of Ortega's expected testimony, reducing the likely adverse effect of exclusion. 669–670 & n.12.

The judge nevertheless criticized the district's conduct. Defense counsel knew of the disputed witnesses in April and May, waited passively for Poitra to narrow his list, and moved only as trial approached. Earlier action could have permitted an adjustment for good cause under Rule 16(b)(4). In another case, that delay might defeat the requested relief; the judge did not conclusively decide whether it was inattention or calculated strategy here. 670–671.

In these circumstances, Poitra's affirmative disclosure failures and continuing witness-list uncertainty outweighed the district's delay for purposes of exclusion. But awarding the district its motion expenses would appear to reward its dilatory conduct, so the court declined them. 671.

## Exact disposition, comparison, and verification

**Motion 58 granted to exclude Ortega as a substantive or merits witness. Attorney's fees and costs for the motion denied.** Nothing in the ruling barred her limited use solely for impeachment. The other three people originally targeted had already been voluntarily removed. This nondispositive order did not prevent Poitra from trying his remaining claims; Note 2 distinguishes a sanction that would effectively dispose of them. 661 n.2, 671 & n.13.

The textbook accurately reproduces the disclosure rationale at 666–668 and identifies the prejudice and trial-management concerns. Its shorthand that the motion to strike and for sanctions was granted needs the precise limitations above: no expense award, no blanket prohibition on impeachment, and no finding of willful nondisclosure.
