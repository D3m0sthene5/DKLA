# James Baird Co. v. Gimbel Bros., Inc.

64 F.2d 344 (2d Cir. 1933) | Contracts

## Decision and setting

The Second Circuit affirmed a judgment for the linoleum supplier because its offer had been revoked before the general contractor accepted it. Using the quoted price in a construction bid did not itself accept the supplier’s offer, and the court declined to use promissory estoppel to supply the missing acceptance. Learned Hand wrote for the panel of Manton, Hand, and Swan on April 10, 1933. The report contains no separate writing or judicial footnote. The caption begins on 344; the judicial opinion occupies 345–346.

## Facts and chronology

Pennsylvania’s Department of Highways sought bids for construction of a public building. Gimbel Bros., a New York merchant, sent an employee to examine specifications held by a contractor in Philadelphia. He underestimated the required linoleum yardage by about half. Unaware of the mistake, Gimbel sent offers to twenty or thirty prospective general contractors on December 24. It proposed two lump-sum prices, depending on quality. Its language contemplated a guarantee if it was awarded the supply contract and prompt acceptance after the general contract was awarded. 345.

James Baird Co., a Washington contractor, received its offer on December 28. Gimbel discovered the mistake that same day and telegraphed a withdrawal, saying it would substitute prices about twice as high. Baird received that telegram during the afternoon, after submitting its own lump-sum bid in Harrisburg using Gimbel’s figures. The public authorities accepted Baird’s bid on December 30. Written confirmation of Gimbel’s withdrawal reached Baird on December 31; Baird formally accepted the original offer on January 2. Gimbel refused to recognize a contract. These were facts found at the stipulated bench trial, not merely allegations accepted on a pleading motion. The opinion supplies the month and day sequence without specifying the transaction year. 345.

## Procedure and the contractor’s theories

Baird sued for damages for breach of a contract to sell and deliver linoleum. Gimbel denied that a contract had been made. The parties tried the case to a judge under a written stipulation, and the judge directed judgment for Gimbel. Contract formation was the only issue the appellate court needed to decide. 345.

Baird argued that Gimbel knew contractors would use its prices in their bids and thereby commit themselves to the public project. The implied arrangement, Baird contended, made the offer irrevocable upon that use, or treated the use as acceptance accompanied by a conditional promise to buy if the general bid succeeded. Withdrawal of Baird’s public bid might still have been possible, but the time to submit a replacement had passed. Losing the entire job, and probably its deposit, over a comparatively small flooring item would impose a disproportionate hardship. Alternatively, Baird invoked promissory estoppel despite the absence of a bilateral contract. 345–346.

## Why use of the bid was not acceptance

The court began with the ordinary rule: an acceptance communicated after withdrawal is too late. The parties could have made their dealings binding at the bidding stage, but the question was what the language and conduct actually manifested. Gimbel’s awareness of the contractor’s predicament gave Baird’s argument some force; it did not establish the proposed exchange. 345–346.

Hand tested the asserted contract from the other direction. If Baird had repudiated the public contract after receiving the award, Gimbel could not have sued Baird for breach merely because Baird had used its figures. Nor could Gimbel have proved a contract claim against Baird’s bankruptcy estate on that basis. The use had not manifested a commitment to buy from Gimbel. The terms also looked forward to an award and subsequent communicated acceptance. Treating that anticipated communication as mere notice of a contract already formed by the general bid strained the offer’s natural meaning. Contractors could instead have secured conditional supply contracts before using the figures. The court declined to rescue an unprotected commercial bidder through a strained construction of the offer. 346.

## Promissory estoppel and the proposed option

The court acknowledged both the charitable-subscription roots of promissory estoppel and its broader formulation in the First Restatement’s §90. It assumed that formulation for argument’s sake, rather than rejecting promissory estoppel in every setting. It nevertheless distinguished a donative promise from an offer seeking an exchange. A gratuitous promisor might become bound when another acted in reliance; this offer sought Baird’s promise to take and pay for the linoleum. Gimbel had not stipulated that submission of the general bid would supply that equivalent. Applying estoppel here would bind it without the counter-promise its offer requested. 346.

The court also refused to construe the offer as an option under which Baird could accept later if it won the public contract while remaining free to buy elsewhere. Nothing indicated that Gimbel intended that one-sided commitment. Importantly, Hand acknowledged that estoppel might operate if an actual option promise were established, noted adverse decisions, and expressly declined to decide that different question. Thus, the holding turns on the nature and terms of this offer; the option discussion is not a universal rejection of reliance-based irrevocability. 346.

## Exact result and assigned-source scope

The judgment was **affirmed**. Baird obtained neither damages nor a remand for a new trial. The opinion did not determine a damages figure or decide a separate mistake defense to an otherwise formed contract. 346.
