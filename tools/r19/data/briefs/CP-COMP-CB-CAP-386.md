# Klipsch Group, Inc. v. ePRO E-Commerce Limited

**880 F.3d 620 (2d Cir. 2018)** — January 25, 2018; argued October 26, 2017; Nos. 16-3637-cv and 16-3726-cv. Lynch, joined by Jacobs and District Judge Crotty, sitting by designation. Civil Procedure: discovery misconduct, compensatory sanctions, and preservation of recovery. Assigned: CivPro.pdf physical page 500, printed page 978, Note 5.

## Facts and procedural posture

Klipsch sued ePRO, a Chinese company operating DealExtreme, in August 2012 for selling counterfeit headphones. ePRO admitted some infringing sales but maintained that its liability was small. Klipsch initially suspected millions of dollars of sales. These positions were not final merits findings. The discovery dispute developed because ePRO failed to disclose responsive materials and preserve electronically stored information. 623–627.

By March 2013 ePRO had produced fewer than 500 documents. Its chief executive acknowledged that it had not implemented a litigation hold, and documents it had denied possessing—including original transaction records—turned out to exist. The magistrate judge first permitted ePRO to correct its failures using its own discovery vendor. FTI's work generated roughly 40,000 documents, including 1,236 original transaction records. But ePRO restricted the vendor's access, and the supplemental production led Klipsch to undertake a second round of depositions. Those examinations disclosed continued failures to impose a proper hold despite court instructions and counsel's warnings. 624–625.

The magistrate judge declined immediate sanctions and eventually authorized Klipsch to conduct its own forensic examination, initially at its expense, subject to later allocation. The examination produced disputes about **structured information**, chiefly the sales database, and **unstructured information**, including emails, files, and communications. Klipsch sought default and larger restraints. After a four-day evidentiary hearing in January 2015, Judge Broderick declined to find proven destruction of structured sales data but found willful spoliation of unstructured information. The court denied default, reduced the hold tied to underlying sales to $25,000, and ordered discovery sanctions. 625–627.

The district court eventually awarded **$2.68 million**, commonly rounded in the opinion and textbook to $2.7 million, for corrective discovery. It also ordered jury instructions and measures preserving payment. Both parties appealed: ePRO challenged the sanctions, evidence, and restraints; Klipsch challenged the refusal to find spoliation of structured sales data. 626–627.

## Issue, rule, and holding

**Can reasonable corrective-discovery costs exceed the likely damages on the underlying claim without making the sanction punitive?** Yes. Costs reasonably incurred, assessed when the corrective steps were undertaken, may be shifted when they were caused by the opponent's discovery misconduct. The costs do not become impermissibly punitive merely because the investigation uncovers fewer infringing sales or less spoliation than the investigating party expected. All challenged orders were affirmed. 623–624, 630–635.

The court had interlocutory jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. §1292(a)(1) over the injunction restraining assets and pendent jurisdiction over the intertwined sanctions issues. It reviewed the challenged rulings for abuse of discretion, including errors of law, clearly erroneous factual findings, and decisions outside the permissible range. 627.

The monetary award rested on **inherent authority**, not Rule 37. Note 6 explained that inherent sanctions required bad faith; the court had no occasion to decide whether the December 2015 amendments to Rule 37 applied. This historical holding should not be converted into a decision resolving all relationships between current Rule 37(e) and inherent power. 632 & n.6.

## Evidentiary findings and jury instructions

The record supported willful failure to preserve unstructured information. It included 4,596 manually deleted documents, all recovered; seven employees using wiping software that resulted in 31 unrecoverable documents; operating-system upgrades by eighteen employees that destroyed usage data; missing email accounts; and refusal to permit access to 32 of 36 private messaging accounts. The evidence supported a cumulative finding; recovery of one class of documents did not negate other losses. Note 4 distinguishes the examiner's initial estimate of 3,489 permanently wiped files from its final estimate of 31. The 4,596 manually deleted files were a separate group. 628–629 & n.4.

The court applied the historical spoliation elements of control and a preservation duty, culpability, and relevance to a claim or defense. Prejudice and relevance were evaluated for unrecoverable evidence. ePRO could not defeat preservation obligations by insisting that employees used personal accounts when those accounts contained company business. Its own failure to separate personal and business material contributed to the problem. 628–630.

The jury **had to be told that ePRO destroyed relevant unstructured information after its preservation duty arose** and **could infer that the evidence favored Klipsch**. The favorable inference was permissive. The court did not mandate an inference proving infringement, and it did not order default. 626, 630.

The exclusion of a late expert report was also within discretion. The parties had exchanged reports and had opportunities to cross-examine the experts. ePRO submitted Hammerquist's further report after the hearing; its claimed impact on Regard's analysis did not require reopening the record. 627–628.

Klipsch's cross-appeal failed. Jelen described interconnected sales, inventory, and shipping modules in which ordinary editing was possible but coordinated erasure would be difficult. Direct proof of sales-record destruction was absent, and even Klipsch's examiner did not unequivocally establish it. Missing backups did not compel an inference about their contents or an automatic sanction. The district court could distinguish willful unstructured spoliation from an inadequately proven structured-data theory. 629–630.

## Why the monetary award was compensatory

The award covered costs caused by ePRO's failures, beginning with the second Hong Kong depositions, which cost approximately $550,000. The court approved successive remedial steps after ePRO had opportunities to correct its conduct. Approval was tied to this history, rather than a general authorization for limitless investigation. The forensic examination was reasonable when ordered even though it did not substantiate every suspected problem. 631–632.

Klipsch's partial failure on structured information did not require a proportional fee reduction. It obtained substantial relief on willful discovery violations and unstructured spoliation. Nor did a potentially small merits recovery excuse compliance with disclosure and preservation duties. The court distinguished damages-oriented and civil-rights fee analogies from reimbursement of expenses the wrongdoer caused. Notes 8 and 9 address the Johnson analogy and explain that ePRO did not challenge particular hours or rates on appeal. The district court had examined the submissions and had excluded approximately $300,000 because the fee materials failed to provide attorney credentials. 626 n.3, 632–635 & nn.8–9.

The court did not endorse unnecessarily broad initial discovery. Ordinary proportionality and burden limits remained relevant. Note 7 invoked Goodyear v. Haeger: a compensatory inherent-power award must reimburse costs that would not have been incurred but for the misconduct. Here that causal limitation was satisfied; the court therefore did not need to reach the special procedural requirements for punitive sanctions. 631–634 & n.7.

## Exact disposition and security

**Affirmed in all respects**, including rejection of Klipsch's cross-appeal. The $2.68 million corrective-discovery award, mandatory and permissive instructions, corresponding roughly $2.7 million asset restraint, and additional $2.3 million security survived. The additional amount secured potential treble damages and remaining recoverable Lanham Act attorney's fees. At ePRO's request the combined roughly $5 million could be held through an asset restraint, a bond, or a combination. 634–636.

The security depended on the district court's dissipation-risk finding concerning a foreign company with few American ties that repeatedly disregarded court orders and its lawyers' instructions. The roughly $5 million was not an adjudicated counterfeit-sales damages award, and the opinion did not establish a universal right to freeze assets for ordinary damages claims. 634–635.
