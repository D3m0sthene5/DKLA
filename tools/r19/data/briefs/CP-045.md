Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins

Citation
Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938). Supreme Court of the United States. Argued January 31,
1938; decided April 25, 1938. Justice Brandeis delivered the opinion. Justice Reed concurred in the result and
much of the reasoning but rejected the constitutional rationale. Justice Butler dissented, joined by Justice
McReynolds. Justice Cardozo did not participate. Assigned excerpt: casebook pp. 445–452. Full opinion: Full
judicial opinion.
Parties
Harry Tompkins, a Pennsylvania citizen, was the injured pedestrian and plaintiff. Erie Railroad, a New York
corporation, was the defendant and Supreme Court petitioner. Tompkins invoked diversity jurisdiction in the
Southern District of New York to pursue a tort claim arising from a Pennsylvania accident. The dispute
concerned the substantive law that the federal court should apply, not the existence of diversity jurisdiction
itself. 304 U.S. at 69–71.
Material Facts
Tompkins was walking on a commonly used path alongside Erie’s tracks in Hughestown, Pennsylvania, when
an object resembling a projecting door struck him from a passing freight train. He claimed that long, open use
of the path made him a licensee to whom the railroad owed ordinary care. The casebook introduction supplies
the July 27, 1934 date and the fact that his right arm was severed; the Court’s account describes the location,
asserted negligence, and competing accounts of his legal status. Id. at 69–70; CB 445.
Erie contended that Pennsylvania decisions classified users of a longitudinal path along a railroad as trespassers
and required only avoidance of willful or wanton injury to an undiscovered trespasser. Tompkins disputed both
that interpretation and its application. He also argued that, without a state statute, the railroad’s duty was a
matter of general law on which a federal court could exercise independent judgment. His additional contention
that he was struck at a transverse crossing remained open. 304 U.S. at 70, 80 & n.24.
Procedural History
The district court declined to rule that Pennsylvania law precluded recovery. A jury returned a $30,000 verdict
for Tompkins. The Second Circuit affirmed, treating railroad negligence as a question of general law and
consequently finding it unnecessary to resolve the competing interpretations of Pennsylvania decisions. It
applied an ordinary-care standard for permissive use of a railroad path and permitted the jury to decide
negligence and contributory negligence. Id. at 70–71, 81–82.
Erie obtained Supreme Court review. The Court reconsidered Swift v. Tyson, under which federal diversity
courts had independently formulated rules in fields classified as general jurisprudence. It reversed because the
lower court had disregarded the state-law inquiry. The Supreme Court did not itself determine the final content
or application of Pennsylvania tort law on remand. Id. at 71–80.
Issue
May a federal court exercising diversity jurisdiction disregard a state’s decisional law on a substantive tort
question because it regards that subject as general law and no state statute controls? More broadly, did Swift
correctly permit federal judges to formulate independent general common-law rules for state-created rights? Id.
at 71–78.

Rule and Holding
Swift’s doctrine was disapproved. Where no governing federal constitutional or statutory law supplies the
substantive rule, the applicable state law includes authoritative state judicial decisions as well as statutes. The
federal court could not replace Pennsylvania’s law of the railroad’s duty with its own conception of general tort
law. Id. at 78–80.
The majority grounded that conclusion both in the proper interpretation of § 34 of the Judiciary Act and in the
constitutional allocation of authority over state law. Its rejection of general federal common law does not mean
that every procedural choice is controlled by state law or that a federal court may never formulate a rule in an
area governed by distinct federal authority. Those further questions require identifying the source and scope of
the asserted federal power. Id. at 72–80; id. at 90–92 (Reed, J., concurring in part).
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Second Circuit and remanded to that court for further proceedings consistent
with its opinion. Tompkins’s judgment could no longer rest on the lower court’s independent general-law
approach. The Court left the disputed state-law questions, including the significance of the claimed crossing
location, for further consideration; it did not enter a merits judgment declaring Erie free of liability. Id. at 80 &
n.24.
Reasoning
The Court began with Swift’s interpretation of the Rules of Decision Act. Swift had read the states’ laws to
include statutes, local usages, and rules concerning matters with a fixed local character, while treating broader
commercial and common-law questions as open to independent federal judgment. That approach allowed
federal judges to regard state decisions as informative rather than binding. Brandeis concluded that the statute
instead called for application of state law in its written and unwritten forms, except where federal law
controlled. He relied in part on historical research into the original Judiciary Act. Id. at 71–73.
The promised national uniformity had not materialized. State courts continued to follow their own
common-law rules; the federal view did not bind them. Within a single state, the legal consequences of the
same transaction could therefore differ depending on whether a litigant could invoke a federal forum.
Disagreement over which subjects were general and which were local created an additional layer of
uncertainty. The majority treated these consequences as reasons to reconsider the doctrine’s operation, not as
independent findings about the merits of Tompkins’s injury claim. Id. at 73–76.
The opinion used the Taxicab litigation as a concrete example. A Kentucky business reincorporated in
Tennessee and invoked federal jurisdiction to enforce an exclusive arrangement that Kentucky common law
would have treated as invalid. The example showed that changing access to a forum could change substantive
rights without changing the underlying local business transaction. Diversity jurisdiction, created in response to
concerns about local bias, had become a route to a distinct body of substantive law. Id. at 73–77.
Brandeis then supplied the constitutional rationale. Law governing a state-created relationship derives its
authority from the state, whether announced by legislators or judges. It is not an independently existing body of
general principles that federal judges have an equal authority to discover and apply differently. Respect for
state autonomy therefore extends to the state’s authoritative judicial voice as well as its statutes. The opinion
drew on earlier dissents by Field and Holmes to explain that source-of-authority principle. Id. at 78–79.
The majority regarded the prior course as an unconstitutional assumption of power in matters reserved to the
states. Its language concerning Congress must be read with its express recognition of matters governed by the
Federal Constitution or acts of Congress. The decision rejected a general authority to prescribe state

The Court expressly denied that it was invalidating § 34 or another federal statute. Its position was that the
judiciary’s application of Swift had invaded state authority. This distinction mattered because the separate
opinions questioned both the necessity of constitutional adjudication and the procedural safeguards required
before invalidating federal legislation. The statutory interpretation and constitutional explanation were related
but distinct parts of the majority’s reasoning. Id. at 77–80, 87–92.
Applied to this case, the immediate error was the Second Circuit’s refusal to decide the Pennsylvania-law
dispute. Both parties had cited Pennsylvania decisions, but the appellate court considered itself free to bypass
them in favor of general law. Once Swift was rejected, that bypass could not sustain the judgment. The Court
therefore remanded rather than selecting between the parties’ readings of the state decisions. Id. at 80.
Separate Opinions
Justice Reed agreed that Swift should be overruled because the statute’s reference to state laws includes state
judicial decisions. He considered that construction sufficient. He did not accept that the Constitution
necessarily compelled the same result in the absence of statutory direction, and questioned the majority’s broad
language about congressional power. Federal power over procedure was unquestioned, while the
substantive-procedural line was difficult; the Judiciary Article and Necessary and Proper Clause might support
relevant legislation. Reed preferred correcting the statutory interpretation without deciding those constitutional
questions. Id. at 90–92; CB 451–452.
Justice Butler, joined by McReynolds, objected that the constitutional question had neither been presented nor
argued. He emphasized the long acceptance of Swift, disputed the force assigned to the historical research, and
believed the Court should at least have ordered reargument. In his account, declaring Congress powerless to
prescribe the established rule effectively invalidated § 34 as previously construed and should have triggered an
opportunity for the United States to participate under the 1937 intervention statute. Id. at 82–90; CB 449–451.
Butler would nevertheless have reversed Tompkins’s judgment. His proposed ground was contributory
negligence under the governing law as he understood it, rather than a constitutional rejection of Swift. The
label dissent therefore must not be read as support for preserving the damages award. His disagreement
concerned the Court’s rationale and remand, and he would have reached a merits-based adverse result for
Tompkins. Id. at 90.
Notes
The casebook introduction describes the Pennsylvania trespasser rule as the operative contrast with general
law. The judicial opinion more carefully records that Tompkins disputed the asserted rule and its applicability,
and leaves those questions open. A brief should preserve that procedural qualification rather than attribute a
final Pennsylvania-law determination to the Supreme Court. 304 U.S. at 70, 80 & n.24; CB 445, 449.
Erie addresses the source of substantive law in a court that already has jurisdiction. It neither abolishes
diversity jurisdiction nor supplies a universal test for every federal-state procedural conflict. York, Byrd,
Hanna, and the later cases in this compilation develop those further questions. The exact result here was
removal of an unauthorized general-law basis for judgment and remand for the state-law inquiry. 304 U.S. at
78–80.
