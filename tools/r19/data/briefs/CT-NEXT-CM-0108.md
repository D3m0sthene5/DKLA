# Market Street Associates Limited Partnership v. Frey
941 F.2d 588 (7th Cir. 1991) | Contracts

## Lease mechanism and the correspondence

In 1968, J.C. Penney sold property to General Electric Pension Trust and leased it back for twenty-five years. Paragraph 34 permitted the tenant to request financing for improvements costing at least $250,000, required reasonable consideration and good-faith negotiations, and allowed repurchase if negotiations failed. The repurchase formula roughly restored the original sale price with six percent annual appreciation. If market appreciation exceeded that rate, the option could be valuable. Market Street acquired the Milwaukee shopping-center lease in 1987. A drugstore later wanted space if Market Street constructed it, but outside lenders required a mortgage Market Street could not grant as a tenant. 941 F.2d at 591.

Partner William Orenstein initially explored buying the property. Trust representative David Erb repeatedly failed to return calls; eventually an associate quoted $3 million, which Orenstein considered excessive. On July 28, 1988, Market Street requested $2 million in improvement financing without mentioning the lease or paragraph 34. An August 16 follow-up referred to financing pursuant to the lease, but still did not identify the paragraph. The next day it received a refusal based on the trust’s policy against loans below $7 million. Orenstein replied that Market Street would seek financing elsewhere, then on September 27 invoked the repurchase option. The formula apparently produced about $1 million; the record did not establish whether market value actually reached $3 million. Id. at 591–592. The assigned summary says $4 million in requested financing; the complete opinion supplies $2 million, used here.

## Summary judgment and preliminary procedural questions

The trust refused to sell. Market Street sued for specific performance, and the trust removed the action to federal court. Cross-motions produced judgment for the trust. The district judge reasoned that failure to identify paragraph 34 both prevented the required negotiations and violated good faith, drawing an inference that Orenstein hoped Erb would overlook the option. Id. at 589, 592.

Before the merits, the Seventh Circuit repaired deficient diversity allegations by requiring citizenship affidavits for every general and limited partner and each trustee. The submissions established complete diversity; jurisdiction depended on those people’s citizenship rather than merely the partnership’s place of formation or the trust’s identity. The court also rejected the argument that Market Street waived trial by moving for summary judgment. Cross-motions do not stipulate that a judge may decide disputed facts on the written record. Each motion must receive the ordinary treatment favoring its opponent when reasonable factual inferences differ. Id. at 589–591.

## The bargain’s limits and the role of good faith

The trust could not rely on the absence of negotiations if its own failure to consider the request reasonably prevented them. Yet Market Street could not rely solely on the absence of an express requirement to identify paragraph 34. An implied obligation might prevent opportunistic conduct in performing the existing bargain. The trust had argued that no remedy existed, so the court did not decide whether the proper remedy for refusal to consider financing should instead be renewed negotiations rather than immediate conveyance. Id. at 592–593.

Judge Posner distinguished contractual good faith from fiduciary responsibility or altruism. A contracting party ordinarily may exploit superior market information in bargaining and need not rescue the other from an allocated risk or financial difficulty. Good faith therefore did not make Market Street the pension trust’s guardian or require universal candor. But deliberately exploiting a counterparty’s oversight about rights under their existing contract was different from preserving gains earned through independent market knowledge. It could create no productive benefit while inducing needless defensive costs and undermining the cooperation the parties’ bargain presupposed. Id. at 593–595.

The court understood the doctrine as supplying limits the parties would have adopted had they foreseen the opportunistic situation without negotiating costs. Examples included actually examining work before invoking a satisfaction condition, avoiding artificial requirements increases solely to resell at a favorable price, and promoting goods under an exclusive dealership. These illustrations did not create a free-floating fairness review or allow rewriting deliberate risk allocations. Because this was a land lease governed by Wisconsin common law, the court did not resolve whether the UCC’s definition of good faith would alter the analysis. Id. at 595–596.

## Why a trial was needed

One possible account was that Orenstein sought a bargain purchase, realized the trust had forgotten the lease term, withheld a costless correction, and lulled it into thinking financing was abandoned until the option could be exercised. That could be bad-faith performance. Another was that he reasonably expected a sophisticated trust to review its own lease, believed it knew or would discover paragraph 34, and understood the flat refusal to mean it would not finance improvements regardless of the repurchase consequence. His earlier preference for outside financing did not conclusively eliminate genuine interest in trust financing once outside financing proved unavailable. Id. at 596–597.

The district judge chose the inference favorable to the trust rather than the permissible inference favorable to Market Street. Orenstein’s state of mind remained disputed. A bench trial could illuminate it through live testimony and credibility assessment; the fact that specific performance would be tried to a judge did not make deposition-based summary judgment an adequate substitute. The appellate court therefore reversed and remanded. It neither found Market Street acted in bad faith nor ordered conveyance. Id. at 597–598.
