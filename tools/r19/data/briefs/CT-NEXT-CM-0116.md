# Simeone v. Simeone
525 Pa. 392, 581 A.2d 162 (1990) | Contracts

## Facts and procedural posture
In 1975 Catherine Walsh, a twenty-three-year-old unemployed nurse, married Frederick Simeone, a thirty-nine-year-old neurosurgeon earning approximately $90,000 annually with assets disclosed at approximately $300,000. On the wedding’s eve Frederick’s lawyer presented the final prenuptial agreement. Catherine signed without independent counsel or advice from his lawyer about the rights surrendered. The agreement limited support after separation or divorce to $200 weekly and $25,000 altogether. The parties separated in 1982, divorce proceedings began in 1984, and Frederick’s intervening payments satisfied that maximum. Catherine sought alimony pendente lite in 1985. A master upheld the agreement and rejected her claim; the common pleas court dismissed her exceptions and the Superior Court affirmed. 525 Pa. at 396–397.

The Supreme Court took the appeal to clarify Estate of Geyer’s uncertain standards. Catherine challenged the adequacy of disclosure about statutory rights, the reasonableness of support, disclosure of Frederick’s car collection, and the circumstances of signing. The court affirmed the order, holding the agreement enforceable and barring alimony pendente lite. Id. at 397–404. Its decision concerns this agreement and the interim-support claim, rather than an award resolving all marital property.

## Rule and reasoning
Justice Flaherty’s opinion discarded Geyer’s review of a prenuptial bargain’s reasonableness and the parties’ informed understanding of surrendered legal rights. Prenuptial agreements were to be evaluated as contracts: absent fraud, misrepresentation, or duress, the parties were bound. Failure to read or understand a term did not ordinarily defeat a signed agreement, and independent counsel was not a categorical prerequisite to enforceability. The court rejected earlier paternalistic assumptions that women necessarily lacked contractual knowledge or equal status, invoking the state’s prohibition of sex discrimination. Id. at 398–401.

The majority also emphasized reliance and allocation of risk. Reexamining fairness at either execution or dissolution would impair the certainty that prompted people to make such agreements. Illness, children, changes in employment, and financial reversals were among the changes a long-term arrangement could anticipate. If parties did not provide for them, the majority regarded the resulting changes in bargain value as contractual risks assumed, rather than independent grounds for judicial revision. Id. at 401–402.

The court retained full and fair disclosure of financial positions. Prospective spouses do not deal entirely at arm’s length: their relationship of trust requires disclosure, although exact valuation is unnecessary. A recital that disclosure occurred creates a presumption, rebuttable by clear and convincing evidence of fraud or misrepresentation. Catherine claimed that the car collection listed at $200,000 was undervalued by approximately $183,000. The master rejected her proof; expert testimony supported the disclosed value, and the list even included cars Frederick merely hoped to inherit. The Supreme Court found ample support for full disclosure. Id. at 402–403.

The statutory right actually at issue was alimony pendente lite, which the agreement expressly mentioned. Alimony and equitable-distribution rights created by the 1980 Divorce Code did not exist when this 1975 agreement was signed, and the code preserved earlier agreements. The court therefore distinguished the existence and express relinquishment of the interim-support right from Catherine’s contention that she had not understood its nature. Id. at 397–398.

Duress remained an available defense, but failed on the credited evidence. Although the final document arrived at 5 p.m. before the wedding, witnesses described several earlier discussions over six months, Catherine’s prior awareness of an agreement, and changes increasing her support provisions before execution. The preparing lawyer reported no reluctance at signing. The master rejected Catherine’s claim of last-minute surprise, and the appellate courts sustained that finding; the decision did not establish that every agreement presented on a wedding’s eve is free from duress. Id. at 403–404.

## Separate opinions and disposition
Justice Papadakos concurred only in the result. He considered this agreement valid under Geyer because disclosure, fairness, and absence of duress were supported, but rejected the majority’s broad assertions about actual equality between women and men. He viewed these agreements as potentially involving unequal authority and favored protection of a subordinate party regardless of sex. Id. at 405.

Justice McDermott, joined by Justice Larsen, dissented. He treated marriage and family protection as public interests warranting more than ordinary commercial-contract enforcement. He would require awareness of existing statutory rights as well as financial disclosure and allow clear and convincing proof of serious unfairness at dissolution. He discussed lost employment opportunities, child care, illness, and contributions increasing reserved property’s value. He agreed that financial disclosure had been adequate here, but would remand for Catherine to prove inadequate understanding of interim-support rights or inequity in the support provisions, without prejudging whether she could succeed. Id. at 406–413 & dissent nn.1–3.

The seven-justice court affirmed, with five supporting the result, including Papadakos’s separate concurrence, and two dissenting. Flaherty’s opinion recorded that the case had been reassigned to him. Id. at 396, 404–405, 413.
