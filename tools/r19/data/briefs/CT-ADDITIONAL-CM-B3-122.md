# DEC Electric, Inc. v. Raphael Construction Corporation
558 So. 2d 427 (Fla. 1990) | Contracts

## Court, review posture, and disposition
The Florida Supreme Court decided DEC Electric on March 22, 1990. Justice McDonald wrote the unanimous opinion; Chief Justice Ehrlich and Justices Overton, Shaw, Barkett, Grimes, and Kogan concurred without separate writings. Exercising certified-question jurisdiction, the court answered affirmatively whether contractor–subcontractor or supplier payment provisions concerning conditions or timing must be construed as a matter of law. It found this subcontract’s payment condition unambiguous and approved the result of the Fourth District Court of Appeal, which had sustained the denial of payment from the general contractor. (427–429.)

## Completed work and the owner’s nonpayment
DEC subcontracted with general contractor Raphael to perform electrical work on a construction project. The owner subsequently shut the project down. DEC’s satisfactory performance and entitlement to $25,612 plus interest for the work were undisputed, but Raphael had not received payment from the owner and refused to pay DEC. The dispute concerned whether Raphael’s obligation was contingent on that receipt, rather than defective performance or the valuation of DEC’s work. (428.)

Paragraph 6 of the subcontract addressed sworn statements, lien waivers, interim draws, and payment according to the general contractor’s estimate of completion on its owner billing. Its operative condition expressly provided that no funds would be owed to the subcontractor unless the owner paid the general contractor in accordance with the sworn statement. It also stated that the subcontractor understood that nonpayment left it recourse against the owner through mechanics’ lien law or other procedures for the sums due. The court read the paragraph as a whole, not merely its interim-draw language. (428.)

## The lower courts’ ambiguity concern
The trial court determined as a matter of law that paragraph 6 unambiguously required owner payment before Raphael owed DEC payment. The district court affirmed, while expressing concern about judicial rather than jury construction. It described the paragraph as somewhat confusing and ambiguous and observed that ambiguity ordinarily calls for a factfinder to assess evidence of actual intent. Believing the supreme court’s Peacock decision might require a different method for construction-payment provisions, it certified the question. The supreme court approved the result, but made its own express finding that the provision was unambiguous. (428–429.)

Raphael argued that ambiguous contract terms ordinarily present a factual issue of intent for a jury. The court accepted that proposition in most settings. It nevertheless maintained the special judicial-interpretation rule for provisions allocating the risk of owner nonpayment between contractors and subcontractors. Thus, the opinion did not convert every ambiguity in every contract into a question of law. It identified a particular category of payment-risk provisions for which the court had already prescribed an interpretive default. (428–429.)

## Judicial interpretation and the burden of clear risk shifting
Peacock treated construction payment provisions as suitable for judicial interpretation because the commercial relationship is common and the usual intention does not vary materially between transactions, even if language does. Small subcontractors generally need payment to remain in business and ordinarily do not intend to assume the owner’s failure to pay. This supported a default against imposing that risk through ambiguous language. (429.)

Within this setting, DEC Electric described two available constructions. An unambiguous risk-shifting provision establishes owner payment as a condition precedent to the general contractor’s payment obligation. An ambiguous provision instead fixes a reasonable time for the general contractor to pay. The general contractor bears the burden of clearly expressing a transfer of the owner-nonpayment risk. Once the judge determines that the provision is ambiguous, this substantive default resolves its effect; there is no remaining interpretive issue for the jury. (429.)

The court declined to recede from Peacock or Aetna Casualty, the latter having applied the same judicial approach in an action on a surety payment bond. The opinion’s sole footnote also referred to the recent OBS decision as reaffirming that interpretation is a matter of law, while acknowledging that OBS did not turn on that issue. These references reinforce the allocation of interpretive responsibility without supplying an additional factual finding about this project. (429 & sole note.)

## Application and limits of the result
Paragraph 6 clearly made payment by the owner a prerequisite. Because Raphael had not been paid, DEC could not recover the unpaid contract balance from Raphael under this provision, despite satisfactory completed work. The court answered the certified question affirmatively and approved the district court’s result. It did not order the $25,612 paid, send contract interpretation to a jury, or decide that every clause mentioning owner payment necessarily shifts the risk. Ambiguity would have produced the opposite construction—a reasonable payment time rather than a condition. (428–429.)

The case is distinct from a jurisdiction’s categorical statutory or public-policy prohibition on pay-if-paid clauses. This opinion allowed an adequately expressed condition under its historical Florida rule and preserved a construction favoring subcontractors where the condition was unclear. It does not certify the effect of later legislation, later cases, or current lien remedies.
