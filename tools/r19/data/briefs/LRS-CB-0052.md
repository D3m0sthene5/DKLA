Parties
The United States prosecuted Pierre Grimaud and J. P. Carajous for unauthorized sheep grazing in the Sierra
Forest Reserve and sought reversal of the judgment sustaining their demurrer. A similar indictment against
Inda produced the companion judgment. The defendants maintained that Congress could not constitutionally
make violation of regulations promulgated by the Secretary of Agriculture a criminal offense. The Secretary
was the administrator whose rule supplied the permit requirement, not the prosecuting sovereign or the official
fixing the punishment. 220 U.S. at 509–510, 514, 522–523.
Material Facts
Federal legislation authorized creation of forest reservations and identified their purposes: protecting forests,
securing favorable water flows, and maintaining a continuous timber supply. The statutes permitted entry and
lawful use, including access by settlers, subject to regulations governing the reservations. Management
authority initially vested in the Secretary of the Interior was transferred to the Secretary of Agriculture in 1905.
The Secretary could regulate occupancy and use and protect the forests against destruction and harmful
activities. Id. at 507–509, 515–516.
Congress expressly made violations of the governing act or authorized rules punishable under a specified
statutory provision. The applicable maximum punishment was a $500 fine, twelve months' imprisonment, or
both in the court's discretion. Congress also directed how money received from forest products or use of forest
land and resources should be handled, including its payment into the Treasury and designated applications to
forest expenses and state or local purposes. Those provisions later supported the Court's conclusion that
grazing charges were authorized. Id. at 508–509, 522.
On June 12, 1906, the Secretary issued Regulation 45, generally requiring a permit before grazing livestock in
a forest reserve. Limited exceptions covered specified animals used by prospectors, campers, travelers, and
resident settlers. The indictment alleged that on April 26, 1907, Grimaud and Carajous knowingly and willfully
grazed sheep in the Sierra reserve without permission and outside the exceptions. These were allegations tested
on demurrer, not factual findings following trial. Id. at 509–510.
Procedural History
The defendants demurred, arguing both that the alleged facts did not constitute a federal offense and that the
statute unconstitutionally delegated legislative power by authorizing administrative rules whose violation could
be criminally punished. The Southern District of California sustained the demurrer and made a similar ruling in
Inda. The government obtained Supreme Court review under the Criminal Appeals Act's authorization of a writ
of error from a decision based on a statute's invalidity. Id. at 510, 514.

The Supreme Court initially affirmed both judgments by an equally divided Court. It then granted rehearing,
restored the cases to the docket, and heard reargument. The reported 1911 opinion set aside the earlier
affirmances and reversed the judgments sustaining the demurrers. The case therefore did not reach the Court as
an appeal from a criminal conviction, and the final decision did not itself find the defendants guilty. Id. at 506,
510, 523.
Issue
Did Congress impermissibly transfer legislative power when it authorized the Secretary to regulate use of
forest reserves and made violations of those regulations criminally punishable? More particularly, could
Regulation 45's permit requirement be enforced through the statutory penalty even though Congress had not
itself expressly forbidden sheep grazing without a permit? Did the statutory scheme authorize charges for
grazing permission? Id. at 514–515, 521–523.
Rule and Holding
Once Congress legislates and indicates its will, it may authorize an administrator to fill in details within the
defined statutory field. The forest statutes established the objects of management, the subjects of regulation, the
requirement of compliance, and the punishment. The Secretary's permit rule implemented that scheme and did
not constitute an unconstitutional transfer of legislative power. Its violation could be punished because
Congress expressly made violation of authorized regulations an offense and fixed the penalty. Id. at 516–518,
521–523.
Congress's treatment of revenue from use of forest resources, together with the general management authority,
also supported authority to charge for grazing. The Secretary's authority remained confined to the statutory
subjects and purposes; the decision did not authorize regulations for any purpose an administrator might
choose. On the allegations admitted for purposes of the demurrer, Regulation 45 was within those limits, so the
demurrers should have been overruled. Id. at 522–523.
Judgment
The Supreme Court set aside its earlier equally divided affirmances and reversed the district court judgments in
both cases. The indictments survived the constitutional and legal objections presented by demurrer. The
opinion imposed no sentence and made no post-trial determination that the defendants had committed the
alleged grazing offenses. Id. at 523.
Reasoning
The Court first read the permit requirement in the context of the whole statutory scheme. Congress had allowed
proper and lawful use of the reserves subject to compliance with regulations, while directing preservation of
forests and water flows. Unrestricted grazing could undermine those objectives; regulated grazing could remain
compatible with them. The statutes therefore contemplated administrative decisions about the permissible
conditions of use. Id. at 515–516.
Local variation explained why Congress could leave these decisions to an administrator. Grazing harmless in
one forest might damage another, and the effects could vary with season and the stage of timber growth. A
single congressional prescription could not conveniently account for all those circumstances. The Secretary's
task involved management of a defined resource according to objectives Congress had supplied.
Administrative discretion over such details did not mean Congress had surrendered the general legislative
decision. Id. at 516–517.

Lamar acknowledged the difficulty of drawing the boundary between lawmaking and execution. He relied on
the distinction described in Wayman v. Southard between subjects requiring legislative regulation and details
entrusted to those administering a general provision. Examples involving rates, safety equipment, bridges,
product labeling, and import quality showed that a statute could identify an obligation or prohibited condition
while an executive officer determined its particular application. The official had to remain within the statutory
field. Id. at 517–520.
The criminal sanction did not, by itself, convert the authorized administrative task into forbidden lawmaking.
Congress had expressly declared violations of these regulations punishable and selected the penalty. The
Secretary prescribed the operational permit requirement; the statute attached the criminal consequence. The
Court accordingly treated the offense as an offense against the United States under congressional legislation,
rather than an offense created solely at the Secretary's discretion. Id. at 520–523.
United States v. Eaton did not require a different result. In the Court's account of that earlier case, the
administrator demanded records beyond the statutory requirement, and Congress had not distinctly made
breach of that additional regulatory requirement punishable. Grimaud's legislation expressly covered violations
of the Secretary's authorized regulations. The contrast concerns the actual statutory basis of criminal liability,
not a categorical rule that all administrative requirements are either criminally enforceable or unenforceable.
Id. at 518–519.
Nor did prior tolerance of grazing create an unconditional right to use the public domain. Congress had
qualified any implied license by requiring compliance with reserve regulations. Once both statute and rule were
in place, grazing contrary to the authorized conditions was an unlawful use of federal property. The relevant
issue was the legal effect of violating the permit requirement, not whether grazing as an activity was invariably
forbidden. Id. at 521.
The fee objection required a separate statutory answer. The Court explained that the charges discouraged
excessive use and contributed toward management expenses. More decisively, legislation predating Regulation
45 expressly contemplated money received from use of forest land and resources and directed its disposition.
That treatment indicated authority to impose charges; later revenue provisions reinforced the reading. The
Court did not derive a general executive taxing power from the need to finance administration. Id. at 522.
Finally, the posture narrowed the application. The indictment alleged, and the demurrer admitted for purposes
of testing legal sufficiency, that the rule served regulation of occupancy and preservation of the forest. On that
premise it fell within the defined delegation. A rule for an unrelated purpose would present a different issue.
Congress's directions, the scope of the rule, and the statutory penalty all contributed to the result. Id. at
522–523.
Separate Opinions
No separate opinion accompanies Lamar's reported opinion on reargument. The earlier equally divided
affirmance is a prior procedural event expressly displaced by the final judgment; it should not be confused with
a reported dissent from the 1911 rationale. The report does not identify the positions of individual Justices in
the earlier division. Id. at 506, 510, 523.

Notes
Grimaud supplies an administrative-detail model alongside the conditional-legislation model in Aurora and
Field. A permit system necessarily leaves choices to the administrator, but the Court located the governing
objectives, regulated subject, obligation of compliance, and penalty in the statute. The analysis should explain
those constraints rather than merely label the task administrative. Id. at 515–518, 522–523.
The case also separates authorization of a rule from authorization of criminal punishment for its breach. The
Court's Eaton discussion makes the latter a statutory question that cannot be answered simply by observing that
an agency may issue regulations. The detailed fee analysis and that comparison supplement the shorter
assigned excerpt. This historical decision should not be read as resolving every later question about notice,
statutory ambiguity, or the validity of a particular criminal regulation. Id. at 518–523; casebook pp. 52–56.
