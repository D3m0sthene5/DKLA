# Train v. Colorado Public Interest Research Group, Inc.
426 U.S. 1 (1976) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, decision, and assigned scope
The United States Supreme Court decided the case on June 1, 1976. Justice Thurgood Marshall wrote the opinion for all eight participating justices; Justice Stevens took no part. No separate writing appears. The assigned Class 4 reading is the seven-page excerpt of this nuclear-materials decision. It must be distinguished from Train v. City of New York, 420 U.S. 35 (1975), which concerns allotment of sewage-treatment funding, and from other Train environmental cases.

## Parties, concrete dispute, and procedural history
Colorado organizations and residents claimed potential harm from radioactive effluents discharged by the Fort St. Vrain Nuclear Generating Station and the Rocky Flats nuclear-weapons-components plant. The facilities operated in conformity with effluent standards imposed under the Atomic Energy Act. The plaintiffs wanted the Environmental Protection Agency to regulate those radioactive discharges under the Federal Water Pollution Control Act as well. Their alleged prospective harm should not be converted into a judicial finding that either plant had caused an injury or violated its applicable nuclear standards. (426 U.S. at 4-7.)

The EPA had excluded three categories of materials regulated under the Atomic Energy Act from its water-pollution permit program: source material, special nuclear material, and byproduct material. Its regulation explained that the water statute's reference to radioactive materials covered substances beyond the Atomic Energy Commission's jurisdiction, such as radium and accelerator-produced isotopes as understood under the then-applicable scheme. The litigation challenged that exclusion, not the existence of federal regulation of the plants altogether. (Id. at 7-9.)

The plaintiffs sued the EPA and its Administrator under the water statute's citizen-suit provision, which permitted an action for failure to perform a nondiscretionary statutory duty. They sought a declaration that all radioactive materials were pollutants and an injunction compelling regulation of their discharge. On cross-motions for summary judgment, the District of Colorado held that the AEC had exclusive authority over discharges of the covered nuclear materials, 373 F. Supp. 991. The Tenth Circuit reversed, 507 F.2d 743. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed the court of appeals. (Id. at 4-5, 25.)

## Governing statutes and the interpretive question
The Atomic Energy Act created a comprehensive system governing production, possession, and use of source, special nuclear, and byproduct materials. It authorized federal standards to protect health and minimize dangers. The AEC regulated permissible releases and required efforts to keep them as low as reasonably achievable. By the time of the decision, the AEC's functions had been divided between the Nuclear Regulatory Commission and the Energy Research and Development Administration. The Court often continued to use AEC as shorthand while explaining that reorganization. (Id. at 5-7 & n.1.)

The water statute separately prohibited discharging pollutants into navigable waters without a federal or approved state permit. It expressly included radioactive materials in its definition of pollutant and broadly defined pollution to include human-induced alteration of water's radiological integrity. It also allowed a substantial state role, including more stringent state limitations. Those features gave the plaintiffs a substantial textual argument that radioactive nuclear effluents were covered. (Id. at 7-10, 16 & n.13.)

The precise issue was whether the broad reference to radioactive materials encompassed the three Atomic Energy Act categories within this water-permit program. It was not whether nuclear effluents were physically radioactive, environmentally harmless, or beyond Congress's regulatory power.

## Holding and practical result
The permit program did not include source, byproduct, and special nuclear materials regulated under the Atomic Energy Act. The EPA acted consistently with its statutory mandate when it declined to regulate their discharge through that program. The Supreme Court reversed the Tenth Circuit, defeating the claimed duty on which the requested injunction depended. (Id. at 24-25.)

The holding preserved a division of regulatory responsibility. It did not exempt nuclear facilities from the entire water statute, immunize discharges of other pollutants, or eliminate every EPA radiation function. In particular, the Court explained that EPA retained authority under a separate reorganization plan to establish generally applicable environmental radiation standards, while the nuclear regulator implemented standards through its licensing system. (Id. at 13, 24-25 n.20.)

## Reasoning: apparent clarity did not exclude legislative history
The Tenth Circuit considered the enacted wording plain: radioactive materials appeared without the exception the EPA wished to recognize, while the statute expressly included other exceptions. It therefore declined to resolve the legislative-history dispute. Marshall rejected any rule categorically forbidding available interpretive aids because statutory words appeared clear on an initial examination. The Court examined the history to determine what those words meant within the larger statutory arrangement. (Id. at 9-10.)

This is a decision about the historically applied interpretive method. Its reasoning should not be rewritten as though the Court merely deferred to the EPA's expertise. Footnote 8 expressly says the Court did not depend on the agency's interpretation in reaching its conclusion. The Court therefore found it unnecessary to resolve the plaintiffs' allegation that EPA had previously taken an inconsistent position. The justices themselves construed the statutes and history. (Id. at 8 n.8.)

The plaintiffs also cited a separate absolute prohibition on discharging radiological warfare agents and high-level radioactive waste, prefaced by notwithstanding other provisions. They argued that expressly banning those substances implied that other radioactive discharges were within the ordinary permit program. The Court acknowledged some logical appeal but thought a complete ban on one class did not itself establish permit-program jurisdiction over other classes. That inference, too, had to be assessed against the legislative history. (Id. at 10-11.)

## Reasoning: weighing committee evidence instead of treating it as uniform
The House committee report addressed the exact problem explicitly. It stated that the radioactive materials covered by the water statute did not include source, byproduct, or special nuclear materials regulated under the Atomic Energy Act. Because the enacted definition retained the same broad wording, the Court treated the report as an explanation of what that wording was understood to cover. (Id. at 11-12.)

The Senate report was less straightforward. It discussed developing EPA's technical capacity to set controls for nuclear-fuels-processing plants. The plaintiffs reasonably inferred anticipated control of radioactive effluents. The Court recognized the inference but distinguished coverage of a source, such as a plant, from coverage of every material it discharged. Nuclear facilities could discharge other pollutants within EPA's jurisdiction. Marshall gave the more specific House explanation greater weight than the implication drawn from the Senate discussion, particularly in light of the other evidence. (Id. at 12-14.)

That comparison matters: the opinion did not discover a perfectly unanimous historical record. It evaluated different kinds of evidence and explained why it considered some more probative of the precise allocation of regulatory authority.

## Reasoning: the floor colloquy and the failed amendment
On the Senate floor, Senator Pastore asked Senator Muskie whether the bill changed the AEC's existing regulatory authority. Muskie assured him that it did not and referred to Northern States Power Co. v. Minnesota, which had treated regulation of the relevant nuclear effluents as federally exclusive under the Atomic Energy Act. The plaintiffs argued that leaving the AEC's authority intact remained consistent with adding EPA authority over the same materials. The Court rejected that reading of the exchange. In context, the assurance was about preserving the existing exclusive arrangement, not merely keeping one agency active while making it subordinate to another. (Id. at 14-17.)

The difference between the statutes' state roles reinforced this conclusion. The water statute permitted state standards and state permitting; the existing nuclear scheme severely limited state regulation of the relevant discharges. Adding the disputed materials to the water program would thus change more than the name of the federal agency supervising them. It would introduce a different allocation of power between state and federal governments. (Id. at 16-17.)

In the House, Representative Wolff unsuccessfully proposed permitting states to regulate radioactive waste from nuclear power plants. The amendment failed by roughly three to one. Supporters wanted stronger local protection and criticized the AEC's combination of promotional and safety responsibilities. Opponents said the existing allocation should remain and cited the committee report excluding the nuclear materials from the water statute. (Id. at 17-21.)

The plaintiffs argued that rejection of state regulation did not foreclose federal EPA regulation. Marshall thought that distinction incompatible with the water statute's actual structure: if these substances were pollutants under the program, the unamended statute already afforded states a regulatory role. The Court therefore read the failed amendment and accompanying debate as additional support for exclusion of the materials, rather than as a decision allowing EPA regulation while withholding the ordinary state role. It also explained why contrary comments from unsuccessful amendment supporters carried less weight. (Id. at 21-22 & n.18.)

Finally, the conference committee retained the same radioactive-materials language. Representative Harsha explained that the conference action preserved the understanding expressed in the report and Senate colloquy. The Court treated that explanation as further corroboration, not as a separately enacted textual exception. (Id. at 22-23.)

## Limits and material restored from the full opinion
The complete opinion supplies the plaintiffs' particular plants, the citizen-suit posture, the competing Senate-report inference, the argument based on the absolute ban on high-level waste, the disclaimer of reliance on agency interpretation, and EPA's continuing general radiation role. Several are omitted or compressed in the assigned excerpt. Their restoration prevents the holding from expanding into a blanket declaration that EPA could do nothing about nuclear pollution.

The study lesson is the Court's treatment of an apparently broad definition against an existing specialized scheme and a detailed, contested legislative record. The case supports that method on the question decided; it does not establish that every reference to legislative history can overcome any express statutory definition. The brief treats the decision on its 1976 terms and does not claim a current-law review of later changes in the agencies or nuclear-material definitions.
