# Walden v. Fiore

**571 U.S. 277 (2014) · Supreme Court of the United States · February 25, 2014**

## A Georgia seizure and a Nevada damages action

Gina Fiore and Keith Gipson were professional gamblers traveling from Puerto Rico through Atlanta to Las Vegas in August 2006. Security agents in San Juan found almost $97,000 in their carry-on bags. Fiore explained that they had been gambling and had residences in California and Nevada, although they presented California identification. An official alerted the Drug Enforcement Administration task force in Atlanta about their connecting flight. Anthony Walden, a Covington, Georgia police officer deputized to assist that task force, approached them at the gate. They described the cash as their gambling bankroll and winnings. Walden used a drug-sniffing dog, seized the money, and told them it could be returned if they established a legitimate source. 571 U.S. at 279–280.

After they departed, Walden moved the cash to a secure location and the matter went to DEA headquarters. Their Nevada attorney telephoned him and later supplied documentation about the funds. The plaintiffs alleged that Walden subsequently helped prepare a false probable-cause affidavit in Georgia, misrepresenting the encounter and omitting exculpatory information, and forwarded it to a federal prosecutor in Georgia to support forfeiture. No forfeiture complaint was filed. The DEA returned the money in March 2007. Id. at 280–281.

These assertions were allegations, not findings of misconduct. The affidavit itself was not in the record, and the Court accepted the plaintiffs’ factual allegations because review followed a motion to dismiss. The plaintiffs also alleged that the dog test was inconclusive; the pleadings did not show drugs or residue had been found on the cash. Id. at 280 n.1, 281 n.2.

Fiore and Gipson brought a Nevada federal action seeking damages under *Bivens* for alleged Fourth Amendment violations, including the seizure, continued retention, and false affidavit. The district court dismissed for lack of personal jurisdiction. It reasoned that Georgian conduct did not establish Nevada contacts merely because Walden knew the plaintiffs lived in Nevada and they felt harm there. The court did not decide venue. Id. at 281.

The Ninth Circuit reversed. It assumed the initial search and seizure did not support Nevada jurisdiction, but considered the alleged false-affidavit aspect sufficient because Walden knew the plaintiffs had significant Nevada connections and would suffer foreseeable harm there. A divided panel and subsequent dissenting judges disagreed about that approach and the characterization of a separate continuing-seizure claim. The Supreme Court reviewed both jurisdiction and a venue question, but ultimately resolved only jurisdiction. Id. at 282 & nn.3–5.

## Holding and the governing connection

The unanimous Court held that Nevada lacked personal jurisdiction over Walden on these facts. Under Federal Rule 4(k)(1)(A), the district court’s reach here was tied to that of Nevada’s courts. Nevada’s long-arm law extended to constitutional limits. The question was therefore whether the defendant’s suit-related conduct created the substantial connection with Nevada required for specific jurisdiction. The plaintiffs relied on specific, case-linked jurisdiction, rather than all-purpose jurisdiction. Id. at 283–284 & n.6.

Justice Thomas explained two connected principles. First, the defendant himself must create the relevant forum contacts. The plaintiffs’ connections with a state, however strong, cannot replace that requirement. Second, the connection must be with the forum state itself, rather than merely with a person who happens to reside there. Interactions with the plaintiff can help establish a defendant’s forum connection, and physical presence is unnecessary, but the plaintiff cannot provide the only link. The due process protection centers on the nonresident defendant’s liberty interest. Id. at 284–286.

These requirements also apply to intentional torts. Deliberately injuring a known forum resident is not a separate shortcut around the contacts inquiry; the intentional conduct must create the relevant relationship with the state. Id. at 286.

## Why Calder did not establish jurisdiction here

The Court explained *Calder v. Jones* through its forum connections, rather than a general rule that an intentional wrong follows its victim home. The article in *Calder* drew upon California sources, concerned California activities, and was written for publication to a substantial California readership. Reputational harm required communication to third persons. The article therefore injured the plaintiff’s standing in the California community and connected the defendants’ conduct with California itself. The injury was not simply a private consequence experienced wherever the plaintiff happened to be. Id. at 286–288 & n.7.

Walden’s alleged conduct had no comparable Nevada connection. He approached and searched the plaintiffs and seized their cash in Georgia. The challenged affidavit was prepared and sent to a prosecutor there. He did not travel to Nevada, conduct activities there, or send anything or anyone there. His knowledge of the plaintiffs’ Nevada connections did not turn those Georgian acts into Nevada-directed conduct. The Ninth Circuit had improperly substituted the plaintiffs’ forum connections for his own. Id. at 288–289.

The plaintiffs emphasized that continued retention deprived them of access to their money while they were in Nevada. But that loss could have been experienced wherever they traveled and wanted to use the funds. Nothing independently occurring in Nevada tied the alleged continued seizure to that forum. The location of the plaintiffs when they felt the loss differed from the meaningful state connection created by California publication in *Calder*. Id. at 289–290.

The remaining suggested contacts were also insufficient. The Nevada attorney’s decision to contact Walden in Georgia was another person’s unilateral activity. The allegation that some cash originated in Nevada described a connection Walden had not created; he seized it in Georgia. The DEA’s later return of the money to Nevada was not Walden’s act. Those facts did not supply the missing defendant-created relationship. Id. at 291.

## Questions reserved and the judgment

The Court did not resolve jurisdiction for Internet or other virtual conduct, such as phishing or fraudulent account access. Footnote 9 explained that such cases raised different questions about how electronic conduct creates forum contacts. Here, the location of the physical seizure and alleged affidavit activity was clear. That reservation limits what can be inferred about virtual wrongdoing from this fact pattern. Id. at 290–291 n.9.

Justice Thomas wrote for all nine Justices, with no separate writings. The Court reversed the Ninth Circuit. The result rejected Nevada jurisdiction; it did not determine whether the seizure was lawful, whether a viable damages remedy ultimately existed, or whether the challenged affidavit allegations were true. Because the jurisdictional ruling disposed of the case before it, the Court left the granted venue question unanswered. Id. at 282 n.5, 291.
