# Netsphere, Inc. v. Baron

**703 F.3d 296 (5th Cir. 2012)** — Fifth Circuit, December 18, 2012; twelve consolidated appellate proceedings headed by No. 10-11202, including In re Novo Point, LLC. Judge Southwick wrote for Judges DeMoss, Southwick, and Higginson. Assigned: CivPro physical PDF pages 604–605, printed 1194–1195, receivership note.

## Posture and principal result

The Fifth Circuit reversed the appointment of a receiver over Jeffrey Baron's property and controlled entities, directing vacatur, discharge, and winding up. The district court had abused its discretion by using receivership over property outside the underlying dispute to pay unsecured attorney claims and control vexatious litigation. **Reasonable receivership expenses could nevertheless be charged to existing receivership cash after reconsideration; further asset sales were forbidden.** The opinion separately dismissed a law firm's appeal, denied an IOLTA subpoena challenge as moot, and held a recusal-affidavit issue waived. (301–302, 305–315.)

## Dispute and procedural setting

Baron and Munish Krishan had a joint venture involving internet domain names. At least seven lawsuits and four mediation efforts preceded an April 2009 memorandum settling disputes. Krishan and Netsphere sued in May to enforce that memorandum after alleged breaches by Baron and Ondova Limited Company. A June injunction compelled compliance and was amended to include a $50,000 daily penalty. On the eve of a July contempt hearing, **Ondova**, caused to act by Baron, filed bankruptcy, staying district court litigation. The opinion does not say that Baron personally filed bankruptcy at that point. (302.)

The bankruptcy court appointed Daniel Sherman Chapter 11 trustee. Renewed negotiations produced a July 2010 global settlement dividing domain portfolios: odd-numbered names to Quantec for Baron's benefit, even-numbered names to Krishan-controlled Manila Industries. The agreement defined a future effective settlement date, and the bankruptcy court approved it July 28, ordering execution by July 30. A signed stipulation to dismiss the district suit with prejudice was attached but never entered by that court. (302–303.)

Meanwhile, repeated hiring and firing created unpaid fee claims. At a September hearing the trustee's lawyer presented a chart identifying forty-five unpaid lawyers; former counsel Gerrit Pronske testified about six months of unpaid work and planned offshore transfers. The Village Trust in the Cook Islands owned Novo Point and Quantec, and Baron was its beneficiary. The bankruptcy court ordered a $330,000 security deposit and later recommended Peter Vogel as special master to mediate fee claims. (303–304.)

After another firing, **the bankruptcy trustee** moved November 24, 2010 for a receiver; the district court granted the motion that day without notice to Baron. The appellate court denied an early stay without prejudging the merits. Subsequent orders clarified that Novo Point, LLC and Quantec, LLC were covered; the original order had identified distinct corporations with similar names. At a January 2011 evidentiary hearing and subsequent denial of vacatur, the district court stressed delay, increased costs, unpaid counsel, ignored orders, and possible offshore transfers. It did not find that Baron failed to transfer the domain names required by the settlement. (304–305.)

The receiver-appointment order was appealable under 28 U.S.C. §1292(a)(2). The court acknowledged uncertainty about later receivership orders but its principal ruling largely made those additional questions unnecessary. (305.)

## Governing limits on receivership

Review was for abuse of discretion. Rule 66 supplied limited guidance. Receivership is an extraordinary means of preserving property pending disposition, justified by a clear need to protect an interest in property, inadequate lesser remedies, and benefits exceeding burdens. Recognized examples included foreclosure, trust administration, judgment enforcement, corporate fraud affecting the disputed assets, and government institutions violating constitutional duties. Those examples did not authorize seizing unrelated personal assets merely to secure potential judgments or manage a litigant. (305–307.)

**Preserving jurisdiction:** the All Writs Act permits measures supporting jurisdiction actually exercised, within limits tied to the case and legitimate ends. Here the underlying business was settlement enforcement and domain transfers. The receiver and trustee identified no record evidence that Baron had failed to transfer the names or threatened to move the disputed names beyond reach. The evidence of disruption and intended movement of other assets supported concern about fees, not a threatened loss of the subject property. (307–308.)

**Paying unsecured attorneys:** unsecured contract creditors ordinarily lack a substantive interest in the debtor's property absent applicable legislation. They could file bankruptcy claims or collection suits, as many did. Their claims were not judgments, nor the underlying subject matter. Fredeman, De Beers, and Grupo Mexicano limited freezes of unrelated assets for potential monetary recovery; the court applied those equitable limits to receivership too. It distinguished rescission and restitution in Deckert. Novo Point and Quantec had signed the settlement but were not named parties in the district lawsuit or bankruptcy; their broader assets and Baron's personal property could not be seized on this rationale. (308–310.)

**Controlling litigation:** tax cases involving a specific statutory receivership power supplied no general authority here. Docket control and frustration with ordinary remedies did not create such power. The court identified contempt, fines or imprisonment for violation of a sufficiently specific lawful order, and requiring Baron to keep existing counsel or proceed pro se. It noted an apparent concession that no clear no-hiring order existed, but expressly found resolution of that point unnecessary. The receivership reached phones, computers, mail, home keys, accounts, and requests for medical-care funds—illustrating its extent beyond the dispute. (310–311.)

## Why improper appointment did not eliminate every fee

Proper receivership expenses normally charge the administered property. Improper appointment sometimes supports charging the party responsible, but the controlling allocation here was equitable. The bankruptcy court recommended the appointment, and the trustee sought it accordingly; the panel did not find wrongful provocation, malice, collusion, or bad faith. Vogel's previous special-master role did not prove a violation or collusion: the court accepted the distinction between a party-paid special master and federal or judicial employment under section 958. (311–313.)

The panel found no benefit to Baron, but his own conduct substantially created the expense-producing circumstances. It therefore permitted reasonable expenses to be paid from cash already held, with all fees reconsidered because their prior calculation assumed a proper receivership. Equity might require meaningful reductions; other payments could also be reconsidered. The court fixed no final fee amount or percentage. (313.)

All noncash property was to be released expeditiously under a district-court winding-up schedule. Baron asserted that cash amounted to $1.6 million; that was his stated figure, not an independently fixed appellate award. Additional approved fees could draw only on that cash. Any deficiency would remain unpaid, and **no further domain-name or other asset sales** were authorized. Note 2 made permanent the stay of closings from a domain auction. This did not itself announce a blanket unwinding of every earlier sale. (313–314 & n. 2.)

## Other rulings and exact mandate

The bank had produced counsel's IOLTA records and the receiver had reviewed them. Finding no effectual relief available, the court denied that challenge as moot. On recusal, section 144 requires sufficiently particular material facts indicating personal bias, not merely judicial disagreement. The district court required record citations, allowed replacement affidavits, and received no compliant one; the appellate issue was waived. The panel did not decide that every substantive bias allegation was false. (314–315.)

Note 1 explains the separate dismissal of Carrington, Coleman, Sloman & Blumenthal's appeal: its $224,232.69 fee claim was allocated to the bankruptcy estate, and it conceded appellate jurisdiction was lacking while its reconsideration motion remained pending. The panel found no need to address the remaining carried motions or Novo Point's mandamus petition concerning notices struck for violating the now-vacated receivership. (305 n. 1, 315.)

**Receiver appointment reversed; directions to vacate, discharge, and charge permissible remaining fees to existing cash. CCSB appeal dismissed; subpoena challenge denied as moot; section 144 issue waived.** Any genuinely overlooked motion could be raised on rehearing. No dissent or concurrence appears. (315.)
