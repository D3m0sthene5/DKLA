Citation
A.F.A. Tours, Inc. v. Whitchurch, 937 F.2d 82 (2d Cir. 1991). United States Court of Appeals for the Second
Circuit. Judge Kearse wrote the opinion. Assigned excerpt: casebook, pp. 318–322; amount-in-controversy
notes and problems, pp. 322–324. Full judicial opinion. The historical amount threshold was more than
$50,000, not the present $75,000 threshold.
Parties
A.F.A. Tours, Inc., doing business as Alumni Flights Abroad, was the plaintiff and appellant. It operated a
travel business specializing in deluxe overseas tours. Desmond Whitchurch, its former tour escort who began
offering competing services, was the defendant and appellee. AFA sought damages and an injunction for
alleged misappropriation of confidential business information. The assigned excerpt does not specify the
parties’ particular citizenships; its jurisdictional dispute concerns the amount in controversy, not a contested
citizenship finding. Casebook, pp. 318–319.
Material Facts
AFA arranged deluxe tours for United States residents to destinations including Australia, New Zealand, and
New Guinea. It alleged that it had invested substantial money, time, and labor in developing customer lists,
marketing information, and tour information that it regarded as confidential trade secrets. Whitchurch served
as its exclusive tour escort in those regions from 1972 through 1989 and had access to some of that
information. Casebook, pp. 318–319.
AFA alleged that Whitchurch resigned around October 1989, misappropriated the confidential information,
created a competing tour business, and solicited or intended to solicit AFA’s customers. It requested an
injunction against any use of the information, compensatory damages not yet ascertainable but believed to
exceed $50,000, and punitive damages of at least $250,000. These allegations and requests were not findings
that the information was protected or that any particular damages had been proved. Casebook, p. 319.
At the hearing below, Whitchurch’s lawyer reported that Whitchurch had mailed solicitations to between 100
and 200 former AFA participants and obtained favorable responses from only two. AFA’s lawyer argued that
the first attempt’s limited success did not measure the entire threatened loss: Whitchurch had escorted about
1,500 AFA customers over the years and intended to organize further tours. The record indicated
approximately seven tours per year over seventeen years, usually with ten to fifteen participants. Casebook,
pp. 319–321.
AFA’s lawyer also relied on the high prices of the tours. The court of appeals accepted that the nature and
duration of the trips could support an inference that a participant might pay about $10,000. But the price paid
by a traveler was not the same as the tour operator’s earnings. The court expressly questioned the suggestion
that the operator would earn the whole $10,000 per customer. This distinction between revenue and legally
recoverable loss or profit was central to the amount analysis. Casebook, pp. 321–322.

Procedural History
AFA filed the diversity action in the Southern District of New York. Whitchurch moved for summary
judgment on whether the information qualified as confidential trade secrets. At oral argument on that motion,
the district judge raised the amount in controversy on the court’s own initiative. At the conclusion of the
same hearing, the judge granted summary judgment and dismissed the complaint for lack of subject-matter
jurisdiction, reasoning that AFA could not prove damages exceeding $50,000. Casebook, pp. 318–320.
AFA appealed, arguing that it had not received a reasonable opportunity to make a jurisdictional showing and
that the district court used an incorrect approach to valuing both the damages claim and the requested
injunction. The Second Circuit concluded that the existing record did not establish to a legal certainty that the
amount was insufficient. Full-opinion addition: it vacated the judgment and remanded, also rejecting the
proposed alternative basis for affirmance on the trade-secret merits because material factual disputes
remained. Casebook, pp. 320–322; full opinion, Part II.B and conclusion.
Issue
May a district court dismiss a diversity action for failing the amount-in-controversy requirement when it first
raises the issue at a hearing on a different ground and does not give the plaintiff a reasonable opportunity to
submit supporting evidence? In evaluating the amount, may the court consider only losses already realized,
or must it also consider legally available punitive damages and the value of preventing future harm through
the requested injunction? Casebook, pp. 320–322.
Rule and Holding
Under the St. Paul Mercury standard, the amount the plaintiff claims controls if apparently asserted in good
faith, unless it appears to a legal certainty that the claim is for less than the jurisdictional amount. Before
deciding that the plaintiff cannot meet the minimum, the court must provide an appropriate and reasonable
opportunity to show a good-faith basis for believing recovery above the threshold is reasonably possible.
Casebook, p. 320.
In a trade-secret action, damages may be measured by the plaintiff’s losses or the defendant’s unjustly
obtained profits. Legally available punitive damages can count. An injunction is valued by the right being
protected and the impairment the injunction would prevent, including potential future harm where
appropriate. The district court’s immediate dismissal and narrow valuation were therefore erroneous on this
record. The Second Circuit did not decide that AFA had established liability or had conclusively proved a
particular jurisdictional value. Casebook, pp. 320–322.
Judgment
The judgment was vacated, and the matter was remanded for further proceedings. AFA received another
opportunity to substantiate the jurisdictional amount and pursue its claims; it did not receive a damages
award or an injunction from the court of appeals. Full opinion, conclusion.

Reasoning
A jurisdictional question may be raised by the court, but the plaintiff must have an opportunity to
answer it. The district judge’s obligation to examine subject-matter jurisdiction did not justify deciding a
newly raised factual valuation issue at once. AFA had prepared for a motion about the confidentiality of its
information, not a separate evidentiary dispute over jurisdictional value. The lack of a developed valuation
record could not fairly be held against it without a reasonable opportunity to supply one. Casebook, pp.
320–321.
The standard was legal certainty, not a prediction of the most likely verdict. AFA had not yet shown
actual losses approaching $50,000. That fact did not establish that the full value of the legally available relief
necessarily fell below the threshold. A judge’s skepticism about the likely recovery is not the same as a
conclusion that a larger claim is legally impossible. At the same time, the good-faith allegation did not
eliminate the plaintiff’s obligation to substantiate jurisdiction when the issue was properly presented.
Casebook, pp. 320–322.
The record already suggested a materially larger possible customer base. Whitchurch’s years of
escorting AFA tours, the approximate number of trips, and the typical group size supported AFA’s estimate
of roughly 1,500 customers. The first solicitation’s two favorable responses could not conclusively establish
the results of every future solicitation. AFA’s evidence also indicated repeat customers, undermining an
assumption that nobody would return to the region or take another tour. Casebook, p. 321.
The court separated gross receipts from profits and used a conditional illustration. A $10,000 tour price
did not mean $10,000 in profit. The court instead illustrated a possible valuation: if the operator earned
seventeen percent of a $10,000 trip, and if thirty former AFA customers—two percent of 1,500—were
diverted, the resulting profit would be $51,000. The arithmetic was 30 × $10,000 × 0.17. Neither the margin
nor the eventual diversion was found as a proved fact. The illustration showed why the record did not
establish a legally certain ceiling below $50,000. Casebook, pp. 321–322.
The injunction covered more than the defendant’s first competing tour. AFA requested protection
against any use of the information, not merely one solicitation. The alleged harm could include sale or
disclosure to other operators better able to exploit the customer information. Preventing that future
impairment might have substantial value even if Whitchurch’s own first effort produced little business. The
district court therefore could not value the requested protection solely by the small loss already realized.
Casebook, p. 322.
Punitive damages were a possible component, not an automatic addition. The opinion said that
controlling New York law apparently permitted punitive damages for sufficiently gross and wanton conduct
in a trade-secret case. Whether AFA could prove such conduct remained open, but the existing record did not
foreclose it. A demand for $250,000 did not guarantee an award or excuse legal scrutiny; it identified a
potentially available form of relief the district court had not properly accounted for. Casebook, pp. 320, 322.
Full-opinion addition: the merits offered no alternative basis for affirmance. AFA submitted affidavits
describing its development and protection of the list; Whitchurch disputed confidentiality. Drawing
reasonable inferences for AFA as the party opposing summary judgment, the court found genuine factual
disputes. It did not declare every customer list a trade secret. Full opinion, Part II.B.

Notes
The historical threshold is part of the holding’s setting. The court’s $51,000 illustration exceeded the
then-applicable $50,000 threshold. It would not, by itself, exceed today’s $75,000 threshold. A current
hypothetical using the same figures would need a different or additional nonduplicative basis for valuation.
Do not update the dollar threshold inside the historical case while leaving its arithmetic unexplained.
Casebook, pp. 321–322; § 1332(a).
Distinct remedies are not necessarily cumulative dollars. The opinion discusses plaintiff loss, defendant
gain, punitive relief, and injunctive protection as legally relevant valuation possibilities. It does not authorize
double counting the same economic injury by adding equivalent measures together. A sound application asks
what relief the substantive law permits and whether the proposed components represent distinct recoverable
interests. Casebook, pp. 320–322; application.
Jurisdictional sufficiency is not merits success. AFA could have enough at stake to invoke federal
jurisdiction and ultimately lose because the information was not protected, because misappropriation was not
proved, or because damages were not established. Lacks likewise distinguishes a court’s competence from
the claimant’s entitlement to relief. The comparison is useful without making the two opinions’ jurisdictional
rules identical. Casebook, pp. 298–300, 320–322.
Valuation perspective is not uniform in every circuit. The assigned note reports disagreement over
whether nonmonetary relief is valued only from the plaintiff’s perspective or may also be valued by the cost
to the defendant. A.F.A. Tours explains the plaintiff-protection approach in the case before it. It should not be
presented as resolving every circuit’s approach to injunction valuation. Casebook, p. 324.
