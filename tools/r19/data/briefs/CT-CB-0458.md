# Ortelere v. Teachers' Retirement Board
25 N.Y.2d 196, 250 N.E.2d 460 (N.Y. 1969) | Contracts | Mental illness, volitional incapacity, and retirement elections

## A retirement election after a nervous breakdown

Grace Ortelere taught in New York City's public schools from 1924 and participated in the teachers' retirement system for more than forty years. In 1958 she selected an option paying a smaller retirement allowance during her lifetime but leaving any unexhausted reserve to her husband at death. In 1960 she also designated him to receive service death benefits if she died before retirement. These were distinct elections governing different contingencies, rather than a single policy guaranteeing every kind of survivor payment. (25 N.Y.2d at 199–200.)

In March 1964, when she was sixty, she suffered a nervous breakdown and took leave from teaching. Her psychiatrist diagnosed involutional psychosis of the melancholia type. Tranquilizer and shock treatments produced some improvement, but they stopped after about six weeks because cerebral arteriosclerosis was suspected and later confirmed. Psychiatric appointments continued monthly. Her husband abandoned an electrician's job paying $222 weekly to care for her full time. She was depressed, unable to care for herself, and left home only with him. They owned a house worth about $20,000, savings of $8,000, and farm land worth approximately $5,000; retirement benefits were consequently central to supporting either spouse. (Id. at 200–201.)

On February 8, 1965, just after her leave expired, Grace wrote the retirement board asking eight detailed questions about available retirement arrangements. The letter sought calculations involving different beneficiaries, borrowing, annuity and pension components, and service credit. On February 11 she submitted an application, effective the next day, choosing the maximum allowance for her own lifetime and no survivor option. That choice revoked the earlier election preserving a reserve for her husband. She also borrowed the maximum permitted sum, $8,760. After the withdrawal, the reserve was $62,165. The maximum allowance was approximately $450 monthly, compared with $375 under the earlier option: the additional $75 was the price of eliminating the survivor interest. The board's chief clerk said he answered her questions orally on February 11; a detailed written response followed on February 15, when a change was technically too late. (Id. at 200–201.)

Her husband accompanied her but did not know the purpose of the visit. He testified that he avoided questioning her because she might become hysterical and interpret it as scolding. On March 28 she collapsed and was hospitalized after an aneurysm. She died ten days later, less than two months after the election. The full opinion reports the cause of death as cerebral thrombosis due to hypertensive heart disease. The assigned editorial introduction compresses this medical history as death from cerebral arteriosclerosis; the fuller record should govern a precise account. Under the new election, no remaining reserve passed to her husband. (Id. at 199–201.)

## Conflicting evidence and the procedural posture

Her husband, acting as executor, sought to set aside the February application for mental incompetence. Following a nonjury trial, Supreme Court found her incompetent when she acted and declared the application ineffective. A divided Appellate Division reversed, holding that the evidence was legally insufficient under the prevailing test of capacity for this transaction. The Court of Appeals therefore had to decide whether that test improperly excluded incapacity despite cognitive understanding. It was reviewing a judgment after trial, rather than assuming the complaint's allegations on a motion to dismiss. (Id. at 199.)

The psychiatric diagnosis was undisputed, and the majority accepted that Grace possessed cognitive awareness when she made the selection. Her treating psychiatrist nevertheless testified that she had never been mentally competent during treatment and could not make decisions, large or small. He described patients with involutional melancholia as unable to decide even whether to get out of bed or prepare toast. A Board of Education internist who examined her on February 2 thought she appeared rational and had recovered from depression, but requested the psychiatrist's report before approving a return to teaching. On February 24 the education board's medical division also requested an examination by its own panel psychiatrist. The majority distinguished an apparent capacity to understand from an ability to act voluntarily and reasonably under serious mental illness. (Id. at 199, 201–202.)

## A cognitive test did not exhaust contractual incapacity

Judge Breitel began with the rule that contracts of a mentally incompetent person not previously adjudicated insane are voidable. Even performance need not preclude avoidance when the parties can be restored to their former positions. The traditional cognitive inquiry asked whether the person understood the nature of the transaction; related doctrines accounted for inability to make a rational judgment or transaction-specific delusions. But these formulations could leave a person able to comprehend an agreement yet unable to control conduct because of mental disease without protection. (Id. at 202–203.)

The majority viewed that gap as a consequence of outdated assumptions that mental illness impaired all faculties together. Modern psychiatric understanding distinguished intellectual comprehension from volitional and affective disruption. Developments in criminal responsibility illustrated the need to reassess an exclusively cognitive model, although the court expressly recognized that civil and criminal policies differed. The relevant civil issue was whether a serious psychosis could make a retirement selection involuntary even when the member understood its financial mechanics. (Id. at 203–204.)

The court adopted the approach expressed in the tentative Restatement (Second) of Contracts, section 18C: contractual duties are voidable when mental illness or defect makes a person unable to act reasonably in relation to the transaction and the other party has reason to know of the condition. The assigned casebook identifies that provision as later renumbered section 15. The awareness requirement preserved protection for parties bargaining in good faith while allowing relief from conduct that was an uncontrolled product of mental illness. The rule therefore did not equate an economically unfortunate choice with incompetence. It required the specified impairment and the counterparty's reason to know. (Id. at 204–205.)

The majority also addressed the equitable qualification omitted from the abbreviated assigned passage. When an agreement is fair, the other party lacks knowledge of the illness, and performance or changed circumstances would make avoidance inequitable, the tentative Restatement terminates the avoidance power to that extent and permits relief on equitable terms. This qualification mattered because contract stability and innocent expectations remained part of the governing balance. The case did not announce that every mentally ill person's completed transaction could simply be undone. (Id. at 205.)

## Applying the standard required a new trial

The retirement system was, or should have been, aware of Grace's condition through her medical leave and the Board of Education's involvement with psychiatrists. The majority regarded the knowledge condition as satisfied. It also found no significant change of position beyond the ordinary actuarial consequences of selecting benefits. A public retirement system's protective relationship with its members differed from an ordinary commercial bargain. Allowing a single act attributable to known psychosis to defeat forty years of participation, without substantial prejudice to the system, warranted examination under the broader rule. (Id. at 205–206.)

The court acknowledged the board's legitimate concern about retrospective selection. Any retirement system depends on prospective choices and mortality experience; generally allowing beneficiaries to change an election after death could undermine its soundness. The majority treated this as a narrower case of a known serious illness allegedly producing the election, with no demonstrated harmful change of position beyond that actuarial consideration. It did not grant a general post-death right to choose whichever benefit proved best. (Id. at 198–199, 205–206.)

The evidence could support a finding that the election resulted solely from serious psychosis. In the majority's view, opting for no survivor payment despite the family circumstances, continuing psychiatric care, and physical illness was sufficiently unwise to permit that inference. It insisted on illness at least as serious as medically classified psychosis, warning against turning ordinary psychological difficulties into grounds to attack almost any contract. Nevertheless, neither the original trial findings nor all the testimony had squarely applied the new standard. The proper remedy was a new trial under that standard, rather than automatic reinstatement of the executor's earlier judgment. (Id. at 206.)

## Jasen's dissent: a rational financial decision and an uncertain rule

Judge Jasen, joined by Judge Scileppi, would have affirmed. He assigned the burden of proving incapacity to the party challenging an election made without any prior adjudication of incompetence. Grace's detailed letter demonstrated command of the system and an ability to compare options; understanding the questions strongly supported the ability to understand the answers. On his reading, two retired spouses needed more present income, and the extra $75 represented a twenty-percent increase. The maximum loan likewise reflected attention to their financial needs. Nothing showed she knew her life expectancy was shortened. A choice that later deprived her husband of a reserve could thus have been both rational and necessary when made. (Id. at 206–208.)

The dissent also disputed whether psychiatry supplied a workable replacement for the existing legal line. Capacity was a legal judgment informed by medical evidence, and the familiar understanding-and-appreciation test already admitted a broad range of psychiatric proof. Juries could apply that test to observed conduct without a fixed diagnosis-to-incapacity formula. Jasen feared that expanding avoidance to people who understood their actions but could not exercise self-discipline would invite frivolous psychological challenges and undermine the expectations of those dealing with apparently rational counterparts. The disagreement concerned both the inference warranted by this record and the consequences of the majority's standard. (Id. at 208–210.)

## Disposition, alignment, and source scope

The Court of Appeals reversed the Appellate Division without costs and granted a new trial. It did not itself restore the earlier survivor election or enter a final monetary judgment against the board. Breitel wrote the majority, joined by Chief Judge Fuld and Judges Burke and Bergan. Jasen dissented, joined by Scileppi. The reported alignment is four to two; the source does not list a seventh participating judge. (Id. at 210.)
