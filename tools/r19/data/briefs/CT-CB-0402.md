# Central Ceilings, Inc. v. National Amusements, Inc.
70 Mass. App. Ct. 172, 873 N.E.2d 754 (2007) | Contracts

## The owner needed accelerated work; the subcontractor needed payment
National Amusements owned a new theater complex under construction in Lawrence, Massachusetts. It hired Old Colony as general contractor in March 2000, and Old Colony accepted Central Ceilings' bid for drywall, acoustical, carpentry, and hardware work. Groundwater problems required structural redesign and undermined the original June 28 completion target. With revised plans available in June, National set a September 3 target so it could open for the lucrative Labor Day weekend and before a competing theater in the area. The schedule was exceptionally tight. 70 Mass. App. Ct. at 173–174.

Old Colony simultaneously had serious cash-flow problems. It had not billed National promptly and owed subcontractors large amounts on prior projects; by June it owed Central more than $1 million for earlier work. Checks had been returned unpaid, and subcontractors were reluctant to resume. The revised project scope would also require Central to increase manpower substantially to achieve the desired completion date. Central's manager, Joseph McPherson, sought assurances from National through Old Colony's project manager. Id. at 174.

At a meeting shortly before July 4, McPherson asked National's vice-president of construction, Peter Brady, to guarantee that Central would be funded. McPherson testified that Brady agreed National would pay half the amount due on the Monday after Labor Day and the rest ten days later. The payment undertaking was not conditional on a prior Old Colony default. Central would continue under Old Colony's direction. It resumed work the next day and substantially completed its work by August 25. The testimony was contested: Old Colony's manager said Brady promised only that National would not delay Old Colony's payments, and another footnote records McPherson's qualification concerning whether he had flatly refused all work without a guarantee. The appellate narrative reports evidence supporting the verdict rather than unanimous testimony about the meeting. Id. at 174–175 & nn.3–5.

National issued a joint $679,949.75 check in November, which Old Colony endorsed to Central in return for a lien waiver. Central asserted an unpaid balance of $593,237.25. National stopped paying after learning it owed Old Colony nothing further. During reconciliation, it also discovered a scheme in which Brady and Old Colony's president had inflated contract prices and split the excess. The existence of that scheme did not itself answer whether Brady had authority to make the payment promise at issue or whether the promise bound National. Id. at 175–176 & n.6.

## Trial findings and appellate posture
Central first sued Old Colony for breach and sought a lien on National's property. After obtaining a default judgment against Old Colony, it added contract, quantum-meruit, and Chapter 93A claims against National. Other claims and the lien request were dismissed at different stages, leaving the oral-contract claim for trial. A jury answered special questions finding a payment promise, Brady's actual or apparent authority, consideration, a main purpose of conferring a benefit on National to which it was not already entitled, breach, and damages of $593,237.25. Id. at 176–177.

The trial court denied National's motions for a directed verdict, judgment notwithstanding the verdict, and a new trial. The appeal came through a Rule 54(b) certification; Old Colony was not a party to it, and cross-claims between Old Colony and National apparently remained. Judge Perretta wrote for Judges Smith and Vuono without a separate opinion. The Appeals Court examined whether the evidence warranted the jury's findings and rejected National's asserted legal grounds for overturning them. Id. at 172–173, 176–177 & n.7.

Although the special questions used “guaranty,” the court explained that the evidence supported an unconditional payment undertaking on a schedule, rather than payment only if Old Colony failed first. National made no adequate appellate argument requiring a different characterization. The court analyzed the promise as proved and argued, while also addressing the statutory exception that made the main-purpose issue decisive. Id. at 176–177 n.8.

## The suretyship clause and the main-purpose exception
General Laws chapter 259, § 1, Second required a signed writing for a special promise to answer for another's debt, default, or misdoings. National argued that the promise concerned Old Colony's debt and was unenforceable because it was oral. The court applied the established leading-object or main-purpose exception. Looking to the entire transaction, a promise primarily aimed at acquiring a benefit for the promisor can be treated as its own undertaking even though performance also satisfies a third party's obligation. Id. at 177–178.

National argued that Central had never released Old Colony, so the promise could escape the statute only through a novation substituting National as debtor. The court distinguished those routes. Novation eliminates the original debtor's liability and makes the new undertaking original. The main-purpose exception, however, addresses a third-party debt that remains in existence and will be satisfied by the new promisor's performance. Release of Old Colony was therefore not a prerequisite to applying that exception. Id. at 179–181.

National cited earlier cases treating owners' promises to pay contractors as within the statute even though completion benefited the owners. The court did not conclude that every owner benefit was sufficient. Instead, it distinguished that line of authority from decisions recognizing the main-purpose exception and considered the concrete benefit National sought here. National wanted an early opening for holiday revenues and competitive advantage. Central was a core subcontractor responsible for essential work, was ready to build, and was among the few firms able to deliver the needed work within the compressed timetable. The evidence warranted a finding that securing its continued and expedited performance was National's primary object. Satisfying Old Colony's debt was incidental to that object. Id. at 180–182.

A full-opinion footnote addressed National's claim that Central's scheduling obligation already entitled National to this performance. National had not identified the relevant contract language or established that duties owed by Central to Old Colony ran directly in National's favor. Merely referring to incorporated scheduling terms did not prove that National already possessed the claimed benefit. Id. at n.13. That distinction connects the statutory inquiry to the actual contractual relationships rather than simply assuming an owner benefits from every subcontract.

## Ordinary proof and the instruction that favored National
National contended that Central had to prove the oral undertaking by clear and persuasive evidence. The court held that the ordinary preponderance standard applied: Central had to show a statutory memorandum or an undertaking outside the statute was more probably established than not. Ryan v. Ryan's stronger-proof requirement concerned a specialized legal-malpractice situation involving an alleged attorney-client relationship and advice about a testamentary promise; it did not change the general contract rule. Id. at 178–179.

The trial judge had in fact used a clear-and-persuasive formulation in instructing the jury on main purpose. The full opinion identifies that as error but harmless to National: the higher burden favored it, and Central nevertheless prevailed. Thus the appellate holding is not that this instruction stated the correct standard, but that National could not obtain reversal from an erroneous instruction that benefited it. Id. at n.12.

## Consideration despite a duty to Old Colony
National separately argued that Central already owed performance to Old Colony and therefore furnished no consideration for National's new promise. Massachusetts precedent recognized that when a party hesitates or refuses to perform for one promisee, a third party who benefits may promise payment to induce that performance, and the requested performance can support the third party's undertaking. A duty to Old Colony did not automatically make the benefit to National legally worthless. Id. at 182–183.

The jury could find that National bargained for Central's decision to proceed and accelerate instead of repudiating its subcontract obligations. National's direct economic interest in timely completion and the evidence of the requested compressed schedule supported consideration. This was an application of the rule concerning an undertaking to a third-party promisor, alongside evidence of the particular performance National sought; it was not a general abolition of the preexisting-duty doctrine.

## The additional evidentiary objection and the judgment
National also challenged the reading of deposition passages in which Brady invoked his privilege against self-incrimination. The court declined to consider the inadequately argued and unpreserved claim. The judge repeatedly explained that questions were not evidence, said she would give no adverse-inference instruction, and instructed against speculation. National did not seek striking the material or press for a special instruction after that decision and did not object to the final instructions. The court also concluded that it would see no error in denying the posttrial motions on that record even if it reached the argument. It did not announce that every invocation may always be placed before every civil jury. Id. at 183–184.

The judgment for Central was affirmed, preserving the $593,237.25 recovery against National. Central's conditional cross-appeal did not require consideration because the judgment stood. Id. at 184 & n.1. The case shows why a promise involving another's debt can still be enforced when its leading object is the promisor's own business benefit, while requiring proof of the undertaking, authority, consideration, and the particular benefit rather than relying only on the word “guarantee.”
