# Vastoler v. American Can Co.
700 F.2d 916 (3d Cir. 1983) | Contracts

## Court and result
Judge A. Leon Higginbotham, Jr. wrote the Third Circuit's February 23, 1983 opinion on a panel with Judges Gibbons and Becker. Becker joined with a separate concurrence and one note. The court sustained rejection of the employee's claim under the pension plan's express terms but reversed summary judgment on promissory estoppel and remanded for trial. The record permitted a finding of substantial detrimental reliance on an alleged pension promise, including forgone alternatives and potentially burdensome supervisory responsibilities. It did not establish ultimate liability or award increased benefits. (916–918, 920; complete archived judicial reproduction, lines 55–62, 76–77, 100–112. Subsequent line locators identify that reproduction, independently compared with the original report.)

## Interrupted service and the promotion bargain
Soloman Vastoler worked for American Can during several periods from 1937 until retiring at fifty-nine on December 31, 1978. Apart from three years of military service, he was an hourly employee from 1937 to 1946, 1947 to 1952, and 1958 to 1963. In April 1963 the company sought to promote him from lithographer to salaried supervisor. According to the record viewed favorably to him, he reluctantly accepted primarily on a promise of pension credit for past service, and secondarily on credit for other fringe benefits. Under the plan's terms, service before 1958 otherwise would not count. (Lines 63–65.)

The company disputed the pension promise. An internal memorandum by plant manager Joe Choborda, who negotiated the promotion, supported Vastoler's account of service-credit discussions. It said the company had indicated sixteen accredited years, overlooking a service break and the actual credit of five years and six months. It discussed vacation and survivorship benefits and acknowledged that the company had needed to sell him on the benefits because he initially gained no financial advantage from promotion. The company granted past-service credit for other benefits but not the pension. The memorandum supported an inference; it was not an appellate finding conclusively resolving the disputed pension promise. (Lines 67–71.)

Under a 1958 start date, benefits were $656.14 monthly until sixty-two and $580.20 thereafter. The claimed 1937 credit would produce $875.23 monthly from retirement, a difference of $219.09 initially and $367.03 at and after sixty-two. Those figures describe competing calculations, not an appellate award. (Line 66.)

## Two theories and the employer's summary judgment
Vastoler first asserted entitlement under the plan itself. His 1952 resignation had canceled pre-1958 accredited service under its express provisions. The appellate court affirmed rejection of that claim. The separate promissory-estoppel claim asserted that the company's promise induced the promotion and a sufficiently detrimental change of position to require honoring it. For summary judgment, the district court assumed the promise and absence of notice that pension credit began in July 1958 rather than March 1937. It nevertheless found no substantial detriment. (Lines 71, 76–79.)

The district court compared fifteen years of financial advantages with the pension shortfall. Benefits included extra paid leave, stock purchases, better insurance, a $2,000 retirement payment, company pension contributions, higher compensation, freedom from union dues, and retention and raises while hourly workers faced layoffs and plant closures. It concluded that these gains exceeded the lost pension credit and eliminated detriment. On review, Rule 56 required favorable inferences for the employee and left genuine material disputes for trial. (Lines 72–75, 80–86.)

## The possible alternatives and nonfinancial burdens
The majority identified an unsupported assumption: without promotion, Vastoler necessarily would have remained an hourly employee at American Can. Possible alternatives extended beyond that company. He testified that pension benefits induced him to remain and said that knowledge of the lower pension would have led him to increase contributions to his Amalgamated Lithographers of America pension. Forgone employment opportunities and retirement planning supported a possible reliance loss beyond comparison with his former job. Their existence and significance remained trial issues. (Lines 87–90.)

The district court had also ignored potential stress accompanying supervision. Vastoler moved from responsibility for individual tasks to responsibility for approximately fifty workers, including assignments, discipline, and possible recommendations for dismissal or layoffs. A jury could find additional emotional burdens. The majority rejected a purely financial measure of those consequences; it did not declare that promotion inherently establishes substantial detriment. (Lines 91–95.)

Even financial circumstances were disputed. Choborda's memorandum acknowledged no initial financial improvement, and Vastoler testified that promotion would disadvantage him without past-service credit. Later incidental benefits did not necessarily substitute for the undertaking allegedly inducing the change. Whether intervening compensation eliminated detriment was for the factfinder. Summary judgment could not resolve the estoppel claim on this record. (Lines 96–100.)

## Becker's qualifications
Becker agreed that evidence of disadvantage justified trial, particularly alleged forgone pension contributions and continued employment in reliance on the pension promise, as his note explains. He stressed three limits. First, the majority's forceful language about the employer reneging did not prejudge ultimate validity: the employee's account was accepted for reviewing summary judgment. Second, failure to deliver the promised pension credit could not itself supply the substantial reliance detriment required by estoppel. Breach of a promise and detrimental reliance on it were distinct. (Lines 102–105; concurrence n.1.)

Third, Becker understood applicable New Jersey law in this diversity case probably to permit stress and anxiety as relevant detriment. If proved, those burdens were one factor to consider with financial circumstances, rather than an automatic independent entitlement. He joined the opinion with these qualifications. The concurrence prevents reading the majority as dispensing with reliance or conclusively finding the employee's account true. (Lines 106–112.)
