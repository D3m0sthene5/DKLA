# Steinberg v. Chicago Medical School
69 Ill. 2d 320, 371 N.E.2d 634 (Dec. 12, 1977) | Contracts

## Decision and posture

The Illinois Supreme Court held that rejected applicants adequately alleged both breach of an agreement to evaluate applications under published criteria and common-law fraud. It also rejected an appellate restriction of the proposed class to a single application year. The court affirmed dismissal of the statutory trade-practice and separately pleaded unjust-enrichment counts, affirmed in part and reversed in part both lower judgments, and remanded for further proceedings, including class-related preliminary determinations. Dooley J. wrote the sole reported opinion; no numerical vote or separate writing is supplied. This was review of a dismissal for failure to state a claim, not a finding that the medical school had actually committed the alleged admissions misconduct. (327–329, 342–343.)

## Allegations and procedural history

Robert Steinberg received the school’s catalog, applied for the 1974–75 academic year, paid the required $15 fee, and was rejected. The bulletin said selection rested on scholarship, character, and motivation, without regard to race, creed, or sex; it specified academic achievement, admission-test results, instructors’ appraisals, and a possible personal interview as evaluation criteria. Steinberg alleged that the school instead selected primarily according to financial contributions pledged or paid by applicants or their families. He sought to proceed for similarly situated applicants. (327–329.)

The complaint contained four counts: breach of contract, statutory consumer-fraud and deceptive-trade-practice violations, common-law fraud, and unjust enrichment. The circuit court dismissed all four. The appellate court restored the contract count, permitted a class restricted to applicants in Steinberg’s year, and affirmed dismissal of the others. The supreme court examined the sufficiency of the pleaded facts, accepting well-pleaded allegations as true for that purpose. The school’s actual practices and any applicant’s entitlement to ultimate relief remained matters for later proceedings. (327–329.)

## Formation and scope of the alleged contract

The court accepted a formation theory more precise than simply calling the catalog an offer of admission. The brochure invited an offer to apply on the published evaluation terms. Tendering an application and the $15 fee made that offer; the school’s acceptance of both accepted the applicant’s proposal. The fee provided legally sufficient consideration for evaluating the application as represented. The court analogized the sequence to a merchant’s invitation to deal on advertised terms followed by the customer’s offer and the merchant’s receipt of payment. It acknowledged that some advertisements can themselves be offers, but that was not the theory used here. (329–330.)

The school’s insistence on a subjective meeting of minds did not defeat the complaint. Observable conduct can manifest assent even where a party denies an internal intention to contract. On the allegations, receiving the application and fee under the stated process manifested an agreement to use the described criteria. Crucially, Steinberg did not demand admission simply because he met listed qualifications. The promised service was evaluation under those qualifications. The court distinguished Tinkoff, where the university reserved a right to reject for any reason and the applicant sought to compel admission. (330–332.)

The contract holding was deliberately limited: the complaint alleged facts that could establish an agreement to appraise applications under represented criteria. The court did not decide that every university catalog creates an enforceable admission promise, award a place to Steinberg, or adjudicate breach on evidence. (331–332.)

## Fraud and the dismissed counts

The common-law fraud allegations identified knowingly false evaluation representations, an intent to induce applications and fees, reliance, and resulting injury. Although a statement about future conduct ordinarily does not support fraud under the rule the court recited, the court recognized an exception where a false future promise is itself alleged to be the means of a fraudulent scheme. The alleged use of a misleading bulletin to obtain application fees fit that exception at the pleading stage. The opinion also quoted evidence discussed in the earlier DeMarco litigation about contributions associated with entering students; that quotation was not a new trial finding about Steinberg’s individual application. (332–334.)

Count II remained dismissed because, under the court’s interpretation of the then applicable statutes, these applicants were not purchasers of merchandise within the consumer definition and the deceptive-trade-practice statute was inapplicable. Count IV also remained dismissed: no fiduciary relationship supported a constructive trust, and the actual-fraud allegations were contained in count III. The court nevertheless discussed an implied-in-law obligation as a possible alternative if a contract in fact could not ultimately be proved. That discussion did not reverse its express disposition of the separately pleaded count IV. (328, 334, 343.)

## Class treatment

During the litigation Illinois enacted Civil Practice Act § 57.2. Because the class statute was procedural, the court applied it to the pending action rather than resolve every conflict in older common-law class doctrine. It required impracticability of joinder, common questions predominating over individual questions, fair and adequate representation, and an appropriate method of fair and efficient adjudication. The modest individual claim and shared alleged evaluation practice supported class treatment; a segregated common fund was no longer required. (334–340.)

Fraud did not automatically prevent a class action, nor did possible individual issues require immediate dismissal. The court discussed separate trials and subclasses as ways to handle such questions, drawing illustrations from federal securities cases without prescribing that every reliance presumption in those cases must apply here. Due process required adequate representation, including suitable counsel, and remained subject to preliminary examination by the circuit court. (338–342.)

The complaint was broad enough to reach applicants outside 1974–75 who paid fees in reliance on a brochure containing the alleged misrepresentations. The appellate court therefore should not have confined the class to Steinberg’s year. The court also stated that commencing the class action suspended the applicable limitations period for asserted class members who would have been parties had it continued as a class action. It directed the circuit court to determine proper class membership, adequacy of representation, the necessity and character of notice, and other appropriate pretrial matters. This was not a final determination that every past applicant belonged in the class or deserved recovery. (342–343.)

## Exact result and reading scope

The contract count’s restoration stood; the fraud count was restored; the dismissals of counts II and IV stood; and the premature temporal restriction of the class was removed. Both the appellate and circuit judgments were affirmed in part and reversed in part, with remand to the circuit court. (343.)
