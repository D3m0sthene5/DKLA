# Housing Authority for La Salle County v. Young Men’s Christian Association of Ottawa

**101 Ill.2d 246, 461 N.E.2d 959 (1984) — March 23, 1984.** Illinois Supreme Court; Justice Simon wrote the sole reported opinion. Assigned source: CivPro physical page 664, printed pages 1316–1317, complete account before Notes and Questions.

## Two disputes over the same title

The Housing Authority sought to quiet title to land and enjoin the YMCA from building a parking lot partly on that disputed parcel. The parties derived their asserted rights from different predecessors: Charles and Ella Deenis for the authority, and First Trust Company for the YMCA. The decisive earlier proceeding was the federal government’s 1932 condemnation action, *United States v. Wirtz*. The government sought flood-plain easements because locks and dams on the Illinois River could change the Fox River’s elevation and flood adjoining property. (248–250.)

The condemnation petition identified confused titles and competing claims, naming 70 persons and corporations. It expressly juxtaposed First Trust’s and Ella Deenis’s deed-based claims to the relevant tracts and referred to a tax lien on the property claimed by both. The Deenises received summons but failed to appear. The district court took the petition as confessed against them concerning their interests in the specified tracts. On the same day it separately found First Trust owned the described land in fee simple and awarded First Trust $650 for the flood easement. A surveyor’s affidavit in the present record established that the earlier judgment’s legal description covered all the presently disputed land. (250–251.)

The current circuit court granted summary judgment to the YMCA on collateral-estoppel grounds. The appellate court affirmed, with one dissent there. The Illinois Supreme Court affirmed the judgments but rejected both parties’ characterization of the governing doctrine. It applied **claim preclusion**, not issue preclusion. No separate Supreme Court writing or numerical voting tally is reported. (249, 252–256.)

## Why claim identity, rather than actual issue litigation, controlled

Claim preclusion bars the same claim between parties or privies after a competent court’s final merits judgment. It reaches matters offered and matters that could have been offered to sustain or defeat that claim. Issue preclusion applies across different causes of action only to controlling questions actually litigated and determined. The difference mattered because the original judgment was entered after a default, not an adversarial trial of the Deenises’ title claim. (251–252.)

Both parties described the original cause as condemnation of an easement and the present cause as quiet title. The court looked within the condemnation proceeding. Federal courts must determine who owns condemned land to distribute compensation correctly and protect the government from later claims by the true owner. Between rival claimants, the government is essentially a nominal participant: its concern is paying the rightful recipient, not which claimant wins. Their competing title claims therefore function as a quasi-interpleader proceeding within condemnation. (252–254.)

Viewed that way, the earlier case presented the same quiet-title claim as the new case. Both required deciding the true owner of the identical land. Proper service summoned First Trust and the Deenises to contest those interests. The default and fee-ownership judgment resolved that claim. A successor could not reopen it merely by bringing a later suit outside the condemnation proceeding. (254.)

## Default and the incentive argument

The court recognized authorities limiting default judgments’ issue-preclusive effect because issues may not have been actually litigated. That did not eliminate their claim-preclusive effect on the ultimate demand presented by the complaint. This holding consequently does not mean every factual allegation in a default becomes binding in every different future cause of action. The bar here depended on the court’s conclusion that the title claim itself was the same. (254.)

The authority argued that the Deenises lacked adequate incentive to litigate during condemnation. The court answered first that incentive can matter in special issue-preclusion circumstances, but it found no authority making that consideration relevant to claim preclusion here. It also rejected the factual premise. The petition identified the adverse title claims and put the Deenises on notice that ownership—not merely the amount of an easement payment—was at stake. A person wishing to retain a fee interest had reason to appear and establish ownership. (255–256.)

The authority also sought equitable relaxation of preclusion under *Adams v. Pearson*. The court distinguished that unusually compelling, nonrecurring situation. Here equity instead favored stability: the ownership judgment had been publicly recorded for more than fifty years and was available to later purchasers. Treating recorded land-title adjudications as perpetually open would undermine reliance on them. (255–256.)

## Judgment and assignment comparison

The Supreme Court **affirmed**. The 1932 judgment barred ownership claims that the authority or its predecessors had **before the date of that judgment**. That temporal qualification should be retained: the court did not announce that no later independently acquired interest could ever exist. The result upheld summary judgment against the present quiet-title and parking-lot injunction action. (256.)

The full opinion contains no judicial footnotes, separate writings, or appendices. The assignment accurately preserves the distinction between claim and issue preclusion, but its closing locator “253” is too narrow for the quotations: the default discussion appears at **254**, and the incentive discussion at **255–256**. Those passages support the rules actually applied, rather than a general assertion that defaults resolve every issue regardless of incentives. The later textbook hypotheticals ask about different claims and therefore require a fresh claim-versus-issue analysis; they are not additional holdings of this decision.
