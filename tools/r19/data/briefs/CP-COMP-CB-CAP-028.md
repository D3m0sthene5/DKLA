# Allstate Insurance Co. v. Hague

449 U.S. 302 (1981) | Civil Procedure | Supreme Court of the United States

## Identity and assigned scope

No. 79-938, argued October 6, 1980, and decided January 13, 1981. The Court affirmed Minnesota's judgment by five votes to three. Justice Brennan's opinion had four adherents: Brennan, White, Marshall, and Blackmun. Justice Stevens concurred in the judgment on separate reasoning. Justice Powell dissented, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justice Rehnquist. Justice Stewart did not participate. Thus, Brennan's analysis is a plurality analysis. (304, 320, 332.)

The supplied CivPro textbook discusses Hague in Hanson Note 3, PDF 81, printed 106–107, and refers back to it in the choice-of-law discussion at PDF 277, printed 517. Both references concern constitutional limits on selecting substantive law. Personal jurisdiction over Allstate was unquestioned; the Court expressly distinguished that inquiry from choice of law. (317 n. 23.)

## Parties and requested relief

Mrs. Hague, as personal representative of her husband's estate, sought a Minnesota declaration permitting the uninsured-motorist coverage for three automobiles to be combined, or stacked. Ralph Hague had paid separate premiums, including separate uninsured-motorist premiums, on coverages of $15,000 for each automobile. The requested combined coverage was $45,000. This was a dispute over coverage, not a Supreme Court award of $45,000 in damages. Allstate sought application of Wisconsin law, which the Minnesota courts understood to prohibit stacking. (305–307 & nn. 3–6.)

## Material facts and procedural history

Ralph Hague lived with his wife in Hager City, Wisconsin, approximately one and one-half miles from Red Wing, Minnesota. He had worked in Red Wing for fifteen years and commuted there daily. He died from injuries suffered while riding as a motorcycle passenger in Pierce County, Wisconsin. Both vehicle operators, like Hague, were Wisconsin residents; neither carried valid insurance. The accident did not occur during Hague's commute or in Minnesota and did not involve one of his three insured automobiles. The policy's uninsured-motorist protection was nevertheless not limited to occupancy of those automobiles. (305, 314–315 & nn. 18, 21.)

After the accident and before the action began, Mrs. Hague moved to Red Wing. She later remarried a Minnesota resident and moved to Savage. The record did not specify the date of the first move. Proceedings began May 28, 1976; remarriage occurred June 19, 1976. The account should not place that remarriage before commencement. She was also appointed the estate's personal representative in Minnesota. The plurality understood the stipulated circumstances to show bona fide residence without a litigation-driven move. (305, 318–319 & nn. 26–28.)

The Minnesota district court applied Minnesota law and granted summary judgment for Mrs. Hague. The Minnesota Supreme Court affirmed, 289 N.W.2d 43, after using Professor Leflar's five choice-influencing considerations: predictability, interstate order, judicial simplification, forum interests, and the better rule of law. It emphasized Minnesota's stacking rule and, on rehearing, the mobility of automobiles and insurance risks. The United States Supreme Court reviewed only the constitutionality of that choice. It neither endorsed Leflar's method as the preferred conflicts rule nor decided the asserted false-conflict question. (306–307 & nn. 6–9.)

## Issue

Did application of Minnesota's stacking law to this insurance dispute violate the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause or Article IV's Full Faith and Credit Clause, given the accident, original residence, and policy connections to Wisconsin?

## Rule and holding

The judgment applying Minnesota law was constitutionally permissible. Under Brennan's plurality formulation, the state must have a significant contact or significant aggregation of contacts with the parties and occurrence or transaction, creating state interests, so that selecting its law is neither arbitrary nor fundamentally unfair. Minnesota's contacts collectively met that standard here. More than one state's law may constitutionally govern the same dispute. Constitutional permission does not establish which state's law is best under ordinary conflicts principles. (307–313, 320.)

The plurality did not decide whether Hague's Minnesota employment and Allstate's Minnesota presence, separately or together, would suffice without the third contact concerning Mrs. Hague's residence and appointment. That reservation appears expressly in note 29. A post-accident move alone remains insufficient under the plurality's reading of Yates. (311, 319–320 & nn. 28–29.)

## Judgment

The Minnesota Supreme Court's judgment was affirmed. Stevens supplied the fifth vote for affirmance; he did not join Brennan's opinion. (320, 332.)

## Plurality reasoning

The plurality reviewed constitutional choice-of-law decisions through the forum's contacts and resulting interests. Dick involved nominal Texas residence and no meaningful Texas connection to the Mexican insurance transaction; Yates involved a widow's move after the insured's death with no other significant connection to the forum. Conversely, Alaska Packers, Cardillo, and Clay II illustrated constitutionally sufficient connections outside the location of injury or original policy issuance. The plurality treated the old requirement to weigh whether the forum's interest equaled or exceeded another state's as abandoned, while distinguishing rules for recognizing judgments and records from the choice-of-law question before it. (308–313 & nn. 10–17.)

Three Minnesota connections operated together. First, Hague belonged to Minnesota's workforce and commuted there regularly. Minnesota furnished facilities and protections to nonresident employees and had interests in their welfare, the effects on employers, and vindication of an employee's estate. The fact that this particular accident was outside Minnesota and unrelated to the immediate commute did not negate every connection. Wisconsin residence was not constitutionally exclusive. (313–317 & nn. 18–22.)

Second, Allstate was present and doing business in Minnesota. Its familiarity with Minnesota insurance law weakened an assertion of unfair surprise and furnished a regulatory connection when combined with the Minnesota employee and resident representative. The policy offered continental coverage and contained no Wisconsin choice-of-law provision. The plurality did not infer an entitlement to Wisconsin's stacking rule whenever an accident happened there. (317–318 & nn. 23–25.)

Third, Mrs. Hague's bona fide Minnesota residence and appointment as estate representative supported Minnesota's compensatory interest. The plurality expressly considered these alongside the other contacts; it did not turn Yates into permission for a plaintiff to select substantive law by moving after an occurrence. It rejected the inference of forum shopping on the stipulated facts. (318–320 & nn. 26–29.)

## Stevens's concurrence in the judgment

Stevens separated two constitutional inquiries. Full faith and credit protects other states' sovereignty and national unity; due process protects litigants against arbitrary or fundamentally unfair adjudication. Although he regarded Minnesota's choice as unsound under ordinary conflicts law, Allstate had not shown an unjustified infringement of Wisconsin's legitimate interests. The policy contemplated accidents outside Wisconsin and expressed no exclusive Wisconsin understanding concerning stacking. (320–326 & nn. 1–13.)

For due process, a forum's familiarity with its own law and interest in efficient administration gave its choice a presumption of rationality, but could not justify fundamental unfairness. Stacking was common, consistent with separate premiums, nondiscriminatory here, and contrary to no express policy provision. Nationwide coverage and Allstate's Minnesota business made application of another state's law reasonably foreseeable. Express terms could inform expectations, although note 20 explains that such terms are not invariably dispositive. (326–330 & nn. 14–23.)

Stevens regarded the widow's unexpected post-occurrence move as irrelevant to the contracting parties' earlier expectations, and Minnesota employment as adding nothing independently to this fairness analysis. The commute could at most reinforce the expectation already supported by national coverage. He concurred because the application produced no constitutional unfairness or sovereignty violation, not because he accepted the plurality's use of those contacts. Federal constitutional review did not authorize the Court to impose a general federal conflicts rule. (331–332 & nn. 24–25.)

## Powell's dissent

Powell accepted most of the plurality's general constitutional formulation but insisted that contacts be evaluated against the relevant substantive policies. He expressly agreed that no reasonable expectations were frustrated here: national coverage and Hague's location near Minnesota made Minnesota law foreseeable. His objection was therefore not an actual finding of unfair surprise. (332–337 & nn. 1–3.)

Instead, he found no legitimate Minnesota policy sufficiently connected to this stacking question. The widow's post-accident residence did not connect the policy to facts existing when the claim arose. Allstate's operation in all fifty states could not justify regulation of every unrelated insurance contract. Hague's employment did not connect a private, nonemployment Wisconsin accident or the amount of policy benefits to Minnesota's employment policy. The commute and workplace could not simply be counted twice. The relevant inquiry required a reasonable connection to the policy advanced by the particular law, rather than a tally of physical contacts. (337–340 & nn. 4–6.)

## Textbook fit and source scope

The Hanson note correctly identifies Brennan's analysis as a plurality and uses Hague to distinguish legislative choice-of-law power from adjudicative jurisdiction. Its abbreviated narrative does not eliminate the other Minnesota contacts. The Klaxon note's question about forum shopping is editorial, not a holding that Mrs. Hague moved for that purpose or that every forum may apply its own law. Neither reference converts constitutional permission into a mandatory Minnesota conflicts rule.
