# General Telephone Co. of the Southwest v. Falcon

**457 U.S. 147 (1982) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 14, 1982.** Justice Stevens wrote for eight Justices; Chief Justice Burger concurred in part and dissented in part. Civil Procedure: Rule 23 commonality, typicality, and the relationship between an individual employment claim and an employment class. Assigned account: CivPro, physical PDF 427, printed 824; the remainder of Note 5 on printed 825 supplies comparative editorial context.

## What happened and what the parties sought

General Telephone began a minority-recruitment program in 1969. It hired Falcon, a Mexican-American, as a groundman in July of that year. Within a year he advanced to lineman and then lineman-in-charge, but he did not obtain further promotions he sought. In October 1972 he applied for a field-inspector position. The company selected white employees who had less seniority. Falcon charged the employer before the EEOC with national-origin discrimination in promotion and with maintaining discriminatory promotion policies. After receiving permission to sue, he filed a Title VII action in federal district court in April 1975. These were allegations of unlawful discrimination, rather than findings the Supreme Court made independently. **147–151 & nn.1–4.**

Falcon wanted more than a remedy for his own denied promotion. He proposed to represent Mexican-Americans who worked for the company and those who had unsuccessfully sought employment. His complaint broadly attacked employment discrimination but did not allege particular facts about discriminatory hiring. His class proposal expanded through interrogatory answers and a supporting memorandum to encompass employees and potential employees at the company's Irving division. The Fifth Circuit's existing “across-the-board” approach treated an employment-discrimination plaintiff as capable of challenging other discriminatory practices of the same employer on behalf of similarly identified persons. Relying on that approach, the district court certified a Rule 23(b)(2) class without an evidentiary hearing and later rejected the employer's requests to decertify it. **150–152 & nn.3–5.**

## The litigation reached a divided result below

The trial's outcomes exposed the difference between Falcon's individual grievance and the class's claims. The district court found discrimination in his own promotion but rejected his individual hiring claim. For the class, it found discrimination in hiring but rejected the promotion-discrimination claim. Falcon's individual case rested on intentional discrimination; the hiring class principally relied on statistical disparate-impact proof. The resulting findings therefore did not establish a single discriminatory practice connecting all the claims. **151–153, 159.**

The district court ordered changes in the company's affirmative-action program and eventually awarded monetary relief to Falcon and thirteen rejected applicants. The reported total was $67,925.49, with costs and interest; Falcon's award was $1,040.33, and the total class-related award included $28,827.50 in attorney's fees. It would be inaccurate to describe the entire reported sum as damages or backpay paid to class members. The company disputed aspects of the hiring theory and award computation, but those matters were not the issue the Supreme Court resolved here. **152–153 & nn.6–7, 154 n.10.**

Both sides appealed. The Fifth Circuit refused to enlarge the class to all company divisions, whose hiring and management arrangements differed, but upheld the class certification under its across-the-board doctrine. It also addressed Falcon's individual claim and found problems with the statistical basis of the hiring-class decision. Following an earlier Supreme Court remand for reconsideration under *Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, the court of appeals vacated the individual merits ruling and reinstated its certification decision. Thus, by this review, neither Falcon's individual merits nor the class merits supplied a final Supreme Court-approved discrimination judgment. The question was whether this class had been properly certified. **153–155 & nn.8–10.**

## Rule and holding: identity alone does not establish representative fit

A private Title VII plaintiff must satisfy Rule 23 just as a plaintiff invoking another substantive law must. The statute's interest in eradicating discrimination does not dispense with numerosity, commonality, typicality, and adequate representation. An EEOC enforcement action proceeds under the agency's own statutory authority and is different from a private representative's Rule 23 action. The representative's claim must fairly encompass the class claims; being a member of the same racial or national-origin group does not make every grievance against an employer sufficiently related. **155–157.**

The Court rejected the across-the-board assumption used here. There is an inferential gap between evidence that one employee suffered a discriminatory promotion denial and proof that the employer's promotion practices generally discriminate, that a broader policy pervades the division, and that this policy produces hiring discrimination as well. Each additional inference needs support. Winning Falcon's personal claim would not automatically establish any of those propositions. His complaint did not identify common questions whose resolution would adjudicate both his denied-promotion grievance and the rejected applicants' hiring claims. **157–158.**

The rule does not require every class member to have an identical history. It requires an actual connection among the challenged practices and claims. Note 15 illustrates the difference: a biased examination used for both applicants and employees could connect hiring and promotion claims. Significant proof of a general discriminatory policy manifested in both processes in the same manner—potentially through wholly subjective decisions—could also support a broader class. Those are possible evidentiary bridges, not a holding that every subjective employment system automatically warrants certification. **159 n.15.**

## Why certification required closer examination

Commonality and typicality overlap because both test whether the claims are sufficiently interrelated for joint litigation to protect absent members and produce procedural economy. They also overlap with adequate representation, although adequacy raises additional concerns about counsel and conflicts of interest. The employer suggested that adding rejected applicants to the workforce could diminish Falcon's own promotion opportunities; the Court expressly did not decide that conflict argument. **157–158 n.13.**

Here the district court presumed the necessary connection rather than examining it. Trial ultimately illustrated the problem: the different evidence supporting individual intentional-discrimination and class disparate-impact theories produced opposing outcomes for promotion and hiring. The Court used those developments to explain why the assumed connection was weak, while expressly saying that certification should not be judged simply by hindsight. The original error was failing to evaluate whether Falcon actually met Rule 23(a) when seeking representative status. **159–160.**

A court may sometimes decide representative fit from the pleadings. In other cases it must examine beyond them, because certification issues can be intertwined with the underlying facts and law. A certification order remains subject to revision as the litigation develops. That flexibility does not excuse initial noncompliance: the court must conduct a rigorous analysis and be satisfied that Rule 23(a)'s prerequisites have actually been met. Careful class definition also protects absent members who may be bound by an unfavorable judgment, rather than assuming that a broad plaintiff victory will benefit everyone. **160–161 & n.16.**

## Judgment and Burger's separate position

The Court **reversed the court of appeals' judgment affirming certification and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion**. It did not decide Falcon's personal discrimination claim or order that every possible revised class claim be dismissed. **161.**

Chief Justice Burger agreed with the general certification principles but wanted instructions to dismiss the class claim. He saw no common question connecting Falcon's comparative qualifications and the employer's reasons for denying a field-inspector promotion with the statistics underlying the hiring claim. His starred note also emphasized the absence of an allegation that the same decisionmakers made the challenged hiring and promotion decisions. Unlike the majority's more general remand, he would use the developed trial record to end the class litigation. **161–162 & n.*.**

Burger pointed to evidence that Mexican-Americans constituted 7.7% of those hired during 1972–1976, compared with 5.2% of the relevant labor force, and to unchallenged evidence about applications and hiring. In his view the hiring-class claim could not succeed, and more proceedings would prolong litigation without serving class-action economy. Those statistics support his proposed disposition; they are not a separate merits ruling adopted by the Court. **163.**

## Reading the assigned account accurately

The textbook quotes the Court's conceptual-gap analysis and its conclusion that Falcon's complaint supplied no common hiring question. Its ensuing comparison with *Gratz* and *Spano* asks when different application or claim circumstances nevertheless permit common representation. That comparison is editorial context here, rather than additional holdings in Falcon. The decision supplies a disciplined inquiry into the connection among claims, not a categorical bar against combining all hiring and promotion challenges.
