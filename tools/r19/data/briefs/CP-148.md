# Martin v. Wilks

## Citation

Martin v. Wilks, 490 U.S. 755 (1989). Supreme Court of the United States. Argued January 18, 1989; decided June 12, 1989. Chief Justice Rehnquist delivered the Court's opinion, joined by Justices White, O'Connor, Scalia, and Kennedy. Justice Stevens dissented, joined by Justices Brennan, Marshall, and Blackmun. ## Parties

Wilks and other white Birmingham firefighters were plaintiffs in the later employment-discrimination suit and respondents in the Supreme Court. They sued the City of Birmingham and the Jefferson County Personnel Board, alleging that promotion decisions denied them equal employment treatment. Black firefighters and other beneficiaries of earlier consent decrees intervened to defend those decrees. Martin was among the petitioners defending the decrees' effect; the consolidated proceedings also included petitions associated with the city and the Board. 490 U.S. at 758–761. 

The earlier lawsuits had been brought by black individuals, an NAACP branch, and the United States to challenge discriminatory public-employment practices. Those plaintiffs had secured decrees establishing remedial hiring and promotion arrangements. The later white plaintiffs had not been parties to the proceedings in which the decrees were entered. The case therefore concerned the effect of a judgment on outsiders asserting their own rights, rather than an attempt by an original party to evade an unfavorable judgment. Id. at 758–763.

## Material Facts

Beginning in 1974, litigation challenged the city's and Board's hiring and promotion practices under Title VII and other federal law. The proceedings eventually produced consent decrees containing goals for hiring black firefighters and promoting black employees. The decrees represented a judicially approved resolution of the claims before the court, but their implementation affected employment opportunities of people outside the original litigation. Id. at 758–760. 

The Birmingham Firefighters Association appeared as an amicus and objected at the fairness hearing. It and two members later sought intervention, but their application was denied as untimely. A separate group of seven white firefighters sought injunctive relief against approval of the decrees and did not obtain it. On review of the intervention ruling, the Eleventh Circuit had indicated that independent discrimination claims remained available. Those proceedings must be distinguished from the later action by the Wilks group. Id. at 759–760. 

The later plaintiffs alleged that they were denied promotions because of race in favor of less qualified black candidates. The Supreme Court recounted that assertion as their allegation; it did not find the selected candidates less qualified or decide that the promotions were unlawful. The city and Board defended on the ground that the contested decisions implemented the decrees. The dispute was whether compliance with decrees entered in someone else's litigation foreclosed the later plaintiffs' independent federal claims. Id. at 760–761, 768–769.

## Procedural History

The district court tried the later case under a framework that treated decree-required promotion decisions as immune from the white plaintiffs' challenge. After finding that the disputed decisions were taken pursuant to the decrees, it dismissed the action. The Eleventh Circuit reversed, rejecting the theory that nonparties were precluded from attacking the legality of employment decisions merely because those decisions implemented the earlier judgments. Id. at 760–762. 

The Supreme Court affirmed. It held that the later plaintiffs were entitled to adjudication of their own claims unless a recognized basis for binding nonparties applied. The Court did not decide whether the promotions ultimately violated federal law. It recognized that merits-related findings could have been influenced by the district court's erroneous view of the decrees' preclusive effect, so further proceedings were necessary under the proper framework. Id. at 769. 

The majority and dissent disagreed substantially over how to characterize the district court's action. The majority saw a preclusion-based bar to independent rights. Stevens believed the district court had already resolved the merits and that the decrees operated as a lawful justification for the challenged decisions. An accurate account must preserve that disagreement rather than attribute either characterization indiscriminately to the whole Court. Id. at 765 n.6, 769–770, 783–790.

## Issue

May persons who were not parties to employment-discrimination consent decrees be denied an opportunity to litigate their own discrimination claims because they knew about the earlier proceedings and could have sought intervention? Should the obligation to secure binding adjudication rest on outsiders to intervene under Rule 24, or on existing parties to join affected persons under the rules governing required parties? Id. at 761–764.

## Rule and Holding

A person ordinarily is not bound by an in personam judgment in litigation to which that person was not a party. Knowledge of pending litigation and an opportunity to intervene do not themselves impose an obligation to intervene or extinguish a later independent claim. Rule 24 provides a mechanism for intervention; it does not generally make intervention compulsory. Existing litigants who seek a judgment binding another person ordinarily must secure that person's joinder, subject to the applicable rules and recognized exceptions. Id. at 761–765. 

The Wilks plaintiffs therefore could not be barred merely because the contested employment decisions implemented decrees to which they were not parties. The Court preserved established exceptions for appropriate representative proceedings and special statutory arrangements. It did not hold that every person practically affected by litigation must always be joined or that every nonparty challenge necessarily succeeds on its substantive merits. Id. at 762 n.2, 765–768.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court affirmed the Eleventh Circuit's reversal of the dismissal. The later plaintiffs' claims required further proceedings without treating the consent decrees as automatically conclusive against them. The Court neither invalidated every provision of the decrees nor adjudged the challenged promotions discriminatory. It resolved the procedural obstacle to hearing the independent claims. Id. at 768–769.

## Reasoning

The majority began with the ordinary limit on a judgment's reach. A litigant is entitled to a hearing on that litigant's own rights, and a judgment between other parties generally cannot supply the missing opportunity. The principle applies even when the first litigation was extensive and its settlement important. The binding character of a decree as between its parties does not by itself establish authority to dispose of an outsider's separate federal claim. Id. at 761–763. 

The petitioners sought to replace that rule with a duty to intervene when a person knew or should have known that pending litigation might affect employment interests. The Court found no such general duty in Rule 24. Intervention allows an interested outsider to become a party, but the rule's availability does not make the outsider responsible for preventing a judgment from prejudicing rights that the original parties lacked authority to surrender. Participation as an amicus or an opportunity to object also differs from becoming a party whose rights can be adjudicated. Id. at 763–765. 

Rule 19 addresses the need to include persons whose interests may be affected. The parties negotiating the decree know the relief they seek and are positioned to identify those whom they wish to bind. Requiring joinder places responsibility on those invoking judicial power over the affected interests. When joinder cannot be accomplished, Rule 19 supplies further analysis concerning whether the action should proceed; the majority did not assume that all cases must end whenever every affected person is absent. Id. at 764–767. 

A mandatory-intervention rule would also introduce practical uncertainty. Outsiders would need to monitor litigation, assess proposed relief, and decide when their interests were sufficiently threatened to make intervention necessary. Later cases would then require litigation over notice, foreseeability, and opportunities to participate. In the majority's assessment, such a rule would shift burdens rather than eliminate them, placing the initial burden on people less informed about the contemplated settlement. Id. at 765–767. 

The Court acknowledged the value of settling complex employment disputes and the difficulty of fashioning relief with consequences for other employees. It nevertheless reasoned that joinder could protect the finality of a settlement while preserving the hearing rights of those whose claims would be affected. Administrative convenience and the desire to secure broad peace did not allow the original parties to settle claims they did not own. Id. at 767–768. 

The majority distinguished exceptional statutory proceedings in which Congress has authorized a comprehensive resolution binding interests through special procedures. Such arrangements require examination of their own legal safeguards. Ordinary employment litigation under Rules 19 and 24 did not acquire the same comprehensive effect merely because many employees were interested or because their later suits might complicate compliance. Id. at 762 n.2, 766–767. 

The decree defendants could still contest the later claims on their merits. The Court's point was that decree compliance could not automatically replace that inquiry. In responding to the dissent, the majority emphasized the legal effect of a rule that defeats an otherwise valid discrimination claim solely because an earlier decree authorized the challenged conduct. Calling that effect a substantive justification rather than preclusion would not answer the concern if it deprived an unheard person of an independent right. Id. at 765 n.6.

## Separate Opinions

Justice Stevens agreed with the general proposition that a judgment does not bind nonparties. He disputed its application to the record. In his account, the earlier decrees had followed extensive litigation exposing unlawful employment practices, and the challenged promotions were part of a lawful remedial program. The later district court had heard the white firefighters' claims and rejected them on the merits, rather than refusing to hear them because they were formally bound by the original judgment. Id. at 769–778 (Stevens, J., dissenting). 

The dissent drew a distinction between a judgment's preclusive effect and the practical or legal consequences of obeying an operative court order. A decree could supply a legitimate explanation for employment decisions without extinguishing every outsider's right to sue. Stevens relied on the district court's findings concerning qualifications, the decrees' limited remedial character, and the defendants' obligation to comply. He did not simply propose that knowledge of the first lawsuit always made the later plaintiffs parties. Id. at 769–774, 778–791. 

Stevens also considered the majority's focus on mandatory intervention unnecessary to resolving the actual judgments. He emphasized the history of objections and unsuccessful intervention, while maintaining that the decisive question was whether the later plaintiffs had shown unlawful conduct. As to the Board, he noted the absence of evidence he considered necessary even for an initial discrimination showing. Those were dissenting evaluations of the record and should not be presented as factual conclusions adopted by the majority. Id. at 780 n.16, 782–783 n.20, 783–790. 

The dissent would have vacated the Eleventh Circuit's judgment and remanded for proceedings consistent with its merits-based understanding. Its disagreement concerned the relationship between lawful judicial remedies and later claims, as well as the proper reading of the trial proceedings. The majority instead required reconsideration free of the decree-based bar it identified. Id. at 790–793.

## Notes

Martin is a nonparty-preclusion case, while Parklane concerns binding a defendant who actually litigated and lost the issue in the first action. Whether the person to be bound had the relevant opportunity to litigate must be answered before deciding whether mutuality is necessary. The existence of a judgment and the importance of its remedial program do not themselves answer that party-status question. 490 U.S. at 761–765; Parklane, 439 U.S. at 327–332. 

Later statutory qualification: Congress added 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(n) in 1991. For specified employment practices implementing and within the scope of discrimination judgments or orders, it restricts challenges by persons who received actual notice and a reasonable opportunity to object, and by persons adequately represented in a prior challenge on the same grounds and similar facts, subject to stated qualifications. It preserves due process and exceptions concerning fraud, collusion, transparent invalidity, and lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. Martin therefore should not be used as a complete statement of present employment-decree law without this separate provision. Statutory text and amendment note: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/2000e-2, subsection (n). 

The casebook excerpt concentrates on the majority's nonparty and joinder analysis. The full opinion supplies the sharply different dissenting account of the trial record. Neither account warrants saying that the Supreme Court found particular black firefighters unqualified, awarded promotions to the respondents, or held all race-conscious remedial decrees unlawful. Those substantive conclusions exceed the judgment reviewed here. 490 U.S. at 768–793.
