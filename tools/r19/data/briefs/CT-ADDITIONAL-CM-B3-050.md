# General Dynamics Corporation v. Superior Court (Rose)
7 Cal. 4th 1164, 876 P.2d 487 (1994) | Contracts

## Court, pleading posture, and judgment

The California Supreme Court decided this case on July 18, 1994. Justice Arabian wrote for a unanimous court, joined by Chief Justice Lucas and Justices Mosk, Kennard, Baxter, George, and assigned Justice Turner. The employer challenged the overruling of a general demurrer to an in-house lawyer's wrongful-termination complaint. The court held that professional status did not categorically bar an implied-in-fact just-cause contract claim or a carefully limited public-policy tort. It affirmed the Court of Appeal's judgment and remanded with directions for further proceedings, allowing amendment of the tort allegations to meet the newly explained requirements. It expressed no view of the merits and awarded neither damages nor reinstatement. (7 Cal. 4th at 1169–1171, 1192.)

## Allegations and procedural history

Andrew Rose alleged that he joined General Dynamics in 1978 as a 27-year-old contract administrator at its Pomona plant, progressed through commendations and promotions, and was on track to become a division vice-president and general counsel. The complaint described his June 24, 1991 discharge as wrongful. It alleged that the company's stated loss of confidence concealed retaliation for investigating workforce drug use, protesting its failure to investigate the bugging of the security chief's office, and advising that a salary policy could violate the federal Fair Labor Standards Act and expose the company to substantial back-pay liability. Those charges were allegations accepted for the demurrer, not findings about drug use, national-security violations, the validity of the compensation advice, or the employer's actual motive. (Id. at 1170–1171 & n. 1.)

His first theory alleged that assurances and the parties' course of conduct created an implied agreement allowing discharge only for good cause. The second asserted that the several alleged reasons for discharge contravened fundamental public policies. General Dynamics argued that an in-house lawyer could be discharged for any reason without these liabilities. The trial court overruled its demurrer; the Court of Appeal denied the ensuing mandate petition, finding both theories sufficient at the pleading stage. The Supreme Court reviewed the categorical legal objection and refined the conditions governing the tort theory. (Id. at 1171, 1192.)

## Power to discharge versus liability for discharge

General Dynamics relied on Fracasse, which protected a client's power to dismiss contingent-fee personal-injury counsel with or without cause, while limiting the discharged lawyer to the reasonable value of services upon a recovery. The Supreme Court reaffirmed the client's unilateral power to end representation but refused to read that power as immunity from all monetary consequences in every relationship. Fracasse involved a particular client, fee arrangement, and risk allocation; permanent in-house employment involved different expectations and economic circumstances. Even Fracasse recognized an obligation to compensate counsel under its applicable conditions. (Id. at 1173–1177 & n. 3.)

Rose was not seeking to force continued representation. The court expressly distinguished damages from reinstatement, which was unavailable here. A client could remove counsel in whom it lacked confidence while remaining answerable for antecedent contractual commitments or a legally recognized retaliatory discharge. A hypothetical fixed-term lawyer who moved across the country in reliance on a bargained agreement illustrated why an unlimited immunity would be unjust. It was a hypothetical explaining doctrine, not Rose's personal history. (Id. at 1175–1177.)

## Implied-in-fact contract claim

The contract claim arose from the parties' actual course of dealing and reasonable expectations. Rose alleged career-oriented employment, promises of job security and retirement benefits, favorable reviews, promotions, salary increases, commendations, and disregard of published discharge procedures. Under Foley, those allegations adequately supported an implied good-cause limitation. The employer's facial demurrer did not challenge their truth, and the court saw no categorical policy reason to deny enforcement merely because the employee was an attorney. (Id. at 1177–1179.)

Matters of compensation, promotion, and tenure could ordinarily be treated like those of other employees without undermining client trust or professional independence. The opinion acknowledged employers' latitude in specifying what constituted good cause for confidential personnel. It did not find that Rose actually possessed an enforceable agreement, that the company lacked cause, or that privilege could never arise in a contract case. Its conclusion sustained the pleaded theory at this stage. (Id. at 1178–1179, 1192.)

## Tort rationale and disagreement with Balla

The public-policy tort was analytically different. It arose from duties imposed by law to protect a fundamental, established public interest, not merely the parties' employment bargain. The court described California's limitations requiring an established policy and a public rather than exclusively private benefit. For in-house lawyers, those purposes intersected with a dual allegiance: loyalty to the client and adherence to mandatory professional norms. Dependence on a single employer could make compliance especially costly and create pressure to suppress wrongdoing. (Id. at 1180–1182.)

The court examined Balla, Herbster, and Willy, which had denied remedies because of confidentiality concerns and because lawyers already had duties to report or withdraw. It accepted the importance of client confidences but rejected the inference that ethical obligations necessarily made a remedy redundant. Telling an economically dependent lawyer simply to resign ignored the cost of complying and could encourage silence. The professional duties furnishing a reason for denying the remedy in those cases could instead furnish a reason for a carefully confined remedy encouraging adherence to important public obligations. The court's criticism did not remove all confidentiality limits. (Id. at 1182–1188.)

## Two paths and additional limits

The first inquiry was whether discharge followed adherence to a mandatory ethical duty prescribed by professional rule or statute. Refusing an employer's demand to commit a crime or disbarring misconduct supplied the opinion's examples. If conduct was merely permissible rather than required, the court specified two further questions: whether a nonlawyer could maintain a public-policy discharge claim for that conduct, and whether an applicable statute or ethical rule permitted departure from confidentiality. A generalized disagreement about desirable corporate policy was insufficient. (Id. at 1188–1190.)

The court limited relevant ethical predicates to California's governing professional rules and statutes, expressly declining to adopt the ABA model codes as independent legal predicates; its note also suggested ordinary choice-of-law analysis for out-of-state conduct. The norm had to be unequivocal and intended to protect the public at large. The plaintiff bore the burden of establishing the norm and impermissible motivation under but-for causation. The employer could show a legitimate alternative reason for discharge. These requirements meant the lawyer's tort remedy was narrower than that of an ordinary employee. (Id. at 1189–1192 & n. 6.)

## Privilege and litigation safeguards

The privilege was not diluted because counsel worked in-house. Existing statutory exceptions, including crime-fraud and specified prevention of criminal harm, remained applicable; recognition of a discharge tort did not create a blanket new exception. If a claim could not be fully established without breaching privilege, it had to be dismissed. The court emphasized that such a conclusion would seldom, if ever, be appropriate on a facial demurrer and would usually arise through discovery or summary-judgment proceedings. (Id. at 1189–1191.)

Courts could consider sealing, protective orders, limited admissibility, restrictions on later use of testimony, and appropriate in-camera procedures to protect legitimate confidences while allowing proof. Those tools did not authorize violation of the privilege. Lawyers disclosing protected confidences during an unsuccessful action could face professional discipline. The decision reconciled a limited opportunity to litigate with strict preservation of protected communications. (Id. at 1190–1192.)

## Application and exact remand

The implied-contract allegations adequately pleaded the essential elements. The tort allegations did not identify the professional rule or relevant statute requiring or supporting the conduct allegedly causing termination, and Rose had apparently assumed ordinary employee tort rules applied without further limits. Because no published California case had previously resolved this precise issue, the court allowed amendment consistent with its new explanation. The formal disposition affirmed the Court of Appeal and remanded through it for further proceedings; no merits conclusion about any asserted retaliatory act was reached. (Id. at 1192.)
