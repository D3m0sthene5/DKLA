# Harding Realty, Inc. v. Turnberry Towers Corporation
436 So. 2d 983 (Fla. 3d DCA 1983) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Florida’s Third District Court of Appeal decided this case on August 2, 1983; rehearing was denied September 21, 1983. Judge Nesbitt wrote for a panel consisting of Judges Hendry, Nesbitt, and Baskin, with no separate writing reported. The court affirmed a final summary judgment for condominium developer Turnberry Towers on broker Harding Realty’s entire action. The result rested on an express closing condition and the inability to obtain quantum meruit recovery in place of a valid express agreement. (983–985.)

## Facts and the disputed allocation of risk
Harding entered written brokerage agreements with Turnberry and procured two corporations as prospective purchasers of two condominium units. The purchasers signed binding purchase-and-sale contracts and paid earnest-money deposits, but defaulted without closing. Turnberry retained both deposits under the purchase contracts. Harding demanded a share and then sued for its commission. These were undisputed facts supporting summary judgment, rather than findings after a damages trial. The report does not specify the deposits’ amounts. (983–984.)

The brokerage agreement, drafted by Harding, provided that Harding would be entitled to a three-percent commission at the time of closing on sales to clients brought by its salespeople. The question was whether closing merely identified when Turnberry had to pay an already-earned commission, or instead had to occur before Harding acquired the right to one. That distinction determined who bore the purchasers’ failure to complete their transactions. Turnberry maintained that the entitlement language imposed an express condition precedent. (984.)

## The ordinary brokerage rule and its contractual qualification
The court first characterized the agreement as employing Harding to effect a sale, rather than merely to obtain a ready, willing, and able buyer. A signed purchase contract is not itself a completed sale. Nevertheless, under the ordinary rule discussed in the opinion, a broker employed to effect a sale earns the commission either by completing the sale or by presenting the seller with a binding, enforceable written purchase-and-sale agreement. Under the latter route, the buyer’s subsequent default generally does not destroy the broker’s entitlement. Nor does a liquidated-damages provision necessarily do so merely because deposit forfeiture supplies an adequate legal remedy and prevents specific enforcement in equity. Harding therefore could not be denied a commission simply on the assumption that every purchaser default prevents a broker from earning one. (984.)

That default rule could be varied by express language conditioning the commission. The court cited Florida decisions recognizing such contractual limitations and noted conflicting decisions elsewhere. It adopted what it described as the greater weight of authority: provisions making commission payment occur at sale, closing, or settlement make that event a condition precedent to the payment obligation. The court did not purport to eliminate the need to interpret the particular agreement. It regarded Harding’s wording as an especially strong example because the provision tied entitlement itself, rather than only payment, to closing. Harding had also drafted that language. (984.)

## Application and the alternative restitution claim
Neither closing occurred, and that fact was uncontroverted. Because the agreement expressly conditioned Harding’s entitlement on closing, production of the binding purchase contracts did not satisfy the agreed condition. Turnberry’s retention of deposits under separate purchase agreements did not satisfy it either. The brokerage contract therefore barred recovery as a matter of law despite the ordinary rule that production of an enforceable purchase contract can earn a commission. The court awarded no commission or share of the deposits. (984–985.)

Harding also sought recovery in quantum meruit. The court rejected that route because the law would not imply a contract when a valid express contract governed the relationship. Restitution could not be used in this action to substitute for the express agreement’s closing contingency. This conclusion supplied an independent reason to affirm summary judgment on the alternative theory, making the affirmance cover the entire action. It was not a general ruling that brokerage services can never support restitution in the absence of an enforceable express agreement. (984–985.)
