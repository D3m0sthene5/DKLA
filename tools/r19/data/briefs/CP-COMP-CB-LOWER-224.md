# Nachshin v. AOL, LLC

**663 F.3d 1034 (9th Cir. 2011) — November 21, 2011.** Circuit Judge N. Randy Smith wrote the opinion for the panel with Judge Betty B. Fletcher and District Judge James S. Gwin, sitting by designation. Assigned source: CivPro physical page 439, printed page 850, own cy pres account.

## The challenged emails and settlement

Four plaintiffs brought an August 2009 class action alleging that AOL inserted promotional footers into emails sent by its subscribers. They asserted electronic-communications privacy, unjust-enrichment, California consumer-protection, and contract claims. The settlement class comprised approximately 66 million current AOL members. These were allegations resolved through a proposed settlement, not a judicial finding in this appeal that every footer insertion was illegal. (1036.)

The settlement provided notifications of the advertisements and an opportunity to opt out. If AOL continued appending them, it would repeat notification every six months for two years; future members would also receive information and an opt-out link. The parties considered individual damages small and difficult to ascertain: even the approximately $2 million maximum trial recovery they identified would yield about three cents per member, with distribution costs exceeding recovery. That $2 million estimate was **not** the charitable amount actually promised in the settlement. (1036–1037.)

Instead, at mediator Judge Tevrizian’s suggestion, AOL agreed to $25,000 donations to each of three charitable recipients: Los Angeles Legal Aid, the Federal Judicial Center Foundation, and the Boys and Girls Club recipient shared between Los Angeles and Santa Monica chapters. Separate donations totaling $35,000, allocated as $8,750 for each named plaintiff’s designation, went to charities selected by those representatives. The resulting total donations were $110,000. The court provisionally certified the class and approved notice, which AOL emailed to more than 60 million members. (1037, 1040.)

## Objection and the district court’s ruling

Objector Darren McKinney argued that the cy pres beneficiaries lacked connections to the lawsuit and nationwide class; that Judge Snyder should recuse because her husband served on Los Angeles Legal Aid’s board; and that notice omitted that one named representative worked for her designated charity. Another objector sought donation to her own foundation; footnote 1 describes that objection and her mother’s permitted opt-out. Some 4,525 subscribers submitted opt-out requests, although the opinion identifies failures to comply with the name and deadline requirements among them. (1037–1038, n.1.)

The district court overruled the objections and approved the settlement. It relied on the experienced mediator’s involvement, prospective relief, and the perceived similarity of the selected charities to those in other approved settlements. Snyder declined recusal, explaining that the parties—not she—selected the charities. McKinney appealed the same three issues. Approval of a settlement and its proposed cy pres distribution was reviewed for abuse of discretion, including application of an incorrect legal standard or unreasonable factual findings. (1038.)

## Governing cy pres principles

Cy pres permits an indirect, next-best use of otherwise unclaimed or impracticably distributable settlement funds. But a good charitable purpose is insufficient by itself. Distribution must be guided by the underlying statutory objectives and absent class members’ interests, including the geographic reach of the class. Without a meaningful nexus, choices can serve the parties’, lawyers’, or court’s preferences and create an appearance of impropriety. The court discussed those institutional risks without finding that this judge had personally steered funds for financial gain. (1038–1040.)

Under *Six Mexican Workers*, a distribution must address the statutory objectives, target the plaintiff class, and supply reasonable certainty that members will benefit. Footnote 2 additionally identifies the ALI’s similar recipient-interest standard; it does not substitute an entirely new rule for the circuit precedent. (1039–1040, n.2.)

## Why these donations failed

The proposed recipients had no demonstrated connection to the electronic-communications and commercial-email interests underlying the claims. Two-thirds of the principal donations would benefit local Los Angeles charities despite a nationwide class. Even for members living there, the record did not indicate they would benefit from those programs. The Federal Judicial Center Foundation had national scope, but no apparent connection to the statutory purposes or demonstrated benefit to the class. The district court therefore applied the wrong distribution standard and abused its discretion. (1040.)

AOL urged deference to the negotiated settlement as fair, reasonable, and adequate. The court separated two inquiries: adequacy of the settlement as a whole and validity of its distribution. McKinney did not challenge the overall amount as inadequate compensation; he challenged how a presumptively adequate settlement allocated the benefits. Cy pres standards apply whether the parties or judge select recipients. An experienced mediator’s participation did not eliminate the required nexus. (1040–1041.)

The class’s size and diversity did not make related beneficiaries impossible to find. All members used the internet, and all claims arose from allegedly wrongful advertising through their outgoing emails. Organizations protecting internet users from online fraud or other malfeasance were plausible alternatives. If no suitable beneficiary could be identified, the district court should consider escheat to the Treasury. The panel did not itself choose a particular charity or unconditionally order Treasury payment. (1041.)

## Recusal, notice, and exact disposition

The court rejected recusal under §455(a): a reasonable informed observer would not necessarily question impartiality merely from the husband’s volunteer membership on an approximately fifty-member charity board. Nothing in the record showed compensation or that the donation benefited him personally beyond supporting legal services for indigent clients. (1041.)

It also rejected §455(b)(4) and (b)(5)(iii). Legal Aid was neither named nor represented as a party; its proposed beneficiary status arose from the mediator’s recommendation. Board membership did not create the identified statutory financial interest in a party, and the record did not establish a personal interest substantially affected by the $25,000 gift. Remote, contingent, or speculative benefits were insufficient. This was a record-specific rejection of the alleged interests, not approval of every possible judicial connection to a beneficiary. (1041–1042.)

The panel **reversed in part, affirmed in part, and remanded**: the cy pres approval failed, but denial of recusal survived. It declined to decide notice sufficiency because the distribution ruling already required further proceedings; it denied AOL’s request to take judicial notice of amended online notice and ordered each side to bear its own costs. (1042.)

The complete opinion includes the designation star and two judicial notes, with no separate writings or appendices. The textbook correctly summarizes the nexus failure, but its description of messages inserted into AOL emails “to subscribers” differs from the actual allegations: the footers were appended to **subscribers’ outgoing messages**. Its unqualified “illegally” is editorial shorthand for the alleged wrongdoing, not a merits holding of this settlement appeal. The brief preserves that distinction and does not certify current cy pres law.
