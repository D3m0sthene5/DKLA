# Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon
262 U.S. 447 (1923) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## The federal program and the two plaintiffs

The Maternity Act of November 23, 1921 provided an initial appropriation and annual appropriations over five years for cooperating with accepting states to reduce maternal and infant mortality and protect health. Participating state agencies would cooperate with federal administration, submit prescribed operational and expenditure reports, and face withholding of payments if funds were improperly spent. A state could accept or reject the offer. Massachusetts had not accepted. (478–480.)

Massachusetts brought an original Supreme Court suit against federal officials administering the program, both on its own behalf and as representative of its citizens. It argued that the appropriations addressed local, rather than national, concerns and induced states to surrender reserved powers. It alleged that industrial states bore disproportionate burdens and faced an unconstitutional choice between retaining sovereignty and receiving their share of appropriated money. The defendants were officials who were citizens of other states, but that formal party arrangement did not itself resolve justiciability. (448, 479–482.)

In the companion suit, Frothingham sued the same officials in the Supreme Court of the District of Columbia. She advanced the same general constitutional objections and alleged that appropriations would increase her future federal taxes, taking her property without due process. That court dismissed her bill, and the D.C. Court of Appeals affirmed. Both plaintiffs sought to restrain the act’s enforcement. The Supreme Court considered the original state suit and Frothingham’s appeal together, while preserving their distinct asserted interests and procedural dispositions. (448, 478–480, 486.)

## Judgment and matters left undecided

On June 4, 1923, Justice Sutherland’s opinion for the Court resolved both cases for want of jurisdiction **without considering the constitutional merits**. It **dismissed No. 24, Original, Massachusetts’s suit**, and **affirmed No. 962, Frothingham’s appeal**. No separate opinion or participation qualification appears in the report. Thus the result was not an adjudication that the Maternity Act fell within federal spending authority, and it was not an appellate affirmance in the state’s original proceeding. (447, 480, 489.)

## Massachusetts’s asserted injury to sovereignty

Original jurisdiction requires a dispute of judicial cognizance; naming a state as a party does not turn every assertion of political power into one. The opinion contrasted recognized proprietary, territorial, or actually threatened quasi-sovereign interests with abstract disputes about the governmental allocation of authority. Its historical examples explain that distinction rather than independently deciding each cited precedent anew. (480–482.)

Here the statute required Massachusetts neither to do anything nor to surrender a power. It could reject the offered cooperation. The alleged taxation burden operated on inhabitants, who were subject to federal as well as state taxation, rather than on Massachusetts itself. An alleged congressional intention to tempt states to yield authority did not establish an invasion where consent was required and could be withheld. The state’s complaint therefore reduced to a contention that Congress usurped reserved powers merely by enacting a law. As presented without an actual or threatened operation on a judicially protected state right, that was an abstract political question outside the Court’s authority. The conclusion concerns this optional program and this asserted injury, not a determination that all federal grant conditions escape review. (482–485.)

## Massachusetts as representative of its citizens

The Court separately rejected the state’s attempt to sue as parens patriae against operation of a federal statute on its citizens. Those individuals were citizens of the United States as well as Massachusetts. In their relations with the federal government, the United States, rather than the state, held the appropriate representative position. A state could not transform that relationship into a right to judicially protect federal citizens against federal statutes in this suit. The opinion did not go so far as to say a state could never bring any suit protecting its citizens against any unconstitutional federal action; it stated the limit at issue here and distinguished states’ other protective functions, including appropriate enforcement of state law or opening state courts to injured individuals. (485–486.)

## Frothingham’s federal taxpayer theory

Frothingham’s alleged future tax increase likewise supplied no sufficiently direct interest for equitable prevention of federal expenditures. The Court distinguished municipal taxpayer suits, whose justification rests on a comparatively direct relationship to the corporate treasury, sometimes analogous to a shareholder’s interest. A federal taxpayer’s stake in a national treasury, funded by taxation and other sources and shared with millions, was comparatively small and indeterminable. The effect of a particular expenditure on that taxpayer’s future liability was remote, fluctuating, and uncertain. It did not establish an individual injury warranting an injunction. (486–487.)

Allowing that interest to suffice would invite every taxpayer to contest every appropriation or statute requiring expenditure, converting public concern into general judicial supervision of the elected departments. The Court required both an asserted invalidity and a direct injury suffered or immediately threatened by enforcement. Judicial power to disregard an unconstitutional statute arises in deciding a concrete legal controversy, not as an independent power to review and annul legislation. Preventive relief would operate on officials’ injurious acts despite the statute, rather than issue merely because someone alleged the statute unconstitutional. Neither plaintiff supplied such a case. (487–489.)

## Assignment and historical scope

The assigned November-b PDF 113, printed 1170, includes a short taxpayer-expenditure comparator under the name Massachusetts v. Mellon. The individual taxpayer holding belongs to the jointly reported Frothingham case; this brief makes that identity explicit instead of describing Massachusetts as an individual taxpayer. The entire page and original scan were read. The complete Sutherland opinion, report 478–489, has no judicial footnotes or separate writings. The official 43-page extract preserves extensive preceding advocacy, which is not the Court’s reasoning, and ends with unrelated Willard material, excluded from this decision’s scope. Later exceptions or developments in taxpayer standing are not silently incorporated into the 1923 holding; this is not a current citator certification.
