# Bank of America National Trust & Savings Association v. Parnell
352 U.S. 29 (1956) | Civil Procedure

## Decision and assigned account

Justice Frankfurter delivered the Court's November 13, 1956 opinion. Justices Black and Douglas jointly dissented, producing a 7–2 judgment. The Court reversed the Third Circuit in two companion matters, No. 21 against Parnell and No. 22 against First National Bank in Indiana, and remanded to that court rather than reinstating the trial judgments. The complete seven-page official report spans 29–35, including the Court's opinion at 30–34 and dissent at 35. There are no numbered judicial footnotes or appendices; the opening starred notation identifies the companion matter. The assignment is Civil Procedure PDF page 284, printed page 532, Note 4. [Complete official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-352/pdf/USREPORTS-352-29.pdf).

## Missing bonds and a private conversion suit

Bank of America alleged that seventy-three Home Owners' Loan Corporation bearer bonds belonged to it and had been converted in Pennsylvania. The bonds carried semiannual interest coupons and a United States payment guarantee. Although their stated maturity was May 1, 1952, they had been called for redemption around May 1, 1944. They disappeared on May 2, 1944 while the bank was preparing them for payment. 352 U.S. at 30–31.

In 1948 Parnell presented the bonds to First National Bank for Rocco. That bank forwarded them to the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, received the proceeds, and issued cashier's checks to Parnell. He passed the money to Rocco after deducting a fee; testimony conflicted about whether the fee was nominal or substantial. Bank of America sued on diversity grounds in the Western District of Pennsylvania. The Federal Reserve Bank was dismissed after the plaintiff's presentation, and Rocco did not appeal. Only Parnell and First National Bank were respondents in the Supreme Court. Id. at 30–31.

## The burden of proving good faith changed the lower-court result

The main trial issue was whether the defendants took the bonds in good faith without notice or knowledge of the title defect. Applying Pennsylvania law, the district court instructed that, if the plaintiff established ownership and theft, defendants who acted as redemption conduits rather than owners for value bore the burden of showing innocent, honest, good-faith conduct. The jury found for Bank of America against both respondents. Id. at 31.

The Third Circuit, sitting en banc, reversed by four votes to three. It treated *Clearfield Trust* as requiring federal law and placed the burden of proving notice and lack of good faith on the plaintiff. It also concluded that the bonds were not overdue as a matter of federal law when presented. Finding no bad-faith evidence against the bank, it directed judgment for that defendant; it found such evidence concerning Parnell but ordered a new trial because of the instructions. Id. at 31–32.

The circuit dissenters accepted federal law for the government's obligations and the bonds' overdue character, but treated private good faith and its proof as matters of state law. They considered the verdict supportable under that allocation. The Supreme Court granted review because the choice raised an important federal–state question. Id. at 32.

## Government paper did not federalize every private transaction

In *Clearfield Trust*, the United States itself sued on a guarantee of prior endorsements on its check. The government's issuance, payment obligations, and nationwide dealings implicated federal rights and duties whose exposure to varying state rules justified federal law. This controversy instead concerned private parties' alleged conversion and the good faith of private recipients. The United States' rights and duties were not at stake. Id. at 33.

A state conversion rule might conceivably affect the government's ability to market securities. But that possible effect was too speculative and remote to justify displacing local law in this private transaction. The Court therefore held that Pennsylvania law governed good faith and the burden of proving it. Id. at 33–34.

The holding was narrower than a rule that federal law can never govern litigation between private parties. The Court expressly left room for a sufficiently substantial federal interest in another dispute and for congressional legislation governing government paper. It also retained federal law for interpreting the rights and obligations created by the bonds themselves. Thus whether the redemption call made these bonds overdue remained a federal-law question, which the Court did not decide. Id. at 34.

## Black and Douglas's objection to divided governing law

The joint dissent would apply a uniform federal commercial law to all transactions in United States paper. It regarded *Clearfield Trust* and *National Metropolitan Bank* as supporting that broader coverage. Dividing a dispute between federal rules for the paper's obligations and state rules for matters characterized as local would itself create uncertainty. Uniform law, in their view, would give every party a definite guide instead of exposure to different state systems. They acknowledged that government business could continue without uniformity, but considered certainty and convenience sufficient reasons to preserve it. Id. at 35.

## Exact mandate and practical significance

The Supreme Court reversed the judgments and remanded both matters to the Third Circuit. It did not reinstate the district court's awards. The appellate court had not addressed all the respondents' points or determined the governing Pennsylvania burden-of-proof law. It first had to do so and then assess the evidence under that law if defendants bore the burden of demonstrating good faith. Id. at 34.

The assigned excerpt accurately emphasizes both the private nature of the transaction and the reservation concerning other federal interests. The complete opinion adds the distinction between good-faith liability and interpretation of the bonds, the two different appellate outcomes, and the remand qualifications. A federal origin for the instrument did not alone make every dispute over its private transfer a federal common-law question. Assigned printed 532; 352 U.S. at 33–35.
