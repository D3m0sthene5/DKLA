# Briones v. Riviera Hotel & Casino

**116 F.3d 379 (9th Cir. 1997) — United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, May 22, 1997, No. 96-15076.** Per curiam before Judges Pregerson, Reinhardt, and T. G. Nelson; no separate writing is reported. Civil Procedure: Rule 60(b)(1), excusable neglect, and review of a motion for relief from judgment. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical pages 596–597, printed 1177–1178.

## Facts and procedural history

Jesus Briones, representing himself, sued his former employer, Riviera Operating Corporation, organized as the Riviera Hotel & Casino. He alleged a racially hostile work environment and unlawful discharge because of his Hispanic heritage. These were allegations, not discrimination findings made by the appellate court. (380.)

Riviera moved to dismiss on July 19, 1995, partly for improper service. Briones did not respond on time. The district court granted dismissal and entered judgment against him on August 18, 1995. His opposition arrived three and one-half months after the filing deadline. He then sought relief from judgment under Rule 60(b), arguing excusable neglect: the court had not warned him that dismissal could occur, and he had failed to inform his translator and typist of the opposition deadline. He was not proficient in English. The district court denied relief, and he appealed that denial pro se. (380.)

The Ninth Circuit’s May 22, 1997 order withdrew its memorandum disposition filed November 8, 1996 and issued this published opinion. The appeal proceeded under §1291. Review was for abuse of discretion and concerned **the denial of Rule 60(b) relief**, rather than the merits of the underlying dismissal or employment claim. (379–380.)

## The notice argument failed

The court rejected Briones’s claim that he had lacked adequate warning. Riviera’s motion expressly requested dismissal and was properly served on him; that motion provided sufficient notice of possible dismissal. A defendant’s successful challenge to service of the complaint and proper service of the defendant’s dismissal motion are different procedural acts. The opinion’s treatment of one does not negate its statement about the other. (380.)

## Holding: apply Pioneer’s equitable framework

Earlier Ninth Circuit authority, particularly *Swimmer*, had stated that ignorance of court rules was not excusable neglect even for a person appearing without counsel. *Briones* held that such a categorical bar could not survive *Pioneer*’s interpretation of excusable neglect. The equitable framework applied to Rule 60(b), as well as the bankruptcy rule considered in *Pioneer*. Negligence could be neglect; whether it was excusable required consideration of the circumstances. (381–382.)

The relevant, nonexclusive factors were prejudice to the opposing party, the delay’s length and potential effect on proceedings, the reason for the delay including reasonable control, and good faith. The court did not make ignorance or carelessness presumptively excusable: inadvertence and rule mistakes ordinarily would not justify relief. It rejected using one circumstance as an automatic exclusion without examining the rest. (381–382 & n.2.)

Pro se litigants remained obliged to follow procedural rules. But Briones’s problem might have involved communication within his group of assistants, rather than only a failure to read and attempt to obey those rules. The district court therefore needed to reconsider the motion using *Pioneer*. The appellate court did **not** determine that this delay actually was excusable, reinstate the employment claim on its merits, or award damages. (382.)

## Both judicial footnotes

Note 1 discusses how the reason-and-control factor might function differently under Rule 60(b). Drawing on *Pioneer*, the court distinguished negligent conduct from a failure caused by circumstances beyond the party’s control and suggested that this difference might make the rule’s excusable-neglect provision broader than similar provisions elsewhere. It expressly declined to decide that specific comparative question. This is a reservation, not a holding that the control factor disappears in every Rule 60(b) case. (382 n.1.)

Note 2 cites *Kyle*, where a lawyer’s mistaken deadline calculation did not constitute excusable neglect under Rule 6(b). The court nevertheless emphasized the equitable framework and ordinarily examining all relevant circumstances rather than having one fact dictate the outcome regardless of the others. *Briones* consequently did not hold that every deadline miscalculation requires relief. (382 n.2.)

## Disposition and textbook comparison

The court **vacated the district court’s order denying relief and remanded for further proceedings under Pioneer and this opinion, with no costs allowed**. Briones’s conduct appeared at least negligent; whether it was excusable remained to be determined. (382.)

The textbook accurately identifies the pro se language problem, Rule 60(b)(1) request, rejection of a per se bar, four factors, and remand. Its statement that the dismissal motion also invoked lack of jurisdiction adds detail not specified in this opinion’s factual account, which says the motion was partly based on improper service. The brief attributes only the latter to the actual appellate report. The textbook’s following *Pincay* discussion concerns a different case and appeal-extension rule; it is not an additional disposition or holding in *Briones*. Its nearby *Patrick*, *Unitherm*, and *Linden* material likewise supplies context. (Assigned 596–597.)
