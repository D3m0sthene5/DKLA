# Jacobson v. Knepper & Moga, P.C.
185 Ill. 2d 372, 706 N.E.2d 491 (1998) | Contracts

## Court, certified question, and disposition

The Illinois Supreme Court decided this interlocutory appeal on December 31, 1998. Justice Nickels delivered the opinion; Chief Justice Freeman dissented and Justice Harrison took no part. The court answered the certified question affirmatively: the attorney employee could not maintain the asserted retaliatory-discharge tort against his nonclient law-firm employer because the professional rules sufficiently safeguarded the public policy implicated here. It reversed both the appellate and circuit court judgments and remanded with directions to grant the firm's motion to dismiss. This was a legal ruling on the availability of the pleaded cause of action, not a trial finding that the alleged collection-law violations or retaliatory motive were proved. (185 Ill. 2d at 372–375, 378–379.)

## Alleged misconduct and discharge

Alan Jacobson alleged that the firm hired him as an associate attorney in July 1994. Soon afterward he discovered consumer-debt collection suits filed in violation of federal and Illinois statutory venue requirements. He told principal partner James Knepper, who assured him the practice would be corrected. In April 1995, Jacobson assumed responsibility for reviewing and signing all consumer-debt complaints and discovered continued violations. He again complained and received another assurance; shortly afterward the firm removed that review responsibility. Within three months he discovered that the practice persisted and approached Knepper a third time. Approximately two weeks later, he was terminated. His one-count complaint alleged discharge in retaliation for insisting upon proper venue. (Id. at 374–375.)

The firm moved to dismiss under § 2-615(a), relying on Balla and Herbster as rejecting retaliatory-discharge claims by lawyer employees. The circuit court denied dismissal but certified whether those precedents prevented a licensed lawyer's tort claim against a nonclient firm employer because of the professional rules. The appellate court initially declined interlocutory review. The Supreme Court denied leave at that stage but issued a supervisory order requiring appellate consideration. After the appellate court concluded that Jacobson could pursue the action, the Supreme Court granted the firm's subsequent petition and reviewed that legal conclusion. (Id. at 375.)

## Narrow tort and ethical safeguards

The court recognized Illinois's general at-will rule and its narrow retaliatory-discharge exception. The employee had to establish retaliatory discharge and contravention of clearly mandated public policy. Existing cases involved workers' compensation claims or reporting illegal or improper conduct. Jacobson invoked the federal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act and Illinois Collection Agency Act as the source of the public policy allegedly violated. The majority identified protection of debtor defendants' property and due process as the relevant public interest; it did not deny that the collection laws served an important policy. (Id. at 376–377.)

The decisive step was remedial. In the majority's view, the tort sought a balance among efficient business operations, employees' livelihoods, and implementation of public policy. Here, the attorney's independent ethical obligations already protected the relevant policy, so extending the limited tort was unnecessary. Knowingly alleging proper venue when venue was improper implicated the candor rule, and dishonest or deceptive conduct implicated the misconduct rule. Because Jacobson's knowledge was unprivileged, the reporting rule required him to report the misconduct to an empowered tribunal or investigating authority. These duties existed independently of his employment incentives. (Id. at 377–378.)

Jacobson sought to distinguish Balla because his employer was a law firm rather than the corporate client of in-house counsel. The majority rejected that distinction. Balla rested both on attorney-client concerns and on the nature and purpose of the narrow tort. Lawyers employed by firms and in-house lawyers shared the pertinent professional obligations. Accordingly, the same safeguards rationale applied to Jacobson as a licensed attorney employed in that professional capacity. This holding concerns the asserted public-policy tort in the described circumstances, not a ruling that firms may violate collection statutes or that every conceivable contract or statutory employment claim by a lawyer is barred. (Id. at 378.)

## Freeman's dissent

Freeman would answer the certified question negatively and affirm both lower courts, allowing the complaint to proceed. He renewed his objection from Balla that confidence in ethical duties as a sufficient shield against employer misconduct was misplaced. Extending that reasoning to law firms denied attorneys a remedy that Illinois provided to other whistleblowing employees. In his view, the economic incentive created by losing one's livelihood without a tort remedy could encourage silence rather than compliance with professional reporting duties. The difference between the opinions was therefore not whether lawyers owed ethical duties, but whether those duties alone made the additional public-policy remedy unnecessary. (Id. at 379.)
