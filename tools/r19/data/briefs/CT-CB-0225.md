Ever-Tite Roofing Corp. v. Green
83 So. 2d 449 (La. Ct. App. 1955)
Court, author, and source scope
The Louisiana Court of Appeal, Second Circuit, decided this case on November 2, 1955;
rehearing was denied November 29. Judge Ayres wrote the opinion. The assigned excerpt,
printed at pages 225–227, emphasizes the mode and timing of acceptance. The complete
opinion additionally supplies the damages calculation, the attorney's-fee ruling, the final
rendered judgment, and the homeowners' rejected excuse for not giving earlier notice. The
retrieved full text has no internal reporter-page markers; citations therefore identify the
quoted contractual clause, Civil Code article, or concluding discussion rather than inventing
page breaks.
Parties and material facts
G. T. Green and Jessie Fay Green wanted their residence in Webster Parish re-roofed. On
June 10, 1953, they signed an instrument setting out the proposed work and a price payable
in installments. Ever-Tite's sales representative also signed, but lacked authority to accept
the agreement for the corporation. The document expressly stated that it would become
binding upon written acceptance by the contractor's principal or authorized officer, or upon
commencement of performance. It also contained a no-cancellation statement, collection
provisions, and an integration clause. 83 So. 2d 449, opinion's quoted agreement and
opening factual discussion.
The job required financing, and the homeowners knew that their credit would be checked
and lender approval obtained. The proposed agreement reached Ever-Tite's office the
following day. Ever-Tite sought a credit report, sent it to the lending institution, and supplied
additional information when requested. The lender then approved financing. The court found
no unreasonable delay in that process. Id., financing and diligence discussion; casebook
225–226.
Immediately after the approval, on the sequence described as June 18 or 19, Ever-Tite
arranged workers and two trucks, loaded the roofing materials in Shreveport, and traveled to
the Greens' home. There the workers discovered another crew already carrying out the
roofing job. The Greens said they had engaged those workers two days earlier and refused to
permit Ever-Tite to proceed. They had given Ever-Tite no earlier notice or attempted notice
of cancellation. Id., factual discussion.
The Greens asserted that they had not known where or how to contact the company. The
court rejected that explanation because their copy of the instrument conspicuously displayed
its name, address, and telephone number. Their private decision to hire someone else
therefore had not itself communicated withdrawal to Ever-Tite. Id., notice discussion.

Procedural history
Ever-Tite sued for damages for breach. The homeowners argued that their proposal had
never been accepted in the prescribed manner before withdrawal. The trial court agreed,
treating the notice given when Ever-Tite's workers arrived as occurring before
commencement of the actual roofing work, and dismissed the suit at Ever-Tite's cost.
Ever-Tite appealed. Id., opening procedural discussion.
The appellate court held that commencement had occurred earlier, when the materials and
workers were loaded and dispatched. It reversed and rendered a money judgment for
Ever-Tite rather than remanding for a new determination of damages. The monetary
evidence was uncontroverted, permitting a final award on the record. Id., acceptance and
concluding damages discussions.
Issues
Did the company's actions constitute acceptance through commencement of performance, as
the homeowners' written proposal permitted, before the homeowners communicated
withdrawal? Had the company acted within a reasonable time given the contemplated
financing process? If a contract had been formed and breached, what damages and attorney's
fees were recoverable under the agreement and the Louisiana Civil Code? Id.
Holding
Ever-Tite accepted within a reasonable time by commencing the specified performance. In
these circumstances, loading the trucks with materials and transporting the workers and
materials to the home was commencement, even though no roofing had yet been performed
on the house. That acceptance preceded the Greens' notice. Their subsequent refusal to
allow performance breached the completed contract. Id., discussion applying Civil Code arts.
1800–1809; casebook 226–227.
The company could recover its established expenses and expected profit. The contract's
provision for attorney's fees in collection proceedings did not authorize fees in this damages
action for breach. The court distinguished the scope of that particular clause from a general
provision covering all litigation arising from the relationship. Id., concluding damages and
fees discussion.
Actual judgment
The appellate court set aside the dismissal and entered judgment for Ever-Tite against both
homeowners for $311.37, with interest at five percent per year from judicial demand until
paid, and all costs. The award combined $85.37 of wasted mobilization expenses and $226 of
expected profit. Attorney's fees were not awarded. The disposition was reversed and
rendered, not simply reversed with an unspecified remand. Id., final paragraphs.

Reasoning
The written instrument controlled the permitted method of acceptance
The homeowners' signature was a proposal to which the contractor could assent in either of
two specified ways. The salesperson's signature was not enough because the representative
lacked the required authority. No authorized written acceptance had occurred. The relevant
question was therefore whether the company's conduct fulfilled the alternative method that
the proposal itself supplied. Id., quoted agreement and formal-acceptance discussion.
This makes the case different from one in which a court must infer that preparation alone
communicates agreement. Here the proposed terms expressly permitted acceptance by
commencing performance. The inquiry concerned which acts constituted that
commencement in the setting of a roofing operation requiring materials and a dispatched
crew. The court construed that language to include loading and transporting the necessary
materials and workers. Id., acceptance discussion.
The financing delay was reasonable
The instrument stated no fixed deadline for acceptance or beginning the work. The court
considered the nature of the proposed work and the parties' knowledge that financing
approval would take time. Ever-Tite requested and transmitted the relevant information with
diligence, and the additional lender inquiry explained the interval. The period before dispatch
was not an abandonment or an unreasonable delay that terminated the offer. Id., diligence
discussion; casebook 226.
The opinion quoted both a Restatement rule on lapse after a reasonable time and provisions
of the Louisiana Civil Code. Article 1809, as then stated, recognized revocation before
acceptance but required allowance of the reasonable time contemplated by the terms or
circumstances. The court's analysis therefore should not be reduced to an unqualified
nationwide common-law proposition that an offer can always be revoked instantly before any
performance. It applied the cited Louisiana framework and the particular written proposal.
Id., Civil Code discussion.
The communicated withdrawal came after acceptance
The trial court placed commencement at the home, effectively requiring the company to
begin physical work on the roof. The appellate court rejected that boundary. The loading and
transport were part of performance of the proposed roofing undertaking. Those acts
occurred before the Greens communicated their refusal to Ever-Tite's crew. At that point, the
offer and acceptance already constituted a completed contract. Id., acceptance discussion;
casebook 227.
The homeowners had hired another contractor earlier, but their undisclosed decision did not
itself notify Ever-Tite. Their claimed difficulty in contacting the company was also
unpersuasive given the contact information on the written form. The decision rests on the
sequence of communicated withdrawal and contractually authorized acceptance, not merely
on disapproval of using a competing roofer. Id., notice discussion.

Mobilization costs and lost profit represented the proved loss
Civil Code articles 1930 and 1934 supplied the remedial framework: a breaching party must
compensate the loss sustained and profit of which the other was deprived. Ever-Tite proved
$85.37 for loading, transportation, unloading on return, and workmen's wages during the
wasted trip. Its manager testified to expected profit of $226. No contrary evidence
undermined either component. Id., discussion of arts. 1930 and 1934.
The award combined the proved wasted expenditure with profit lost because the job could
not be carried out. The court did not award the entire roofing price in addition to all saved
costs, and it did not grant punitive damages for the homeowners' conduct. The actual
computation is important when connecting formation doctrine to the remedy that made the
dispute worth litigating. Id.
The collection clause did not cover the breach action
The form provided attorney's fees when placed with an attorney for collection or collected
through court. The court construed that language as addressing collection under the
agreement, rather than damages for preventing the roofing performance. With no applicable
agreement or law authorizing fees for this action, it denied them. The absence of fees was
therefore a separate legal ruling, not an accidental omission from the money judgment. Id.,
attorney's-fee discussion.
Separate opinions
No separate concurrence or dissent is reported. November 29 is the rehearing-denial date; it
should not displace November 2 as the date of the principal opinion.
Study analysis and limits
The comparison with White v. Corlies & Tifft turns on what mode of acceptance the offer
invited and what the conduct objectively signified. Generic work on materials at a builder's
shop may fail to manifest an acceptance in one setting; dispatch of an identified crew and
materials may satisfy an express commencement clause in another. Ever-Tite does not
announce that every preparatory purchase or loading act always forms a contract.
The no-cancellation language also should not be used to bypass the formation inquiry. The
court analyzed whether and when the proposal became binding; it did not simply reason that
the words made the document irrevocable from the homeowners' signature despite the
express acceptance condition. The controlling path was diligent processing, commencement
in the prescribed manner, and belated notice of withdrawal.
Finally, the full disposition illustrates why the original opinion must supplement the excerpt.
The casebook's abbreviated reversal omits the rendered amount, interest, costs, and denial of
attorney's fees. Those matters establish what the court actually gave the plaintiff and prevent
an otherwise plausible formation summary from misdescribing the judgment.
