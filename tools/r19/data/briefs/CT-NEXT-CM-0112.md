# Shadis v. Beal
685 F.2d 824 (3d Cir. July 20, 1982), amended Aug. 4, 1982 | Contracts

## Civil-rights litigation and funding restraints

Medically needy Pennsylvania residents brought a § 1983 class action in 1975 challenging termination of Medicaid benefits without notice or hearings and eligibility requirements that conflicted with federal law. The classes obtained declaratory and injunctive relief and essentially prevailed; a 1979 settlement was approved that December. Their counsel, Community Legal Services, then sought fees under 42 U.S.C. § 1988. The state opposed fees by invoking provisions in its legal-services funding arrangements. 685 F.2d at 826, 832 & n.1.

CLS was a nonprofit serving poor clients. About half its operating funds came directly from the federal Legal Services Corporation and half through Title XX, routed through the Pennsylvania welfare department and Pennsylvania Legal Services Center. The latter route combined federal funds with a twenty-five-percent state matching grant. Beginning in 1978–1979, the department required no-fee provisions in its contract with the Center; later it required corresponding restrictions in local programs’ contracts. The 1979–1980 CLS agreement prohibited requesting fees against the state or its employees and allowed awarded fees to be deducted from later invoices. The following agreement also prohibited accepting them. Id. at 826–828 & n.4.

CLS signed while expressly reserving objections. In July 1980, threatened loss of all funding, an unexpected $118,000 reimbursement reduction, and prior funding and staffing cuts intensified the pressure. Those circumstances supported a separate economic-duress argument, but the court expressly declined to decide it because public policy resolved the appeal. Earlier litigation concerning ratification of a no-fee provision and a later consent order preventing offset of a fee award supplied context; they were not the basis for treating this provision as enforceable. Id. at 827–828 & nn.5–6.

## Governing law and statutory policy

The district court invalidated the restraints and awarded fees. The parties agreed that, if recovery was permitted, $85,000 was reasonable; judgment in that amount followed. The appeal therefore concerned entitlement despite the contract, not an unresolved calculation of reasonable hours or rates. Id. at 826 & n.3.

Federal law controlled because the underlying claim and fee request arose under federal civil-rights statutes; the funding contract was raised defensively against that remedy. This was different from an ordinary contract suit merely touching federal grant funds. Pennsylvania public-policy law could be instructive, but it did not displace the controlling federal question. Id. at 828 & n.7.

Congress enacted § 1988 to encourage private enforcement of civil rights and compliance by potential defendants, including states. Its fee-shifting policy specifically included legal-aid organizations and clients represented without charge. Such organizations must allocate limited resources; potential awards both support difficult enforcement work and expand future services. The state’s subsidy did not eliminate those incentives or authorize it to buy protection from suits by an important portion of the civil-rights bar. The statute also supplied federal courts with authority over fee awards that contrary private arrangements could not defeat in this setting. Id. at 829–831 & nn.9–11.

## Enforcement interests and public-policy balance

The state argued for predictable budgets, protection of taxpayers, and avoiding payment for legal services twice. The court recognized an interest in fixed funding but distinguished a subsidy for general legal services from a fee obligation incurred as a losing civil-rights defendant. The latter paid litigation costs associated with a statutory violation, rather than repeating the grant. General advance funding did not create the same incentive for the state to obey civil-rights law or settle appropriately. Public enforcement benefited taxpayers too. Id. at 832–833 & nn.12–13.

Restatement §§ 178 and 179 supported balancing the parties’ justified expectations, forfeiture, and public enforcement interests against the strength, effectiveness, seriousness, and directness of the contrary policy. The state insisted that only an express statutory ban on the contract, or a directly contradictory mandated performance, could justify nonenforcement. The court rejected that limit. A statute’s purpose, history, and entire scheme could establish a sufficiently strong policy against a term even when the term was not literally prohibited. Earlier precedent upholding unrelated reimbursement clauses lacked the contradictory policy present here. Id. at 833–834 & nn.14–15.

## Disposition and source scope

Judge James Hunter III wrote for a panel with Gibbons and visiting District Judge Gerry; no separate opinion appears. The court held the no-fee provisions void as contrary to the federal policy underlying § 1988 and affirmed the fee judgment. It did not void all funding obligations or decide economic duress. Id. at 834 & n.6.
