# Executive Software North America, Inc. v. United States District Court

**Civil Procedure · CP-039 · 24 F.3d 1545 (9th Cir. 1994), revised opinion filed May 16, 1994.** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, PDF pp. 209–211, printed pp. 374–378; relocated editorial notes n–o at PDF p. 219. The January opinion at 15 F.3d 1484 and its dissent were withdrawn and replaced. This brief addresses the revised decision.

## The dispute and the order under review

Donna Page, a Black woman employed by Executive Software, alleged that the company required employees to study Church of Scientology teachings written by L. Ron Hubbard. She said that after refusing, she was accused of work errors, denied an opportunity to contest the accusations, and fired. According to her complaint, the accusations concealed religious, racial, and sex discrimination. These were allegations in an employment suit, not facts established by the court of appeals. (Majority ¶ 2.)

Page sued the company and Craig and Sally Jensen in state court on April 8, 1993. Her federal claims invoked Title VII and § 1983. Her three state claims alleged religious and racial discrimination under California's Fair Employment and Housing Act, wrongful termination contrary to the California Constitution, and negligent supervision. The defendants removed the action because of the federal claims. The district court then issued a May 20 order asking why it should not return the state claims to state court while keeping the federal claims. (¶¶ 2–5.)

The order listed reasons associated with United Mine Workers v. Gibbs: predominance of state claims, novel or complex state law, jury confusion, and the expenditure of substantial additional judicial time. But it also expressly said that Congress's 1990 supplemental-jurisdiction statute, § 1367, did not restrict Gibbs discretion; in the district court's view, Congress merely repaired Finley's restriction on jurisdiction over additional parties. The court subsequently remanded all three state claims without explaining which listed reason supported its decision. (¶¶ 4–7.)

The defendants sought mandamus to undo that partial remand. Page substantially agreed with their arguments; the majority's footnote 1 therefore uses “Petitioners” for arguments advanced by both sides. They argued that § 1367 required a case-specific statutory analysis and an explanation, and that a proper analysis would not justify remand. The appellate majority accepted the first objection but did not decide that the state claims necessarily had to remain in federal court. (¶¶ 1, 63, 70; n.1.)

## The legal framework and the questions

The central question was how § 1367 changed the discretion previously recognized in Gibbs. Section 1367(a) grants supplemental jurisdiction over related claims forming the same Article III case or controversy. Subsection (b) restricts certain claims in actions founded solely on diversity. Neither subsection (b) nor an express federal statutory prohibition prevented jurisdiction here. The dispute therefore concerned subsection (c), not the existence of the underlying jurisdictional power. (¶¶ 38–45; nn.7–8.)

Subsection (c) allows a district court to decline supplemental jurisdiction when a state claim raises a novel or complex state-law issue, substantially predominates, all claims within original jurisdiction have been dismissed, or exceptional circumstances present other compelling reasons. Gibbs supplied the familiar values of judicial economy, convenience, fairness, and comity. The interpretive issue was whether those values remained an independent, unrestricted basis for declining jurisdiction, or instead informed decisions within the statute's four categories. A second question was whether the unexplained remand amounted to the clear legal error necessary for extraordinary mandamus relief.

## The majority's holding and reasoning

Judge D. W. Nelson, joined by Judge Tang, held that once supplemental jurisdiction is available under subsections (a) and (b), subsection (c) supplies the exclusive grounds for declining it. The statute's mandatory grant, its express exceptions, and the need to give subsection (c) independent work all supported that reading. Treating Gibbs as a separate source of unrestricted discretion would largely bypass Congress's enumeration. (¶¶ 39–45.)

The court nevertheless retained Gibbs's values. The statute changes their place in the analysis: a judge must identify a factual predicate fitting a subsection (c) category, then exercise discretion in light of economy, convenience, fairness, and comity. The first three categories largely codify established applications of those values. The presence of a category authorizes discretion; it does not automatically dictate remand. Nor must a court add subsection (c)(4)'s separate exceptional-circumstances requirement to categories (1)–(3). (¶¶ 48–49; n.9.)

Category (4) leaves room for new applications but imposes two related requirements. “Other compelling reasons” refers to reasons comparable to the values embodied in the established categories. “Exceptional circumstances” separately requires the judge to explain why the situation is sufficiently unusual to justify going beyond categories (1)–(3). A conclusion that remand would be convenient cannot alone establish that the circumstances are exceptional. Otherwise those distinct statutory terms would collapse into one unrestricted balancing test. The majority found this reading consistent with the House Report's recognition of occasional additional compelling reasons in exceptional circumstances. (¶¶ 50–59.)

This distinction matters in practice. Ordinary supplemental claims commonly require additional work. Allowing that fact alone to justify remand could turn Congress's usual exercise of jurisdiction into the exception. The district court's reference to extra judicial time might have been an attempt to invoke a broader Gibbs ground without satisfying subsection (c)(4). If it simply meant easing docket congestion, that was impermissible. The majority did not, however, resolve whether jury confusion falls within subsection (c)(2), subsection (c)(4), or otherwise: footnote 14 expressly reserved that disagreement. (¶¶ 61–62; nn.14–15.)

The court also rejected the petitioners' categorical claim that every failure to give written reasons itself violates the statute. An unexplained decision might still be sustainable if its permissible basis were apparent from the record. Here the district court had announced an incorrect understanding of the statute, mixed recognized statutory grounds with potentially impermissible ones, and supplied no explanation of its ultimate choice. The appellate court could not determine that it had selected an authorized ground and performed the required analysis. That combination established clear error. (¶¶ 63–64.)

## Why mandamus issued, and its limited result

In this 1994 decision the court treated discretionary remand as outside § 1447(d)'s prohibition on review of remands for the grounds specified in § 1447(c). It also concluded that this order did not qualify for ordinary collateral-order review and could instead be reviewed through mandamus. Failure to seek § 1292(b) certification did not automatically bar that extraordinary remedy. These procedural conclusions must be read with the later-treatment limitation below. (¶¶ 7–14.)

The court applied the Bauman considerations governing mandamus, using clear legal error rather than ordinary abuse-of-discretion review. Splitting the federal and state claims could impose burdens that an eventual appeal could no longer repair. The relationship between Gibbs and the new statute was also an important question of first impression affecting numerous cases. The court did not decide whether certification under § 1292(b) was actually available here; even assuming it was, the remaining circumstances favored relief. (¶¶ 16–18, 66–69.)

**The writ was granted and the district court's remand order was vacated.** This required a proper reconsideration of supplemental jurisdiction, not an adjudication of Page's discrimination claims and not an unconditional command to keep every state claim. The majority expressly left the district court responsible for assessing supplemental jurisdiction at each stage under the correct statutory framework. (¶¶ 70–72.)

## Leavy's dissent

Judge Leavy would deny mandamus. He agreed that clear legal error was necessary but objected to treating uncertainty about the district court's ground as proof of such error. The order had listed grounds expressly found in the statute, and jury confusion or unusually substantial additional work could also supply compelling reasons. Without knowing the actual basis, the majority could not establish that the district court exceeded its authority. (Dissent ¶¶ 74–87.)

Leavy also emphasized the difference between a statute saying a court “shall have” supplemental jurisdiction and one commanding that it “shall exercise” it. He understood Congress to codify the existing discretionary doctrine, supported by legislative materials and other circuits' decisions. He feared intrusive appellate supervision through mandamus whenever a district court exercised, declined, or inadequately explained its discretion. His disagreement therefore concerned both the statutory understanding and the extraordinary-remedy standard, not the merits of Page's employment allegations. (¶¶ 89–97.) The panel divided 2–1; the source contains no additional separate writing.

The checked alternative pinpoints above are that reproduction's numbered judicial paragraphs, rather than invented reporter-page divisions.

Powerex Corp., 533 F.3d 1087 (9th Cir. 2008), overruled this decision's mandamus route in favor of an appeal under § 1291.
