# International Minerals & Chemical Corp. v. Llano, Inc.
770 F.2d 879 (10th Cir. 1985) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
The Tenth Circuit reversed a judgment requiring a gas purchaser to pay $3,441,869.79 for untaken gas and remanded for a declaratory judgment consistent with its opinion. Judge Barrett wrote for Judges Doyle and McKay as well; no separate writing is reported. The August 9, 1985 decision distinguishes an unsuccessful general force-majeure defense from a successful contractual minimum-bill adjustment. (879–881, 885–887.)

## Contract and changed circumstances
IMC operated a potash mine and refinery near Carlsbad, New Mexico, supplied by Llano under a 1972 gas contract lasting until June 30, 1982. The contract covered plant fuel requirements but imposed a daily minimum of 4,800 million BTUs and an annual minimum of 355 times that daily figure. If IMC took less, it had to pay for the deficiency. This take-or-pay allocation compensated Llano for readiness to supply gas and protected it against falling requirements. A separate provision allowed a specified reduction in the daily minimum on six months' notice following partial plant closure, with a price adjustment. (881–883.)

Paragraph 15 excused performance prevented by listed force-majeure events, including governmental interference and causes beyond reasonable control, subject to notice and reasonable preventive or mitigating steps. Paragraph 16 separately required an appropriate adjustment of paragraph 7's minimum purchase requirements when the seller could not deliver or the buyer could not receive gas for reasons beyond reasonable control, or when paragraph 15 force majeure occurred. These provisions had to be read together with the minimum-payment obligation rather than treated as redundant general excuses. (882, 885–886.)

Nine gas-fired evaporators produced fertilizer and emitted substantial particulates. Regulation 508, promulgated by the New Mexico Environmental Improvement Board in December 1978, imposed emissions limits. The ordinary compliance deadline was December 31, 1982; replacement technology could meet an intermediate limit then and the final limit by December 31, 1984. IMC participated in developing the alternative provisions. Scrubbers and other equipment presented technical problems: fine particles, clogged filters and sludge, or plugged and corroded tubing. A proposed solar process also proved infeasible after experimentation. IMC then successfully developed a chemical salting-out process under an amended compliance schedule. In March 1981 it reported that the new process was commercially operational and that the old evaporators could be eliminated. (883–884 & n. 1.)

The evaporators had consumed approximately 60 percent of plant gas. Their shutdown left IMC below its gas minimum during January 1981 through June 1982. IMC had telephoned Llano in May and August 1980 about testing and reduced consumption, but had not explained the environmental reasons, identified the potential permanent reduction, or identified a force-majeure situation. Llano sold the untaken gas elsewhere at a higher price. (883–884.)

## Procedure and questions presented
IMC sought a declaration of excuse, and Llano counterclaimed for $3,564,617.12. The trial court rejected the buyer's defenses and awarded the somewhat smaller sum stated above. It read UCC § 2-615 primarily as a seller's defense and construed the contractual clauses to require absolute impossibility or illegality of buying the gas. It also regarded IMC's early voluntary compliance and regulatory cooperation as failures to exhaust available means of performance. (881, 884.)

On appeal IMC invoked common-law and statutory impracticability, paragraphs 15 and 16, an allegedly penal minimum-payment term, unconscionability, and errors in damages computation. The appellate court considered only the two parts of its first contention. The judgment therefore does not independently establish that the payment clause was a penalty, that the contract was unconscionable, or that the resale proceeds would dictate a particular damages calculation. (884.)

## Holding and reasoning
The starting point was reasonable construction of the parties' contract under New Mexico law. Impracticability and UCC § 2-615 provided the commercial background for interpreting the agreed exemptions. Note 2 reproduces the statute and treats Official Comment 8 as persuasive interpretive authority, not statutory text. The court's holding rests on construing the buyer's contractual protection; it does not simply erase the distinction between sellers and buyers in the statute's language. (884–885 & n. 2.)

Paragraph 15 did not excuse the payment obligation. First, the telephone notice omitted the pertinent reasons for decreased consumption and did not trigger that clause's protection. Second, even assuming the regulation prevented taking gas, it did not prevent paying. A promise capable of performance through alternative methods is not discharged merely because one alternative becomes impracticable while the other remains practicable. IMC could take and pay, or pay the minimum bill without taking. Thus the general force-majeure clause, at most, excused taking. (885.)

Paragraph 16 supplied the additional protection that mattered. It explicitly adjusted paragraph 7's minimum requirements; because the minimum bill depended on those requirements, lowering them also lowered the bill. Llano's proposal to adjust the quantity without adjusting the payment contradicted that relationship. Nor did the word “unable” demand scientific impossibility. A buyer could physically receive and vent gas even after destruction of its facilities; that literal construction would make the protection commercially absurd. In context, inability meant impracticability involving extreme or unreasonable difficulty, expense, injury, or loss, measured against the parties' reasonable contemplation. (886.)

The trial findings established no technically suitable way to comply with Regulation 508 without shutting down the evaporators and adopting the lower-gas process. Paragraph 16 was therefore triggered for January 1, 1981 through June 30, 1982. IMC should not have to pay for the gas it had not taken during that period. The later final deadline did not require it to stall environmental compliance. Cooperation with proper regulation deserved encouragement, and governmental policy need not invariably take an explicitly mandatory form to create impracticability. The court nevertheless preserved a good-faith limit: causing or collusively inducing governmental action to escape one's contract would defeat excuse. IMC's cooperation toward the Clean Air Act's public-health objective was not improper collusion. (886–887.)

## Exact disposition and source scope
The judgment for Llano was reversed and the case remanded with directions to enter the appropriate declaratory judgment. The appellate court neither fixed a new monetary award nor resolved the other asserted defenses.
