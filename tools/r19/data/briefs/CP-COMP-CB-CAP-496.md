# Lauro Lines S.R.L. v. Chasser

**490 U.S. 495 (1989).** Justice Brennan wrote for a unanimous Supreme Court on May 22, 1989. Justice Scalia joined that opinion and concurred separately. The assigned account is CivPro physical page 620, printed page 1227, Note 1(c).

## Facts and procedural path

Passengers on the Achille Lauro, and representatives of passengers’ estates, sued the vessel’s Italian owner, Lauro Lines, in federal court in New York. The claims sought compensation for injuries from the Mediterranean hijacking in October 1985 and for the wrongful death of Leon Klinghoffer. Each passenger’s ticket contained a clause purporting to require litigation connected with the contract in Naples, Italy, and to renounce suit elsewhere. The question before the Supreme Court concerned appellate access to a forum ruling, not liability for the hijacking or Klinghoffer’s death. (496.)

Lauro Lines moved to dismiss based on that clause. The district court held that the ticket as a whole did not reasonably notify passengers that they were waiving suit in a domestic forum. The company appealed without seeking certification under §1292(b). The Second Circuit dismissed because the denial was interlocutory and did not qualify as a collateral final order under §1291. The Supreme Court granted review to resolve disagreement among circuits about immediate appeal of denials of contractual forum-selection motions. (497.)

## Issue and holding

Was denial of the motion to dismiss immediately appealable under the collateral-order doctrine? The Court held that it was not, because the asserted contractual right remained sufficiently vindicable on appeal after final judgment. The Court did not uphold the district court’s determination that notice was inadequate as a substantive ruling, or decide whether the clause was ultimately enforceable. It affirmed dismissal of the **appeal**, rather than affirming a final merits judgment against the shipowner. (498–501.)

Ordinary finality was absent: denying dismissal kept the litigation going rather than ending it. The company therefore needed the narrow collateral-order exception. That required a conclusive decision, an important issue entirely separate from the merits, and effective unreviewability after final judgment. The Court expressly left the first two conditions undecided. Failure to satisfy the third disposed of the case. (497–498.)

## A right to one forum differed from immunity from suit

The company argued that the contract supplied both a right to trial in Italy and a corresponding right not to be sued elsewhere. Once a trial occurred in New York, an appeal could not undo the inconvenience, expense, and loss of its selected forum. The Court acknowledged that an eventual correction would not perfectly preserve the bargain and could leave the company with unnecessary trouble and expense. But those features appear whenever an interlocutory ruling is mistaken. Litigation costs and the advantage of winning before trial do not, by themselves, overcome Congress’s finality requirement. The exception required a right essentially destroyed by postponing vindication. (499–501.)

The opinion contrasted rights not to be tried at all with rights to insist on litigation elsewhere. The first category included the constitutional protections and immunities discussed in the precedents: double jeopardy, Speech or Debate protection, and civil immunity from suit. A claim that a particular court lacked jurisdiction did not necessarily confer such an immunity. Van Cauwenberghe, for example, had distinguished immunity from civil service of process following extradition from immunity from the underlying suit. These were comparisons used to define the right at issue, not fresh holdings on the scope of every immunity. (499–500.)

The Court recognized that the forum clause could be characterized in different ways: a right not to be sued anywhere except Naples, or a right to have the binding adjudication occur there. Even accepting the company’s more favorable first formulation, it was not a right to avoid suit altogether. That distinction was substantive, rather than something a litigant could overcome simply by calling its forum preference a right not to stand trial. A claim to Naples was at least as vindicable after judgment as an objection to the trial court’s personal jurisdiction. It therefore failed the effective-unreviewability requirement. (500–501.)

## Enforcement policy did not itself create an immediate appeal

Lauro Lines invoked The Bremen and asserted a strong federal policy favoring foreign forum-selection clauses. It argued that immediate review was an essential companion to meaningful enforcement. The Court separated that asserted policy from the jurisdictional question. It could support the company’s position on the clause’s enforceability and ultimate merits, but the third collateral-order condition turned on the right’s contours, not the likelihood that the company would eventually win. A persuasive substantive defense was not automatically an appealable interlocutory order. (501.)

The distinction preserved two separate questions: whether the passengers were bound by this ticket’s clause, and when a ruling on that issue could be reviewed. The Supreme Court answered the second. Its conclusion did not decide that a foreign forum clause could never be enforced, or establish that this particular clause was invalid. Nor did it decide a certification route the company had not requested. (497, 501.)

## Scalia’s concurrence and exact judgment

Scalia agreed with the Court but made explicit an importance judgment he thought implicit in the analysis. In his view, after-the-fact reversal did not literally preserve the right to avoid a New York trial: once trial occurred, that particular entitlement had been violated. The reason later vindication was legally adequate was that the law did not regard this forum right as sufficiently important to overcome the policies against interlocutory appeals. Importance had been central to Cohen from the start. He compared contractual forum rights with jurisdictional limitations imposed by legislation or treaty and considered the case for immediate appeal no stronger when the asserted right came from private agreement. (502–503.)

That emphasis should not be attributed as a separate majority holding that both the second and third collateral-order conditions failed. Brennan’s opinion expressly declined to decide the first two conditions and resolved the third. Scalia joined it while explaining why adequacy of later review entailed a value judgment about the particular right. (498, 502–503.)

The Court **affirmed the Second Circuit’s dismissal of the appeal**. The federal damages litigation could continue without this immediate §1291 review. The complete decision contains no dissent, judicial footnotes, or appendix. (501–503.)

## Assigned account and source scope

The assigned paragraph correctly describes the request for an Italian forum and the refusal to allow immediate collateral-order appeal. The complete source adds the district court’s notice rationale, the absence of a §1292(b) request, the expressly unresolved first two collateral-order conditions, and Scalia’s explanation of importance. Those qualifications keep a timing decision distinct from the contract’s substantive enforceability.
