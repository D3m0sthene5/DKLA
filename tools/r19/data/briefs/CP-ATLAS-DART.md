# Dart Cherokee Basin Operating Co., LLC v. Owens

**Civil Procedure · 574 U.S. 81 (2014) · Supreme Court of the United States · December 15, 2014**

Justice Ginsburg wrote for a **5–4 Court**, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Breyer, Alito, and Sotomayor. Justice Scalia dissented, joined by Justices Kennedy and Kagan and by Justice Thomas except for the final sentence. Thomas also wrote separately. The Court held that a removal notice needs a plausible allegation of the jurisdictional amount, rather than supporting evidence attached to the notice. It vacated the Tenth Circuit’s denial of permission to appeal and remanded, finding that denial infected by the district court’s erroneous legal requirement. The divided opinions principally concern whether the Court could reach the merits through that discretionary appellate posture.

## Facts and procedural history

Brandon Owens filed a putative class action in Kansas state court against Dart Cherokee Basin Operating Company and Cherokee Basin Pipeline. He alleged that they had underpaid royalties owed under oil and gas leases and requested fair and reasonable compensation without specifying a dollar amount. Dart removed to the federal district court under CAFA, alleging the required class size and minimal diversity and stating that the underpayments exceeded $8.2 million. **574 U.S. at 84–85.**

Owens moved to remand because the notice contained no evidence establishing more than $5 million in controversy. Dart responded with an executive’s detailed declaration calculating more than $11 million, excluding interest. Owens did not challenge that calculation; he argued that the evidence came too late to cure the original notice. Reading Tenth Circuit precedent to require proof within the removal notice itself, the district court remanded. **Id. at 85.**

CAFA permits a court of appeals to accept an appeal from an order granting or denying remand, notwithstanding the ordinary restrictions on reviewing remand orders. A divided Tenth Circuit panel denied Dart permission to appeal, and an evenly divided court denied rehearing en banc. Judge Hartz’s dissent from that denial warned that the rule would effectively become entrenched because future defendants would supply evidence rather than risk remand. The Supreme Court granted certiorari on the notice’s required contents. **Id. at 85–87.**

## The removal pleading rule

Section 1446(a) requires a short and plain statement of the grounds for removal. The Court held that this requirement calls for a plausible allegation of the amount in controversy. It does not require the removing defendant to submit evidence with the notice. A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction can rely on a good-faith amount allegation; similarly, a defendant’s allegation should be accepted unless the plaintiff contests it or the court questions it. **Id. at 87–89.**

The statutory language deliberately borrows Rule 8’s pleading formulation. The corroborating legislative history explains that Congress wanted the ordinary liberal pleading rules to govern removal allegations, rather than detailed evidentiary pleading. Requiring defendants to prove an amount within the notice while allowing plaintiffs to allege it would impose an unexplained asymmetry. **Id. at 87–88.**

Evidence remains necessary when the allegation is disputed. Section 1446(c)(2)(B) supplies a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard for determining the jurisdictional amount. In that event, both sides submit proof and the court decides whether the threshold is met. The decision therefore distinguishes adequate initial pleading from proving jurisdiction after a challenge; it does not eliminate the jurisdictional threshold or the need for proof when genuinely questioned. **Id. at 88–89.**

Footnote 1 preserves a statutory assumption. Sections 1446(c)(2) and (c)(2)(B) expressly refer to jurisdiction under § 1332(a), whereas this case involved CAFA jurisdiction under § 1332(d). Because the parties did not dispute application of those provisions to CAFA, the Court assumed their application without deciding that point. The brief’s statement of the proof procedure retains that qualification. **Id. at 88 n.1.**

The district court also relied partly on a presumption against removal. The Court rejected such a presumption for CAFA, which Congress enacted to facilitate federal adjudication of certain interstate class actions. It expressly left undecided whether an antiremoval presumption is appropriate in ordinary diversity cases. **Id. at 89.**

## Why the Court could review the appellate refusal

An amicus, Public Citizen, raised a problem the parties had not presented: the Tenth Circuit had declined permission to appeal, so its exercise of discretion, rather than the district court’s merits ruling, was the decision before the Supreme Court. Unless that refusal itself warranted reversal, the Court could not simply review the remand ruling. **Id. at 89–90.**

The majority concluded that Dart’s application for permission made the case one in the court of appeals within § 1254. It therefore had jurisdiction to review what the Tenth Circuit did with that application. Discretionary review still must be exercised on a legally sound basis; relying on an erroneous view of law can constitute an abuse of discretion. **Id. at 90–91 & nn.2–3.**

The majority inferred that the Tenth Circuit’s denial rested on acceptance of the district court’s legal premise. The parties’ submissions focused on whether evidence had to accompany the notice, and the denial said it had considered those submissions and applicable law. The circuit’s own guidance favored hearing important, recurring CAFA issues likely to escape later review. Leaving this remand undisturbed would encourage responsible defendants to comply with the evidentiary demand rather than generate another appeal. The majority thus considered the notice-content error and the abuse of appellate discretion closely connected. **Id. at 91–95 & nn.4–7.**

This was an inference from an unexplained denial, not an express statement by the Tenth Circuit that it adopted the district court’s reasoning. Footnote 7 acknowledges the need for caution while defending an inference fairly supported by the record. Most importantly, footnote 8 leaves the Tenth Circuit free on remand to identify and explain a permissible basis for declining Dart’s appeal. The Supreme Court did not make discretionary acceptance mandatory irrespective of any valid ground the circuit might supply. **Id. at 95 n.7, 96 n.8.**

## Scalia’s dissent

Scalia would have dismissed certiorari as improvidently granted. Failing that, he would have affirmed the Tenth Circuit because the record did not establish an abuse of discretion. Thomas joined the dissent except for that final proposed affirmance, consistent with his separate jurisdictional objection. **Id. at 96–102.**

Scalia argued that the majority attributed an unexpressed, erroneous reason to a discretionary denial that could have rested on legitimate grounds. The circuit might have considered the case a poor vehicle, doubted its ability to decide within CAFA’s expedited period, or viewed the question as one of general removal pleading rather than a distinct CAFA issue. Considering the parties’ arguments was not the same as adopting them. Deferential review should not presume an incorrect basis when an order permits a correct reading. **Id. at 97–99.**

He also disputed the prediction that the issue could never recur. Lawyers could make mistakes, or a later appeal about insufficient evidence could present the broader question whether evidence was required at all. He criticized the majority’s treatment of briefing omissions: Dart had not meaningfully argued abuse of appellate discretion until its reply, yet the majority faulted Owens for failing earlier to refute that argument. Scalia’s dissent challenged the Court’s route to the merits; it should not be characterized as a holding that removal notices must contain evidence. **Id. at 99–102.**

## Thomas’s separate dissent

Thomas raised a more fundamental objection: § 1254 authorized review of cases in the courts of appeals, and an unsuccessful application for discretionary permission was not itself such a case. He disagreed with extending the Court’s certificate-of-appealability precedent to this situation and also considered that precedent wrongly decided. Unlike a habeas certificate application, permission to appeal a remand could be denied without assessing the underlying merits. Because the Tenth Circuit had not accepted the appeal, Thomas would have dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. **Id. at 102–104.**

## Judgment and assigned significance

The Court **vacated the Tenth Circuit’s judgment and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion**. It did not award royalties, certify a class, or decide the truth of the alleged underpayments. It rejected the district court’s requirement of proof inside the notice while preserving the evidentiary procedure for a contested amount and the circuit’s opportunity to explain a lawful reason for declining review. **Id. at 89, 96 & n.8.**
