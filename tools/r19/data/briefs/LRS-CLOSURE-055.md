# United States House of Representatives v. Mnuchin; later Yellen disposition
976 F.3d 1 (D.C. Cir. 2020); 142 S. Ct. 332 (2021) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and requested relief

After extended disagreement with President Trump about border-wall funding, Congress appropriated $1.375 billion for specified pedestrian fencing in the Rio Grande Valley, with restrictions on locations and permissible designs. The President signed the legislation but announced that he would obtain additional resources through executive action. He identified the Treasury Forfeiture Fund, Defense Department counterdrug funding under 10 U.S.C. § 284, and military-construction resources under § 2808. The House challenged the latter two sources, not the forfeiture fund. Its central allegation was that the Executive planned to spend $8.1 billion despite Congress’s authorization of only $1.375 billion. The unchallenged source did not supply enough to cover the contested expenditure and therefore did not make the case moot at this stage. (Official panel opinion 3–5.)

The counterdrug appropriation was $517.171 million, much of which the House believed had already been spent. The President transferred $1 billion in March 2019 and an additional $1.5 billion in May, invoking §§ 8005 and 9002 of the 2019 Defense appropriations legislation. The House alleged that the statutory conditions for transfers were not met. The President also planned to redirect $3.6 billion under § 2808, which authorizes certain military construction necessary to support armed forces during a national emergency requiring their use. He declared such an emergency on February 15, 2019, and Congress did not override it. Those statutory predicates and their alleged absence were pleaded matters, not appellate merits findings that the transfers were illegal. (Opinion 5–6.)

On April 5, 2019, the House sued the departments and their Secretaries, seeking declaratory and injunctive relief under the Appropriations Clause and the Administrative Procedure Act. The district court denied a preliminary injunction for want of a cognizable injury and dismissed the case. It allowed an amended complaint adding a § 9002 transfer claim, again found no standing, and entered final judgment. The House appealed. (Opinion 4–7.)

## Procedural stage and judgment

The September 25, 2020 decision was a **panel opinion** by Senior Judge Sentelle, joined by Judges Millett and Wilkins, with no separate writing or judicial footnote. Although the appeal had been reheard en banc, it was returned to the original panel on August 7 after the Circuit’s en banc McGahn decision rejected a categorical bar against a House suit alleging injury to its own legislative rights. The opinion’s caption preserves both the en banc rehearing and the remand to the panel; that history does not make this September judgment an en banc opinion. (Caption; opinion 2, 10–11.)

The panel **affirmed the dismissal of the APA claims, vacated the dismissal of the constitutional claims, and remanded for further proceedings**. It did not order an injunction against the transfers or finally decide whether the Executive had spending authority. Review of the jurisdictional dismissal was de novo. Because the matter was evaluated under the dismissal standard, the court accepted material allegations, drew reasonable inferences for the House, and assumed the correctness of its legal position on the underlying merits for purposes of determining standing. That analytical assumption should not be converted into a substantive judgment. (Opinion 3, 7–8, 20, 24.)

## The House’s distinct appropriations injury

The House contended that each chamber has an independent power to block unauthorized expenditure. Appropriations require legislative approval; absent that approval, Treasury money is unavailable. This differs from a chamber demanding enforcement of a law it could not enact alone. A refusal to approve spending is effective without the other chamber’s agreement. The defendants argued that any appropriations injury belonged only to Congress as a whole and that one chamber could not sue independently. (Opinion 8–10.)

The court used constitutional structure and founding history to explain why the House’s alleged injury was its own. The Appropriations Clause requires affirmative authorization for expenditure, rather than permitting expenditure unless Congress prohibits it. Separating the legislative purse from the executive sword checks executive power. The historical image of two purse strings illustrates that each chamber can refuse the necessary consent. The customary origination of appropriations measures in the House illustrated its role but did not itself create an Article III interest. (Opinion 10–13.)

The panel reviewed Coleman, Raines, Arizona State Legislature and Bethune-Hill. Together, those cases directed attention to whether the challenged conduct curtailed a legislative institution’s power and whether the plaintiff was the institution possessing that power. Individual legislators’ abstract loss of influence in Raines differed from an institutional body’s nullified authority. Bethune-Hill denied a single chamber standing to assert an interest assigned to the whole legislature; it did not categorically prevent a chamber from protecting a prerogative belonging to that chamber independently. The opinion also recognized the relevance of historical practice, political remedies and separation-of-powers concerns, while relying on the recently controlling McGahn en banc decision rather than redoing its entire analysis. (Opinion 13–19.)

On the assumed allegations, the Executive had removed the House from a constitutionally indispensable decision. The House had withheld additional wall funding and calibrated the spending it did authorize. Spending despite that decision allegedly nullified the House’s independent withholding power. The court’s two-keys analogy captured this particular injury: the House held one of the keys needed to authorize Treasury expenditure, and the Executive allegedly took away that key. The institution bringing suit matched the institution whose consent had been bypassed. (Opinion 19–21.)

Rejecting that injury would invert the constitutional arrangement. The Executive would effectively be free to spend until both chambers enacted a veto-proof prohibition, instead of being unable to spend without prior authorization. Even a requirement that both chambers sue together would deny the House’s unilateral ability to withhold consent. Applying especially rigorous standing analysis in an interbranch dispute therefore reinforced, rather than defeated, recognition of this claimed institutional injury. The holding concerned the House’s concrete constitutional prerogative, not a general entitlement to litigate every executive disagreement with Congress. (Opinion 21–23.)

## Why the APA claims remained dismissed

The court treated the APA allegations separately. A claim that the President had exceeded statutory authority did not identify an injury distinct to the House. Once legislation is enacted, a legislative body’s interest in having the Executive comply with that statute is insufficient by itself to establish a particularized injury. The alleged constitutional bypass of the House’s appropriations veto and the alleged violation of transfer statutes were therefore not interchangeable standing theories. The panel rejected standing for the APA claims while permitting further proceedings on the constitutional claims. (Opinion 23–24.)

## The later Supreme Court judgment and the assigned account

On **October 12, 2021**, in No. 20-1738, Yellen v. United States House of Representatives, the Supreme Court granted certiorari, **vacated the judgment**, and remanded to the D.C. Circuit **with instructions to dismiss the case as moot**, citing Munsingwear. This is a distinct later judgment, not part of the 2020 panel opinion. The complete summary order states no reasons beyond that disposition and citation, contains no judicial notes, and records no separate opinion or numerical vote. It does not decide that the panel’s standing analysis was substantively correct or incorrect. The vacated 2020 judgment must not be described as a presently operative merits or jurisdictional judgment. (Official October 12, 2021 order list 1, No. 20-1738.)

The assigned November-b PDF 125, printed 1182, contains a dedicated paragraph about Mnuchin’s specific institutional injury and expressly supplies the later Yellen citation. Both stages are preserved here. The textbook’s shorthand that Mnuchin was declared moot should be read with the actual order’s more exact vacatur and remand instruction.
