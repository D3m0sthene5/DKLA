# Mistretta v. United States
488 U.S. 361 (1989) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and assigned setting

The Supreme Court decided Nos. 87–7028 and 87–1904 on January 18, 1989, after granting certiorari before judgment in the Eighth Circuit. Justice Blackmun wrote for eight Justices: Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Brennan, White, Marshall, Stevens, O’Connor, and Kennedy joined, although Brennan did not join footnote 11. Justice Scalia alone dissented. The Court affirmed the Western District of Missouri’s judgment and upheld the Sentencing Reform Act against the delegation and separation-of-powers challenges presented. 361–363, 370–371, 412.

## Sentencing system and statutory design

Before the reform, Congress prescribed broad sentencing ranges, district judges selected sentences within them, and executive parole officials often determined the actual duration of imprisonment. Judges’ individualized determinations received great appellate deference. Congress became dissatisfied with large differences among similarly situated offenders, uncertainty about time actually served, and the limitations of rehabilitation and parole as correctives. Its earlier sentencing institutes and the Parole Commission’s guidelines had not eliminated those problems. 363–367.

The 1984 Act replaced that arrangement with basically determinate sentences, reduced by good-behavior credit, and prospectively abolished parole under the timetable then prescribed. Congress rejected both rigid fixed sentences and merely advisory guidelines. The Commission’s guidelines bound sentencing courts, while permitting departures for aggravating or mitigating circumstances not adequately considered by the Commission. Judges had to explain sentences and give specific reasons for departures. The statute provided limited appeals, including defendants’ challenges to above-range sentences, government challenges to below-range sentences, and either side’s challenges to incorrect guideline applications. 367–368. These descriptions concern the statutory system reviewed in 1989.

Congress created an independent Commission within the Judicial Branch, with seven voting members appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate. At least three had to be federal judges, selected after consideration of a six-judge list from the Judicial Conference. No more than four could belong to the same political party. The Attorney General or a designee served without a vote. Voting members ordinarily served six-year terms, with no more than two full terms; the President could remove them only for neglect of duty, malfeasance, or other good cause. The statute also temporarily included the Parole Commission’s chair as a nonvoting member. 368–369 & n.4.

The Commission had to formulate and revise guidelines, consult criminal-justice authorities, report amendments and annual operational analyses to Congress, recommend changes in statutory penalties, issue policy statements, collect sentencing records, and conduct training and related administrative work. The scale mattered: courts supplied records for more than 40,000 sentences annually. 369–370.

## Facts, procedure, and competing claims

Mistretta and another defendant were indicted on December 10, 1987, on three counts arising from a cocaine sale. He sought a ruling that the Guidelines were unconstitutional because Congress had delegated excessive legislative discretion and created a Commission inconsistent with separation of powers. The district court rejected those arguments. Its explanation characterized the Commission as executive despite the statute’s judicial placement, and analogized the Guidelines to other agencies’ substantive rules. The Supreme Court’s own analysis did not simply adopt that characterization. 370.

Mistretta pleaded guilty to conspiracy to distribute cocaine under 21 U.S.C. §§ 846 and 841(b)(1)(B); the other counts were dismissed on the Government’s motion. His guideline sentence was eighteen months’ imprisonment, followed by three years’ supervised release, a $1,000 fine, and a $50 special assessment. After he appealed to the Eighth Circuit, both sides sought Supreme Court review before the appellate judgment. The Court accepted because of imperative public importance and disagreement among lower courts about the new system. 370–371 & nn.5–6.

Mistretta argued that the Commission’s authority to determine the relative seriousness of crimes and weight of offender characteristics was excessive delegation. He also contended that judicial placement improperly combined legislative and judicial power; judicial membership compromised independence and impartiality; sharing policymaking with nonjudges improperly shared judicial power; and presidential appointment and removal exposed judges to political influence. 371, 377–378, 383–384, 397, 409.

## Nondelegation holding and statutory constraints

The Court applied the intelligible-principle standard. Congress may obtain assistance from other bodies when it supplies a general policy, identifies the implementing institution, and establishes boundaries. The presence of substantial judgment or subsidiary policy choices does not alone make the delegation unconstitutional. The relevant inquiry is whether the statute supplies standards allowing a reviewing court to determine whether Congress’s will has been followed. 371–374, 378–379.

The Act gave much more than a bare direction to invent punishments. It identified the objectives of certainty and fairness, reduction of unwarranted disparity with appropriate flexibility, and the advancement of knowledge about human behavior. It specified punishment’s purposes and rejected imprisonment as a means of promoting rehabilitation. It required guidelines to remain within statutory penalties and ordinarily constrained each imprisonment range to a spread no greater than twenty-five percent or six months, whichever was greater; a minimum of thirty years could have life as the maximum. 374–375.

Congress listed seven offense considerations and eleven offender characteristics, directed how to address specified recidivists and serious offenses, and identified circumstances favoring imprisonment, alternatives, or mitigation. It prohibited race, sex, national origin, creed, and socioeconomic status as considerations and restricted possible proxies. Existing average sentences supplied a starting point, not a command to preserve inadequate past punishments. The resulting hierarchy extended from near-maximum imprisonment through substantial or lesser imprisonment to alternatives. The Commission retained important discretion, but within detailed statutory direction suitable for an intricate task involving many crimes and offenders. 375–379 & nn.8–10.

Footnote 11 is a separate qualification to the voting account. The Court assumed without deciding that the Commission could implement existing statutory death-penalty provisions consistently with Supreme Court decisions; it emphasized that the Commission could not create a death penalty for an offense Congress had not made capital. Brennan did not join that footnote. The opinion therefore did not adjudicate the disputed scope of that particular authority. 378 n.11.

## Judicial placement and separation of powers

The Court rejected a requirement of airtight separation. Its concern was unconstitutional accumulation of power, encroachment on another branch’s assigned functions, or impairment of judicial integrity. Some nonadjudicatory work is compatible with judicial responsibilities. Procedural rulemaking, judicial councils, and administrative institutions illustrated that Article III’s case-or-controversy limitation does not prohibit every administrative or rulemaking function within the Judicial Branch. 380–390 & nn.12–16.

Sentencing had always involved all three branches, and judges had long made the kinds of judgments now centralized in the Guidelines. The Commission’s work closely related to that judicial mission. The Court acknowledged that sentencing policy involved more political and substantive judgment than ordinary procedural rules; it did not resolve the case by declaring all guideline decisions merely procedural. Instead it examined the plan’s practical consequences. 390–393 & nn.17–19.

The Commission was not a court, did not exercise judicial power, and was not controlled by or accountable to the Judiciary. Its members could include a minority of judges. Congress could revise or revoke the Guidelines, the President had limited removal authority, and rulemaking was subject to notice and comment. The Court expressly reserved whether Congress could give equivalent sentencing-rulemaking authority to an actual court. 393–395 & n.20.

The Guidelines did not define new crimes or replace Congress’s statutory minimum and maximum penalties. They constrained the sentencing discretion judges had previously exercised. Judicial placement therefore neither accumulated new judicial power at another branch’s expense nor prevented judges from adjudicating. The Executive had not previously exercised the particular authority assigned to the Commission, and its appointment, removal, and nonvoting membership relationships were not diminished merely by the Commission’s statutory location. 386–387 n.14, 395–397.

## Judicial membership and presidential control

The Court distinguished individual judges’ extrajudicial service from the exercise of Article III jurisdiction. Unlike legislators, judges were not subject to a constitutional incompatibility clause barring another office. Founding-era and subsequent practice supported the permissibility of some extrajudicial assignments, although the history was contested and did not establish that every assignment was wise or constitutional. Hayburn’s Case and Ferreira also distinguished courts’ judicial decisions from judges’ work as individual commissioners. 397–404 & nn.21–26.

Commissioners who happened to be judges exercised administrative authority conferred by their separate appointments. No particular judge was compelled to accept. The Court construed the statute as conferring no coercive power to force acceptance and reserved the question of compulsory service by an individual judge. Mere participation in drafting rules did not necessarily require recusal from all cases applying them. Judicial involvement here drew upon expertise in sentencing and did not enlist judicial prestige in defining crimes or enforcing prosecutions. The shared Commission authority accordingly was not Article III power distributed to nonjudges. 404–408 & nn.27–29.

Presidential removal from the Commission could not remove a judge from the bench or diminish Article III status and judicial salary. Good-cause limits further protected Commission independence. The hypothetical treatment of the additional salary received by a district judge serving as commissioner was reserved. The challenge here concerned presidential influence over the Judiciary, not whether Congress had impermissibly curtailed presidential removal of executive officials. The narrow conclusion was that good-cause removal of an Article III judge from this nonadjudicatory agency gave the President no control over adjudication. 409–412 & nn.30–35.

## Scalia’s dissent

Scalia agreed that the statute’s intelligible standards were adequate under the Court’s delegation precedents. Because every law leaves some judgment to those administering or applying it, deciding how much discretion is too much involves difficult questions of degree. Congress was better equipped to assess many governmental necessities. This concession is the central point of the first assigned selection. 413–416.

His objection concerned the recipient and nature of the authority. In his account, permissible rulemaking ordinarily accompanies the lawful exercise of executive or judicial power. A court may elaborate rules while deciding cases; an executive agency may make rules as part of administering a field committed to executive authority. Even a rulemaking-only agency could fit if controlled by the President and ancillary to presidential executive power. 416–419 & n.2.

This Commission, he argued, exercised no executive or adjudicative power and was controlled by neither the President nor courts. Data collection and advice did not supply the missing governmental function. Its binding sentencing policy was therefore freestanding lawmaking rather than subordinate discretion in executing or adjudicating law. Congress could not fix that problem by labeling the institution judicial. Scalia feared that such a structure would make politically insulated expert legislatures attractive while avoiding the ordinary political cost of strengthening an existing branch. 419–425 & n.3.

He also rejected case-by-case judgments about acceptable degrees of interbranch mixing as a substitute for the Constitution’s prescribed structure. The Commission’s potential usefulness did not cure its status as an additional legislative institution. He would have reversed the district court. 425–427. His capital-punishment footnote similarly described a disputed possible authority, not an actual exercise or judicial holding approving it. 413 n.1.

## Exact judgment and limits

The Supreme Court affirmed the district court and upheld the Act against the challenges decided. 412. It did not decide that every judicial commission, compulsory judicial assignment, or delegation to a court would be permissible. The statutory account is historical, and this brief does not certify later sentencing or removal law.
