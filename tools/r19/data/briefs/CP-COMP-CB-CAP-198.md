# Wiwa v. Royal Dutch Petroleum Co.

**226 F.3d 88 (2d Cir. 2000). September 14, 2000.** Judge Leval wrote for Judges Oakes, Leval, and Pooler. No separate writing is reported. Assigned account: CivPro PDF 237, printed 432, Note 7.

## Allegations and the defendants’ New York presence

The plaintiffs were three Nigerian émigrés and a woman proceeding as Jane Doe. They alleged grave abuses against themselves or deceased family members in retaliation for opposition to oil development in Nigeria’s Ogoni region. Their complaint alleged that Nigerian authorities imprisoned, tortured, or killed protesters and that the defendants, through their Nigerian subsidiary, instigated and supported these actions. Ken Saro-Wiwa and John Kpuinen were executed after convictions allegedly based on fabricated evidence. Other allegations concerned Owens Wiwa’s detention, violence against Jane Doe, and attacks on family members attending the trial. The court assumed the allegations for the threshold issues, expressly implying no view about their truth. (91–93.)

The defendants were holding companies: Royal Dutch was incorporated and headquartered in the Netherlands, and Shell Transport in England. Their jointly operated group included the Nigerian subsidiary. The American complaint sought relief under the Alien Tort Claims Act, RICO, international law, Nigerian law, and state tort law. The panel noted uncertainty about whether the theory rested on the parents’ own acts, responsibility for the subsidiary, or both. **Two of the four plaintiffs**, Blessing Kpuinen and Owens Wiwa, lived in the United States when suit began; none lived in the Southern District of New York. Owens later moved to Canada. The assigned account compresses this group into three émigrés living in the United States; the complete opinion supplies the more precise filing-time facts. (93–94.)

The parents maintained a New York investor-relations office nominally administered through Shell Oil. Its manager and activities were devoted to the parents: responding to investors, distributing information, and arranging meetings with officials and financial analysts. The parents reimbursed its expenses, approximately $500,000 annually, and approved important decisions. Their shares or depositary receipts traded in New York, but the office performed discretionary investor cultivation beyond the mechanical requirements of exchange listings. (93, 95–98.)

## The rulings below and the appellate questions

The magistrate recommended dismissal for lack of personal jurisdiction or, alternatively, forum non conveniens in favor of England, reserving the difficult merits questions. The district judge found jurisdiction through the investor office but accepted the foreign-forum recommendation. On reconsideration, she conditioned dismissal on defendants’ consent to English service, compliance with discovery, payment of a judgment, waiver of a security bond, and waiver of a limitations defense if the English action began within the specified year. The defendants accepted. Plaintiffs appealed the forum dismissal; defendants challenged jurisdiction as an alternative reason the case could not proceed. (94.)

## Historical general-jurisdiction reasoning

The panel upheld jurisdiction under the New York doing-business and due-process framework it applied in 2000. Under its agency analysis, an in-state representative’s activities could be attributed to a foreign corporation when they were sufficiently important that the corporation would perform equivalent services without the representative. The office worked entirely for the parents, was funded by them, and cultivated access to capital markets. The parents’ substantial investment showed its importance. The possibility that an office could theoretically be located elsewhere did not undo their deliberate choice of New York. (94–97.)

Listing securities and performing essential ancillary tasks did not alone establish general jurisdiction. But those activities were not legally invisible, and this permanent, staffed investor-relations operation exceeded that minimum. The panel found continuous and systematic business presence and did not need to rely solely on the district court’s “solicitation plus” characterization. It also found no compelling unfairness: the globally active defendants had extensive resources, an established American presence and counsel, and little language difficulty. The opinion left other proposed jurisdictional theories unresolved. This recounts its historical jurisdictional holding without certifying the present validity of its general-jurisdiction formula. (97–99.)

## Forum non conveniens: assumed adequacy, insufficient convenience

The defendant must establish an adequate alternative forum and then show that private and public interests strongly favor it. Review normally respects district-court discretion, but that deference assumes correct legal standards. Plaintiffs disputed English adequacy because double-actionability, transmissibility, and act-of-state doctrines might prevent consideration of their claims. Defendants undertook not to invoke the first two, but the panel questioned whether party undertakings could control rules grounded in judicial or governmental policy. It nevertheless **assumed England could reach the merits**, rather than deciding these disputes, because the balancing independently required reversal. (99–101.)

The district court had counted the absence of Southern District residence against the plaintiffs without crediting two plaintiffs’ United States residence. That was legal error. Forum choice attracts greater deference as a plaintiff’s ties increase because litigating abroad is more likely to impose hardship, not because foreign citizens deserve unequal justice. A resident’s home forum for this international comparison need not be the particular American district of residence, especially if the defendants cannot be sued there. Residence is a practical consideration, not an absolute shield against dismissal. Strong public interests or disproportionate defense burdens can still overcome it. (101–103.)

The panel also found a relevant American policy favoring a forum for torture claims in the Alien Tort Claims Act as supplemented by the Torture Victim Protection Act. The latter’s substantive liability and inclusion of United States citizens strengthened the inference that such abuses were an American legal concern. The court did not abolish or significantly diminish forum non conveniens: the policy reinforced the requirement of genuinely strong reasons for foreign dismissal. Nor did it resolve the distinct choice-of-law question whether international standards necessarily supplied the substantive causes of action in this suit. These were the panel’s historical statutory analysis and reservations, not a determination that these corporate defendants were liable. (103–106.)

## Practical burdens and the exact disposition

Starting again in England would impose substantial expense on plaintiffs with minimal resources. They already had pro bono American counsel and had invested time and effort here, with no guarantee of comparable representation abroad. The defendants offered document-shipping expenses and somewhat longer flights for Nigerian witnesses, but did not establish excessive burdens relative to their resources. English interests in a British corporation were relevant, yet one defendant was Dutch and the United States also had interests concerning residents. Nigeria’s Commonwealth membership did not decide convenience. The panel concluded that these weak considerations could not outweigh the concrete plaintiff burdens. (106–108.)

The court **reversed the forum non conveniens dismissal and remanded for further proceedings**, including consideration of the unaddressed Rule 12(b)(6) motion. It said the other retention factors were sufficient even without the TVPA policy consideration. The proposed Netherlands alternative shared the same disadvantages and did not require a separate remand for reconsideration. The judgment preserved a forum; it did not establish a viable cause of action, corporate responsibility for the Nigerian abuses, or damages. (107–108.)
