# Fisch v. Manger

**CP-124 · Civil Procedure · 24 N.J. 66, 130 A.2d 815 (1957) · April 1, 1957 · Jacobs; Heher, joined by Oliphant, concurring in the result**

The New Jersey Supreme Court upheld the constitutional power to use additur but rejected its use in this case because the increased award still inadequately compensated the plaintiff and rested on a mistaken view of his prior condition. All six participating justices voted for reversal and a damages-only new trial; two separately argued that additur itself infringed the state jury-trial guarantee.

## Accident, injuries, and trial result

On September 17, 1953, a traffic officer stopped vehicles on Amboy Avenue to let schoolchildren cross. William Fisch’s car was third in the southbound line and stopped before a truck driven by Bernard Clausi and owned by the Manger and Branin partnership struck it from behind. Fisch and the officer testified that Clausi said his foot slipped off the brake. Clausi denied that statement and described a renewed movement and sudden stop by Fisch, but admitted striking the rear and acknowledged that Fisch’s stoplights worked. The jury expressly found the truck driver negligent. Both the trial judge and Supreme Court regarded liability as clearly established. 24 N.J. at 67–68.

Fisch initially thought he could drive home, but pain forced him to stop. Treatment for whiplash included medication, diathermy, and a neck brace. Persistent severe pain led to hospitalization, unsuccessful traction, confirmation of a ruptured disc by myelogram, and surgery. He returned to work part-time in February 1954 and did not resume full-time work until September. The medical testimony described continuing pain, absent left-side reflexes, muscle atrophy, and restricted ability to do his former work. The treating physicians connected the surgery to the 1953 accident, and defendants offered no contrary medical testimony. Id. at 68–70.

Defendants nevertheless suggested an earlier 1950 accident accounted for some injury. Although Fisch had claimed back injury in the earlier discontinued action, the record showed normal heavy work and sports between recovery and the 1953 accident. The doctor’s reference to a chronic back condition described the condition after the 1953 collision, not a preexisting condition. Actual medical expenses exceeded $2,200, and lost wages were approximately $620. The jury’s $3,000 award therefore left substantially nothing for serious suffering and permanent injury. Id. at 70–71.

Fisch sought a new trial for inadequate damages. The trial court ordered that it would grant a damages-only trial unless defendants consented to increasing the award to $7,500. They consented, and the court denied the motion. Fisch appealed, challenging both the power to impose additur and the inadequacy of the increased amount. The Supreme Court certified the appeal on its own motion. Id. at 67, 71.

## Constitutional power: majority reasoning

Additur conditions denial of a plaintiff’s new-trial request on the defendant’s consent to an increased award; remittitur conditions denial of a defendant’s request on the plaintiff’s consent to a reduction. Jacobs treated their practical operation as analogous devices for attaining substantial justice without retrial costs and delay. He acknowledged that *Dimick v. Schiedt* prohibited additur in federal courts under the Seventh Amendment while permitting remittitur. His doubts about Dimick’s reasoning and possible future treatment were observations, not a holding that Dimick had been overruled. The controlling inquiry here concerned New Jersey’s constitution and history, because the Seventh Amendment did not apply to these state-court proceedings. Id. at 71–75.

The state jury guarantee protected substantive integrity without freezing every procedural form. New Jersey had long used remittitur, and *Gaffney v. Illingsworth* recognized parallel discretion for additur. Later decisions and treatises had accepted both practices. The 1947 constitutional convention operated against that background of judicial controls, including special verdicts and conditional or partial new trials, without indicating that those controls should be curtailed. Fair use of additur and remittitur therefore did not violate the state constitution. Id. at 75–80.

## Why this particular additur failed

Constitutional permission did not validate every figure selected by a judge. Appellate review could remedy abuse of discretion or manifest denial of justice. The trial judge had limited compensation because he believed Fisch already had a back condition. The record instead indicated that the earlier condition had cleared and was unrelated to the severe injury from this collision. That misconception produced the insufficient $7,500 figure. Under the special circumstances, a second jury should determine damages. Liability was separately and clearly resolved and did not require retrial. Id. at 80–81.

## Heher and Oliphant’s concurrence in the result

Heher, joined by Oliphant, agreed with the damages-only retrial but rejected the majority’s constitutional premise. He understood the state guarantee to preserve the common-law jury right as it existed in 1776. Courts could set aside verdicts reflecting mistake, passion, prejudice, or partiality, but could not simply replace permissible jury factfinding with their own preferences. Id. at 81–82.

His extensive historical analysis distinguished uncertain personal-tort damages from liquidated sums and older exceptional practices. Following Dimick’s distinction, reducing an excessive award could be understood as removing an excess from an amount already found by the jury; increasing an inadequate verdict added compensation no jury had assessed. A defendant’s consent alone did not preserve the unwilling plaintiff’s right to a jury assessment. Procedural convenience could not create constitutional power. Heher also observed that the court itself ordered a jury retrial rather than selecting a new adequate figure, reinforcing his preferred remedy. Id. at 82–88.

## Exact judgment and scope

The judgment was reversed with directions for a new trial limited to damages. The listed vote was six for reversal and none for affirmance: Vanderbilt, Heher, Oliphant, Burling, Jacobs, and Weintraub. Heher and Oliphant concurred only in the result, leaving four justices supporting Jacobs’s reasoning. The complete report contains the majority and one joint separate concurrence, with no judicial footnotes. This brief explains the historical state ruling; it does not treat the majority’s speculation about federal doctrine as verified later law. The central distinction is between possessing power to condition a new trial and exercising that power on an accurate assessment of the evidence.
