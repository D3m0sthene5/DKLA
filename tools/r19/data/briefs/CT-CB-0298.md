# Klocek v. Gateway, Inc.

104 F. Supp. 2d 1332 (D. Kan. 2000)

## Citation, court, and assigned scope
Judge Kathryn H. Vratil of the District of Kansas issued this memorandum and order on June 15, 2000. The assigned excerpt, printed pages 298–303, concerns Gateway's attempt to enforce terms shipped with a computer. The full opinion also resolves a separate defendant's jurisdictional motion, class certification, and several motions filed by the unrepresented plaintiff. Reporter-page references below distinguish those rulings from the contract-formation issue.

## Parties and requested relief
William S. Klocek sued Gateway over a computer and technical-support package and Hewlett-Packard over a scanner. He alleged that Gateway made false technical-support promises and breached contract and warranty duties concerning compatibility with peripherals and Internet services. He alleged that Hewlett-Packard failed to warn that its scanner was incompatible with Gateway computers. He attempted to proceed individually and for a class. Gateway sought dismissal based on an arbitration provision; Hewlett-Packard separately challenged subject-matter jurisdiction. 104 F. Supp. 2d at 1334–35, 1342.

## Material facts and unresolved transaction facts
Gateway routinely enclosed four pages of Standard Terms with its computers. A prominently boxed notice stated that retaining the computer more than five days after delivery constituted acceptance. Paragraph ten prescribed binding arbitration in Chicago before a single arbitrator under International Chamber of Commerce rules. Klocek's continued possession of the computer was the assent on which Gateway relied. Id. at 1335, 1341.

The evidentiary record did not reliably establish how or where the sale occurred. Gateway submitted an affidavit saying that it shipped a computer to Klocek on or about August 31, 1997, but supplied few details. Klocek said in his response that he purchased the computer in person at the Gateway store in Overland Park, Kansas, on August 27 and took it with him. His residence was in Missouri. The court did not select one account as a proven fact. It analyzed the motion in light of the evidentiary deficiency and the apparent absence of a material difference between Kansas and Missouri law. Id. at 1336–37.

Gateway produced no evidence that, at the time of the sale, it told Klocek the transaction was conditioned on the enclosed terms, that he agreed to a five-day review-and-return mechanism, or that the parties contemplated subsequently supplied terms. The court had proof of a sale through payment and receipt; it lacked proof that this sale included the claimed arbitration agreement. Id. at 1337, 1340–42.

A later Gateway publication apparently offered additional arbitration-provider alternatives. The court observed that neither party explained why Gateway would be entitled to change an enforceable agreement unilaterally; Klocek denied receiving the later notice. It did not resolve an amended agreement’s enforceability because Gateway had not established the initial agreement. Id. at 1335 n. 1.

## Procedural history, burden, and choice of law
Gateway moved to dismiss the claims as arbitrable. The court described Gateway's initial burden as similar to the summary-judgment burden: it had to present evidence supporting an enforceable arbitration agreement. Only then would Klocek have to produce evidence of a genuine factual dispute. Federal policy favoring arbitration did not dispense with proof that these parties had agreed to it. Id. at 1335–36.

The court applied Kansas choice-of-law principles in the diversity action, including the place of the last act necessary to form the contract. Because Gateway did not establish that place, a definitive choice was not possible. Kansas and Missouri both applied Article 2 to this sale and appeared not to differ materially on the issue; some other state's law nevertheless could require separate consideration on a better record. A South Dakota choice clause in the disputed Standard Terms could not itself establish the enforceability of the document containing it. Id. at 1336–37 & n. 5; 1342.

The opinion's discussion of dismissing arbitrable claims reflects the procedural authorities it applied in 2000. The disposition here did not compel arbitration or order an arbitral stay; Gateway failed the antecedent formation showing. The brief does not present that historical dismissal discussion as a current general rule for all FAA motions.

## Issues
Did the shipment and retention of a computer establish assent to the arbitration term enclosed with it? Was § 2-207 inapplicable merely because there was only one written form? Could Gateway be presumed the offeror with power to postpone formation until the review period expired? Did its insert make acceptance expressly conditional on the buyer's assent? Separately, had Klocek alleged a sufficient amount in controversy against Hewlett-Packard, and could he represent a class without counsel?

## Rules and holdings
Gateway had not shown that the parties agreed to arbitrate. On the assumed Kansas/Missouri analysis, the purchaser ordinarily made the offer and the seller accepted by completing the sale, agreeing to ship, or shipping. The written insert was an acceptance or confirmation containing additional terms, governed by § 2-207; the statute did not require two competing written forms. Id. at 1337–40.

Gateway did not establish an expressly conditional acceptance. Because Klocek was not a merchant, the proposed additional terms required his express assent. Keeping the computer beyond five days did not supply that assent when Gateway had not shown prior agreement to the review mechanism. The uncertain governing law independently prevented a ruling for Gateway on its deficient motion. Id. at 1340–42.

The court separately held that Klocek had not established the required jurisdictional amount against Hewlett-Packard and that he could not adequately represent absent class members pro se. Id. at 1342–44.

## Actual judgment
Gateway's motion to dismiss was denied. The court allowed Gateway an opportunity to submit a factually supported supplemental motion if it contended that another state's law controlled. It would then consider whether a jury trial on formation was warranted. Thus the order did not conclusively invalidate every possible arbitration agreement between these parties under every potentially applicable law. Id. at 1342, 1344.

Hewlett-Packard's motion was granted for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The court denied Klocek's motions for class certification, sanctions and related relief, a writ of certiorari, and verification concerning a disciplinary complaint. Klocek succeeded in resisting Gateway's existing arbitration motion, but he did not receive damages or succeed against both defendants. Id. at 1343–45.

## Reasoning
### Arbitration policy did not answer whether a contract to arbitrate existed
The FAA enforces agreements; it does not manufacture them. A court first must determine whether the parties formed the claimed arbitration agreement using the applicable ordinary state-law principles. Gateway's failure to document elementary transaction facts prevented it from satisfying its initial burden. The policy of resolving doubts about an arbitration clause's scope could not replace the distinct inquiry whether the clause had ever been accepted. Id. at 1335–37.

### Payment and receipt already evidenced a sale
Regardless of the competing accounts of delivery, Klocek paid for and received a computer. That conduct demonstrated a sale of goods. The remaining question was which terms governed it. Gateway therefore could not simply label its later insert the offer and assume that no contract existed until the buyer retained the machine for five days. It needed evidence of a transaction structured that way. Id. at 1337, 1340.

The court acknowledged that a seller sometimes is the offeror. Its objection was to treating that conclusion as automatic. Ordinary purchase orders are offers; a vendor's agreement to ship or actual shipment can accept them. The identity of the offeror depends on the communications and conduct, not on which party drafted more detailed boilerplate. Id. at 1340.

### Section 2-207 can apply to a single written confirmation
Hill and ProCD had characterized § 2-207 as irrelevant when only one form was exchanged. Vratil rejected that reading. The statutory language expressly includes a written confirmation, and its commentary describes an oral agreement followed by one or both parties' written memoranda adding previously undiscussed terms. Kansas and Missouri authorities recognized that application. A statute commonly used in battles of forms is not confined to a contest between two physical forms. Id. at 1338–40.

The opinion therefore provides a text-based objection to Hill's commercial-practicality analysis. The two decisions also differ in their factual records and state-law predictions. Klocek is not a controlling reversal of Hill; it is a district court's contrary analysis of the law it predicted would govern the transaction before it.

### The conditional-acceptance proviso was not satisfied
A seller relying on § 2-207(1)'s proviso must clearly communicate that it is unwilling to proceed without assent to its additional terms. Gateway offered no evidence of that communication when the sale was made. Shipping the product with an attached statement of conditions did not itself communicate the necessary unwillingness to proceed. Id. at 1340–41.

The footnotes acknowledge competing interpretations of the proviso. The court did not choose between the strict Dorton approach and an intermediate approach because Gateway failed under both. It declined to use the discredited rule equating any materially different acceptance with a conditional counteroffer. The holding is grounded in the missing communication, rather than in the proposition that conditional acceptance is impossible in consumer sales. Id. n.12.

### The buyer's nonmerchant status made affirmative agreement necessary
Under § 2-207(2), automatic inclusion between merchants does not apply to a consumer purchaser. The insert proposed additional terms. Gateway could not prove acceptance of those proposals by relying solely on a provision within the same unaccepted document declaring that silence or retention would count. Klocek had no established contractual duty to return the machine to preserve the earlier agreement's terms. Id. at 1341.

The court also noted that treating the insert as a proposed modification under § 2-209 would not change the result: Gateway still lacked the buyer's express assent under the authorities it applied. Practical concerns did not require telephone recitation of every clause. A seller could communicate either the terms or the condition that additional terms would be part of the transaction at the time of sale. Id. nn.13–14.

### A renewed showing remained possible
The court expressly left room for a better-supported motion invoking another state's law and identifying the facts that made it applicable. That procedural reservation matters. The opinion resolves the insufficiency of Gateway's existing showing and explains why Kansas and Missouri law would not enforce the clause on the assumed facts. It does not purport to adjudicate facts Gateway had failed to supply. Id. at 1342, 1344.

### Hewlett-Packard's dismissal turned on federal jurisdiction
Klocek separately requested approximately $24,000 plus unspecified punitive damages against Hewlett-Packard. He alleged neither facts supporting the stated actual loss nor facts supporting punitive damages approaching the jurisdictional threshold. General allegations about much larger claims against Gateway did not establish the amount against Hewlett-Packard. The procedural joinder rules did not independently supply subject-matter jurisdiction. Id. at 1342–43.

The court dismissed that defendant without deciding its alternative failure-to-state-a-claim argument. This ruling does not hold that a scanner manufacturer can never owe the alleged warning duty. It holds that this plaintiff failed to establish federal jurisdiction over this claim on these pleadings. Id. at 1343 & n.18.

### The remaining motions did not change the contract ruling
A representative must adequately protect absent class members, and Klocek, as a nonlawyer proceeding pro se, could not represent them. His requested writ sought access to a state-court record through a device that did not make the district court an appellate reviewer of that court. The sanctions and verification motions likewise supplied no basis for relief. These were independent procedural rulings; none resolved the merits of Gateway's alleged product or support failures. Id. at 1343–45.

## Separate opinions
There is no separate opinion. This is one district judge's memorandum and order. Its criticism of Hill and ProCD must be distinguished from a dissent within either appellate decision.

## Use and limits
Ask first what formed the sale and what evidence establishes agreement to the later insert. Then identify whether § 2-207 treats the insert as acceptance, confirmation, or conditional counteroffer; determine merchant status; and identify the conduct alleged to accept the proposed additional terms. Klocek's decisive factual weakness was Gateway's failure to document assent and the transaction itself. A five-day period is not automatically invalid, and a thirty-day period is not automatically sufficient: the competing cases disagree about formation, statutory interpretation, and what the evidence proves.
