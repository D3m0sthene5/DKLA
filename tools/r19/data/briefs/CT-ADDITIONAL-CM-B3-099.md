# New York Football Giants, Inc. v. Los Angeles Chargers Football Club, Inc.
291 F.2d 471 (5th Cir. 1961) | Contracts

## A professional agreement kept secret for the Sugar Bowl

Charles Flowers was an outstanding University of Mississippi football player in fall 1959. His team was scheduled to face Louisiana State University in the January 1, 1960 Sugar Bowl, and he especially wanted to participate. The Southeastern Conference and NCAA eligibility rules described in the opinion excluded players who had entered professional arrangements. Footnote 1 supplies the historical SEC rule concerning an express or implied agreement with a professional team and the NCAA rule concerning accepted pay or a promise of pay for athletic participation. These are the rules the court considered in this case, rather than a statement of present eligibility policy. (291 F.2d at 472 & n.1.)

While Flowers was in New York for other reasons, Giants official Mara invited him to the office and urged him to sign for two seasons beginning in 1960. Flowers made clear that he did not want to destroy his Sugar Bowl eligibility. Mara’s quoted cross-examination admitted both that he understood this concern and that he proposed keeping the signing confidential. He knew disclosure would prevent Flowers from playing and believed Coach Vaught would exclude him if he learned of the contract. The appellate court treated that testimony as uncontradicted evidence of a deliberate plan to deceive the coach, university, opposing team, and football public, rather than innocent postponement of publicity. (Id. at 472–473.)

Flowers signed the standard NFL form on December 1 and received $3,500 in bonus checks. The form required the Commissioner’s approval before becoming binding. As part of their arrangement, Mara agreed not to submit it until after January 1. After Flowers attempted to withdraw by telephone around December 5, however, the Giants promptly filed the document. The Commissioner approved it on December 15, but at Mara’s request withheld announcing the approval until after the bowl game. On December 29 Flowers negotiated a more favorable offer from the Chargers, wrote to the Giants withdrawing from their agreement, and returned the uncashed bonus checks. He played in the Sugar Bowl and formally executed the Chargers contract afterward. Keeping the arrangement confidential did not, in the court’s account, actually restore his amateur eligibility; it concealed the event that implicated the rules. (Id. at 473.)

## Contract validity below, equity on appeal

The Giants sought equitable enforcement against the Chargers and Flowers, including preventing Flowers from playing for the competing club. The trial court ruled for both defendants on contractual grounds. It held that the agreement was not binding until approved by the Commissioner, and that the approval procured by Mara contrary to the promise to delay submission did not make it binding. On that reasoning, Flowers retained a legal right to cancel until January 1. (Id. at 473.)

The Fifth Circuit decided the Giants’ appeal on June 14, 1961. Chief Judge Tuttle wrote for a panel also comprising Rives and District Judge De Vane. No separate opinion is published. The court expressly declined to resolve the merits of the contract-law questions. Instead, it held that the Giants’ misconduct in bringing about and concealing the purported agreement barred equitable assistance. Thus the appellate decision does not endorse the trial court’s legal account of approval or definitively hold either professional contract valid or invalid. (Id. at 471, 473–475.)

## Clean hands concerns the transaction for which relief is requested

The opinion began with Judge Wright’s criticism in a different football-contract case of recruiting practices and competing professional agreements. That quotation supplied context; it concerned Johnny Robinson and was not a finding that Flowers was the player in that separate litigation. Tuttle then concluded that, in an appropriate case such as this one, a federal equity court must decline to aid a transaction offensive to honest dealing in its inception. (Id. at 471–472.)

Drawing principally on Precision Instrument, the court explained that clean hands bars equitable relief for bad faith related to the matter in which relief is sought, even if the defendant’s own behavior has been improper. It does not require a plaintiff to have lived without blame in unrelated matters. Nor must the misconduct be criminal or independently actionable; a willful act concerning the asserted cause that transgresses equitable standards can suffice. The equitable discretion is especially significant when public interests accompany the parties’ private interests, because refusing relief can prevent public injury as well as denying a wrongdoer the fruits of the conduct. Deweese likewise distinguished possible rights at law from entitlement to a court of equity’s assistance. (Id. at 473–474.)

The Giants’ requested remedy was directly tied to their own deception. They asserted a binding contract while agreeing to represent, in effect, that no such contract existed so that people with material interests in the game would be misled. A straightforward signing and filing would not have created the legal difficulties they later asked the court to untangle. The club could not create a false public status through deceit and then ask equity to disregard that status and enforce its rights as though it had acted honestly throughout. Once Mara’s uncontradicted testimony made that plan apparent, the court held, the suit should have been dismissed on clean-hands grounds without adjudicating the underlying contractual issues. Flowers’s participation in the scheme did not entitle the Giants to equitable enforcement against him. (Id. at 474–475.)

## Exact disposition and its limits

The appellate court affirmed the dismissal of the amended complaint and the costs imposed on the Giants. But it struck paragraphs B and C of section 5 of the final judgment, which made legal determinations about the contracts’ validity. As modified, the judgment was affirmed. This precise modification matters: the result is denial of equitable assistance while removing unnecessary contract-validity determinations, rather than an affirmance that establishes Flowers had legally cancelled an invalid contract. The opinion does not award contract damages or decide whether a separate action at law would succeed. (Id. at 475.)

The same assigned paragraph contrasts Houston Oilers v. Neely, whose later court rejected a possible categorical application to every secret pre-bowl professional signing. That comparison is an editorial account of another decision. This brief preserves the particular deception and discretionary equitable reasoning in Giants without turning the textbook comparison into a separate holding of this court.
