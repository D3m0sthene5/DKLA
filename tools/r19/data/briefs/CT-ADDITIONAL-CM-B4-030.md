# Nirdlinger v. American District Telegraph Company
245 Pa. 453, 91 A. 883 (1914) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Justice Stewart delivered the Pennsylvania Supreme Court’s May 22, 1914 opinion reversing a judgment entered on a $2,937.05 verdict for Nirdlinger. The Court rejected the finding that the alarm company’s negligence proximately caused the burglary, but preserved recovery for its admitted breach of contract. It awarded a venire de novo: a new jury would determine damages properly resulting from that breach under appropriate instructions. It neither entered judgment for the company nor fixed recovery at the amount spent wiring the house. (454–456, 461–462.)

The reported panel comprised Chief Justice Fell and Justices Brown, Mestrezat, Potter, Elkin, Stewart, and Moschzisker. No separate writing or numerical division accompanies this decision. The adjacent material ending on 453 belongs to Hogarty, and the next caption on 462 belongs to Becker. (453–454, 462.)

## Undertaking, burglary, and disputed negligence
For annual consideration, American District Telegraph undertook to install and maintain an electrical alarm in Nirdlinger’s North Broad Street dwelling in Philadelphia. Disturbance of a door or window would transmit a signal to its office; upon receipt, the company would immediately dispatch an agent. This was the undertaking shown by the evidence. The company had not insured the valuables against theft or promised that every intrusion would be defeated. Nirdlinger sued for himself and for the use and benefit of his insurer. (454, 456, 459.)

Early on October 26, 1910, while the household was absent, a burglar entered through a raised window and stole valuables. The company admitted receiving no alarm and sending no agent. The preceding morning Nirdlinger had asked its agent to open the house so he could retrieve articles and then restore the electrical connection. The agent opened it and withdrew, asking Nirdlinger to close the door and promising to return shortly to reset the alarm. Nirdlinger secured the door upon leaving. Whether the agent returned and restored the current became the central disputed fact. Plaintiff’s evidence supported an inference that he had not. (456–458.)

The equipment was exclusively managed by the company, and the relevant circumstances other than the burglary itself were within its control. The evidence permitted a prima facie inference of negligence, subject to rebuttal. The Court nevertheless distinguished a failure to restore the current from an unexplained malfunction after proper restoration: the latter fact alone would not establish negligence without more. The jury found for Nirdlinger after instructions requiring both negligence and proximate causation. The appellate decision therefore did not rest on insufficient evidence of the agent’s negligent failure to reset the apparatus. (457–458.)

## Why the burglary did not satisfy the Court’s causation rule
The trial judge correctly described proximate cause in terms of a natural and continuous sequence, unbroken by a new cause, producing a natural and probable rather than merely possible consequence. But the Court held that applying this instruction was a judicial question here. There was no conflicting evidence about the intervening human agency; the jury should not have been allowed to determine that the negligent omission proximately caused the theft. (458–459.)

Under the Court’s test, negligence must operate continuously through the succeeding events to the loss. An independent voluntary act, outside the defendant’s control and uninfluenced by its act, could break that causal chain. The immediate antecedent of the loss was the felonious entry. The company’s omission had not produced that entry. It might have enabled the burglar to accomplish his purpose, but enabling the crime was not equivalent to causing it under the doctrine applied in this report. (459–461.)

Nirdlinger argued that an operating alarm would have signaled the invasion, brought an agent, and prevented the loss. The Court treated the proposed chain as speculation. The success of the intervention depended on numerous contingencies, and the record supplied no legal inference that the valuables would have been saved. Thus, the problem was more than uncertainty about the monetary value of stolen articles: the causal link between deficient alarm service and prevention of this theft had not been established. (460.)

The Court illustrated its reasoning with a reported case in which a promised guard was not supplied and a convict set fire to the lessee’s establishment. Failure to provide the guard might have allowed the arson but was not its direct cause; the quoted court looked to the expense of the promised guard rather than the resulting fire loss. Nirdlinger used that analogy to distinguish a failed protective service from the independent criminal act. It did not simply adopt the alarm company’s proposed wiring-cost measure as the exclusive contract remedy. (460–462.)

## The surviving contract claim and earlier appeal
The company’s second assignment of error asserted that damages should be limited to the amount paid to wire the property. The Court sustained the challenge to the trial’s damages treatment while expressly declining that particular limitation. It directed the new jury to determine what damages properly resulted from breach of the undertaking. The opinion therefore establishes neither a recovery of all stolen-property value in contract nor a final conclusion that only installation expense could be recovered. (455, 461–462.)

Nirdlinger invoked the earlier appeal reported at 240 Pa. 571. The Court explained that this earlier judgment had addressed a demurrer and the form of the action. The declaration alleged a contract, breach of duty, and negligence; it was sufficient to call for a plea even though the action was framed in trespass. Reversal with leave to plead had not adjudicated whether the alleged tort was independently actionable or whether the omission proximately caused the burglary. That procedural ruling did not settle the causation question arising after trial. (461–462.)

Despite the failure of the tort theory, the declaration still supported recovery for the admitted contractual breach. When the subject of an action framed as a tort rests on contract, the action carries the incidents of an action on the contract. That principle explains the new trial rather than an outright defense judgment. The Court’s final mandate reversed the judgment and awarded a venire de novo. (462.)
