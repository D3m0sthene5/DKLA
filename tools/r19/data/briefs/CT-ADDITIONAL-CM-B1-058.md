# Hobbs v. Massasoit Whip Company
158 Mass. 194, 33 N.E. 495 (Mar. 1, 1893) | Contracts

## Decision and posture

The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court overruled the company’s exceptions to a jury instruction allowing acceptance to be inferred from silence and retention of goods in the context of the parties’ prior dealings. Hobbs was not an unsolicited seller dealing with a stranger. The jury could find that his conforming shipment, coupled with the company’s failure to reject it for an unreasonable time, manifested assent. Holmes J. wrote the sole opinion; Field C.J. and Allen, Holmes, Knowlton, and Barker JJ. were listed as present, with no numerical vote or separate writing reported. (194, 196–197.)

## Facts and disputed evidence

Hobbs lived in Saugus and supplied eel skins for a whip manufacturer in Westfield through Harding in Lynn. Four or five earlier shipments sent in the same way had been accepted and paid for. The manufacturer’s president acknowledged prices of five cents per skin over twenty-seven inches and two cents for skins between twenty-two and twenty-seven inches, provided they were suitable for the business. Hobbs understood that he should send skins in good condition and longer than twenty-two inches. (194–195.)

On February 18, 1890, Hobbs delivered the disputed shipment to Harding, who sent it on that day or the next. The covering letter identified 2,050 skins at five cents and 300 at two cents, totaling 2,350 skins and $108.50. Hobbs said they met the size and condition requirements. The company’s witnesses said the shipment received February 19 or 20 was short, badly conditioned, and useless. Its president denied ordering or purchasing these particular skins and said he promptly informed Harding in writing that they were unfit and held subject to Hobbs’s directions. Hobbs denied receiving notice of rejection. The skins remained at the factory for months and were later destroyed; the actual report does not specify the cause of destruction. (194–196.)

The parties also disputed whose agent Harding was. Hobbs denied that Harding was his agent; Harding described forwarding goods, receiving payments, and passing along information for Hobbs. The company argued that notifying Harding therefore notified Hobbs. The conflicting agency evidence went to the jury under instructions to which no exception was taken. (194–196.)

## Jury instruction and appellate issue

Hammond J. instructed that Hobbs could not recover for skins under twenty-two inches or skins in the condition described by the company’s witnesses. The challenged instruction additionally permitted a plaintiff’s verdict if the company remained silent while having reason to believe the sender understood that it was accepting the skins. The jury found for Hobbs, and the company argued on exceptions that this instruction let any sender impose a purchase obligation on an unwilling recipient merely by shipping goods. (196–197.)

Holmes recognized that the instruction, read in isolation, might suggest that impermissible result. He instead examined what the jury would understand in the evidentiary setting. Hobbs had repeatedly supplied the same kind of goods by the same route, and the company accepted and paid for them. In view of the verdict and the quality instruction, the appellate court took the skins to be suitable and sufficiently long and took Hobbs to have received no rejection notice. The question was therefore not whether the company must buy goods it had timely rejected or goods that failed its stated requirements. (196–197.)

## Reasoning and rule

The company’s own testimony supported an understanding that it would take suitable skins meeting the stated dimensions, and Holmes described a standing offer for such skins. Even if shipment alone did not complete the contract under that offer, the established relationship warranted Hobbs in sending conforming goods and called for the company to act upon receiving them. Silence combined with retention for an unreasonable time could then justify the jury in finding acceptance. (197.)

The operative principle was objective manifestation: conduct importing assent counts as assent regardless of an undisclosed contrary mental state. Silence acquired significance from the prior transactions, the understood requirements, receipt of conforming skins, and continued retention. The court did not allow a stranger to create a duty to pay merely by announcing that failure to respond would be acceptance. Nor did it decide an objection to the agency instructions, which had not been preserved. (195–197.)

## Result, assigned account, and source accuracy

The exceptions were overruled, leaving the plaintiff’s verdict intact. (197.) The complete assigned account is in the “Silence Not Ordinarily Acceptance” discussion on C&M Textbook 1–350 physical PDF page 280, printed 235. Its quoted reasoning emphasizes the prior dealings and asks what conduct existed beyond silence. The complete actual report, 194–197, including all four original scans, the trial evidence, both instructions, and Holmes’s entire opinion, was read; there are no judicial footnotes or separate writings.

The original report confirms 2,350 skins, although CAP and a derived web transcription misread the number as 2,850 in the opening procedural account. The original reporter headnote also says shipment did not impose a duty and refers to retention for a reasonable time; the actual judicial opinion on 197 says shipment did impose a duty in these circumstances and refers to an unreasonable time.
