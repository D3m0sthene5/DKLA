# U.S. Bancorp Mortgage Co. v. Bonner Mall Partnership

**513 U.S. 18 (1994) · Supreme Court of the United States · November 8, 1994**

Justice Scalia wrote for a unanimous Court. The Court denied Bancorp’s motion to vacate the Ninth Circuit’s judgment and dismissed the case as moot. The assigned account is within Note 3 on CivPro PDF page 663, printed 1315.

## Facts and litigation sequence

Northtown Investments built Bonner Mall in Idaho in 1984 and 1985 with bank financing. In 1986, Bonner Mall Partnership acquired the mall and U.S. Bancorp Mortgage acquired the loan and mortgage. Bonner defaulted on real-estate taxes in 1990, and Bancorp scheduled foreclosure. The day before the sale, Bonner filed for Chapter 11 reorganization in the District of Idaho. Its proposed plan depended on the disputed “new value exception” to bankruptcy’s absolute-priority rule: existing shareholders would obtain an interest in the reorganized debtor through new capital despite objections from a creditor class not paid in full. That description came from the Ninth Circuit; the Supreme Court expressly declined to decide whether the exception existed under the Bankruptcy Code. 513 U.S. at 19–20 & n.1.

Bancorp sought relief from the automatic stay of foreclosure, arguing that the plan could not be confirmed for several reasons, including the supposed unavailability of the exception. The bankruptcy court agreed that the exception had not survived enactment of the Code and lifted the stay, while staying its order pending appeal. The district court reversed, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed the district court. Bancorp obtained Supreme Court review. After merits briefing, however, the parties agreed to a consensual reorganization plan, which the bankruptcy court approved. Both agreed that confirmation settled the controversy and made it moot. Bancorp nevertheless asked the Supreme Court to vacate the adverse Ninth Circuit judgment; Bonner opposed that request. The Court separately heard the vacatur question. Id. at 20.

This was therefore a dispute over the consequences of settling after an adverse adjudication, not a remaining merits dispute about the reorganization plan. Vacatur would remove the judgment rather than merely end the pending review.

## Question and holding

Does settlement-induced mootness ordinarily justify vacating a federal civil judgment under review? No. Vacatur is an equitable remedy, and a losing party that voluntarily abandons appellate review through settlement ordinarily cannot demand removal of the adverse judgment. Exceptional circumstances may conceivably justify relief, but a settlement provision calling for vacatur is not itself such a circumstance. Id. at 24–29.

The Court first distinguished its power to dispose of the case from its power to decide the now-moot merits. Section 2106 authorizes appellate courts to affirm, modify, vacate, set aside, reverse, or remand judgments properly brought before them. Article III prevents adjudication of a moot legal controversy’s merits, but it does not prevent necessary ancillary dispositions, such as dismissal, an award of costs, or an appropriate vacatur. Otherwise, even an appellate finding that the district court lacked Article III jurisdiction could not be followed by an order setting aside the unauthorized judgment. Bonner’s suggestion that mootness deprived the Court of power to entertain the motion therefore failed. Id. at 20–22.

## The equitable distinction: happenstance versus voluntary settlement

Bancorp relied heavily on Munsingwear’s account of the practice of vacating judgments when cases become moot during review. Scalia examined what Munsingwear actually decided. The government there had failed to ask for vacatur before dismissal of its appeal, then faced the lower judgment’s preclusive consequences in related damages litigation. The holding denied relief because the government had failed to use the available procedure. Its broad description of the practice was dictum, and both earlier and later dispositions were less uniform than Bancorp suggested. Unexplained summary dispositions did not resolve the present equitable question through developed reasoning. Id. at 22–24 & n.2.

The central consideration was who caused the loss of review. A party prevented from obtaining review by circumstances beyond its control ordinarily has an equitable basis for vacatur. So does a losing party whose opponent unilaterally renders the dispute moot. Settlement is different: the losing party has elected to give up the ordinary remedy of appeal or certiorari. Its judgment has not become incapable of review through external events; it remains unreviewed because the party chose settlement. Id. at 24–25.

The Court retained the happenstance principle, while reserving a narrower attribution question about Munsingwear itself. That litigation became moot after the government’s regulations were annulled by executive action; the Court did not decide whether such governmental action should properly be attributed to the Executive Branch litigating in the government’s name. Id. at 25 n.3. Bancorp thus did not decide that every governmental change automatically counts as faultless happenstance.

Bancorp argued that Bonner also agreed to settlement, so neither party had a superior equitable position. The Court answered that the existing judgment established the relevant status quo: Bonner had won, and Bancorp sought extraordinary relief from that result. Equal responsibility for settlement did not establish Bancorp’s entitlement to vacatur. The burden remained on Bancorp, whose voluntary abandonment of review weighed against relief. Id. at 26.

## Public interests and rejected policy arguments

A judicial judgment also has significance beyond the settling parties. The Court adopted the view that precedents are valuable to the legal community and are not merely private property that litigants may bargain away. The ordinary statutory route for relief from judgments is appellate review; settlement-related vacatur could become a substitute collateral attack that bypasses that route. Id. at 26–27.

Bancorp offered several systemic arguments. First, because the Supreme Court often reverses cases it agrees to hear, a grant of certiorari supposedly made the lower judgment suspect. Scalia rejected vacatur based on predictions about merits that mootness prevented the Court from deciding. Second, Bancorp argued that removing the judgment would encourage further debate within the circuit. The Court thought ordinary inter-circuit disagreement sufficiently informed its work, while existing decisions provided valuable legal resolution. Third, Bancorp invoked settlement incentives. Vacatur might encourage late settlements, but it might also discourage earlier ones by allowing a litigant to try its luck and erase a loss afterward. The Court did not claim to know the net empirical effect. Id. at 27–28.

The same principal equity applies when a court of appeals is reviewing a district-court judgment. Review as of right does not change the losing party’s voluntary abandonment of review, and estimates of likely reversal again cannot replace adjudication of the merits. Id. at 28–29.

## Disposition, qualifications, and assigned context

The Court denied Bancorp’s motion to vacate the Ninth Circuit judgment and dismissed the Supreme Court case as moot. It neither affirmed nor reversed the underlying bankruptcy merits. The adverse lower judgment was left in place. Id. at 29.

The holding is not an absolute prohibition on settlement-related vacatur. The Court preserved the possibility of exceptional equitable circumstances, while expressly excluding the mere fact that the settlement agreement requires vacatur. It also stated that a court of appeals may remand a request concerning a district-court judgment for the district court to consider under Rule 60(b). That procedural possibility is not an automatic entitlement to relief and did not produce a remand here. Id.

The textbook uses the case to question whether parties may strip an adjudicated judgment of preclusive effects by agreement. Its surrounding discussion contrasts Neary’s California approach and a later California statutory amendment. Those are distinct authorities and editorial comparisons; they do not change Bancorp’s federal equitable holding. The assigned note’s phrase “extraordinary circumstances” also must not obscure the Court’s explicit exclusion of a contractual vacatur clause as sufficient by itself.
