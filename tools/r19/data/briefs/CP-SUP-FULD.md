# Fuld v. Palestine Liberation Organization
606 U.S. 1 (2025) | Civil Procedure

## Facts and procedural history

The Palestine Liberation Organization and Palestinian Authority exercise, respectively, foreign and domestic governmental functions for portions of the West Bank and Gaza Strip. Although the United States does not recognize them as sovereign states, they maintain diplomatic facilities internationally, and the PLO maintains a United Nations mission in New York. Congress has long regulated their operations and assistance in response to terrorism and foreign-policy concerns. The Antiterrorism Act of 1990 gives U.S. nationals a civil action, with treble damages, for injury or death caused by international terrorism, including specified aiding-and-abetting liability. It provides nationwide service and venue and exclusive federal jurisdiction. Majority slip opinion at 2–3.

Two ATA lawsuits reached the Court. The first, filed in 2004, involved Americans and their estates and survivors harmed in attacks in Israel. Known in the District Court as *Sokolow* and later recaptioned *Waldman*, it produced a 2015 jury verdict against the PLO and PA: $218.5 million, trebled to $655.5 million. In 2016 the Second Circuit vacated that judgment and directed dismissal for lack of personal jurisdiction, reasoning that the attacks were neither connected with the forum nor expressly directed at the United States. Congress responded in 2018 through the Anti-Terrorism Clarification Act, deeming specified U.S. activities or acceptance of certain American assistance consent to jurisdiction. The Second Circuit declined to recall its mandate under that statute. Majority slip opinion at 3–4 & n.1.

While the resulting certiorari petition was pending, Congress enacted the Promoting Security and Justice for Victims of Terrorism Act in December 2019. The PSJVTA superseded the ATCA’s jurisdictional provisions and named the PLO and PA specifically. The Supreme Court vacated the earlier Second Circuit judgment and remanded for reconsideration in light of that legislation. Shortly afterward, the family of an American stabbed in a 2018 West Bank attack filed the separate *Fuld* action, also invoking the PSJVTA. Majority slip opinion at 4–5.

Both groups alleged conduct triggering the new statute. The District Courts found evidence satisfying at least its payments predicate, but held the resulting personal jurisdiction unconstitutional. The United States intervened to defend the law. Applying circuit precedent that equated Fifth and Fourteenth Amendment standards, the Second Circuit affirmed: conduct inadequate to establish jurisdiction directly could not be treated as consent to that jurisdiction. After denial of rehearing en banc, the Supreme Court reviewed the two private suits and the Government’s companion petition. Majority slip opinion at 5–7.

## Statutory predicates and parties’ positions

Section 2334(e) deems the PLO and PA to have consented to personal jurisdiction in ATA cases if, after specified periods, they engage in either of two categories of conduct. The payments category reaches payments made because of imprisonment to a payee designated by a person fairly tried or pleading guilty and imprisoned for terrorism injuring or killing a U.S. national, and payments made because of death to a family member of someone who died while committing such terrorism. The activities category reaches maintaining or procuring facilities in the United States, or otherwise conducting activities while physically present here. The statute expressly excludes the United Nations mission and its ancillary activities. These are targeted predicates, rather than a provision subjecting all foreign defendants to all civil suits. Majority slip opinion at 4–5.

The respondents maintained that essentially identical due-process wording required the familiar minimum-contacts standard under both amendments. Even conceding that interstate federalism does not limit the National Government, they argued that fairness and individual liberty independently imposed equivalent restrictions. The private plaintiffs urged the broader conclusion that Fifth Amendment due process places no territorial limits on congressional jurisdiction. The Government advocated a narrower resolution upholding this particular statute, warning that unlimited jurisdiction was historically uncertain and could provoke reciprocal foreign assertions against Americans. Majority slip opinion at 9, 13–14.

## Issue, holding, judgment, and voting alignment

Does the PSJVTA’s personal-jurisdiction provision violate Fifth Amendment due process? No. The Fifth Amendment does not incorporate the Fourteenth Amendment’s minimum-contacts standard, and this narrow statute reasonably links jurisdiction over the PLO and PA to conduct involving the United States and sensitive foreign-policy matters entrusted to the political branches. The Court did not define the outer limits of permissible federal jurisdiction over foreign defendants. Majority slip opinion at 12, 14–21.

The Court reversed both Second Circuit judgments and remanded for further proceedings. All nine Justices agreed with that result. Chief Justice Roberts’s seven-Justice opinion was joined by Justices Alito, Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh, Barrett, and Jackson. Justice Thomas concurred only in the judgment; Justice Gorsuch joined Part II of Thomas’s concurrence. There was no dissent. The jurisdictional reversal is not an adjudication of the new action’s liability or damages and does not itself order payment of the earlier vacated verdict.

## Why the two due-process clauses impose different jurisdictional constraints

The Court’s modern minimum-contacts decisions ordinarily concern the Fourteenth Amendment’s limits on state authority. Even many cases originating in federal court involve those limits because Rule 4(k)(1)(A) generally incorporates the relevant state’s jurisdictional reach. This case instead concerns congressional authorization under Rule 4(k)(1)(C), which directly presents the previously reserved question about federal power under the Fifth Amendment. No party claimed that the PLO and PA were at home in the United States and therefore subject to general jurisdiction here. Majority slip opinion at 7–9.

The Fourteenth Amendment’s framework addresses both fair treatment of defendants and interstate federalism. States occupy bounded sovereign spheres as coequal members of the Union. Minimum contacts prevent one state from reaching beyond those limits through its courts. The National Government occupies a different sphere: the Constitution gives it nationwide and extraterritorial authority, including foreign-commerce powers and authority to address violence against Americans abroad. The constitutional relationship among states has no equivalent between the United States and foreign nations. Mechanically imposing the same territorial constraints on both sovereigns would disregard those differences. Majority slip opinion at 9–12.

The respondents invoked decisions describing personal jurisdiction as an individual-liberty protection rather than a sovereignty restriction. The majority understood those statements as protecting individuals against unlawful sovereign power; whether power is lawful still depends on the authority of the relevant sovereign. Interstate federalism can defeat a state’s jurisdiction even if litigation there imposes little practical inconvenience, as *World-Wide Volkswagen* and *Bristol-Myers Squibb* explain. General fairness therefore does not establish that federal and state jurisdiction must have identical limits. Majority slip opinion at 13–14.

The majority accepted without revisiting the lower courts’ conclusion that these nonsovereign entities have due-process rights: neither the private petitioners nor the Government requested reconsideration. That assumption does not decide whether every foreign governmental body qualifies as a constitutional person. Majority slip opinion at 9 n.2.

## Application to the PSJVTA

Foreign affairs remain subject to constitutional limits, so the political branches’ action does not eliminate judicial review. Nevertheless, courts owe respectful review to coordinated congressional and executive judgments concerning terrorism, national security, and international relations. The PSJVTA forms one part of a longstanding policy toward two particular entities and gives Americans harmed by terrorism an effective forum for seeking statutory compensation. Majority slip opinion at 14–17.

Its narrowness matters. It applies only to ATA suits and only to the two identified respondents. Its payments predicate advances Congress’s policy against payments it regards as promoting terrorism against Americans; its activities predicate regulates specified conduct on U.S. soil while excluding United Nations work. The respondents already have meaningful ties and longstanding relationships with the United States. The provision thus bears a meaningful connection to the forum sovereign’s legitimate concerns rather than asserting indiscriminate authority over foreign persons. Majority slip opinion at 17–19.

The respondents characterized the payments as social welfare rather than rewards for terrorism and reported formally revoking the program in February 2025. The Court expressed no view on the proper characterization and stated that the reported revocation did not affect its constitutional analysis. It also rejected the criticism that the statute excluded other terrorist organizations: that targeted scope reinforced its character as a nuanced foreign-policy judgment. The Court declined to second-guess why Congress chose this narrower tool. Majority slip opinion at 17 n.3, 18–19.

Because the specified conduct itself adequately supports jurisdiction under the proper Fifth Amendment analysis, the Court did not decide whether the statute satisfied the separate line of cases defining express or implied consent. The statutory label “deemed consent” therefore should not be mistaken for a holding that any legislative declaration can manufacture consent from constitutionally insufficient conduct. The majority’s route made a separate *Mallory*-style consent inquiry unnecessary. Majority slip opinion at 19.

The Court also reserved whether the Fifth Amendment requires an additional reasonableness inquiry. Assuming such a requirement, the law satisfied the familiar considerations: the sovereign has an exceptionally strong interest in providing a forum for American terrorism victims, plaintiffs have a strong interest in obtaining relief here, and respondents neither alleged inadequate notice nor identified an unfair or unmanageable litigation burden. Their resources, international facilities, longstanding U.S. presence, and decades of ATA litigation supported that assessment. The statute clearly warned them that specified conduct would expose them to jurisdiction. Majority slip opinion at 19–21.

## Thomas’s concurrence and Gorsuch’s limited joinder

Thomas would have adopted the broader text-and-history theory that the Fifth Amendment imposes no territorial restriction on Congress’s authority to extend federal jurisdiction. His Part I, which Gorsuch did not join, raised two tentative threshold concerns: whether foreign nonsovereign governmental entities possess constitutional rights, and whether the original concept of due process limited legislative power at all. He reasoned from the treatment of foreign sovereigns, states, territories, and municipalities to question whether withholding recognition should give foreign governmental entities greater constitutional protections. Because the petitioners had not pressed that argument, he would not resolve the case on it. Concurrence slip opinion at 2–8 & nn.1–2.

Thomas also examined competing understandings of due process. A law-of-the-land account might require only adherence to constitutional and statutory authorization; *Murray’s Lessee* instead treated due process as limiting legislation and requiring settled procedural protections. He did not choose between those accounts because, on either, respondents’ objection failed. Concurrence slip opinion at 9–11.

Part II, joined by Gorsuch, located early territorial restrictions in the law of nations rather than Fifth Amendment due process. Article I and Article III contemplate extraterritorial legislative and judicial authority, and early federal practice exercised it. Courts sometimes refused recognition to judgments exceeding international-law limits, but understood Congress could override those subconstitutional rules through clear enactment. Thomas relied especially on *Picquet v. Swan* and *Toland v. Sprague*, which construed statutes as not authorizing the asserted reach while acknowledging that courts would follow an express congressional command. Concurrence slip opinion at 11–17.

For Thomas, possible retaliation by other countries supplies a policy reason for restraint, rather than a constitutional barrier. Leaving such choices to the political branches preserves their control of foreign relations, including settlement of international claims. The PSJVTA unmistakably supplies the required congressional command. He separately reserved whether due process requires notice and an opportunity to be heard, distinguishing those procedural protections from territorial authority and finding them satisfied here even if applicable. Concurrence slip opinion at 18–21 & nn.3–4. This unlimited-territorial-authority theory is the concurrence’s position; seven Justices deliberately decided the case on narrower grounds.

The decision establishes that the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendment jurisdictional standards differ and validates this statute. It leaves the Fifth Amendment’s full boundaries, the necessity of a separate reasonableness test, a freestanding consent analysis, and the substantive disposition of the underlying actions for other proceedings.
