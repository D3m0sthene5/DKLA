# Elsinore Union Elementary School District v. Kastorff
54 Cal. 2d 380, 353 P.2d 713 (Cal. 1960) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and disposition
Justice Schauer wrote for the California Supreme Court sitting in bank on July 1, 1960. Chief Justice Gibson and Justices Traynor, McComb, Peters, White, and Dooling concurred, making the reported decision unanimous among the seven participating justices. A contractor and his surety appealed a judgment awarding a school district damages after the contractor refused to execute a building contract at his submitted bid. The court held that the honest clerical error and prompt rescission relieved him from that obligation and reversed the judgment. It did not award reformation to substitute a higher bid or direct performance at a judicially chosen price. (380–382, 389.)

## Bid preparation and the error
The district invited bids for additions to its school buildings, due at 8 p.m. on August 12, 1952. Contractor E. J. Kastorff used worksheets listing subcontractors’ quotations on the left and selected amounts in a right-hand totals column. Subcontractors commonly delayed bids until the last moment. Kastorff received a plumbing bid for $9,285 during the afternoon and later another for $6,500. Believing that the first amount had already been included in his total, he deducted approximately $3,000 to account for the cheaper replacement. In fact he had carried neither plumbing figure into the total. His submitted $89,994 bid therefore contained no allowance for the plumbing work. (382.)

That bid was $11,306 below the next lowest of five bids. The superintendent and four board members asked whether his figures were correct. Kastorff checked with his clerical assistant in the hall and confirmed them, but did not have the worksheets or other estimating papers with him. The board voted that evening to award him the contract. This premature assurance and award mattered, but the court did not treat them as automatically foreclosing equitable relief. (382–383.)

## Discovery, notice, and the district’s demand
The next morning Kastorff examined the worksheets and discovered the error. He immediately drove to the architects’ Los Angeles office and showed the worksheets to Rendon, who confirmed a clerical omission: the plumbing figure appeared on the worksheet but had not been carried into the final total. Rendon telephoned the superintendent that morning, described the error, and conveyed Kastorff’s request for release. Kastorff wrote directly to the board on August 14. On August 15 the board declined his request. Written notice awarding the contract arrived on August 28; the district then sent a formal contract, which Kastorff returned on September 8 with another explanation and request to withdraw. (383.)

The bid form required execution of the contract within five days after written notification of acceptance received within forty-five days of bid opening. The published invitation also purported to prohibit withdrawal for forty-five days. The court distinguished this ordinary binding bid arrangement from the equitable power to rescind for an established mistake. Judicial note 2 expressly reserved whether, following his rescission, the district could have accepted the original next-lowest bid before the forty-five days expired. (383–384 & nn.1–2.)

The district instead received additional bids and contracted with the lowest new bidder for $102,900. It sought $12,906 from Kastorff, the difference from his erroneous bid, and $4,499.60 from his surety under the bid bond. Kastorff pleaded honest error, prompt notice, and rescission; his pleaded intended total was $99,494. The trial court nevertheless entered judgment for the district in the amounts sought. (383–385.)

## Trial findings and appellate evidentiary correction
The trial court acknowledged the worksheets, multiple subcontractor bids, and an omission in transferring the plumbing and heating figures. It also made adverse findings: the use of the totals column could not be ascertained, the district did not know that the submitted bid differed from the intended bid, enforcing it would not be inequitable, and Kastorff had ample time to check his figures. Those adverse findings, rather than favorable findings of complete excusability, were the obstacle the Supreme Court addressed. (384–385 & n.3.)

The Supreme Court found certain findings unsupported and others immaterial to the governing standard. The worksheets and uncontradicted testimony, including testimony from the district’s own architect, superintendent, and board member, established the clerical mechanism and the board’s receipt of notice. The court inspected the worksheets itself: both plumbing quotations appeared on the left, while the totals column contained none. The district’s August 15 refusal to release him occurred with knowledge of the asserted error. Mere time and opportunity to check the estimate did not establish a neglect of legal duty sufficient to bar relief. (388 & n.4.)

## Rescission rule and its application
The court relied on Kemper and Lemoge. Once opened and declared, a bid subject to a nonwithdrawal condition could function as an irrevocable option, a contract right that ordinary unilateral withdrawal could not destroy. Nevertheless, the bidder could rescind if the requirements for equitable relief were established. As described in the precedents, the mistake must be material and not result from neglect of a legal duty; enforcement must be unconscionable; the other party must be capable of restoration to the status quo; and the mistaken party must promptly elect rescission and restore or offer to restore any value received. Knowledge, or reason to know, of the other party’s mistake supported relief rather than a right to exploit it. Ordinary carelessness was not invariably the neglect of legal duty that defeated rescission. (385–388.)

Kemper supplied an example in which the trial court had actually found an excusable material omission, prompt withdrawal, knowledge, and no prejudice; that case’s judgment granting relief had been affirmed. Lemoge, by contrast, involved a bidder who discovered the mistake but went ahead, executed the contract, performed, and later sought reformation. Reformation was denied, though the court recognized that timely rescission had been available. These examples explain why Kastorff’s prompt withdrawal, rather than performance followed by a request for a better price, was central. (385–388.)

Kastorff’s assurance at bid opening did not deprive him of relief. He had no worksheets present then, checked them at the earliest practicable opportunity, and promptly notified the district. The bid form’s specified written notification came about two weeks after rescission. The district expected a complete building including plumbing, and had no legitimate expectation that the necessary plumbing would be furnished without any corresponding charge. Enforcing the known omission would create an unfair bargain. Whether the omitted amount was considered $6,500 or $9,285, it was material relative to $89,994; the court rejected the district’s immateriality argument. The judgment was reversed. (388–389 & n.5.)

## Assigned-source discrepancy and historical scope
The account at printed 209–210 correctly identifies clerical omission, next-day discovery, refusal to release, and reversal. But its statement that the Supreme Court agreed with favorable trial findings about Kastorff is inaccurate: the favorable finding passage in the actual opinion describes Kemper, while the Kastorff trial made adverse findings that the Supreme Court rejected. The assignment also compresses the replacement procurement into hiring the second-lowest bidder, whereas the actual report describes additional bids and the new $102,900 contract. This brief follows the original judicial record. (383–388.)
