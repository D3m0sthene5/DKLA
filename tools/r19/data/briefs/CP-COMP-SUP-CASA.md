# Trump v. CASA, Inc.

**Citation and court:** 606 U.S. 831 (2025), Supreme Court of the United States, applications Nos. 24A884, 24A885, and 24A886, decided June 27, 2025. Justice Barrett wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh. Thomas concurred, joined by Gorsuch; Alito concurred, joined by Thomas; Kavanaugh concurred. Sotomayor dissented, joined by Kagan and Jackson. Jackson also wrote a dissent and joined Sotomayor's dissent in full.

**Assigned reading and sources:** Civil Procedure Supplement, printed pages 518–519, physical PDF page 347, the complete own-caption account between Riley and Coney Island. **Facts and challenges.** Executive Order 14160 directed federal officials not to issue or accept citizenship documents for certain children born in the United States: children whose mothers were unlawfully present, or lawfully present only temporarily, when their fathers were neither citizens nor lawful permanent residents. The challenged policy would apply to covered births after the order's prospective implementation date. Individuals, organizations, and States brought three suits in Maryland, Washington, and Massachusetts. Each district court concluded that the policy likely violated the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause and §201 of the Nationality Act of 1940, codified at 8 U.S.C. §1401, and entered a preliminary injunction protecting everyone subject to the policy. The Fourth, Ninth, and First Circuits declined to stay those injunctions. 606 U.S. at 837–839.

The Government sought relief only from the injunctions' breadth. The Supreme Court did not take up the legality of the citizenship policy. The lower courts' merits assessments therefore remain part of the procedural history, rather than a Supreme Court merits holding in this decision. Id. at 839.

**Issue and holding in the stay posture.** The question was whether the general equitable authority Congress supplied through the Judiciary Act of 1789 permitted injunctions extending beyond what was necessary to afford complete relief to each plaintiff with standing. The Government was likely to succeed on its contention that the challenged universal injunctions exceeded that authority. The Court partially stayed them to the extent of that excess, subject to tailoring by the lower courts. Id. at 839–841, 859–862.

Universal describes **who receives protection**, whereas nationwide describes geographic reach. A valid injunction protecting particular parties can operate throughout the country. The problem identified here was relief for nonparties without a sufficient connection to complete relief for the actual plaintiffs. Id. at 837–838 n.1. The assigned account groups universal and nationwide injunctions together; the full opinion supplies this distinction.

**Historical equitable authority.** Under Grupo Mexicano, the general statutory grant reaches forms of equitable relief traditionally administered by the English Court of Chancery at the founding. The majority found no adequate historical analogue for an injunction giving relief to every person potentially affected by executive action, regardless of party status. Chancery ordinarily resolved the rights of parties before the court. Early American practice, including Scott, Perkins, Frothingham, and Doran, reinforced that party-oriented understanding. Id. at 841–847.

The majority treated the absence of universal injunctions from founding-era practice as significant, although a court's failure to reject such an injunction was not itself a holding against its authority. It disputed the dissent's reading of nineteenth- and twentieth-century examples. Scott was illustrative rather than independently decisive; Smyth's actual decree protected the railroad parties; later decisions concerning Lewis, Pierce, Barnette, and Wirtz did not establish the requisite founding-era analogue. Id. at 843 n.6, 845–846 nn.7–8. It also explained Ex parte Young through historical antisuit and officer-relief principles rather than an unrestricted power to invent remedies. Id. at 846–847 n.9.

**Bills of peace and class actions.** A historical bill of peace could represent a cohesive group and bind its members, including through an adverse result. A universal injunction instead could benefit nonparties who would not be bound by an unsuccessful plaintiff's judgment. Modern Rule 23 retains representation and procedural safeguards, including numerosity, commonality, typicality, and adequacy. The majority rejected treating universal relief as a substitute for those requirements. Id. at 847–850. It did not prohibit nationwide relief for a properly certified class.

**Complete relief and incidental benefits.** Relief must be sufficient for the plaintiffs but cannot exceed what is necessary for them. An injunction may incidentally benefit nonparties, and an indivisible injury may require an order with wider practical effects. A nuisance or certain districting injuries can illustrate that distinction. Complete relief is a ceiling, rather than a guarantee of the maximum relief a plaintiff requests. Id. at 850–854 & nn.11–13.

For the individual pregnant plaintiffs, protecting their children would afford complete relief; extending protection to unrelated people added nothing necessary for those plaintiffs. The States presented a more complex question. They argued that citizenship determinations affect public benefits, administration, and children moving across state lines. The Court left the lower courts to decide whether narrower measures could provide complete relief to each State. It did not resolve every State's standing or hold that a plaintiff-specific injunction could never have nationwide effects. Id. at 852–854.

**Policy and the stay factors.** The parties disputed uniformity, forum shopping, overlapping injunctions, and the consequences of leaving some people unprotected while appellate review proceeds. The majority regarded these competing considerations as insufficient to create statutory remedial authority. Universal injunctions also allow one district judge to supply broad relief without binding all beneficiaries to an adverse result. Id. at 854–856 & nn.15–16.

The majority rejected the proposition that the Executive's duty to obey the law gives courts unlimited authority to choose a remedy. It distinguished the constitutional obligation from the judicial power to enforce it in a particular case. District-court reasoning does not bind other courts or the Executive everywhere merely because it identifies unlawful conduct. Id. at 857–859 & n.17. This was the majority's response to the dissents, not a holding that the Executive may lawfully violate the Constitution.

Applying Nken, the Court found irreparable harm in enforcement of orders extending beyond judicial authority and concluded that the balance favored a partial stay. Its likelihood-of-success inquiry concerned that remedial authority; it did not decide the citizenship merits as a prerequisite. The lower courts remained able to protect plaintiffs through appropriately tailored relief. The Solicitor General's representations about obtaining review and respecting judgments were recorded, rather than a guarantee about every future litigation event. Id. at 859–861 & n.18.

**Express reservations.** The Court did not decide whether Article III would independently prohibit statutorily authorized universal injunctions. Id. at 841 n.4. It did not decide the lawfulness of the citizenship order, the organizations' standing on behalf of unidentified members, or the States' third-party standing. Id. at 838 n.2, 839. It also left open whether §706(2) of the Administrative Procedure Act authorizes courts to vacate agency action with universal effect. Id. at 847 n.10. Class relief remained available subject to Rule 23.

**Thomas's concurrence.** Thomas emphasized that complete relief is only one limit on equitable authority. Other traditional requirements still apply. A plaintiff cannot obtain universal relief merely by presenting compliance as indivisible or practically difficult to tailor. His discussion of Frothingham linked standing and equitable remedial limits; he also raised serious Article III concerns if Congress tried to supply broader authority. Those concerns were his view, joined by Gorsuch, rather than an Article III holding of the Court. Id. at 862–865, including the asterisk note at 864.

**Alito's concurrence.** Alito cautioned against evading the decision through expansive third-party standing or casual class certification. State litigation on others' behalf must satisfy the relevant standing requirements, and Rule 23 requirements must actually be established rather than merely pleaded. He distinguished injury to the State itself from an assertion of its residents' rights. He did not adjudicate the States' standing or deny a particular class certification in these applications. Id. at 866–868.

**Kavanaugh's concurrence.** Kavanaugh joined the Court in full and described how nationwide interim legal rules may emerge through certified classes, APA litigation where available, and Supreme Court emergency review. In major disputes, lower-court preliminary rulings often function as an interim step before the Supreme Court's interim decision, with final merits review later. That description did not enlarge district courts' general authority to grant universal injunctions. His discussion of APA vacatur was separate from the Court's express reservation. Id. at 868–879.

His notes distinguish incidental nonparty benefits, acknowledge direct-review routes for some statutes, and compare preliminary-injunction and stay standards. Supreme Court stays include a likelihood that review will be granted and a fair prospect of reversal, together with irreparable harm and equitable considerations. Id. at 869 n.1, 873 n.2, 874 nn.3–4. These observations explain his view of appellate management during litigation rather than deciding the citizenship policy's merits.

**Sotomayor's dissent: merits and equities.** Sotomayor would deny the applications. She considered the citizenship order plainly unconstitutional and contrary to longstanding statute and practice. Her discussion drew on the Citizenship Clause's text and history, the Civil Rights Act of 1866, Wong Kim Ark, and later precedents addressing citizenship and immigration. She acknowledged historical exceptions involving diplomats and hostile occupation, and the historical tribal exclusion later addressed by citizenship legislation. This was dissenting merits analysis, not a merits judgment issued by the Court. Id. at 879–894 & nn.1–2.

She denied that preventing likely unlawful action could establish the Government's irreparable harm, and considered the prevailing legal regime the proper status quo. She disputed a historical test requiring a precise founding-era match, reading equity as capable of adapting established principles to later legal conditions. Bills of peace, taxpayer litigation, Smyth, Lewis, Pierce, Barnette, and Ex parte Young supported broader authority in her account. She distinguished prudential restraint from a categorical lack of remedial power and criticized the majority's treatment of Frothingham and Grupo Mexicano. Id. at 895–908 & nn.3–7.

**Dissent on plaintiff relief and practical consequences.** Even using the Court's complete-relief standard, Sotomayor believed the records justified broader injunctions. State administration depends on citizenship documents, Social Security numbers, benefits programs, and families moving across borders. She argued that the Government had forfeited particular narrowing proposals and had not shown that workable narrower relief existed or that the district courts abused their discretion. She also emphasized difficulties protecting large organizational memberships. Id. at 908–915. Her note 8 recognizes that the Court's stay reached only relief exceeding complete relief; note 9 disputes the majority's description of the Washington district court's reasoning. Id. at 909 n.8, 911 n.9.

She stressed potential statelessness, removal, administrative burdens, and unequal access to litigation for newborns and families. The Government's promise to pursue review did not, in her view, adequately assure timely merits resolution. She recognized Rule 23(b)(2) remained available, while warning that certification and interlocutory proceedings could impose delay and expense. Id. at 915–921 & nn.10–11. These were dissenting assessments of harm and judicial administration, not findings that the Court decided every affected person's citizenship.

**Jackson's dissent.** Jackson argued that restricting relief to litigants weakens the judicial role in enforcing generally binding constitutional limits on the Executive. In her view, a lawsuit invokes tools for enforcing an existing universal duty; it does not create that duty. She emphasized the consequences for people unable to sue, unequal protection in practice, and the risk of leaving enforcement dependent on individual resources and executive discretion. Id. at 921–941.

Jackson expressly did **not** claim that a district court's reasoning alone has the force of a judgment everywhere. Her note 3 separates that proposition from the Executive's independent obligation to follow the law. Id. at 930–931 n.3. She also disputed the majority's characterization of benefits and formal relief for nonparties under Rule 71. Compare id. at 928–929 n.2 with id. at 851 n.11. Her constitutional arguments, historical warnings, and alternatives such as focused venue rules and expedited review remained separate views rather than the statutory holding. Id. at 926 n.1, 933–934 nn.4–5, 936–937 nn.6–8, 939 n.9.

**Exact disposition.** The Court **granted the three applications for partial stays only to the extent the injunctions were broader than necessary to provide complete relief to each plaintiff with standing**. The lower courts were to proceed expeditiously to tailor relief consistently with the opinion and equitable principles. The Court also stayed the injunctions insofar as they prohibited executive agencies from developing and issuing public guidance concerning implementation. Section 2 of the Executive Order could not take effect until thirty days after the opinion's date, consistent with the Solicitor General's representation. Id. at 861–862. The Court did not vacate every injunction, terminate the suits, or decide the citizenship merits.

**Course significance.** Distinguish statutory remedial authority, plaintiff standing, geographic scope, and merits. A partial stay of excess relief differs from a decision that the challenged policy is lawful. The assigned account identifies the limit on universal injunctions; the complete decision adds plaintiff-specific tailoring, class and APA reservations, five separate writings, and the implementation conditions.
