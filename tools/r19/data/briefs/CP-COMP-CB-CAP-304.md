# Wilson v. Citigroup, N.A.

**702 F.3d 720 (2d Cir. 2012) · Second Circuit · December 26, 2012 · Civil Procedure**

**Assigned account:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 374, printed page 714, complete Note 5 before the 715 marker. The issued opinion's running title is Langrock Sperry v. Citigroup; its caption and published report identify the same appeal, No. 11-5085-cv.

**Decision in one sentence.** The court reversed a $24,398.83 fee sanction against two law firms for a four-day-late opposition filing: the record warranted no sanction, the district court made no explicit bad-faith finding, and counsel received no prior notice or opportunity to defend against sanctions. 702 F.3d at 723–725.

## Facts and procedure

Robert Wilson sued Citigroup and other defendants in March 2011 over his alleged exclusion from profits generated by investments in Brazilian companies. Langrock Sperry & Wool and Lankford & Reed represented him. When Citigroup moved to dismiss, the firms obtained opposing counsel's consent to extend their July 11 opposition deadline to July 28, with a corresponding reply extension. Their July 7 electronic stipulation was rejected under local filing procedures; they emailed it to the clerk the next day. The judge denied it without explanation at that time. Id. at 722.

On July 11, counsel faxed a renewed request detailing competing trial preparation, depositions, discovery, pretrial deadlines, court appearances, and professional obligations. The court did not promptly decide that request. Counsel filed their opposition on July 15 with a motion for leave to file late: four days after the original deadline, but before the date opposing counsel had accepted. Id. at 722–723.

On September 13, the district court granted the late-filing motion, expressly finding the scheduling conflicts supplied good cause. It nevertheless required counsel to pay Citigroup's reasonable fees and costs for preparing the reply, ostensibly to cure prejudice from the delayed filing. Citigroup claimed $24,398.83. The firms objected to the sanction's type and size and requested reconsideration; the judge ordered prompt payment, which the firms made on October 21. After dismissal of the complaint for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, they appealed **only the sanctions**, not the underlying dismissal. Id. at 723.

## Issue, standard, and reasons for reversal

The court reviewed the exercise of inherent sanctions authority for abuse of discretion. Deference did not dispense with restraint or a supported legal basis, especially when sanctioning counsel through their opponents' legal bills. Id. at 723–724.

**The conduct supplied no substantive justification.** The appellate court first concluded the firms had done nothing warranting a sanction. They sought an agreed schedule, explained good cause before the deadline expired, and filed before the agreed extended date. The court found no disobedience of an order and no prejudice to opposing counsel. Id. at 724. That assessment rested on the complete sequence, including the district court's eventual good-cause finding; it did not establish that a private agreement automatically changes every judicial deadline.

**An inherent-power fee sanction required an explicit bad-faith finding.** The district court made none. The circuit's cases required clear evidence that the challenged conduct lacked a legitimate basis and served harassment, delay, or another improper purpose, supported by specific findings. This record supplied no such clear evidence. The timely consented request, the renewed explanation, and the later good-cause finding contradicted an inference of improper delay. Id. at 724–725. The holding concerns the inherent-power attorney-fee sanction reviewed here; it should not be converted into a subjective bad-faith prerequisite for every sanction under every rule or statute.

**Due process independently required notice and an opportunity to respond.** Counsel had to know the particular conduct alleged to be sanctionable, the assessment standard, and the authority being invoked, and have a chance to defend against the charges before sanctions were imposed. Those protections were absent. An opportunity to object after the fee order was not the prior process the court required. Id. at 725; issued slip opinion at 8–9.

Citigroup argued counsel had waived appellate review by failing to raise the bad-faith issue below. Note 2 rejected that argument because counsel had not had a timely opportunity to make the point in the district court. Id. at 724 n.2; slip opinion at 7 n.2. The lack of advance process therefore could not itself become the reason to bar the challenge.

## Judgment, panel, and assignment

The court **reversed the district court's orders**. Note 1 explains that it had already issued a December 12, 2012 order reversing the sanctions and directing return of the money; the December 26 published opinion explains that result. Slip opinion at 3 n.1, 9. This opinion did not revive Wilson's substantive investment claims or decide their merits.

The decision is per curiam for Judges Katzmann, Parker, and Wesley. No separate writing, dissent, or appendix appears. Both judicial footnotes were read.

The assignment accurately emphasizes the four-day interval, lack of prejudice, missing bad-faith finding, and absence of notice. Its surrounding discussion places inherent authority beside Rule 11 and section 1927; the actual court's fee-sanction analysis supplies the precise doctrinal setting. Consent, eventual good cause, and failure to provide required process are concrete reasons for reversal rather than a blanket exemption for late filings.
