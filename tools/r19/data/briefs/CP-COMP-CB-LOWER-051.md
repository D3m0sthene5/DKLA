# Catrett v. Johns-Manville Sales Corp.

**826 F.2d 33 (D.C. Cir. 1987), No. 83-1694; decided August 7, 1987.** United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit. Judge Starr wrote the majority opinion, joined by Chief Judge Wald; Judge Bork dissented, producing a 2–1 reversal. Civil Procedure. Assigned: CivPro.pdf, physical page 522, printed pages 1023–1025, complete Note 1 following the Supreme Court’s Celotex excerpt.

## Facts and litigation chronology

Myrtle Nell Catrett, administratrix of her husband Louis’s estate, sued fifteen asbestos manufacturers or distributors in 1980. Louis had died the preceding year after a construction career involving asbestos products. Celotex was the only defendant before this panel. The disputed link was whether Louis had encountered an asbestos product for whose ordinary liabilities Celotex was responsible. (33.)

In February 1981 the defendants served interrogatories asking for knowledgeable witnesses and detailed information about Louis’s asbestos work. Myrtle initially deferred answers to those inquiries. Her other responses identified his employment with Anning-Johnson and his work as a fireproofer. Celotex first sought summary judgment in September 1981 because she had not shown exposure to its products. Her opposition supplied Louis’s earlier workers’ compensation testimony, a letter by Anning-Johnson assistant secretary T. R. Hoff, and an insurer’s letter repeating Hoff’s information. Celotex withdrew that motion. (34–35 & nn. 2–5.)

Celotex then requested transfer to Illinois, relying on Louis’s Chicago-area employment. Its own supporting material included purchase orders showing Firebar shipments to Anning-Johnson during 1971. The documents identified the seller, Carey-Canadian Asbestos, as a Panacon division. Celotex’s interrogatory answers acknowledged that Panacon merged into Celotex in 1972 and that Celotex assumed Panacon’s ordinary liabilities. These defense-supplied documents eventually became important to the plaintiff’s showing. (35–36; 35 n. 3.)

A renewed summary-judgment motion emphasized the absence of exposure within the district court’s geographic boundaries. Myrtle again relied on the three documents and supplemented her interrogatory responses to identify Hoff as a trial witness. At the July 1982 hearing, Celotex challenged the admissibility of Louis’s prior testimony, although it had itself used that testimony for venue purposes. The judges later disagreed about whether counsel also effectively objected to Hoff’s letter. The district court granted summary judgment orally, finding no showing of exposure in the District of Columbia or elsewhere within the statutory period. It issued no written opinion. (36–37 & nn. 6–9.)

The circuit initially reversed because Celotex had not supported its motion with affirmative evidence. The Supreme Court reversed that legal ruling in Celotex Corp. v. Catrett and remanded for consideration of Myrtle’s actual showing. This 1987 decision is that distinct remand stage. The majority treated the Supreme Court’s majority opinion as controlling the moving party’s initial burden; it declined the invitation to reconsider that burden using a narrower reading of Justice White’s separate concurrence. (33–34 & n. 1.)

## Question and governing standard

The question was whether the record **when the district court granted this motion** contained a genuine factual issue about exposure to Celotex-related asbestos. The panel expressly reserved whether a better-developed record might later justify summary judgment. It was not deciding that the case necessarily had to be tried. (37.)

Under the then-current Rule 56 and Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, a genuine issue requires enough evidence for a jury to return a verdict for the nonmoving party. The inquiry parallels directed-verdict analysis: is the evidence sufficiently disputed, or must one side prevail as a matter of law? The Supreme Court had also explained that the opposing party need not present all evidence in its final trial-admissible form. Whether the material could become admissible evidence remained important. (38–39 & nn. 11–12.)

## Majority’s reasoning

The majority evaluated the cumulative evidentiary chain. Hoff’s letter said Louis supervised and trained crews applying Firebar during his Anning-Johnson employment. Myrtle’s supplemental answer identified Hoff as someone who knew relevant facts and would testify. Read together, the letter and answer indicated a witness capable of providing admissible testimony about exposure; the court did not treat a bare witness name in isolation as automatically sufficient. (37–38 & n. 11.)

The court did not finally decide that the letter itself satisfied the business-records exception. It found inadmissibility uncertain and, more importantly, concluded that Celotex had not timely objected to its consideration. Counsel discussed the letter’s substance, the district judge requested it, and the judge examined it before ruling. The majority therefore treated it as part of the summary-judgment record. In note 10 it rejected Bork’s contrary interpretation of the interrupted hearing exchange, also reasoning that the judge’s actual consideration of the letter implicitly overruled any unclear objection. (37–38 & n. 10.)

Other evidence reinforced the exposure inference. Celotex’s purchase orders placed Firebar at Anning-Johnson while Louis worked there. Interrogatory responses identified his fireproofing occupation, and Celotex’s own merger admissions connected Carey-Canadian, Panacon, and Celotex. Although each piece was less than conclusive alone, together they supplied a chain from Louis’s work to Firebar and from Firebar to Celotex’s assumed ordinary liabilities. The panel did not adjudicate Celotex’s ultimate liability for his injury or death. (38–40 & n. 14.)

The majority also explained what it did **not** use. It excluded Louis’s compensation testimony because admissibility was doubtful and Celotex specifically objected below. The insurer’s letter added nothing independent because it merely repeated Hoff. Thus the reversal did not rest on a blanket acceptance of all three documents originally offered by Myrtle. (39 n. 13.)

Nor did the majority excuse discovery failures categorically. It acknowledged that Myrtle still had not answered the detailed exposure interrogatories. But the defendants’ motion to compel had never been decided; in these circumstances, the majority treated noncompliance as a matter for district-court discovery enforcement rather than a sufficient answer to the record’s existing factual showing. (36 n. 7.)

## Bork’s dissent

Bork would have upheld summary judgment. He agreed that the Supreme Court had resolved Celotex’s initial burden and focused on the plaintiff’s required response. Neither listing Hoff nor describing general employment duties established that a witness possessed personal knowledge of actual asbestos exposure. The corporate relationship and shipping records might identify the manufacturer if exposure were shown, but they did not supply that missing personal-knowledge step. (40–42 & dissent n. 2.)

He also regarded the letter as inadmissible hearsay. It was unsworn, did not purport to rest on personal knowledge, and appeared to have been prepared in response to litigation inquiries rather than as a regularly kept business record. Bork read counsel’s interrupted statement and subsequent reference to inadmissibility “either” as an objection to the letter. He therefore rejected the majority’s waiver premise. (41–42 & dissent n. 1.)

His broader concern was that assembling vague material into possible future evidence would effectively grant an unrequested extension of discovery. Summary judgment should test the showing already made, not evidence that might emerge later. He believed the majority’s approach weakened the directed-verdict standard and the Rules’ aim of resolving cases efficiently. (42–43.)

## Judgment, assignment, and source scope

The panel **reversed the district court’s judgment and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion**. It established neither proven exposure nor a mandatory eventual trial. The assigned excerpts preserve the central dispute over the Hoff letter and Bork’s objection; the complete opinion adds the purchase records, merger admissions, excluded materials, and express limitation to the record then before the district court. (40; assigned 1023–1025.)
