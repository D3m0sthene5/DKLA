# Morrison v. Olson
487 U.S. 654 (1988) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, posture, and judgment

The Supreme Court reversed the D.C. Circuit’s judgment holding the independent-counsel provisions of the Ethics in Government Act unconstitutional. Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote for Brennan, White, Marshall, Blackmun, Stevens, and O’Connor. Scalia alone dissented; Kennedy took no part. The June 29, 1988 decision was therefore 7–1, not an eight-Justice unanimous resolution. It upheld the statutory arrangement against Appointments Clause, Article III, and separation-of-powers challenges. The case concerned grand-jury subpoenas and contempt orders, not a determination that Olson or the other witnesses had committed a crime. 487 U.S. at 654, 658–660, 668–670, 696–697.

## What the statute authorized

The Act provided for investigation and possible prosecution of specified high-ranking executive or presidential-campaign officials for federal crimes beyond minor misdemeanors or infractions. It also allowed the Attorney General to investigate other persons where ordinary Justice Department investigation might create a personal, financial, or political conflict. Upon sufficient information, the Attorney General conducted a preliminary investigation. If he determined there were no reasonable grounds for further investigation, the Special Division could not appoint counsel. If grounds existed, or the ninety-day period expired without a contrary determination, he had to seek an appointment. The application supplied information for selecting counsel and defining jurisdiction. Id. at 660–661 & nn. 1–4.

The Special Division consisted of three circuit judges or Justices selected by the Chief Justice for two-year terms, including one D.C. Circuit judge and no two from the same court. It appointed counsel and defined prosecutorial jurisdiction. Within that jurisdiction, counsel exercised the Justice Department’s investigative and prosecutorial powers, including grand-jury proceedings, indictments, litigation, and appeals. Counsel could hire staff and obtain Department assistance. The Department had to suspend its own proceedings concerning referred matters. Counsel generally had to follow established Department policy except where compliance was not possible, and could dismiss matters consistently with that policy. Id. at 661–663 & nn. 3–7.

Removal and termination were distinct. Apart from impeachment, removal required the Attorney General’s personal action for good cause, disability, incapacity, or another condition substantially impairing performance. He had to report the facts and reasons to the Special Division and congressional Judiciary Committees. Under the version reviewed, counsel could challenge removal in district court, with reinstatement or other relief available; Special Division members could not decide that litigation. Separately, counsel could declare the work completed or substantially completed, and the Division could terminate the office when the work had reached that stage. Reporting, expenses, fees for unindicted subjects, and congressional oversight supplied additional statutory duties. Congress could request that the Attorney General seek counsel, but could not itself make the appointment. Id. at 663–665 & nn. 7–9.

## Events leading to the constitutional litigation

The controversy grew from 1982 House subpoenas for EPA documents relating to Superfund enforcement. On Justice Department advice, President Reagan instructed EPA’s Administrator to withhold certain enforcement-sensitive documents under executive privilege. The House held the Administrator in contempt, and the Administrator and United States sued the House. A March 1983 agreement eventually allowed limited access. Olson, then Assistant Attorney General for the Office of Legal Counsel, testified on March 10, 1983. A later Judiciary Committee investigation produced a 1985 report alleging false or misleading testimony by Olson and improper withholding by Deputy Attorney General Schmults and Assistant Attorney General Dinkins. These were allegations, not findings of guilt in this case. Id. at 665–666.

The Department’s Public Integrity Section recommended independent investigation of all three. The Attorney General sought counsel only for Olson, concluding that Schmults and Dinkins lacked the requisite criminal intent. His request included other matters related to the Olson allegation. The Special Division appointed James McKay on April 23, 1986; after McKay resigned, it appointed Alexia Morrison on May 29 with the same jurisdiction. Id. at 666–667 & n. 11.

In January 1987, Morrison requested referral of the allegations against the other two officials as related matters. The Attorney General refused. The Division concluded that it could not override the final decision not to seek separate investigations, but that the original grant allowed investigation of whether Olson had conspired with others, including those officials, to obstruct the Committee. Morrison then caused grand-jury testimony and document subpoenas to be issued in May and June. The witnesses moved to quash on constitutional grounds. The district court rejected their challenge, denied the motions, and entered contempt orders when they continued to refuse compliance, staying those orders for appeal. Id. at 667–668.

The divided D.C. Circuit reversed on several grounds: counsel was a principal officer, judicial appointment of an executive officer was improper, the Division’s duties violated Article III, and the statute impermissibly impaired executive control. The Supreme Court rejected Morrison’s preliminary claim that Blair barred consideration of those objections in a contempt appeal. She had not raised it in district court and could waive it; it did not eliminate an actual adversarial controversy or the courts’ power to resolve it. The Court did not decide whether subsequent cases had narrowed Blair. Id. at 668–670.

## Inferior-officer status

The Appointments Clause ordinarily requires presidential nomination and Senate consent for principal officers, while allowing Congress to vest appointment of inferior officers in the President alone, department heads, or courts of law. The Court did not announce an exhaustive boundary between principal and inferior officers. It found Morrison inferior based on several features of this office. Id. at 670–673.

First, a higher executive official could remove her, showing some inferiority in rank and authority despite her independent discretion. Second, her duties were restricted to investigation and prosecution; she lacked general government policymaking authority or administrative responsibilities beyond operating her office. Third, she could act only within jurisdiction defined through the statutory process. Fourth, although there was no fixed deadline, her office existed for a particular task and ended when it was completed, rather than carrying continuing responsibilities. The Court considered these factors together sufficient. Its comparisons included temporary vice-consuls, election supervisors, United States commissioners, and the Watergate Special Prosecutor. The counsel’s status as an officer rather than an employee was undisputed. Id. at 671–673 & n. 12.

## Interbranch appointments and Article III

The Court rejected a categorical rule limiting each branch to appointing its own inferior officers. The Clause’s text gave Congress discretion to choose among the listed appointing authorities, and Ex parte Siebold supported interbranch appointment. Constitutional history did not show a contrary restriction. That discretion was qualified: an appointment arrangement could impair a branch’s constitutional functions or be incongruous with courts’ ordinary work. Here courts had relevant experience with prosecutors, and the Special Division judges were barred from participating in matters concerning counsel they appointed. Judicial appointment therefore was not inherently incompatible with the courts’ role. Id. at 673–677 & nn. 13–14.

Appointment authority came from the Appointments Clause independently of Article III’s case-or-controversy limitation. In this temporary, fact-specific office, defining jurisdiction could be incidental to appointment. The Court nonetheless required the jurisdiction to be demonstrably related to the circumstances behind the Attorney General’s investigation and request. Expansion required the Attorney General’s request; referral of related matters did not authorize unilateral expansion. Id. at 677–680 & nn. 15–18.

The Division’s miscellaneous duties also did not give it supervisory power over prosecution. Receiving reports did not entail approving their contents. Fee awards, time extensions, and disclosure decisions resembled functions courts performed elsewhere. The termination power was more troubling because it required monitoring completion, but the Court construed it narrowly to avoid constitutional problems: it could end an office whose work was truly completed or substantially completed, not remove counsel during an ongoing investigation or proceeding. That latter power belonged to the Attorney General. Id. at 680–683 & nn. 19–21.

The statute also insulated the Division’s judges from adjudicating challenges to counsel’s conduct or removal. The Court warned against unauthorized expansion of the Division’s powers, identifying examples of orders concerning conflicts of interest or delaying investigations that the Act did not authorize. Upholding the statute did not approve such activities. Nor did the Court decide the merits of the Division’s interpretation of Morrison’s original grant or related-matter ruling; it found authority to consider the request and clarify the grant. Id. at 683–685 & n. 22.

## Removal restrictions and the whole statutory arrangement

The Court distinguished Bowsher and Myers: Congress had not retained direct removal authority or required congressional consent to an executive removal. The Attorney General alone exercised the relevant removal power, subject to judicial review. The Court instead applied the reasoning of Humphrey’s Executor and Wiener, while declining to make validity turn rigidly on whether an official’s duties were purely executive, quasi-legislative, or quasi-judicial. Functions mattered insofar as they showed whether restrictions impeded the President’s constitutional responsibilities. Some purely executive officials still had to be removable at will; the Court rejected a universal categorical rule. Id. at 685–691 & nn. 23–30.

Counsel’s functions were executive law enforcement. Yet her inferior status, limited mission, and absence of broad policymaking responsibilities meant that good-cause protection did not unduly restrict presidential authority. The Attorney General retained means to ensure competent performance consistent with the Act. The Court did not exhaustively define good cause, but legislative history included misconduct. Judicial review ensured compliance with the statutory removal restriction and did not itself assign the removal decision to the Judiciary. Id. at 691–693 & nn. 31–33.

Examining the Act as a whole, the majority found neither congressional aggrandizement nor judicial usurpation. Congress received reports, exercised oversight, and could request an appointment, but did not direct counsel. The Division could not initiate appointment on its own or supervise prosecutorial choices. The Attorney General controlled the initial statutory determination, supplied the facts defining the inquiry, could remove for good cause, and retained the benefit of Department policies. The Court acknowledged reduced executive control compared with ordinary prosecutors, but found the remaining control sufficient for the President to discharge constitutional duties. It reversed the appellate judgment. Id. at 693–697 & n. 34.

## Scalia: executive control and accountability

Scalia would uphold the D.C. Circuit’s invalidation. For him, Article II vested all executive power in the President. Criminal investigation and prosecution were quintessentially executive, and the statute deliberately displaced exclusive presidential control. Once those propositions were established, a judicial judgment about how much retained control was sufficient could not save the arrangement. The Constitution assigned powers rather than inviting a standardless balancing of their importance. Potential conflicts involving presidential associates had to be addressed through the structure’s political and institutional checks, even if those checks could not prevent every wrong. Id. at 697–711.

He also disputed the majority’s practical assessment. The threshold for mandatory referral was low, the preliminary investigation lacked ordinary tools, and congressional pressure could make referral politically unavoidable. Good-cause protection was designed to restrain control, not confer it. Following Department policies only when possible did not restore authority over the balancing of resources, legal priorities, foreign relations, or national-security interests inherent in prosecution. Investigations of particular executive officials could chill advisers and damage the Presidency during interbranch disputes. These were Scalia’s institutional arguments, not a finding that Olson was innocent or that Morrison acted improperly. Id. at 699–715 & n. 1.

## Scalia: inferior officers and qualified removal

Scalia read inferior as necessarily subordinate, though subordination alone would not always suffice. A narrow jurisdiction or a task-based office did not make an official inferior if she wielded full prosecutorial power within that sphere without a superior’s direction. Removal for cause did not supply the necessary supervision, and tenure could be lengthy. He distinguished precedents involving officers subject to actual institutional supervision or a revocable executive appointment arrangement. Morrison therefore required presidential appointment and Senate consent. Id. at 715–723 & nn. 2–3.

His fourth footnote is an important qualification often lost in shortened accounts. Scalia did not claim that every executive officer had to be personally removable at presidential pleasure. He accepted good-cause protection for inferior officers supervised by principal officers who themselves had the President’s confidence, at least where appointment came from the President or a principal executive official; cause would include refusing supervision. His objection was that this counsel was a principal officer outside that chain of control. He criticized the majority’s shift from functional categories to an inquiry into whether the President retained enough authority. Id. at 723–727 & n. 4.

Finally, Scalia linked the structure to individual fairness. An ordinary prosecution competed with other demands and could produce political accountability for the President. A prosecutor assigned specifically to one person, with no comparable competing portfolio, risked overinvestment, loss of perspective, and selective prosecution without an accountable political superior. He expressly declined to accuse the Special Division or Morrison of those abuses in this case and praised their integrity. The objection concerned what the institution permitted, rather than the character of these individuals. Id. at 727–734.
