# Shoemaker v. United States
147 U.S. 282 (1893), No. 1197 (January 16, 1893) | Legislation and the Regulatory State | Additional duties of existing officers and Rock Creek Park condemnation

## A park statute combined land acquisition with existing offices

Congress's September 27, 1890 statute authorized a public park along Rock Creek in the District of Columbia, dedicated to the enjoyment of the people of the United States. The tract could not exceed 2,000 acres. Congress appropriated $1,200,000 for acquisition and associated expenses, charging half against District revenues. A five-member commission selected and surveyed the property: three civilians appointed by the President with Senate consent, the Army Chief of Engineers, and the District's Engineer Commissioner. The two latter members already held presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed offices. A majority could act. 147 U.S. at 284–287, 300–301.

The commission first sought voluntary purchases at prices approved by the President. If agreement failed, the Supreme Court of the District of Columbia appointed three competent, disinterested appraisers. The President then decided whether the appraised prices were reasonable for the government's acquisition. Payment to owners, or deposit into court when owners declined payment, completed the statutory transfer. The statute also provided assessments against nearby properties specially benefited, with notice and judicial review. These acquisition, appraisal, and benefit-assessment functions were distinct. Id. at 284–287.

The initial map was recorded April 16, 1891. Only five of eighty-four selected tracts were acquired by agreement, and the commission petitioned the District court on May 20. Pierce Shoemaker died after the petition; his heirs and devisees were substituted, and his executors challenged the proceedings. They objected to the appointments, the President's role, the appropriation ceiling, condemnation for a park, the valuations, exclusion of alleged gold deposits, and later omission of some mapped tracts. Id. at 287–296.

## The owners preserved their objections through final condemnation

The court rejected the initial constitutional challenge. Attempts to obtain immediate review failed because that ruling was interlocutory. Appraisers were appointed, heard evidence, and returned their report in December 1891. The court overruled the owners' exceptions and confirmed the valuations. To stay within the appropriation, the park commission reduced the acquisition area. The President approved the appraised prices for that reduced selection in April 1892. Id. at 290–295.

The owners refused the appraised sums and demanded interest dating from the first map. In May the court ordered payment of the awards into its registry without that interest. On July 16, 1892 it entered a final decree vesting fee-simple title in the United States and directing delivery of possession. The owners then obtained a writ of error bringing the final judgment and earlier preserved objections before the Supreme Court. The challenged judgment therefore concerned the actual taking and possession of their properties, rather than simply approval of a proposed commission. Id. at 295–296.

Justice Shiras delivered the Court's opinion. The Court rejected the asserted constitutional and legal defects and affirmed the District court's judgment. The report contains one Supreme Court opinion and no separate concurrence, dissent, or individually tabulated vote. Its long reproduction of Justice Cox's lower-court mineral-title opinions is expressly adopted reasoning, not a separate Supreme Court opinion. Id. at 297, 307 & accompanying note, 322.

## Why Congress did not make a new appointment

The owners argued that Congress could create an office but could not appoint its occupant. Because three commissioners required presidential nomination and Senate confirmation, the other two commissioners likewise performed an officer's functions and supposedly needed fresh appointments. The Court accepted the significance of constitutional appointment rules but rejected the premise that a second appointment was required here. The two challenged members had already been appointed through the constitutional process. Congress had added duties germane to those existing offices. Id. at 300–301.

Congress may enlarge an existing office's powers and duties without requiring its incumbent to be nominated, confirmed, and appointed again. The line between military and civil duties might sometimes be difficult, but park duties assigned to these military officers were not dissimilar to or outside the sphere of their existing official responsibilities. The Court thus treated the statute as an enlargement of duties, not Congress's selection of new officers. That reasoning does not authorize Congress to place any preferred individual in any newly created office simply by assigning that individual an unrelated job. Id. at 301.

The District court had alternatively reasoned that the three civilian members could act as a majority even if the two other memberships were irregular. The Supreme Court's explanation instead upheld the added germane duties themselves. The distinction matters when identifying the actual appointments holding. Id. at 289, 300–301.

## Public use and the District's special constitutional position

A public park for recreation, health, or business served a public use. Courts could determine whether a proposed use qualified, but once that issue was resolved, the amount of land needed rested in legislative discretion, subject to just compensation. The Court did not determine the limits of federal eminent domain exercised within a State. Congress's power of exclusive legislation over the District supplied both national and municipal authority in this case. Id. at 297–300.

The Maryland cession proviso protecting individual property did not surrender eminent-domain power. It preserved private rights against the proposition that cession itself transferred everyone's land to the United States; it did not prohibit a later compensated public taking. The Court read the proviso in its historical setting alongside Maryland's grant of full right and exclusive jurisdiction. Id. at 298–300.

## Presidential approval did not determine an owner's entitlement

The President's appointment of commissioners was executive action. His approval of the negotiated or appraised price also was not a judicial determination of the owner's compensation. The price had already been established by agreement or appraisers; the President decided whether the United States would acquire the property on those terms. He could decline the purchase, but could not take the property while disregarding its appraised value. Id. at 301–302.

Likewise, the $1,200,000 appropriation limited aggregate government expenditure rather than prescribing an artificially low valuation for an individual tract. Appraisers still had to determine just value without regard to the cap. When the complete selection exceeded the available appropriation, the government could reduce the acquisition. The original map facilitated negotiations; it did not irreversibly commit the commission to every tract or invalidate the entire park if boundaries later changed. Id. at 302, 308–320.

The Court also recognized legislative power to charge proportionate park costs against specially benefited property. It declined extended treatment of that feature because there was no specific assignment of error, no special request below, and no plaintiff in error shown to own property actually assessed for those benefits. The case did not adjudicate every possible objection by a benefit-assessment payer. Id. at 302–303.

## Valuation, mineral ownership, and interest

Appraisers could use personal inspection and informed judgment as well as the parties' evidence. The approved oath required faithful, just, impartial valuation according to their best skill and judgment; it did not authorize them to disregard evidence. The Court approved instructions excluding speculative value produced by the park project itself and addressing present cash market value. It refused to transfer the appraisers' valuation function to the court merely because witnesses disagreed or owners considered the award too low. Review remained available for gross error showing prejudice, corruption, or plain mistake. Id. at 303–306.

The owners also sought value for supposed gold deposits in tract 39. The Supreme Court examined the record and adopted the lower court's detailed conclusion that any such deposits belonged to the United States. The adopted opinions traced express reservations of royal mines in proprietary patents, Maryland's Revolutionary confiscation, and the cession of Maryland's public property to the United States. This was a chain-of-title holding; the opinions expressly distinguished the ordinary American rule that ownership of land includes minerals unless separately reserved. Id. at 306–307 & note reproduced at 307–320.

An earlier 1760 patent without a mineral reservation did not displace the later 1772 resurvey grant with a reservation. Acceptance of a new, inconsistent entire grant surrendered the prior title as between grantor and grantee, even if old documents could remain relevant to priorities against third parties. A later deed conveying a confiscated cotenant's interest conveyed that interest rather than independently transferring reserved mines. Maryland could not convey its former public property after the 1791 cession. An 1800 resurvey warrant and 1803 patent supplied no effective alternative mineral title; continuation of private-right laws and preservation of judgments between private parties did not preserve Maryland's ability to dispose of ceded public land. Those grounds were considered in both the original ruling and the rejected motion to rescind based on additional evidence. Id. at 307–320 n.1.

Finally, the owners were not awarded interest from the map's filing to payment into court. The Court rejected the assumption that filing the map had already deprived them of possession. They continued receiving rents, issues, and profits while compensation was fixed, and the inconvenience of delay was presumed reflected in the award. No agreement called for interest; the government had not failed to pay an already due assessment, and deposit into court was equivalent to payment when owners declined it. This explanation concerns the timing and circumstances of this taking, not a universal rejection of interest as part of condemnation compensation. Id. at 320–321.
