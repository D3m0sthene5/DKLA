# Heifetz Metal Crafts, Inc. v. Peter Kiewit Sons’ Co.
264 F.2d 435 (8th Cir. 1959) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and judgment
Circuit Judge Johnsen wrote for a panel with Sanborn and Van Oosterhout on March 5, 1959. Heifetz sued to rescind and cancel a kitchen-equipment subcontract on a Veterans Hospital construction project in Topeka, Kansas. General contractor Kiewit counterclaimed for breach and joined Heifetz’s performance-bond surety. After a nonjury trial, the court denied rescission and awarded Kiewit $50,100. Heifetz and the surety appealed. The Eighth Circuit affirmed; rehearing was denied April 6. No separate opinion or judicial footnotes appear. (435–436, 439–440.)

## The bid, assurance, and later executed subcontract
Kiewit initially submitted a general-contract bid $52,000 higher than the $17,942,200 for which it ultimately obtained the hospital project. On bid-opening day, Heifetz, with which Kiewit had never previously dealt, telephoned and volunteered a $99,500 quotation for the kitchen-equipment work. That was $52,000 below Kiewit’s next lowest quotation. Kiewit specifically asked whether the figure conformed to the plans and specifications. Heifetz confirmed that it did, that it would remain firm, and that the same quotation was being given to all competing general contractors. Kiewit reduced its own bid by $52,000 and won by $9,000. Without that reduction it would not have been the lowest bidder. (436.)

After bidding closed, Kiewit obtained written confirmation but did not immediately tie Heifetz to a contract. Heifetz repeatedly asked whether it would receive the work. Several weeks later the parties executed a formal subcontract at the quoted amount. When Kiewit requested rough-in drawings, Heifetz asked for all plans and specifications. Kiewit then urged submission of the principal building’s drawings first and the other buildings’ drawings later. That request revealed Heifetz’s mistake: it had estimated only the principal kitchen in Building 20, although smaller installations were required in several other buildings. (436–437.)

## Origin of the mistake and trial findings
Heifetz’s president had obtained Building 20’s two blueprint sheets from another general contractor, together with section 502 of the specifications. That section addressed the kitchen work generally and revealed that multiple buildings were involved. Neither the president nor employees responsible for preparing and approving the quote checked it on that point. The president’s trial explanation that an employee simply selected the sheets differed from his deposition description of jointly determining which sheets were needed. The trial judge, having heard him, found that he participated in selecting the plans and that the mistaken judgment was his responsibility. The judge also found that disregarding the available specifications failed to exercise due diligence. Kiewit had neither caused that mistake nor supplied the incomplete plans. (437.)

The judge separately found that Kiewit neither knew nor was chargeable with knowledge of the error. Heifetz argued that a quotation approximately one-third below the next lowest should itself have warned Kiewit. Kiewit’s employees testified that they saw a substantial price difference but no reason to attribute it to a plans-and-specifications mistake, especially after the express assurance. Kiewit did not estimate work in this specialized field. There was testimony that contractors could quote unusually low amounts for reasons connected to obtaining a particular job. Significant spreads appeared elsewhere on this same project: electrical quotations ranged from $1.4 million to $1.85 million, lathing and plastering from $672,000 to $1.028 million, and mechanical work from $3.647 million to $6 million. (437–438.)

Finally, Kiewit had relied in good faith by lowering its own general bid. After Heifetz refused to perform, Kiewit had to pay $50,100 more to another subcontractor. The trial court treated that expenditure as a concrete reliance-related prejudice, rather than simply Kiewit’s desire to retain the favorable price. (438–439.)

## Review standard and governing rescission principles
The Eighth Circuit reviewed the nonjury findings for clear error. It found substantial support for all the relevant determinations, with added deference to credibility and good-faith evaluations that depended on the trial setting. Heifetz’s preferred inferences did not justify substituting new appellate findings. Kiewit’s actual reduction of its own bid and its failure immediately to lock Heifetz into a contract also supported the finding that it was not attempting to exploit a suspected mistake. (438.)

Heifetz urged Nebraska law as the law of contract formation; Kiewit urged Kansas law as the place of performance. The court did not need to select between them because the relevant rescission principles were the same. Equitable relief from a unilateral material mistake could be available where enforcement would be unconscionable, the mistaken party exercised ordinary diligence, and relief would not seriously prejudice the other party beyond loss of its bargain. Conversely, a failure to exercise reasonably expected diligence ordinarily would not justify rescission without some additional equitable consideration. (438–439.)

Heifetz failed both on its responsibility for the error and on Kiewit’s resulting prejudice. The available specifications could have revealed the omission. Independently, even if Heifetz had been faultless, its volunteered quotation and assurance had caused Kiewit to reduce its general bid. Rescission would shift the ensuing $50,100 burden onto a party found to have acted without knowledge or bad faith. The court explained that restoring the parties’ former positions was central to rescission; where that was impossible, only the strongest equities could justify relief. Heifetz’s equities did not preponderate. (439–440.)

## Limits and disposition
Heifetz also suggested that a $50,100 loss was insignificant beside an approximately $18 million project and could be absorbed from Kiewit’s profit. There was no evidence whether Kiewit made any profit at all, and relative financial capacity did not supply the missing equitable justification. Because Kiewit had passed the low quotation into its own reduced bid, it had not retained an unjust windfall from the error. The court affirmed the judgment. (440.)

The opinion expressly notes that the parties’ bidding representations might have had a different legal consequence if no subsequent contract had been executed. It does not decide a standalone promissory-estoppel claim compelling an otherwise unaccepted subcontract bid. Its holding concerns rescission of the actual executed subcontract and the breach counterclaim. Likewise, the price disparity was evidence assessed in context, not a rule that low bids never impose a duty of inquiry. (439.)
