# Psenicska v. Twentieth Century Fox Film Corp. — district court
2008 WL 4185752 (S.D.N.Y. Sept. 3, 2008) | Contracts

## Three lawsuits over participation in Borat
This decision resolved three related suits arising from the use of the plaintiffs' images in the 2006 film Borat. The plaintiffs alleged that the filmmakers obtained their participation by misleading them about the production and then exposed them to offensive situations. The defendants sought dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6), relying on signed consent agreements that released claims concerning the film and specifically disclaimed reliance on representations about its nature and participants. Judge Loretta A. Preska granted all three motions. Her ruling enforced those particular agreements; it did not find after a trial that the plaintiffs' accounts of deception were false. Slip pp. 1–3, 11–12, 19–20.

Michael Psenicska had operated a driving school for thirty-two years. According to his complaint, Todd Lewis Schulman contacted him in May 2005 about a documentary concerning the integration of foreign people into American life. Psenicska agreed to meet the crew in Washington, D.C., on June 13, but told Schulman that he needed to leave by 5 p.m. for a 6 p.m. class. The crew allegedly arrived ninety minutes late, brought $500 and papers described as necessary for the documentary, and left him rushed. He had not expected to sign a document and had not brought his reading glasses. He nevertheless signed the Standard Consent Agreement without reading it, asserting reliance on the earlier conversations. Slip pp. 4–5.

During filming, Sacha Baron Cohen, playing Borat, allegedly drove erratically and made derogatory remarks. Psenicska accused people he believed were producers of setting him up and later tried unsuccessfully to obtain an explanation from Schulman. He filed suit on December 3, 2007. The opinion treats these details as allegations, rather than adjudicated findings of misconduct. Id. at 5.

Cindy Streit owned an etiquette-training business, ETS. Schulman allegedly asked her to train a Belarus dignitary and arrange a dinner party for an educational documentary intended for Belarus television. Streit and Springland Films signed a services contract on October 24, 2005. The filming arrangements changed: Borat would skip the training but attend the dinner. Sarah Moseley, Ben McKinnon, Michael Jared and Lynn Jared were among the dinner guests. According to the plaintiffs' account, the crew arrived late, the consent forms were presented just before Borat's arrival, and they were rushed to review them. Each signed. Id. at 6–8.

At dinner, Borat allegedly made sexist remarks and introduced a woman as a prostitute. The film showed some guests leaving and portrayed Borat insinuating that they were racially intolerant. The Streit plaintiffs sued in Alabama, and their action was transferred to the Southern District of New York in February 2008. A judicial footnote also records a separate, later release: after filming but before this action, Streit, ETS and Springland signed a Release of Liability under which Streit received $4,450 in exchange for releasing claims of Streit or ETS against Springland and related entities. That separate release was not the common textual ground on which the court disposed of all three lawsuits. Id. at 6–8 & n.6.

Kathie Martin operated the Etiquette Training School of Birmingham. Her complaint alleged an arrangement to provide dining instruction to a foreign reporter being filmed for Belarus television. When Schulman learned that Martin's husband knew another Cohen character from Da Ali G Show, he allegedly postponed filming to reduce the chance of recognition. At the rescheduled meeting, Martin received $350 and signed what Schulman called a standard filming release. Cohen allegedly made sexist and anti-Semitic remarks and displayed nude photographs of his supposed son. The director apologized for the photographs; Martin's husband later identified Cohen, and Martin unsuccessfully tried to contact Schulman. She filed suit in February 2008. Id. at 9–10.

## What the consent agreements actually said
The agreements were materially identical, although Psenicska's named One America and the others named Springland. Martin's also expressly covered claims arising from viewing sexually oriented materials or activities. The forms consented to participation in a documentary-style motion picture intended for a young-adult audience using entertaining content and formats. Their waiver provisions identified categories of claims, including publicity or likeness claims, intrusion, prima facie tort and fraud involving deception or surprise about the film or consent agreement. Slip pp. 7–8 n.8, 10–11 & n.11.

Paragraph 5 went beyond a general assertion that the writing contained the entire agreement. It stated that the participant was not relying on promises or statements about the nature of the film or the identities of other participants or people involved in it. That subject-specific disclaimer connected the contract to the very representations on which the fraud claims rested. The distinction between a general merger clause and a targeted disclaimer is central to the court's reasoning. Id. at 11, 16–18.

## Review posture and the ambiguity objection
On a motion to dismiss, the court accepted the material factual allegations as true and drew reasonable inferences for the plaintiffs. It also could consider documents attached to or incorporated in the complaints, and documents known to the plaintiffs and relied upon in suing. Thus the agreements could be considered without converting the proceeding into a trial of the surrounding allegations. The complaints still had to state plausible claims. Id. at 11–12.

The plaintiffs first argued that documentary-style film was ambiguous or did not describe Borat. If the contracts applied only to another kind of production, their waivers would not necessarily reach this film. Under the undisputed New York choice-of-law provision, however, ambiguity was a threshold question of law. A term is ambiguous when, in the context of the integrated agreement and relevant usage, it reasonably carries more than one meaning. A party cannot create ambiguity simply by proposing a strained interpretation or disagreeing with the other side. Id. at 12–14 & n.12.

Psenicska himself supplied a definition accepted by the other parties: documentary-style describes a work displaying the characteristics of a film that provides a factual record or report. The court distinguished the meaning of the term from a debate over which films belonged to the resulting category. No competing reasonable definition was at issue. The operative qualification was style: a fictional character's story could be told using real interviews and events in the manner of a documentary. Id. at 14–16.

Borat fit that description because it depicted real people and events as part of the fictional protagonist's journey. Fictional characterization did not eliminate the documentary form; the contrast helped produce the film's effect. Humor likewise did not disqualify a film from that style. The court's footnote used examples of humor in documentaries to answer Martin's objection about satire. Consequently, the agreements were unambiguous and could be enforced at the pleading stage. Id. at 15–16 & n.13.

## Fraudulent inducement and the specificity of nonreliance
The plaintiffs alternatively contended that false statements about the film and the people making it induced their signatures. They argued that the merger clause was too general to bar those claims. The court applied New York authority requiring reasonable reliance as an element of fraudulent inducement, along with a knowing material misrepresentation or omission, intent to defraud and resulting damage. A sufficiently specific contractual disclaimer can defeat the asserted reliance on the subject it addresses. Id. at 16–18 & n.14.

Here, the alleged deception concerned exactly the nature of the film and identities of the people involved. Paragraph 5 expressly disclaimed reliance on statements about those subjects. The court therefore treated the disclaimer as specific enough under Danann, rather than as a boilerplate integration clause that merely excluded additional contractual terms. Signing the writing and later alleging reliance on the expressly disclaimed representations did not state a viable inducement theory under the law the court applied. Id. at 17–18.

That analysis has two distinct steps. The waiver released the substantive categories of claims arising from participation in the film. The nonreliance language defeated the attempted fraud-based escape from that waiver. The court did not reason simply that any signed release defeats all fraud, nor did it establish that filmmakers may make any representation without legal consequence. Its holding depended on these plaintiffs, the specified subjects of their alleged reliance and the language of their agreements.

## Superior knowledge did not change the result
The plaintiffs also invoked a duty to disclose when one party has superior information unavailable to the other and knows the other is acting under a mistaken understanding. They cited an exception concerning facts peculiarly within the knowledge of the party invoking a disclaimer. Judge Preska rejected their attempt to avoid these waivers by recasting the same representations as failures to disclose the true film or participants. On these pleadings, changing the label from misrepresentation to omission did not supply the reasonable reliance the express disclaimer negated. Id. at 18–19 & n.15.

The opinion did not announce that superior knowledge can never matter. Nor did it decide every additional defense the filmmakers raised: it expressly declined to reach their other arguments after resolving the contracts and inducement issues. The later appellate decision develops the peculiar-knowledge exception more explicitly; that separate decision is briefed separately so its reasoning is not retroactively attributed to the district court. Id. at 19.

## Exact judgment and sources
The court granted the motions to dismiss in Psenicska, Streit and Martin, directed the clerk to close each action and denied all pending motions as moot. This was dismissal of the plaintiffs' claims based on enforceable contractual waivers, not an award of damages to the defendants or a factual verdict exonerating them. Id. at 19–20.
