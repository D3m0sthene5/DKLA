# PSI Energy, Inc. v. Exxon Coal USA, Inc.

17 F.3d 969 (7th Cir. 1994) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B2-012

## Contract mechanism and the second appeal

This decision followed a 1993 appeal holding that PSI's long-term coal contract with Exxon remained in effect and remanding to determine its Base price for 1993–1997. The contract required price renegotiation every five years. If negotiations failed, Exxon's last offer prevailed unless PSI produced a qualifying competitive offer from another supplier that Exxon elected not to match. The earlier appeal had already held that PSI's Black Beauty offer did not qualify. The 1994 decision adjudicated whether Exxon's $30-per-ton offer was sufficiently complete and made in good faith; it did not newly decide every issue from the first appeal. 17 F.3d at 970, 974–975.

Section 7.03 required each side to negotiate in good faith. If they could not agree, PSI had to accept Exxon's last offer or present a firm, written competing offer for the remaining contractual supply that it was willing to accept, with documentary support and access for Exxon to the underlying information. Exxon could meet that offer. If it declined a qualifying offer, the contract would end at the prescribed point, subject to PSI's option for temporary continued supply. Section 7.05 confined renegotiation to Base and Exhibit A, excluding demands to change other contract terms. Exhibit A translated changes in twelve cost categories into Price adjustments. “Base” and the ultimately delivered “Price” were related but distinct defined concepts. Id. at 970–971.

After an April 1992 stalemate, PSI asked for a formal last offer. Exxon proposed a $30 Base, F.O.B. PSI's Gibson generating station, effective January 1, 1993. It promised updated Exhibit A figures and specified interim adjustments for union welfare, taxes and fees, and new laws or regulations. It would absorb other interim changes in wages, freight, and cost of living. PSI argued that the missing completed Exhibit A made the offer incomplete and that Exxon's bargaining was dishonest. On remand, the district court agreed and declared a Base of $23.266, the figure Exxon had offered to match Black Beauty if that offer qualified. Id. at 970–972.

## Appellate jurisdiction and standards

PSI had bought Black Beauty coal from January through July 1993, resuming Exxon deliveries only after the first appellate decision. The appellate court's July 12 supplemental order had suggested resolving damages for PSI's failure to accept deliveries. Those damages were not assessed in this suit, but Exxon had brought a separate action for them. All issues actually presented here had been resolved, so the judgment was final and appealable. This ruling did not quantify the separate damages claim. Id. at 970.

Easterbrook wrote for a panel of Bauer, Reavley of the Fifth Circuit sitting by designation, and Easterbrook. The panel unanimously dispensed with additional argument after hearing the earlier appeal. No separate writing appears. Rehearing and rehearing en banc were denied March 21; the report notes that Cummings and Coffey did not participate in that consideration. Contract interpretation was reviewed plenarily because the parties agreed the agreement was unambiguous, while the finding of bad faith received deferential clear-error review. Id. at 969–971, 973.

## Completeness of the last offer

The district court was factually correct that Base and Exhibit A were both needed to calculate a final delivered Price. It was legally mistaken to conclude that all future numerical adjustments had to accompany the offer. Section 7.03 contemplated agreement on a Base followed by appropriate updates to adjustment factors. Exxon's proposal provided the Base and formulas from which the later Price could be computed once the relevant economic changes were known. The parties had formed the original contract similarly, agreeing on Base and formulas before deliveries while leaving later numbers to be supplied. Id. at 971–972.

The result might have been more complicated if the parties were actually negotiating a change to Exhibit A's structure, because an offer referring to an updated schedule might then leave unresolved whether a proposed structural change was included. The court assumed PSI could propose such changes under § 7.05, but PSI had made no concrete proposal concerning the schedule's text or structure, and Exxon had made its opposition clear. Thus the fixed $30 Base was a valid last offer under the actual negotiations. Even the district court's substitution of $23.266 assumed that the remaining schedule adjustments could be worked out afterward. Id. at 972.

## Good faith and the evidence of alleged deception

The court applied the contract's good-faith obligation consistently with the UCC's then-applicable honesty-in-fact formulation. That duty did not require kindness, concessions, disclosure of reservation prices or negotiating strategies, or abandonment of commercial advantages the contract allocated. It still prohibited trickery and certain forms of obstinate misconduct; the court did not hold that express negotiating duties were meaningless. Id. at 972–973.

Evidence supported the district court's finding that Exxon expected a lower market price: high-sulfur coal prices were weak, and its own assessments suggested approximately $20.50 at the mine or $25 delivered. But the three-million-ton annual commitment could command a premium, and neither side disclosed its internal assessments. PSI's proposals were persistently below market while Exxon's were above it. Departure from a market figure did not by itself establish dishonesty. Id. at 973.

The actual finding of bad faith rested on Exxon's alleged lies about market prices. Its negotiating handout listed about forty existing long-term utility contracts, with prices from $22.97 to $50.48 and an average of $31.70, drawn from regulatory filings. PSI's negotiator immediately recognized many as old contracts and discounted their relevance. More importantly, the data were accurate; there was no finding of doctored figures or selective omission of lower prices. Treating those accurate, accurately described data as false was clear error. Treating their presentation as bad faith merely because Exxon expected to settle lower was a legal mistake equivalent to requiring revelation of its bargaining floor. The district court had also found neither actual deception of PSI nor a risk of it; an approximately $100 million price reduction was not supported by a breach causing no injury or potential injury. Id. at 973–974.

## Allocation of risk and exact result

PSI appealed to the unfairness of paying above-market prices. The contract did not provide automatic adjustment to an abstract market price; it gave the seller's last offer priority unless the buyer obtained the specified competitive offer. That allocation could compensate Exxon's costly development of a new mine and encourage PSI to seek a compliant alternative bid. Replacing it with a general fairness adjustment would rewrite the negotiated risk allocation. Id. at 974–975.

The court **reversed** and determined that the governing Base for 1993–1997 was **$30 per ton**, subject to the contractual adjustment system. Its mandate states reversal; it does not award damages in Exxon's separate delivery-refusal suit or adopt $30 as an unadjustable all-in delivered Price. Id. at 971–972, 975.
