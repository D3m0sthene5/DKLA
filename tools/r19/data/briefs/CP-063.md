# Garrity v. State Board of Administration

**99 Kan. 695, 162 P. 1167 (1917) · Civil Procedure · CP-063**

Garrity's effort to recover the value of a fossil illustrates why choosing a contract theory can solve one procedural problem without creating substantive liability or preserving claims against newly added defendants. The Kansas Supreme Court allowed the petition to be construed as seeking recovery on an implied promise, defeating the two-year tort limitation argument. Nevertheless, claims against the former regents added later were barred by the three-year contract limitation, and the successor board could not be sued for the former board's alleged wrong. The court affirmed the demurrer.

According to the petition, in July 1911 the University of Kansas board of regents, acting through its assistant curator of mammals, entered Garrity's Wallace County farm without his knowledge or permission. They removed a valuable fossil, placed it in the university museum for exhibition and scientific use, and paid nothing. Garrity alleged a value of $2,500. The district judge described the controversy as one over the value of an eighteen-million-year-old lizard. These details describe the pleaded claim, not facts established after a trial. The appeal followed a demurrer and Garrity's decision to stand on the petition. (99 Kan. at 695–96.)

The original petition, filed June 9, 1914, named only the state board of administration of educational institutions. That board had replaced the board of regents under a 1913 statute. Garrity alleged that the new board succeeded to the former board's rights, obligations, debts, and contracts. On November 2, 1914, he added the administration board's members, E. W. Hoch, Ed T. Hackney, and Mrs. Cora Lewis, and former regents' trustees Frank Strong and Rodney A. Elward. This chronology was critical: the original filing preceded the expiration of three years from the July 1911 taking, but the amendment naming the former regents followed that expiration. (Id. at 695–96.)

Defendants first argued that the petition, if it stated a cause of action at all, alleged a tort governed by a two-year statute. Garrity claimed he could waive the tort and recover the fossil's value upon an implied promise to pay. The court accepted that construction conditionally: if either petition stated an actionable claim against defendants, the alleged facts were sufficient to permit an implied-contract theory. The court relied on decisions directing consideration of the whole petition, especially its leading allegations, and favoring a contract construction where the averments left the choice doubtful. Consequently, the two-year tort statute did not bar the action. This ruling did not establish that any particular defendant actually owed the implied obligation. (Id. at 696–97, discussion 1.)

The second limitation argument independently defeated the newly added claim against the former regents. The applicable three-year period governed unwritten contracts. Garrity argued that the original implied-contract petition tolled that period and that the amendment related back to commencement. The court rejected that argument even assuming that the stated facts otherwise disclosed a claim against the former regents. A lawsuit naming one defendant did not suspend limitations against another person simply because its factual allegations could support liability against that person. Before the amendment, no action seeking liability against the former regents was pending; by the time they were made defendants, the statutory bar had attached. (Id. at 697, discussion 2.)

That discussion is important full-opinion material omitted from the assigned principal excerpt between its numbered discussions 1 and 3. The omitted material prevents reading the case as solely a dispute about labels or sovereign liability. Garrity overcame the shorter limitation by characterizing the alleged wrong as implied contract, yet did not thereby obtain relation back against new defendants. The opinion's rule concerns adding those parties under the Kansas law it applied, rather than a ruling under subsequently adopted Federal Rule 15.

The third issue concerned the original defendant, the administration board. The earlier board of regents had been a statutory corporation; the 1913 legislation abolished it and transferred management of educational institutions to a new state agency that did not receive corporate powers. The new board's authority included managing university property, disbursing appropriations, investing or spending designated funds, and executing trusts or obligations committed to the institutions. Garrity relied on succession and this obligation language to reach the successor board. (Id. at 697–98, discussion 3.)

The court refused to construe that administrative authority as making the board or its members liable for a tort committed by the former regents. Waiving the tort and pleading an implied promise could not create such liability. The university and its managing board were state agencies established for education and administration; absent express authority permitting suit, and with no corporate powers conferred on the new board, the action against it could not be maintained. Management of property and public funds was therefore different from legislative authorization to answer in court for the predecessor's alleged wrongful taking. (Id. at 698.)

The judgment sustaining the demurrer was affirmed. Justice Porter delivered the opinion; the assigned text expressly states that all Justices concurred. The full reported opinion contains no judicial footnotes or separate writing. Its two limitation discussions and statutory-agency analysis were all read, as was the entire assigned principal passage. The case does not find that the removal was lawful or that the fossil lacked value. It holds that these pleaded claims could not proceed against these defendants for the separate reasons given. The lesson is to examine classification of a claim, timing and identity of defendants, and statutory authority to sue separately: success on the first question does not establish the other two.
