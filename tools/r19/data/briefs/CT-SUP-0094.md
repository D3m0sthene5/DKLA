# Anderson v. Binance
2026 WL 538293 (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 26, 2026), No. 20-cv-02803 (ALC), ECF No. 164 | Contracts | Online amendments, notice, retroactivity, and an ambiguous class-action waiver

## Initial assent and later changes were different transactions

Five named plaintiffs alleged that Binance offered and sold digital tokens without complying with federal and state securities-registration requirements. Those allegations supplied the underlying class action; this opinion decided a motion concerning the forum and manner of litigating claims, rather than whether the tokens were securities or the defendants owed damages. The plaintiffs lived in California, Nevada, and Texas and created their Binance accounts between September 27, 2017, and April 15, 2018. Each agreed through the registration process to the July 6, 2017 Terms of Use. Their assent to that initial clickwrap contract was undisputed. (Slip pp. 2–4; assigned PDF pp. 94–95.)

The 2017 terms contained no arbitration clause, choice-of-law clause, or class-action waiver. They did permit Binance to announce amendments on its website without individual notice, stated that amendments became effective upon announcement, and treated continued use as acceptance unless users stopped using the service. On February 20, 2019, Binance posted revised terms introducing arbitration, Singapore law, and prominent references to a class-action waiver. The arbitration clause covered claims relating to the agreement, including its existence and validity. It incorporated Singapore International Arbitration Centre rules and specified a single arbitrator, English proceedings, and a Singapore seat. A heading and warning mentioned waiver of class proceedings, but no operative provision detailed such a waiver. (Slip pp. 4–5; assigned PDF pp. 95–96.)

Several plaintiffs traded after the revised terms were posted, and Anderson still held assets in his account in July 2024. Binance argued that continued use, together with the initial amendment clause, bound them to the 2019 contract. Plaintiffs contested adequate notice and the purported waiver, as well as retroactive application to earlier claims. The issue was assent to an amendment of an existing relationship, not initial acceptance of all terms displayed in a new-account clickwrap screen. (Slip pp. 3, 6, 10–11.)

## The procedural history narrowed the claims actually remaining

The original complaint was filed in April 2020. After a March 2022 dismissal, the Second Circuit reversed in March 2024, and plaintiffs filed a third amended complaint that the opinion dates May 10, 2024. Binance moved to compel arbitration in July 2024. The amended complaint proposed subclasses covering purchases before and after February 20, 2019. (Slip pp. 5–6 and n. 3.)

A March 28, 2025 district-court opinion denied arbitration for pre-amendment claims but left two issues unresolved: claims accruing after enactment and before actual notice, and the purported class-action waiver. At all parties' request, the court vacated that opinion on April 16 and ordered supplemental briefing. Before November 2025 argument, plaintiffs dismissed their claims on or after the effective date. A joint stipulation followed, which the court signed on November 25. The issue concerning that later group therefore became moot. The February 26, 2026 opinion decided the remaining pre-February 20, 2019 claims. This chronology is absent from the assigned edited excerpt but materially limits the judgment. (Slip pp. 6–8.)

## Formation law could not come from an unaccepted choice-of-law clause

The FAA favors enforcement of arbitration agreements, but the obligation remains contractual. Ordinary state formation principles determine whether the parties agreed to arbitrate. The court used a standard resembling summary judgment, considering admissible declarations and documents and drawing reasonable inferences for the nonmovants. Although parties may delegate arbitrability, an agreement and sufficiently clear delegation cannot simply be presumed. (Slip pp. 3–4 n. 2, 8–10.)

Binance argued that the 2019 Singapore-law clause governed whether plaintiffs accepted the 2019 terms. Judge Andrew Carter rejected that circular approach. Applying the provision to determine whether its adoption occurred would assume the formation fact in dispute. The uncontested 2017 contract had no such provision. The court instead used an interest analysis considering contracting, negotiation, performance, subject matter, and the parties' locations. The transaction was online and unnegotiated, Binance described itself as decentralized without headquarters, and the identifiable plaintiffs were domiciled in California, Nevada, and Texas. Their states' laws governed. The court treated them as substantively similar for this formation question, consistent with the parties' agreement that the outcome would be the same. (Slip pp. 11–13 and n. 5; assigned PDF pp. 97–98.)

## Constructive notice failed, while actual notice eventually arose

For passive assent through use of a website, the court examined whether a reasonably prudent user had actual or constructive notice of the disputed terms. Binance relied on Needleman, where an online-banking customer had agreed to receive account disclosures through a specified statement system. There, the bank sent individual communications announcing a new statement, the statement page prominently linked an arbitration notice, and the disclosure included an opt-out opportunity. A customer who failed to read it could nevertheless be reasonably on notice through the agreed channel. (Slip pp. 13–14 and n. 6.)

Binance had supplied no comparable contemporaneous individual communication. The record also lacked evidence that the arbitration provision was announced as the initial contract prescribed or that users were told where to look for amendments. The amendment clause did not obligate customers to inspect the website's contract periodically. Allowing it to impose unlimited unnoticed amendments would undermine mutual assent. The court therefore found no sufficient constructive notice from merely posting the new terms and users' continued activity. This conclusion concerned the record and notice mechanism presented, rather than a holding that every internet amendment requires individual notice regardless of circumstances. (Slip p. 15; assigned PDF p. 99.)

Actual notice was different. The original complaint itself identified the date Binance imposed arbitration. Williams, the only remaining plaintiff named in that complaint, had actual notice at least by April 3, 2020. The other four plaintiffs had actual notice by June 8, 2020, when they moved with Williams to become lead plaintiffs. Thus all five eventually had reasonable notice through the litigation. The court did not find perpetual ignorance of the clause. This later notice did not settle retroactive coverage of already accrued claims. (Slip pp. 16–17; the assigned excerpt summarizes this portion editorially on PDF p. 99.)

## The modification did not reach pre-existing claims

The court gave an alternative ground: even assuming constructive notice of the 2019 terms, the arbitration amendment did not apply to claims accruing before its effective date. California's implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing restricted a unilateral modification clause silent about known or accrued claims. The court distinguished provisions expressly allowing unilateral changes to affect accrued claims, which the cited Peleg decision had treated as illusory, from silent provisions that were instead limited by the covenant. It did not label Binance's whole agreement illusory. (Slip pp. 17–18; assigned PDF pp. 99–100.)

The phrase covering any claims did not supply temporal information. The clause could have specified a period reaching back before enactment but did not. Accordingly, it could not impose arbitration on known or accrued pre-February 20, 2019 claims. Later actual notice and continued use did not displace this restriction in the claims remaining before the court. The ruling was about a unilateral amendment of an earlier agreement; it was not a general declaration that a newly negotiated contract can never address existing disputes. (Slip pp. 17–18.)

## The class-action language did not waive proceedings in federal court

Binance separately sought to bar class relief in court even if arbitration could not be compelled. The 2019 heading referred to a class-action waiver, and the warning stated that the section involved a waiver of certain rights, including class proceedings for United States residents. But no operative clause explained what was surrendered or expressly barred class actions in federal court. Judge Carter found that wording ambiguous, particularly the reference to what the section involved. He distinguished language in Maldini expressly requiring disputes to proceed individually without class actions. (Slip pp. 18–20.)

These standardized, unnegotiated terms were a contract of adhesion. The court construed ambiguity against Binance as drafter, taking the narrower reading. It did not treat every adhesion contract as invalid. At best, the headings and the six ensuing subsections concerned disputes in arbitration. The warning's reference back to that section did not extend a waiver into federal litigation. Supreme Court cases about the absence of consent to class arbitration did not establish a bar to class proceedings in court. (Slip pp. 20–21.)

The court also rejected the proposed retroactive waiver. Decisions involving releases or indemnification in initially agreed contracts did not address a later unilateral amendment of the kind before it. The same retroactivity analysis governing the arbitration modification provided an additional reason to reject Binance's demand. The result concerned the remaining pre-amendment claims, not certification of a class or a finding that all requirements for class litigation were satisfied. (Slip pp. 21–22.)

## Exact order, ancillary sealing, and limits

The court denied the motion to compel arbitration of the remaining claims predating February 20, 2019 and held the purported class-action waiver unenforceable in federal court for those claims. Claims arising after the amendment's effective date had already been dismissed by stipulation, so the court did not decide their entitlement to arbitration on the merits in this order. Plaintiffs obtained a forum ruling, not a securities-law victory or damages award. (Slip pp. 7–8, 23.)

The court granted sealing of exhibits containing users' financial and identifying data and allowed redaction of Zhao's personal email address from a service certificate. It directed the clerk to file the proposed exhibits under seal, make that redaction, and terminate the specified motions. Carter issued the opinion alone, with no separate writing. (Slip pp. 22–23.)
