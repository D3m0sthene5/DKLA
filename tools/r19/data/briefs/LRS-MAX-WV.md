# West Virginia v. Environmental Protection Agency

**Course:** Legislation, Regulation & the State  
**Citation:** 597 U.S. 697 (2022)  
**Court and date:** Supreme Court of the United States, June 30, 2022  
**Opinion:** Chief Justice Roberts, joined by Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett. Gorsuch, joined by Alito, concurred. Kagan, joined by Breyer and Sotomayor, dissented. Judgment 6–3.  
**Assigned source:** LRS November pages 165–end scan, PDF pages 23–36, printed pages 967–980, principal text before Notes.  
**Brief ID:** LRS-MAX-WV

## Case orientation

Congress did not authorize EPA under Clean Air Act § 111(d) to set carbon-emissions caps using the Clean Power Plan's generation-shifting approach. Because that assertion of power presented a major question, EPA needed clear congressional authorization; the general authority to select the best system of emission reduction did not suffice. The Court reversed the D.C. Circuit and remanded. It did not categorically hold that every permissible system must operate within an individual plant, or forbid all emissions trading. (Roberts slip opinion at 16–20, 28–31.)

## Regulatory structure and facts

The Clean Air Act uses several programs to regulate stationary-source pollution. Section 111 addresses categories of sources. EPA identifies the best adequately demonstrated system of emission reduction, taking account of cost, nonair health and environmental effects, and energy requirements. A standard of performance reflects the emissions limitation achievable through that system. For existing sources under § 111(d), EPA establishes the required degree of reduction while states prepare and enforce implementing plans. That program fills gaps left by the Act's other pollution programs. (Roberts at 2–6.)

The 2015 Clean Power Plan addressed carbon dioxide from existing coal- and gas-fired power plants. Its first building block involved heat-rate improvements at coal plants. Its second shifted generation from coal to gas; its third shifted generation from coal and gas to renewables. Operators could reduce their own generation, invest in cleaner generation, or acquire allowances or credits through trading. EPA modeled a feasible national shift from coal's 38 percent share of generation in 2014 to 27 percent in 2030, then derived emissions limits that existing coal plants could not meet without generation shifting. (Roberts at 7–10.)

The majority cited substantial projected compliance costs, plant retirements, and employment effects. Those were projections: the Plan never took effect. Kagan also emphasized projected benefits and later market changes that met the Plan's national emissions target without its implementation. The brief should not convert either side's prospective modeling into proven consequences of an enforced rule. (Roberts at 10; Kagan at 23–24, note 6.)

## Proceedings

The Supreme Court stayed the Plan in 2016. After a change in administrations, EPA repealed it in 2019, interpreting § 111 to restrict the best system to measures applicable at individual facilities. EPA substituted the Affordable Clean Energy rule, based on equipment and operating improvements to plants' heat rates.

States and private parties challenged that repeal and replacement; other states and companies intervened to defend them. The D.C. Circuit concluded that EPA had misunderstood its statutory authority, vacated the repeal and replacement rule, and remanded. After another change in administrations, EPA obtained a partial stay of the mandate so the Clean Power Plan would not return to effect while it developed a new rule. The Supreme Court reviewed consolidated petitions by parties defending the repeal. (Roberts at 10–13.)

## Standing and mootness

The state petitioners had appellate standing because the judgment below purported to restore a rule requiring them to regulate power-plant emissions more stringently. Reversal would redress that injury. EPA's later intention not to enforce the Plan concerned mootness, not the initial existence of that appellate injury.

The government bore the burden of showing that its voluntary cessation made recurrence unreasonable to expect. It continued defending its authority to impose generation-shifting limits and did not disclaim their future use. Its plans for replacement rulemaking and the lower court's stayed mandate therefore did not moot the controversy. The Court did not decide that every petitioner independently established standing. Kagan questioned exercising discretionary review of the defunct rule, while acknowledging the majority might be correct under strict mootness doctrine. (Roberts at 14–16; Kagan at 4.)

## Major questions doctrine

The majority drew from Brown & Williamson, Utility Air, Gonzales, and other decisions involving consequential agency claims of authority. In extraordinary cases, the history and breadth of the asserted power and its economic and political significance give reason to hesitate before accepting a merely plausible statutory reading. Separation-of-powers considerations and legislative intent require the agency to identify clear congressional authorization for that power. The majority expressly treated this inquiry as distinct from ordinary statutory interpretation. (Roberts at 16–20.)

The doctrine does not mean every consequential regulation automatically fails, nor that agencies lack authority to address important subjects. The inquiry examines the particular power claimed in its statutory and institutional context.

## Why this assertion was extraordinary

EPA invoked a longstanding, rarely used gap-filling provision to restructure the electricity sector's generation mix. The majority saw that as a transformative expansion from prior § 111 standards designed to make regulated sources operate more cleanly. It distinguished prior use of trading to comply with caps based on source-level technology from using generation shifting itself to determine the caps. It also distinguished incidental market-share effects of pollution controls from setting limits deliberately premised on a desired national generation mix. (Roberts at 20–24, notes 1–3, and 27 note 4.)

The majority rejected the argument that cost, feasibility, and grid-reliability constraints sufficiently limited this power. On EPA's reading, the agency would still balance major national energy choices that Congress was unlikely to have delegated through this provision. EPA's traditional expertise did not necessarily include every transmission, distribution, storage, and national-energy-policy issue implicated by that claim. Congress's repeated failure to enact comparable carbon cap-and-trade measures reinforced the majority's skepticism. These were components of the Court's reasoning, disputed by the dissent; failed legislation was not itself a new enacted prohibition. (Roberts at 24–28.)

## Insufficient clear authorization and preserved limits

The term system can broadly describe generation shifting, but the majority found that definitional possibility insufficient for this extraordinary claim. Other statutory provisions expressly authorized trading or market-based compliance. The majority distinguished those schemes' independently established caps from EPA's claimed authority to derive the emissions limit from generation shifting itself. (Roberts at 28–30.)

Crucially, the Court declined to decide whether the statutory phrase excludes every measure beyond improvement of an individual source's pollution performance. It answered the narrower question whether the particular best system selected in the Clean Power Plan was authorized. The decision therefore should not be stated as categorical approval of EPA's earlier at-the-source-only interpretation or a wholesale ban on trading as a compliance mechanism. (Roberts at 30–31, note 5.)

## Separate writings

Gorsuch, joined by Alito, explained the doctrine as a clear-statement rule protecting Article I, democratic accountability, federalism, and fair notice. He described nonexclusive indicators of major questions: great political significance, regulation of a substantial part of the economy or large expenditures, and intrusion into traditional state domains. He then described clues bearing on sufficient authorization, including statutory context, age and focus, prior interpretations, and mismatch with an agency's assigned expertise. He acknowledged that older statutes can apply to new circumstances. His organized lists and broader constitutional account were a two-Justice concurrence, not separate holdings that every listed factor was required. (Gorsuch at 1–19 and all 8 notes.)

Kagan, joined by Breyer and Sotomayor, read the statutory grant as deliberately broad. System encompassed coordinated market mechanisms; cost, demonstrated effectiveness, and energy constraints supplied meaningful limits. Other provisions expressly imposed technological restrictions, while amendments had removed relevant technological language. In her view, the majority confused broad authorization with vague authorization. (Kagan at 5–13.)

Kagan understood earlier cases as ordinary contextual interpretation rejecting a mismatch between the agency's mission, asserted authority, and statutory structure. EPA regulating greenhouse gases from power plants through a familiar pollution-control tool did not present that mismatch. She disputed the distinction between generation shifting and conventional controls because either changes costs and therefore the grid's generation mix. She also disputed the claimed novelty and scale, the inference from unsuccessful later legislation, and the majority's constitutional account of delegation. Congress could reasonably give expert agencies broad authority to meet evolving problems. (Kagan at 13–33 and all 8 notes.)

## Judgment and exam use

The Court reversed the D.C. Circuit and remanded the consolidated cases. Its six-Justice majority held the Clean Power Plan's generation-shifting basis unauthorized under § 111(d). It did not decide the validity of a replacement rule not before it.

Identify the precise asserted power, why it is extraordinary, and where Congress clearly authorized it. Distinguish the system used to calculate a regulatory cap from flexibility allowed to comply with that cap. Keep the majority's reserved question about other systems separate from its rejection of this one.
