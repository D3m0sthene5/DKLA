# Douglass v. Pflueger Hawaii, Inc.
110 Hawaiʻi 520, 135 P.3d 129 (Haw. 2006) | Contracts | Minority, handbook arbitration, assent, and illusory promises

## Employment, injury allegations, and the signed acknowledgment

Pflueger hired Adrian Douglass as a lot technician on August 31, 2001. He was seventeen, a recent high-school graduate, and less than four months from his eighteenth birthday. At a September 13 orientation he received a sixty-page employee handbook and signed its acknowledgment page. The employer's arbitration provision appeared on page twenty, forty pages before that signature. It called for final, binding arbitration in Honolulu under the Federal Arbitration Act and American Arbitration Association procedures of employment and termination claims, presented within two years after actual or constructive awareness. It also said both parties would refrain from suing each other on covered claims. (110 Hawaiʻi at 522–524.)

The acknowledgment conveyed a different contractual message. It described the handbook as information that did not constitute an employment contract, characterized its guidelines as not conditions of employment, and reserved the company's power to change the handbook at any time without advance notice. Douglass acknowledged receiving, reading, and understanding the information and promised to comply with policies to the best of his ability. The signature page did not mention arbitration. A separate disclaimer likewise said the policies did not create a contract. These provisions and their placement mattered to whether receipt of information demonstrated assent to a binding agreement giving up a judicial forum. (Id. at 523–524, 532–534.)

On November 29, 2001, Douglass was injured in an incident involving an air hose. His later complaint alleged that a supervisor directed compressed air at his buttocks, causing penetration and inflation of his anus, rectum, and colon and requiring emergency treatment and overnight hospitalization. The appeal did not adjudicate those allegations as findings of sexual assault. After filing an administrative discrimination complaint in May 2002, he obtained a September 25 right-to-sue letter and filed in circuit court on December 17. He asserted hostile and unsafe working conditions, sexual assault and discrimination, and negligent training and supervision. (Id. at 524.)

The chronology qualifies what remained in the case when arbitration was compelled. After a deposition, the parties stipulated on September 11, 2003, to dismiss the negligent-training and negligent-supervision claims with prejudice. Pretrial statements followed. Pflueger moved to stay and compel arbitration on December 1. At the December 29 hearing, the judge reasoned that an employee accepting contractual benefits should not disavow another contractual provision. A written order issued December 30, and Douglass appealed January 27, 2004. The appeal concerned that stay and arbitration order, not a final decision on the remaining injury and discrimination claims. (Id. at 524.)

## The majority's rejection of the infancy defense

Chief Justice Moon first distinguished at-will employment from the absence of a contract. The parties had an oral employment agreement: an offer of work at a stated wage, acceptance, and consideration supplied through the employee's services and employer's payment obligation. The power to end that relationship did not erase the agreement. The threshold question was whether minority gave Douglass an absolute right to disaffirm that employment contract, including the alleged arbitration term. (Id. at 525.)

The majority recognized the common-law infancy doctrine and its protective rationale, including a minor's ordinary right to avoid obligations and the exception for necessaries. No party contended that this employment was a necessary. The majority instead interpreted Hawaii's child-labor scheme as incorporating the protections that supported infancy and making employment agreements binding. The 1969 legislation relaxed permit requirements for sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds: instead of an employment-specific certificate requiring parental involvement, they could obtain an age certificate valid for lawful employment. Younger minors remained subject to more protective requirements. The Department of Labor and Industrial Relations could refuse or revoke certificates when the work threatened a minor's welfare. (Id. at 525–529.)

From that structure the majority inferred legislative judgment that older minors could enter binding employment contracts. It also described parental approval of younger minors' employment as supporting binding agreements in that setting. Although the record did not show whether Douglass actually obtained an age certificate, the majority said the omission would not invalidate his employment: the scheme did not expressly make the contract invalid, and he was a seventeen-year-old graduate nearing adulthood doing work not shown to have been rejected as harmful by the department. Thus the majority rejected his minority-based disaffirmance argument. This was the four-justice court opinion's rationale; the separate concurrence sharply opposed it. (Id. at 528–530.)

## Formation remained necessary before arbitration could be compelled

Review was de novo under the same evidentiary standard governing summary judgment. A policy favoring arbitration did not eliminate the need for an agreement. Applying Brown v. KFC National Management Co., the majority required a writing, an unambiguous intent to submit to arbitration, and bilateral consideration. The historical Hawaii arbitration statute governing this agreement was former chapter 658; the opinion explained that chapter 658A's later effective date did not make it applicable to the September 2001 agreement. The scope of the provision, if validly agreed to, was not the disputed point. (Id. at 524–525, 530–532; majority nn.9–11.)

The first requirement was met. The arbitration provision was written and identified by a heading and table-of-contents reference. Its wording, considered alone, also used the language of reciprocal agreement. But wording on a page did not establish that the parties assented to it as a binding contract within this handbook's circumstances. The acknowledgment conveyed receipt and understanding of information, while disclaiming contractual effect and omitting any mention of the arbitration clause. (Id. at 531–533.)

The majority distinguished Brown, where the arbitration undertaking was set out as a discrete agreement, boxed separately, identified by its own subheading, and immediately above the applicant's signature. Here arbitration used the same font and ordinary heading style as other handbook policies, was not separately boxed, and appeared far from the signature. The human-resources administrator stated that she reviewed handbook provisions during orientation, but did not say she specifically explained arbitration. Douglass recalled being shown the handbook without remembering any discussion of surrendering a lawsuit. His statement that he was shown the entire book did not establish notice of the provision's binding effect. On this record the second requirement failed. (Id. at 533–534.)

The court's twelfth footnote limits that reasoning. It did not authorize someone who knows he is entering a contract to escape its terms merely by failing to read them. The familiar rule binding an assenting party despite failure to read remained intact. This case concerned whether the circumstances created the alleged contract at all. Receipt of a document expressly framed as informational did not supply the missing contractual assent. (Id. at 534 and majority n.12.)

## The employer's unrestricted amendment power also defeated consideration

The majority supplied an independent ground: even assuming assent, the agreement lacked binding reciprocal consideration. On its face, each side promised to surrender a judicial forum. But the company's reserved right to change the handbook at any time without notice extended to the arbitration policy. It could revoke the term while insisting the employee remain bound. That reservation made the supposed exchange illusory. (Id. at 534–535.)

Again the court distinguished Brown and a comparable federal decision enforcing a separate undertaking. A reservation covering general employment information need not invalidate arbitration when a severable, standalone agreement is contractually distinct from the policies subject to change. In this case no such distinct undertaking insulated the arbitration clause from the handbook's disclaimer and unilateral-change provisions. The decision consequently does not establish that any handbook amendment provision invariably invalidates every arbitration agreement. (Id. at 535–536.)

Because there was no valid arbitration agreement, the majority did not decide whether disputed facts independently required a trial on formation or whether Pflueger waived arbitration through its participation in litigation. Its final footnote discusses the statutory trial requirement when the existence of an agreement presents a genuine issue; it does not order such a trial here. (Id. at 536 and majority n.13.)

## Acoba's concurrence: minority and construction against the drafter

Justice Acoba agreed that Douglass could pursue his case in court but would first resolve the matter under infancy. Employment was not established as a necessary, and statutory permission to hire a minor did not itself abolish the right to disaffirm the terms of an employment contract. Unlike statutes expressly making certain medical or counseling agreements binding despite minority, chapter 390 contained no language removing disaffirmance. Acoba read the 1969 amendments as easing permit administration, rather than superseding common-law capacity protections. He also objected that the majority developed this statutory theory without the parties having argued it. (Id. at 536–537, 540–542.)

He rejected the employer's reliance on policies favoring arbitration and equal treatment of adult and minor employees. Requiring adults to sign the same agreement could not negate the minor's distinct capacity defense. Nor was Douglass demanding a benefit under the employment contract while repudiating its burdens: he sought statutory and tort remedies for alleged workplace injury. Acoba distinguished cruise-ticket decisions about where a minor could sue from an arbitration undertaking excluding the judicial forum. He relied on contrary authority involving minors and arbitration, including an unpublished federal decision; his extensive fifth footnote explained why decisions from other jurisdictions could be cited persuasively despite Hawaii's restrictions on citation of its own unpublished dispositions. (Id. at 537–540 and concurrence n.5.)

Acoba also emphasized civil-rights enforcement, the statutory right-to-sue letter, and the value of a jury forum in harassment litigation. He found no knowing waiver of those procedural protections and thought Brown should be reconsidered or confined when extended to this setting. These positions were his separate reasoning, not additional holdings adopted by all five justices. His footnote about the employer's failure to mention arbitration in its answer and pretrial statement was part of that discussion; the majority expressly left litigation waiver undecided. (Id. at 542–544 and concurrence nn.7–10.)

Alternatively, Acoba would read the handbook as a whole and resolve its conflicting messages against the employer. Mandatory-sounding arbitration language sat within a document repeatedly declaring its contents nonbinding and alterable. That ambiguity made arbitration directory rather than compulsory. He shared the majority's concern about illusory promises, but criticized its catalogue of placement, boxing, and typography factors as burdening employees with deciphering which portions of a supposedly noncontractual handbook might later bind them. He preferred the unifying principle of construing ambiguity against its drafter and would limit Brown to its facts. (Id. at 544–545.)

## Disposition and reading scope

The court vacated the December 30 stay and arbitration order and remanded for further proceedings. It awarded no damages and resolved no discrimination or assault merits. Moon wrote for Levinson, Nakayama, and Duffy. Acoba concurred only in the result, so all five supported the disposition while four adopted the court opinion. The opinion issued May 25, 2006. The May 30 correction order changes the final majority footnote's reference from 14 to 13; it does not change the substantive result. (Id. at 522, 536; correction order p.1.)
