# AM/PM Franchise Association v. Atlantic Richfield Company
526 Pa. 110, 584 A.2d 915 (1990) | Contracts

## Court and procedural result
The Pennsylvania Supreme Court, in Justice Cappy’s December 28, 1990 opinion, reversed dismissal of the franchisees’ warranty claims and remanded, while affirming dismissal of their tort and exemplary-damages claims. The case came on preliminary objections in the nature of a demurrer, following affirmance by the Superior Court. Accordingly, allegations and reasonable inferences were accepted for the limited question whether the law certainly foreclosed recovery. The decision allowed an attempt to prove loss, not an award or a finding that ARCO’s gasoline caused it. (110, 114–115, 121–122, 132.)

Flaherty, joined by Chief Justice Nix, concurred on primary and secondary lost profits and on rejecting tort and exemplary relief. They dissented from deciding the recoverability of goodwill because the majority itself determined these plaintiffs had not claimed that category. The panel also included Larsen, McDermott, Zappala, and Papadakos. The disagreement thus concerned the announced goodwill doctrine, rather than the warranty remand or dismissal of the other theories. (114, 132–133.)

## Franchise arrangements and alleged defective gasoline
The plaintiffs claimed to represent more than 150 ARCO franchisees operating AM/PM Mini Markets in Pennsylvania and New York. Their arrangements comprised a premises lease, gasoline agreement, and mini-market agreement; they were required to sell only ARCO petroleum products. From early 1982 through September 30, 1985, ARCO allegedly supplied an unleaded blend containing oxinol: 4.5 percent methanol and 4.5 percent gasoline-grade tertiary butyl alcohol. The franchisees alleged they were given no opportunity to buy regular unleaded gasoline from ARCO. (115–116.)

According to the complaint, customers experienced poor engine performance and fuel-system damage. Alleged accumulation of alcohol or water impaired operation, and swelling of plastic or rubber components damaged certain vehicles. ARCO’s representations allegedly warranted high quality, environmental advantages, and suitability for older and newer automobiles. The franchisees asserted nonmerchantability, unfitness, and reliance on ARCO’s skill, with an ensuing decline in sales and profits. A preceding history of increasing sales allegedly changed when the blend was introduced. These assertions supplied a pleaded causal theory, not adjudicated technical findings about the fuel. (116, 121–122.)

The lower courts treated lost profits as categorically unrecoverable goodwill loss and held that good-faith duties arose in contract rather than tort. The franchisees sought lost profits and incidental and consequential damages under warranty counts, along with other claims. Copies of the agreements had not been attached, so the Supreme Court could not decide such issues as whether they were adhesion contracts. Incidental damages had not been addressed by the parties or lower courts. (115–117 & nn.1–3.)

## The Code’s remedial categories
For accepted goods, section 2714 generally measures the difference between value as delivered and value as warranted, while permitting appropriate incidental and consequential damages under section 2715. Consequential loss includes needs or requirements the seller had reason to know at formation and loss that could not reasonably be prevented through cover or otherwise. The Court distinguished general damages from three forms of consequential lost profit: primary profits on the defective product, secondary profits on related products, and goodwill or prospective profits after acceptable supply resumed. (117–119.)

Its hypothetical tire transaction illustrated, rather than quantified, these categories. Worthless tires cause direct value loss; foregone resale margin is primary profit; foregone hubcap sales accompanying tire purchases are secondary profit; customers’ failure to return even after the product problem ends can affect goodwill. Lost profits are a type of consequential damage, not a separate remedial category added to consequential damages. (118–119, 122 n.8.)

Lampus had already replaced the restrictive tacit-agreement test with the Code’s reason-to-know approach. A seller need not consciously agree to assume special damages if the relevant needs and resulting loss are reasonably foreseeable. That change undermined earlier goodwill cases insofar as they relied on absence of actual contemplation. It did not eliminate the separate requirements of causation, preventability, and adequate damages proof. (120–121, 126 n.12, 131.)

## Primary profits despite sale of the gasoline actually purchased
ARCO argued that the franchisees had sold all the gasoline they bought and therefore had no relevant loss. The Court found that this missed the requirements-contract setting. The alleged defect reduced the amount customers would buy, which in turn reduced the quantity the franchisees acquired and could sell profitably. They did not have to buy their usual volume and leave it unsold before claiming lost profits; section 2715 expressly permits mitigation by cover or otherwise. The hypothetical monthly volume figures were illustrative only, not allegations made by these plaintiffs. (122–123 & nn.9–10.)

The contractual obligation to obtain all gasoline from ARCO also allegedly prevented purchase of a substitute from another supplier. Thus the pleaded loss was not avoidable through cover. The Code’s direction to administer remedies liberally to approximate performance supported allowing proof of lost gasoline profits during the defective-supply period. It did not establish that the plaintiffs had proven every element or the magnitude of the claimed decline. (123–125 & n.11.)

## Secondary profits and the limits of the announced rule
The plaintiffs also alleged reduced mini-market sales because customers bought sundries when stopping for gasoline. Gasoline was their primary product; the reduction in its sales could foreseeably produce a ripple effect on associated sales. The Court allowed a claim for secondary profits where the nonconforming item was a primary product and the buyer could not obtain substitutes through cover. A nonprimary defective product presented a more attenuated causal relation under the rule announced here. (125–126.)

Footnote 13 defines a primary product by substantial revenue dependence and severe impairment without it, leaving application to each case’s facts. Footnote 12 expressly warns that proving the fuel defect caused both gasoline and mini-market loss is arduous and reserves whether these plaintiffs can meet that burden. Permission to sue did not establish that all losses during the period were attributable to ARCO. (126 & nn.12–13.)

## Goodwill doctrine, actual claim, and separate writing
The majority defined goodwill in this requirements-contract context as lost sales after the seller stopped providing defective goods or the buyer obtained substitutes. It distinguished loss during defective supply from continuing effects on reputation and returning customers once acceptable goods were again available. The lower courts’ wholesale classification of the claimed profits as goodwill was therefore wrong: these plaintiffs claimed losses only during the oxinol period, not continuing goodwill losses after it ended. (127–128, 131–132.)

Nevertheless, the majority reconsidered the categorical prohibition in Michelin, Rubin, and Kassab and expressly overruled them to the extent they barred alleging goodwill damages as a matter of law. Modern market analysis, forecasting, and other proof methods, along with accepted recovery of prospective profits elsewhere in contract law, undermined automatic rejection as inherently speculative. Plaintiffs should have an opportunity to establish causal linkage and a reasonable basis for the factfinder’s calculation. Exact mathematical proof was unnecessary; unsupported conjecture was not thereby made sufficient. Footnotes 19 and 20 develop the comparison with other contract cases and permissible rational estimation. (128–131 & nn.16–20.)

Flaherty and Nix objected specifically to that step. Because no goodwill claim was before the Court, they considered the supposed holding dicta and its resolution jurisprudentially unsound. Their writing expresses an inclination to disagree and calls for awaiting a case actually raising the issue, while approving the rest of the majority’s rationale. That objection should remain visible alongside the majority’s express overruling, rather than being omitted from the statement of this stage. (132–133.)

## Remaining claims and source scope
The Court treated the relationship here as contractual and upheld the tort dismissals; it also found no authorized exemplary recovery under the case law or Code. Warranty claims alone returned for further proceedings. The opinion does not create a general tort action for every deficient commercial promise, establish fuel liability, or calculate a damages award. (132.)
