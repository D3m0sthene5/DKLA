# Peterson v. Meritain Health, Inc.
2022 WY 54, 508 P.3d 696 | Contracts

## Holding and judgment
On April 20, 2022, the Wyoming Supreme Court reversed summary judgment for a health-plan administrator on contract, third-party-beneficiary, and bad-faith theories because material factual questions remained about its actual role and undertakings. It affirmed the challenged discovery rulings and remanded. It did not hold that Peterson had already proved beneficiary status, contractual breach, bad faith, or an entitlement to damages. Gray wrote for the court; the listed panel consisted of Fox, Davis, Kautz, Gray, and district judge Rumpke, with Davis's reassignment and Rumpke's resignation noted on the cover. No separate writing appears. (Cover; ¶¶ 1–2, 97.)

## Plan, administrative agreement, and denial of benefits
Memorial Hospital of Converse County offered employees a self-funded health plan. Meritain drafted the plan and the separate Administrative Services Agreement, or ASA. The plan identified the hospital as sponsor, administrator, and fiduciary with discretionary authority over interpretation, eligibility, and disputes. Meritain was described as a third-party administrator providing processing and ministerial services. The ASA denied Meritain discretionary authority to interpret the plan or adjudicate claims and required referral of questionable claims and appeals to the hospital for exclusive final resolution. (¶¶ 4–6.)

David Peterson began hospital employment in February 2013 and entered the plan that August. He had received cardiac treatment in 2012 and later was diagnosed with congestive heart failure and cardiomyopathy, receiving a defibrillator in November 2013. His medical bills totaled $247,934.74. Meritain paid some bills but denied $207,423.67 as relating to a preexisting condition, then denied two levels of appeal without hospital involvement. A collection action later sought $188,328.85 for the defibrillator surgery, a distinct amount from the total denied coverage. (¶¶ 7–8; n. 4.)

Peterson sued in March 2017. The hospital was dismissed for a reason the record did not disclose. After Peterson died in March 2018, his wife, Janet, continued as estate representative. The September 2020 third amended complaint asserted breach of the plan, breach of the ASA as a beneficiary, and breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing, with punitive damages and attorney fees requested. (¶¶ 1, 9 & nn. 1–2.)

In opposition to summary judgment, an expert described Meritain's discretionary control over claims and appeals and criticized its investigation. He opined that it had assumed treatment of a preexisting condition from medication use without obtaining adequate records, failed to investigate creditable prior coverage, inconsistently paid related claims, delayed its decisions, and did not secure hospital decisions on appeals. These were evidentiary submissions supporting disputed issues, not appellate findings establishing that all bills were covered. The district court nevertheless held lack of privity and beneficiary status dispositive of the contract and bad-faith claims. (¶¶ 10–13 & n. 5.)

## Review, choice of law, and the ERISA argument
Summary judgment was reviewed de novo with favorable reasonable inferences for the opponent; admissible evidence remained necessary to create a material dispute. Discovery rulings were reviewed for abuse of discretion, with legal interpretation of the sanctions rule reviewed de novo. Meritain first invoked the ASA's New York choice-of-law provision on appeal. The court held that argument waived because choice of law was neither jurisdictional nor fundamental under its preservation doctrine, and applied Wyoming law. (¶¶ 14–20.)

Meritain ordinarily could not be sued for breach of a plan contract between the hospital and its employee merely because it administered the plan. ERISA did not itself supply a different result: the undisputed governmental plan was exempt and could not opt into the statute. Lawful ERISA terms could be adopted contractually, but references to claims procedures, definitions, and discretion did not incorporate the statutory fiduciary cause of action against third-party administrators. The court rejected that route to liability while continuing to examine agency and separate contractual undertakings. (¶¶ 21–30 & n. 6.)

## Agency could create a distinct factual route to contractual liability
An agent acting with authority for a disclosed principal ordinarily is not a party to the principal's contract and is not liable for its breach. Peterson therefore could not prevail against Meritain merely by showing an agency relationship. But an agent acting beyond its authority could incur contractual liability under the principles applied by the court. The opinion acknowledged that this principle more commonly addresses unauthorized contract formation than later breach and considered an analogous insurance-adjustment decision. (¶¶ 31–44 & nn. 7–9.)

The record raised both agency and authority questions. Meritain had taken over processing, investigation, decisions, payments, and two levels of appeals, while the hospital apparently neither participated nor received the decisions. That conduct potentially exceeded the ministerial role described in the plan and ASA. Whether Meritain acted as agent, and whether it exceeded its authority, had to be resolved before categorical rejection of the plan-breach claim. Summary judgment was therefore unwarranted; liability remained conditional on those factual determinations. (¶¶ 34–35, 45–47.)

## Third-party-beneficiary intent despite an express exclusion
The ASA separately stated that it conferred no rights or remedies on third parties, explicitly including plan participants. The court treated that unambiguous exclusion as evidence of contracting intent supporting Meritain's initial summary-judgment position. It did not deny the clause's existence, declare it linguistically ambiguous, or finally invalidate it. (¶¶ 48–53.)

Peterson identified more specific operational promises concerning participants: administration, processing and payment of claims; eligibility and coverage determinations; resolution of disputes; explanations of denials; and referral of appeals to the hospital. Those provisions and the companion plan's purpose of paying employee health expenses supplied evidence of intended benefit. Under Wyoming precedents, beneficiary intent required examination of the agreement as a whole and the circumstances of execution, not isolated reliance on the exclusion. Even recognizing that the exclusion had been drafted unambiguously to avoid beneficiaries, the court found a factual question about intended benefit from the whole arrangement. (¶¶ 49–57.)

The actual scope of the ASA also remained disputed. Meritain invoked a signed-writing modification requirement; Peterson relied on the hospital's possible agreement to Meritain's assumption of claims and appellate decisions. Wyoming law permitted waiver or modification of written terms orally or through conduct, notwithstanding such language, but required clear and convincing proof of the asserted modification. The factfinder would need to determine beneficiary status, modification, and breach; the court did not itself establish any of them. (¶¶ 58–61.)

## Bad faith depended on function, not contractual label alone
The court considered the special insurer-insured relationship, unequal bargaining power, and the incentive for faithful investigation and payment. Earlier Wyoming decisions left the precise administrator framework unresolved. Other jurisdictions differed: some required an insurer-like role plus financial risk, while others emphasized control of claims regardless of direct underwriting risk. After reviewing these authorities, the court adopted the principle that an administrator acting sufficiently like an insurer owes the insured a duty of good faith. Direct contractual privity was not an absolute prerequisite in that circumstance. (¶¶ 62–77 & nn. 10–14.)

Meritain's ministerial label did not conclusively establish what it actually did. Evidence of exclusive claim and appeal decisions, provider lists, lack of hospital participation, and finality of Meritain's determination supported a factual inquiry. The ASA also provided contingency fees for savings generated by negotiating bills or reducing amounts. Meritain disputed the connection of those fees to claim determinations, but the court also recognized market incentives to minimize payments. These considerations precluded summary judgment on whether it acted sufficiently like an insurer; they did not establish bad faith as a matter of law. (¶¶ 78–82 & n. 15.)

## Remedies and discovery were separately resolved
Punitive damages and attorney fees had fallen with the underlying claims below. Their availability was remanded with those claims for determination under the resulting evidence and governing requirements, rather than awarded on appeal. (¶ 83.)

The court affirmed refusal to impose the additional requested discovery sanctions. Earlier orders had already imposed attorney-fee sanctions; the later request concerned delayed identification of analysts and production of a further claims manual. The parties disputed prejudice, culpability, and explanations, and the court found the refusal of further sanctions within the trial court's broad discretion. The lengthy reproduction of Rule 37 in note 16 was part of this analysis. (¶¶ 84–90 & n. 16.)

It also affirmed refusal of wholesale personnel-file production. Relevance remained constrained by proportionality and sensitive information, and the requested training, qualifications, or incentives could be explored through depositions or interrogatories. Peterson had not shown that the information was exclusively obtainable from the files. On remand, the district court could revisit particular documents connected to the bad-faith theory and use protective orders or in-camera review. This preserved discretion rather than establishing a permanent immunity from personnel-file discovery. (¶¶ 91–96.)

## Assigned account and reading scope
The short assigned example at printed 1188 correctly reports that beneficiary-related provisions prevented summary judgment notwithstanding a general exclusion. The full opinion's rationale considers the whole ASA and execution circumstances and leaves intent and possible modification for factual resolution. It does not announce a final holding that the specific provisions invariably override the exclusion or that Peterson conclusively was a beneficiary.
