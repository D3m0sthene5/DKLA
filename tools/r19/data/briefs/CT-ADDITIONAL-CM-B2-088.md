# Wilcox v. Wilcox
406 S.W.2d 152 (Ky. 1966) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Kentucky's Court of Appeals decided the case on March 25, 1966; the published report records denial of rehearing on October 7, 1966. Judge Hill wrote the opinion, which records no dissent or separate writing and supplies no numerical voting lineup. The court reversed the judgment relieving the father of his contractual support obligation at age eighteen and returned the case for proceedings consistent with the opinion. The contract's reference to the age of majority meant twenty-one, the legal age when the parties made their agreement, subject to its alternative endpoint when the child became self-supporting. (152–153.)

## Agreement and intervening statute
Alberta and Garland Wilcox's divorce action began on November 14, 1951, and judgment was entered on November 24. The mother received custody and control of their daughter, Brenda Sue. A settlement contract provided that the father would make child-maintenance payments until the daughter reached majority or became self-supporting. The court approved that agreement and incorporated it into the divorce judgment. The relevant obligation therefore came from an agreed settlement embodied in a judgment, rather than an appellate ruling establishing a universal statutory duty to support every adult college student. The report treated the facts as undisputed. (153.)

At contracting, Kentucky's age of majority was twenty-one. Effective January 1, 1965, the legislature lowered it to eighteen. KRS 2.015 declared eighteen the age of majority generally, while retaining twenty-one for alcoholic-beverage purchases and the care and treatment of handicapped children. When this dispute arose, Brenda was eighteen, attended college full time, and was not self-supporting. Garland moved to be relieved of further payments. (153.)

The trial court identified the previous support obligation as $40 monthly and the amount in controversy as $1,440, representing thirty-six months. It concluded that the change in legal majority ended the father's duty at eighteen, except that its policy would continue support for a child still in high school through graduation. Brenda's college attendance did not fit that policy. The appellate court rejected the conclusion that the legislative reduction ended this agreed obligation. The $1,440 figure described the trial court's amount-in-controversy finding, not a separate quantified appellate damages award. (153.)

## Reasoning: determine the reference at the time of agreement
The court explained that contractual intention governs and must be determined by asking how the parties meant the agreement to operate when they entered it. It also invoked the familiar principle that constitutional and statutory provisions in effect when a contract is made become part of that contract. When these parties used majority in 1951, its legal referent was twenty-one. The later statutory revision did not substitute eighteen as the endpoint of their existing promise. The father consequently remained obligated until the daughter turned twenty-one or became self-supporting. (153.)

The alternative self-support condition mattered: the court did not hold that age twenty-one was the only possible termination event. Nor did it deny that eighteen had become the statutory age of majority for general purposes. Its ruling identified the duration of a contractual promise made under the earlier law. The trial court's high-school extension policy did not replace that promise's original endpoint. The judgment was reversed for further consistent proceedings, rather than affirming relief at eighteen or independently ordering an expressly itemized arrears award. (153.)

## Assigned account and limits
C&M physical page 274, printed page 581, uses the decision to ask how incorporated or legally defined terms respond to later changes. Its short account accurately describes the twenty-one-year endpoint despite the subsequent statutory reduction, but omits the alternative self-support condition and the contract's incorporation into the divorce judgment. Those details limit the holding: this opinion resolves the parties' historical settlement and does not establish a rule that every reference to an external legal instrument freezes every provision forever.
