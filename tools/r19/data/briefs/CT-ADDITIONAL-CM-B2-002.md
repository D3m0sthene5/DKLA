# Precision Testing Laboratories, Ltd. v. Kenyon Corporation of America

644 F. Supp. 1327 (S.D.N.Y. 1986) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B2-002

## The automobile-certification project

This diversity action arose from efforts to develop emissions systems for imported automobiles sold through the gray market. Asher Hiesiger, a lawyer and real-estate investor, owned Precision Testing Laboratories (PTL). Arthur Ellis operated Texas Coach and related businesses in Houston and had successfully developed emissions systems. Lawrence Kenyon wholly owned Kenyon Corporation of America (KCA). KCA held small-volume-manufacturer status, but initially lacked a successful certification vehicle. That status, coupled with a conforming prototype, would allow application of the approved system to similar vehicles without testing each separately. The parties thus possessed potentially complementary financial, technical, and regulatory resources. 644 F. Supp. at 1328–1333.

Their dealings did not begin as a single clearly defined venture. During 1983, Hiesiger funded an unsuccessful Porsche project with Kenyon, later obtained testing time for a Mercedes, and allowed Kenyon to occupy a Manhattan apartment rent free. Independently, he dealt with Ellis, paying for research and a Porsche conversion. Engineer Claus Liphardt had developed the components that became the foundation of the Mercedes system; the court did not credit a claim that Kenyon originated that engineering. A December meeting led chiefly to arrangements for Ellis and Kenyon to exchange information in Houston, rather than a concluded agreement among all three. Id. at 1330–1333.

In January 1984, Ellis and Kenyon tested components and discussed working together, including a possible half-interest in KCA for Ellis. Ellis continued researching and adjusting the system. He selected a different catalytic converter, developed a configuration without an air pump, investigated the fuel block, and reengineered the fuel-pressure regulator. Meanwhile, Hiesiger advanced $100,000 to Ellis without informing Kenyon. A separate January letter acknowledged outstanding funds and pledged assets including Ellis' anticipated interest in KCA; Kenyon knew nothing of that pledge. These transactions showed why financial transfers could not automatically be treated as performance of a joint venture with Kenyon. Id. at 1333–1334.

## The February discussions and unresolved terms

Plaintiffs claimed that their discussions culminated in a binding joint venture on February 17, 1984. The court examined testimony and Kenyon's contemporaneous notes rather than accepting either side's characterization wholesale. The evidence supported discussion of a 50–25–25 stock split, with each plaintiff paying $5,000 for a quarter-interest. Numerous other proposals concerned inventory purchases, component supply, a parts-finder fee, vehicles owned by Kenyon's mother, housing, and a separate Gelaendewagen business. Some proposals were accepted; others, particularly assumption of corporate liabilities, remained disputed. Id. at 1335–1337.

At the meeting's close, the parties telephoned Kenyon's attorney Kenneth Bloom to arrange formal documents. Kenyon and Bloom said any arrangement required a writing and counsel's approval. Hiesiger characterized the documents as a memorialization, yet himself identified an unresolved control problem: a 50–25–25 division could produce deadlock. He requested that counsel develop a solution. The court found that the discussions supplied a framework for continued dealing rather than a final binding agreement. Footnote 3 also resolves a testimonial disagreement: Bloom spoke with Kenyon and Hiesiger on that occasion, not Ellis. Id. at 1337–1338 & n. 3.

Work nevertheless continued. Ellis' technicians spent 93 hours preparing the coast-down test, and Ellis spent five weeks at the Ohio Automotive Testing Laboratory, sometimes working eighteen-hour days. The court found that he attended more frequently than Kenyon, performed most physical work, and supplied the intellectual contribution responsible for success. KCA paid the laboratory's bills. The vehicle achieved certification levels on April 13; additional documentation work followed. These were trial findings, supported partly by independent laboratory testimony, not merely plaintiffs' allegations. Id. at 1338–1339.

Formal negotiations continued in May. Counsel proposed a 51–24½–24½ division, $175,000 financing, directors allowing Kenyon control, and compensation for his daily management. Plaintiffs objected, and ownership, management, salary, financing, and transfer restrictions remained unresolved. A $10,000 check delivered in June was never negotiated; Hiesiger stopped payment, and counsel returned it denying a meeting of minds. The lawsuit followed in July. KCA received conformity certificates in August 1984 and January 1985. A competing company owned by Hiesiger's wife separately obtained certification with Ellis' assistance. Id. at 1339–1342.

## Trial posture and the absence of a binding bargain

Judge Irving Ben Cooper tried liability from February 10 through February 14, reserving damages for a later phase. The August 26, 1986 opinion thus followed a bench trial and made credibility and factual findings. It credited Ellis most strongly, while finding significant portions of Hiesiger's and, to a lesser extent, Kenyon's testimony unconvincing. It also rejected defendants' unsupported request to dismiss Kenyon individually, because the record supported causes of action against both defendants. Id. at 1328, 1331, 1342, 1351.

Under New York law, parties may bind themselves orally, but an expressed intention to await execution of a formal instrument must be respected. The court balanced four factors: reservation of the right not to be bound, agreement on all terms, complexity and magnitude of the transaction, and partial performance. No one factor was decisive. This was an intent inquiry, not a universal requirement that a joint venture always be written. Id. at 1342–1343.

First, counsel's participation and the parties' own conduct supported the finding that they reserved final commitment. Drafting was meant to resolve ambiguities and outstanding problems, not merely transcribe an already complete bargain. Even Ellis later said there had never been a three-party deal and explored continuing with Kenyon alone. Second, the stock split, deadlock solution, sharing of risks and liabilities, and other material terms remained unsettled. Id. at 1343–1345.

Third, the contemplated venture was substantial and complicated: at least fifteen terms had been discussed, six documents totaling over twenty-five pages were being prepared, and the project combined stock acquisition, financing, supplies, technical services, and an ongoing import-and-certification business. Such matters ordinarily called for definitive writings. Fourth, some acts constituted performance of discussed proposals, notably Ellis' technical work and inventory sales, but much promised performance never occurred. The unnegotiated stock-purchase check did not complete the stock sale; asserted financing and administrative assistance lacked convincing proof. Earlier laboratory access and apartment use preceded the alleged venture. After balancing actual partial performance with the other evidence, the court found no intent to be bound before execution. Plaintiffs could not recover for breach of the asserted joint venture. Id. at 1345–1348 & n. 4.

## The independent joint-venture deficiency

The court provided a second reason for rejecting the contract claim. Even assuming an intention to be bound orally, New York required agreement to create a joint venture, sharing of profits and losses, joint business control, and contributions of property, resources, skill, or knowledge. Absence of one essential element would defeat the claimed venture. Id. at 1348.

The parties did intend to create an association of the requisite character once formalized, and their proposed financial, technical, property, and sales contributions satisfied the contribution element. But no definite arrangement governed sharing profits and losses. The unresolved stock division and risk questions were material, and the parties had not otherwise settled allocation. Nor had they resolved who would direct and manage KCA. The contemplated share ownership did not itself establish agreed joint control. Two necessary elements therefore were missing. The court's conclusion was not that collaboration or contributions never occurred, but that those acts did not create the asserted fiduciary venture. Id. at 1349–1350.

## Restitution for Ellis, but not for every participant

Failure to form a venture did not end the inquiry. Unjust enrichment required enrichment of defendants that was unjust at the claimant's expense. The court found both elements with respect to Ellis' contribution to KCA's certificates. His staff's work and his own prolonged engineering effort were essential to the successful certification vehicle. Although he learned a system usable elsewhere, that benefit to him did not erase the substantial labor devoted to making KCA's vehicle succeed. His services were designed to benefit KCA, distinguishing Songbird's ordinary negotiation and self-protective resale activities. Defendants obtained valuable certification through work for which the principal contributor had received no monetary compensation. Id. at 1350–1351.

The court reached a different conclusion for Hiesiger. The $100,000 advanced privately to Ellis was not shown to have funded KCA's success rather than the two plaintiffs' independent projects; even Hiesiger denied that it was an obligation of the supposed venture. PTL's $15,055.41 payment purchased parts for Texas Coach, whose true beneficiary was Ellis. Apartment use benefited Kenyon, but the court found no corresponding expense to Hiesiger because existing arrangements prevented him from accepting rent. Thus the benefit and expense had to be proved for the particular claimant, not inferred from that claimant's participation in negotiations or financial dealings. Id. at 1351.

## Exact order and assigned reading

The court **dismissed the complaint in its entirety except for Ellis' right to recover from both defendants the portion of money earned by his services certifying KCA's test car**. It directed the parties to attempt agreement on a reasonable damages amount and submit a proposed judgment. If they could not agree within sixty days after filing the opinion, they were to notify the court, which would set a damages trial limited to Ellis and those services. No dollar amount was awarded in this liability-stage opinion, and the court did not grant plaintiffs the promised corporate ownership or joint-venture profits. Id. at 1351.
