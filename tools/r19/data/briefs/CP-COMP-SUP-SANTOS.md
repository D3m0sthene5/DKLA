# Santos-Zacaria v. Garland

**598 U.S. 411 (2023) • Supreme Court of the United States • May 11, 2023**

**Assigned source:** Civil Procedure Supplement, printed p. 507 (PDF 342). Justice Jackson wrote for seven Justices: Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett joined. Justice Alito concurred in the judgment, joined by Justice Thomas. All nine agreed with the result; seven joined the jurisdictional holding.

## Facts and procedural history

Leon Santos-Zacaria, who uses the name Estrella, fled Guatemala as a teenager. She testified that she had suffered physical harm and death threats as a transgender woman attracted to men. Immigration authorities removed her from the United States in 2008; she returned and was apprehended in 2018. She sought protection from removal, including withholding based on likely persecution. An immigration judge reinstated the prior removal order and denied the requested protection. 414.

The Board of Immigration Appeals upheld the denial of withholding. It agreed that Santos-Zacaria had suffered past persecution, entitling her to a presumption of future persecution, but found the presumption rebutted on an issue the immigration judge had not reached. She petitioned for Fifth Circuit review, arguing among other things that the Board had impermissibly performed factfinding reserved to the immigration judge. 414–415.

The Fifth Circuit, 2–1, dismissed that challenge for lack of jurisdiction under § 1252(d)(1). The Government had not raised exhaustion; the court raised it itself because it considered the requirement jurisdictional. It held that she needed to ask the Board for reconsideration before seeking judicial review. The Supreme Court granted review of both the jurisdictional characterization and the asserted reconsideration requirement. 415 & nn.2–3.

## Questions and holdings

Section 1252(d)(1) permits review of a final removal order only if the noncitizen has exhausted administrative remedies available as of right.

**First holding:** That exhaustion requirement is **nonjurisdictional** and therefore subject to waiver and forfeiture. 416–423.

**Second holding:** The statute does **not** require seeking discretionary review such as Board reconsideration. A right to request such review does not make the review itself available as of right. 423–431.

The Court did not decide the Government's alternative grounds for enforcing exhaustion sua sponte, because Santos-Zacaria had satisfied the provision even if it applied here. It also reserved the broader question of obligations to present particular issues during agency proceedings. 423, 430–431 n.10. It did not grant withholding or decide whether the Board's factfinding was unlawful.

## Reasoning on jurisdiction

Jurisdictional requirements define adjudicatory authority, must be enforced by courts despite party waiver or forfeiture, and bring potentially harsh consequences. The Court therefore demands a clear congressional statement. Multiple plausible readings, only one jurisdictional, cannot easily supply that clarity. 416–417.

Two features together defeated the Government's reading. First, exhaustion is ordinarily a claims-processing requirement designed to promote efficiency and agency resolution. Treating it as jurisdictional can force useless proceedings or unravel completed litigation after a late objection. Second, Congress repeatedly used express no-jurisdiction language elsewhere in contemporaneously enacted immigration provisions, including neighboring parts of § 1252, but chose different language here. That contrast was meaningful where the requirement was already an ordinary procedural one. 417–419 & nn.4–6.

The Government's reliance on the words “court” and “review” failed because procedural instructions can address courts and methods of review too. This provision also addresses the litigant's exhaustion conduct. Its predecessor supplied no definitive jurisdictional interpretation: Stone and Nken had not actually decided the technical status of this exhaustion requirement, and older lower-court labels were insufficient. Congress had also rewritten the predecessor language without adopting explicit jurisdictional terms. Finally, the contention that § 1252 furnished the exclusive review route did not make every condition within it jurisdictional. 420–423 & n.7.

## Reasoning on reconsideration

“As of right,” in this setting, identifies guaranteed rather than discretionary review. By longstanding regulation and practice, the Board has discretion whether to grant reconsideration or reopening. Reconsideration addresses prior errors; reopening concerns new facts. Reconsideration was the pertinent procedure because Santos-Zacaria alleged legal error. 424–425 & n.8.

The right to file a motion does not convert discretionary review into a remedy available as of right; otherwise that phrase would add nothing to “available.” Nor did the statute distinguish discretion supplied by regulation from discretion supplied by statute. Review for abuse of discretion limited the Board's discretion without eliminating it. 425–428.

The Government's interpretation also conflicted with a scheme allowing judicial review and reconsideration to proceed in parallel within their respective thirty-day windows. Requiring reconsideration in every case would produce unnecessary motions and judicial petitions deemed prematurely unexhausted despite being authorized by that scheme. 428–429.

The Government narrowed its argument to issues not previously presented to the agency, including alleged error newly introduced by the Board's decision. But reconsideration remained discretionary regardless of the issue's origin. The narrower rule still created premature petitions and practical uncertainty for litigants, courts, and the Board. The Court rejected that proposed reconsideration obligation, while expressly declining to resolve issue-presentation duties more generally. 429–431 & nn.9–10.

## Alito concurrence in the judgment

Alito, joined by Thomas, agreed that discretionary Board reconsideration was not an administrative remedy that had to be exhausted under the statute in these circumstances. That alone resolved the case. He would therefore leave open whether the provision was jurisdictional as to remedies it actually required. 431. The concurrence disagreed with reaching the first issue, rather than dissenting from the judgment or adopting a jurisdictional holding of its own.

## Exact disposition and course significance

The Court **vacated the portion of the Fifth Circuit's judgment dismissing the petition for review and remanded**. 431. Its disposition was partial vacatur, not an unqualified reversal of every issue in the appeal or a grant of immigration relief.

The assigned account illustrates the clear-statement rule's application to exhaustion. The full opinion adds an independent statutory answer: discretionary reconsideration was outside the exhaustion duty, regardless of the jurisdictional classification.
