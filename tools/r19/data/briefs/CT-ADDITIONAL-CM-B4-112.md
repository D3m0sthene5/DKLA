# Autry v. Republic Productions, Inc.
30 Cal. 2d 144, 180 P.2d 888 (1947) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
The California Supreme Court, sitting in bank on May 23, 1947, reversed a declaratory judgment requiring Gene Autry to complete his prewar acting obligations after leaving military service. Justice Shenk wrote; Chief Justice Gibson and Justices Carter, Traynor, and Schauer concurred. No separate opinion is reported. The judgment should declare that, absent a new agreement, Autry owed no post-discharge services under these contracts. (144, 146, 157.)

## Facts and negotiated allocation of risk
Autry acted in western films for Republic Productions. Their September 22, 1938 agreement covered ten pictures during an initial year beginning July 1, at $6,000 for each of the first two and $10,000 for the remaining eight. Four successive annual options covered eight films per year, with an annual $1,000-per-picture increase; the fourth option would pay $14,000 per film. The first three options were exercised, and written agreements set expiration under the third at March 6, 1943. (146–147.)

After Autry was ordered to a selective-service examination and classified 1-A in April 1942, the parties made a May 11 agreement granting an additional annual option at $15,000 per film. Its paragraph 24 provided that, if he was required to serve or volunteered for military service, they would agree upon their mutual rights and obligations in view of that service. Autry had sought automatic termination upon military entry; Republic rejected that proposal, and the future-agreement provision was their compromise. Autry enlisted on July 26, 1942, with five pictures still unmade under the third option, and performed no further services for Republic. The remaining options were exercised, producing an ordinary contractual termination date of March 6, 1945. (147, 152.)

Republic announced on March 3, 1944 that it would extend performance under the fourth option by the duration of military service. Autry gave notice of termination in June and filed this declaratory action on June 27, while still in the Army. The trial decision came in February 1945 and was entered March 20; his discharge occurred in July. Meanwhile the parties tried to arrange three pictures during military service, with Army permission and compensation to the Army Relief Fund, and a proposal to pay Autry's wife for horses and equipment. No agreement resulted. Later negotiations for a replacement postwar contract also failed. (147, 152–153.)

## Procedure and opposing positions
The trial court treated military service as temporary impossibility that suspended performance. It declared the contracts still binding, requiring five films under the third option, eight under the fourth, and eight under the additional option after discharge. It found delayed performance would create no substantially greater burden. The judgment excluded the statutory seven-year employment limit from its force; the appellate proceeding also excluded possible statutory reemployment obligations toward an ex-serviceman. Autry challenged continued liability through frustration and impossibility, whereas Republic relied on contractual suspension or extension provisions and treated paragraph 24 as meaningless. (147–149, 151.)

## Rules and interpretation of the agreements
Frustration and impossibility are related but distinct. Frustration concerns destruction of the expected value of an otherwise possible performance. This case did not strictly involve that destruction of consideration, and parties who contract with reference to war cannot simply treat the contemplated contingency as fortuitous. Under the Restatement rule discussed by the court, temporary impossibility suspends duty unless performance after it ends would impose a substantially greater burden, in which event discharge is permanent. The court did not need to decide whether military entry, standing alone under an unamended 1938 agreement, always permanently discharges employment obligations. (148–149.)

The 1938 provisions did not authorize Republic's proposed extension. Paragraph 12 covered specified events, including war, that prevented film production, but allowed Autry to terminate after four weeks of unpaid suspension unless Republic elected to pay the specified weekly amount. Republic had not followed that provision. Paragraph 13 concerned illness, disfigurement, or comparable physical or mental impairment; inability caused by military service was outside its subject. Paragraph 14 addressed voluntary refusal or neglect while capable of rendering the contracted service. Wartime enlistment could not be treated as that kind of default merely because enlistment was voluntary. (149–151.)

More importantly, the 1942 paragraph specifically governing military service controlled inconsistent general provisions. An agreement to agree cannot itself support enforcement of undefined future terms, and a court cannot supply those terms. But paragraph 24 was part of an existing agreement and therefore still affected the existing allocation of rights. It was not meaningless merely because the promised future bargain could not be judicially manufactured. Negotiations before and after enlistment showed the parties had deliberately left their obligations in this contingency for renewed agreement. (151–154.)

One interpretation consistent with their conduct was that the contracts remained in force during military service, without suspension of the running option periods, and expired March 6, 1945. If instead military service suspended performance as the trial court held, paragraph 24 nevertheless abrogated the old terms for the contingency and required substituted terms to be agreed upon. Failure to reach those terms did not restore the superseded obligations. Under either treatment, paragraph 24 could not be ignored in favor of automatic postwar continuation. (153–155.)

## Alternative reasoning about delayed performance
Even assuming suspension and revival of the original terms, the finding of no substantially greater burden lacked support. The issue was not simply whether making twenty-one films would require the same amount of work at the same nominal rates. Autry's popular western career depended on physical agility, audience following, and a limited period of peak performance. Military service consumed several years of that period, and postponed performance would consume still more. Wartime economic changes also reduced the real value of the agreed compensation. Republic's annual options protected it against declining popularity and other adverse conditions; the actor had no comparable automatic protection unless the negotiated military clause received effect. (154–156.)

Autry assumed economic risks for the agreed contractual duration, not necessarily for an extra duration created by war. Time was material to both the options and paragraph 24. The historical probative facts were found, but their support for the ultimate conclusion of no added burden was a legal question. The undisputed contracts and conduct showed substantial hardship and could not sustain the trial court's declaration. This reasoning does not make every temporary interruption an automatic discharge; it applies to the particular services, delay, allocation of options, and negotiated clause. (155–157.)

## Exact disposition and source scope
The declaratory judgment was reversed. The court specified that, without a new agreement continuing the relationship, Autry had no duty to serve Republic after discharge; it did not award damages or resolve the excluded statutory questions.
