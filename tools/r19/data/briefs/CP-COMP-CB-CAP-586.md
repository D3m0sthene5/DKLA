# Nwosun v. General Mills Restaurants, Inc.

**124 F.3d 1255 (10th Cir. 1997) · United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit · September 8, 1997 · Civil Procedure**

The court affirmed summary judgment barring a second action concerning the same employment discharge. An earlier Title VII judgment based on an untimely EEOC charge had claim-preclusive effect, including against a related state claim omitted from that federal action. Uncertainty whether the first federal judge would have retained supplemental jurisdiction did not establish that the plaintiff lacked a full and fair opportunity to litigate. The assigned account spans CivPro physical PDF pages 693–694, printed pages 1378–1379, within Note 4 before Note 5.

## Two cases arising from the same discharge

Ben Nwosun filed an Oklahoma state retaliatory-discharge action against General Mills on June 16, 1995. The opinion calls that action Nwosun I. Four days later, he filed a federal Title VII race-discrimination action concerning the same discharge, Nwosun II. General Mills sought a stay of the state case pending resolution of the federal case. **124 F.3d at 1256–1257.**

The federal court entered summary judgment for General Mills because Nwosun failed to submit a discrimination charge to the EEOC within the required time. The Tenth Circuit affirmed that ruling in a separate 1996 decision. Meanwhile, on December 14, 1995, Nwosun amended his state complaint to include the Title VII allegation. General Mills removed the amended case to federal court. That court held the repeated federal claim precluded by Nwosun II and the state retaliatory-discharge claim precluded because it could have been brought there under supplemental jurisdiction. The present appeal reviewed that second summary judgment. **Id. at 1256–1257.**

This posture matters: the state case was filed first, but the federal case produced the judgment on which the preclusion defense rested. Calling the matters I and II identifies filing order rather than which judgment was rendered first.

## Four elements and the meaning of a merits judgment

Chief Judge Seymour, writing for a panel with Judges Porfilio and Murphy, treated res judicata as an affirmative defense on which General Mills bore the burden. With the facts undisputed, the court reviewed application of preclusion law de novo. It identified four requirements: a prior judgment on the merits, identical parties or privity, the same cause of action, and a full and fair opportunity to litigate in the earlier proceeding. **Id. at 1257.**

The parties were the same. The prior judgment also counted as on the merits for preclusion purposes. The court characterized the EEOC filing deadline as a limitations requirement, relying on Zipes, and applied circuit authority treating a limitations judgment as a merits judgment. That characterization does not mean that the first court tried whether racial discrimination actually occurred. It identifies the preclusive effect the 1997 panel gave the time-bar ruling. **Id. at 1257.**

## One transaction, different theories

The circuit used a transactional definition of a cause of action. Claims and legal theories arising from the same transaction, event, or occurrence ordinarily had to be presented together. Nwosun's discharge supplied the transaction underlying both his race-discrimination claim and his state retaliatory-discharge claim. Different legal labels therefore did not make them different causes of action for this analysis. Note 2 adds that Oklahoma also followed a transactional approach. **Id. at 1257 & n.2.**

The holding does not say that every dispute between an employee and employer belongs to a single perpetual claim. Its factual linkage was the particular discharge challenged in both complaints.

## Supplemental jurisdiction and a full and fair opportunity

The difficult issue was whether the omitted state claim could fairly be precluded when the federal claim had ended before trial. The court acknowledged the ordinary practice of dismissing supplemental state claims without prejudice after pretrial disposition of the federal claim. Had Nwosun joined both theories, his state claim would have been ripe for such dismissal. That possibility arguably limited his ability to obtain a federal adjudication of state law. **Id. at 1257–1258.**

But the district court retained discretion to decide the supplemental claim. It would be speculation to conclude that the court would necessarily have declined jurisdiction over a claim never presented. The panel refused to conduct that hypothetical reconstruction merely because supplemental dismissal was a common course. It concluded that uncertainty about a discretionary jurisdictional decision did not establish a denial of a full and fair opportunity. **Id. at 1258.**

The court connected this result to finality: avoiding repeated litigation reduced expense and vexation, conserved resources, and protected reliance on judgments. It examined procedural limitations, incentives, and the parties' relationship as relevant fairness considerations. Its cited comparisons included a different state-law setting in which a court clearly would have declined jurisdiction; the panel did not turn every possible jurisdictional barrier into an irrelevant consideration. Rather, the speculative possibility asserted here was insufficient. **Id. at 1257–1258.**

The opinion also found nothing in Oklahoma preclusion law suggesting that the state action could have continued after the federal judgment had it remained in state court. It therefore affirmed the bar to the omitted retaliatory-discharge claim as well as the repeated Title VII claim. **Id. at 1258.**

## Exact judgment and assigned-source qualification

The district court's judgment was **affirmed**. The report contains no separate writing or appendix. Note 1 records the panel's unanimous agreement to decide on the briefs without oral argument at the parties' request; that procedural agreement is not a separately reported numerical tally on the merits. **Id. at 1256 n.1, 1258.**

The textbook describes the first judgment as based on failure to exhaust administrative remedies. The actual opinion is more specific: the defect was an **untimely EEOC charge**, expressly treated as a limitations bar. Preserving that distinction explains why this panel called the judgment one on the merits for preclusion. The assigned quotation accurately includes the court's refusal to speculate about supplemental jurisdiction. The surrounding discussion of Semtek and §1367 is textbook context; it does not convert this 1997 judgment into a holding addressing later diversity-preclusion doctrine. **Assigned physical 693–694, printed 1378–1379; actual 124 F.3d at 1256–1258.**
