# Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.

**467 U.S. 837 (1984) — Supreme Court of the United States — June 25, 1984**  
**Course:** Legislation and the Regulatory State  
**Assigned materials:** November part a, PDF 153–158, printed 933–938, and part b, PDF 8–9, printed 952–953. The first assignment contains a developed account and retained judicial footnotes 9 and 11, followed by editorial discussion of later decisions. The second contains Loper Bright’s later treatment of Chevron, rather than additional text of the 1984 decision.

## Concrete dispute and procedural history

The Clean Air Act’s 1977 amendments required States with areas that had not attained national air-quality standards to establish permit programs for new or modified major stationary pollution sources. These permits carried demanding conditions, including the lowest achievable emission rate. The disputed question was what counted as a source within an existing industrial plant: each emitting unit, or the plant as a whole. EPA’s October 1981 regulation allowed States to use the latter approach for industrial activities in the same grouping on contiguous or adjacent property under common control, excluding vessel activities. Under this “bubble,” a plant could replace or modify one component without triggering the nonattainment permit requirements if the changes did not increase the plant’s total emissions. This concerned a particular permit trigger; it did not eliminate every other emission requirement. 467 U.S. at 839–841 & nn.1–3, 858–859.

NRDC, Citizens for a Better Environment, and the North Western Ohio Lung Association petitioned the D.C. Circuit to review the rule. Industry representatives intervened. The D.C. Circuit set it aside in the separately reported 1982 decision, 685 F.2d 718. That court acknowledged that neither the statute nor its legislative history gave a clear answer. Nevertheless, drawing on its own precedents, it treated plantwide definitions as acceptable for maintaining clean air but impermissible for programs intended to improve dirty air. Chevron, the American Iron and Steel Institute, and EPA Administrator Ruckelshaus obtained review through three petitions addressed by this Supreme Court opinion. Those petition numbers represent the joint review instrument, not three independently dated merits opinions. 467 U.S. at 837, 840–842 & nn.4–7.

The environmental respondents did not defend the D.C. Circuit’s precise reasoning in the Supreme Court. Instead, they argued that the statute’s language, legislative history, and pollution-reduction purposes independently required rejection of EPA’s construction. The Court could consider those alternative grounds because appellate review concerns the judgment, not merely the lower court’s stated explanation. Id. at 842 n.8.

## Question, holding, and judgment

The question was whether EPA permissibly interpreted the nonattainment permit provisions to allow a plantwide definition of stationary source. The Court held that Congress had not resolved the precise unit-versus-plant issue and that EPA’s construction reasonably accommodated the statute’s competing environmental and economic objectives. It **reversed the D.C. Circuit’s judgment**. The opinion’s concluding mandate says “Reversed”; it does not separately announce a remand or award a permit to a particular plant. Id. at 859–866.

Justice Stevens wrote for all six participating Justices: Chief Justice Burger and Justices Brennan, White, Blackmun, Powell, and Stevens. Justices Marshall and Rehnquist did not participate in consideration or decision; Justice O’Connor did not participate in the decision. There were no separate opinions. This was unanimity among six participants, not a nine-Justice vote. Id. at 839, 866.

## The original two-question framework

The opinion framed judicial review of an administering agency’s statutory construction through two questions. First, has Congress directly answered the precise question? If so, both the court and the agency must follow Congress’s clear answer. Footnote 9 makes the judicial role explicit: courts use traditional statutory-construction tools, and an intention those tools reveal governs. Chevron did not instruct courts to disregard statutory text merely because an agency preferred another policy. Id. at 842–843 & n.9.

Second, when Congress had not directly resolved the issue, the historical Chevron framework asked whether the agency’s answer was a permissible construction. An agency did not have to show that its interpretation was the only permissible one, or the reading the court would independently prefer. Footnote 11 supplies that important distinction. The opinion also distinguished an express delegation to fill a regulatory gap from an implicit delegation on a particular statutory question. Regulations implementing express delegations received controlling weight unless arbitrary, capricious, or manifestly contrary to the statute; an implicit delegation likewise prevented a court from substituting its own construction for a reasonable administrative interpretation. Id. at 843–845 & nn.10–14.

These propositions explain the Court’s criticism of the D.C. Circuit: after determining that Congress had not dictated the definition, that court imposed a static judicial definition nonetheless. The Supreme Court required an assessment of the agency’s permissible policy choice within the statutory scheme. Its lengthy analysis of text, regulatory development, and legislative history mattered to whether that space actually existed. Id. at 842, 845–859.

## How the statutory and regulatory history supported the result

The 1970 Act combined federal air-quality standards with State implementation plans and supplied a source definition for the separate new-source performance-standard program. That definition did not itself settle the later nonattainment permit question. When many areas failed to meet the standards, EPA developed offset policies permitting economic development subject to emission reductions elsewhere. Congress’s 1977 amendments created a more structured nonattainment scheme but balanced progress toward clean air with growth and State flexibility. The provisions required permits for major sources, regional compliance measures, and demanding emission technology, without unequivocally specifying whether each internal component had to be treated as the relevant source. Id. at 845–853 & nn.15–25.

EPA’s own treatment had changed. Its 1979 approach allowed a plantwide definition where an adequate implementation plan existed while applying stricter treatment elsewhere. The 1980 rule adopted a dual plant-and-component definition, influenced by the D.C. Circuit’s distinction between maintaining and improving air quality. In 1981, a new administration reconsidered that policy. EPA reasoned that the dual definition discouraged replacement of older equipment with cleaner machinery and unnecessarily complicated administration. A uniform plantwide approach could encourage modernization while retaining the requirement that implementation plans make progress toward attainment. New-source performance standards remained applicable, and a net increase at a major plant still implicated new-source review. Id. at 853–859 & nn.26–30.

The Court evaluated the respondents’ textual argument that statutory references to buildings, structures, facilities, and installations necessarily required regulation of individual units. Those terms could overlap and could also describe an integrated industrial operation. The definition of a major source as one emitting or potentially emitting at least 100 tons annually did not by itself specify the physical unit to which the threshold applied. Legislative references to stringent treatment of new sources similarly presupposed, rather than answered, the disputed definition. States remained free to adopt a narrower unit-based definition; EPA’s rule permitted the bubble approach instead of compelling every State to use it. Id. at 859–862 & nn.31–35, especially n.33.

The economic explanation also fit Congress’s compromise. EPA could reasonably conclude that modernization incentives served both production and environmental improvement, and the record included supporting environmental-agency comments and economic studies. The Court did not hold that growth defeated pollution control. It held that Congress had left room to reconcile these objectives through a permissible source definition. Id. at 863–864 & nn.36–38.

## Policy changes, accountability, and limits

A changed agency interpretation did not automatically lose deference under the 1984 framework. Agencies could reconsider policy through informed administration; their first interpretation was not permanently fixed. That observation did not dispense with statutory limits or reasoned decisionmaking. Here EPA had addressed a technically complex scheme in detail, explained the incentives created by its earlier approach, and chosen a construction the statute could bear. Id. at 863–866.

The Court linked this allocation of responsibility to institutional competence and political accountability. Agencies had relevant expertise, and the President was electorally accountable for legitimate executive policy choices. Judges lacked a constituency authorizing them to choose environmental policy for themselves. Where Congress had left the choice unresolved—whether deliberately, through lack of attention, or because no legislative coalition could settle it—the Court treated reasonable administrative accommodation as controlling under the historical framework. Those explanations concerned policy choices within statutory authority; they did not license an agency to override an ascertainable congressional command. Id. at 865–866 & nn.39–41.

## Assigned emphasis and verified later treatment

The assigned account emphasizes both Chevron’s familiar two-question formulation and the tension between that formulation and the opinion’s extensive statutory analysis. It also records later disputes about the doctrine’s reach. Those textbook observations should be distinguished from what Stevens’s 1984 opinion itself held. The retained footnotes 9 and 11 are particularly useful: one preserves courts’ duty to identify clear congressional intent, while the other explains why a historically permissible administrative reading need not be a court’s preferred reading. Assigned printed 933–938.

**Chevron’s deference framework was overruled by Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo in 2024.** Loper Bright requires independent judicial judgment on statutory authority while respecting actual delegations and allowing executive expertise to inform interpretation. It expressly states that earlier holdings upholding specific agency actions—including Chevron’s Clean Air Act holding—remain subject to statutory stare decisis; mere reliance on the former methodology is not itself sufficient to overturn those holdings. That later distinction is reproduced in the assigned printed 952–953 and verified against Loper Bright’s official opinion, Roberts slip pp.34–35. This brief describes Chevron’s original reasoning, rather than presenting its deference framework as current law.
