# Lee v. Joseph E. Seagram & Sons, Inc.

552 F.2d 447 (2d Cir. 1977) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B2-008

## Transaction and the jury's finding

Harold Lee and his sons Lester and Eric owned fifty percent of Capitol City Liquor Company, a Washington, D.C., wholesale distributorship. Harold's brother and nephew owned the balance. Seagram brands generated a substantial part of its sales. Harold had worked for Seagram for thirty-six years, including leadership of its Calvert subsidiary, and had bought Seagram's Capitol City shares in 1958 to introduce his sons to the business and provide Seagram with a strong local distributor. He had known executive vice president Jack Yogman for thirteen years. 552 F.2d at 449–450.

In May 1970, when the owners wanted to sell, Harold discussed a sale with Yogman, conditioning his proposal on Seagram's relocation of himself and his sons to another distributorship. Another Seagram officer, John Barth, subsequently negotiated the purchase of Capitol City's assets on behalf of a replacement distributor, Carter. The asset sale closed September 30 under a written agreement. The relocation promise was not written into it. Fifteen months later, the Lees sued, maintaining that they had performed by agreeing to the asset sale while Seagram had failed to provide the promised business despite opportunities to do so. Id.

The appellate court reviewed the facts the jury could have found, rather than treating every competing description of the oral promise as established. Under the unchallenged relevant instruction, the jury found a commitment to provide a Seagram distributorship within a reasonable time, in a location acceptable to the Lees, at a price requiring approximately the capital they received from selling their Capitol City interest. The court described its value and profit potential as approximately half Capitol City's. Some evidence and the district court's discussion described merely giving notice of opportunities to negotiate; other evidence supported the stronger commitment to provide a business, and that was the agreement the jury found. This difference was crucial both to definiteness and to damages. Id. at 450 & n.2, 453 & n.6.

## Procedure and issues

Judge Tenney entered judgment on a $407,850 jury verdict and denied Seagram's Rule 50(b) motion for judgment notwithstanding the verdict. He had earlier denied summary judgment. Separate antitrust claims had been dismissed by directed verdict and were not on appeal. The Second Circuit considered whether the parol evidence rule barred the oral undertaking, whether that undertaking was too indefinite or illusory to enforce, and whether the damages evidence was speculative or incompetent. Judge Gurfein wrote for a panel including Medina and Oakes; no separate opinion appears. Both parties assumed New York law, and the court applied that law without resolving a contested choice-of-law question. Id. at 449–451 & nn.1, 3.

## Why the writing did not exclude the oral undertaking

The parol evidence rule was substantive law, but it did not categorically exclude every oral promise associated with a written transaction. The court asked whether the writing, objectively considered in its setting, integrated all the parties' promises and whether the asserted undertaking was one they would ordinarily put in that writing. Seagram argued that the relocation promise induced the sale or formed part of its consideration and therefore had to be included. The Lees answered that it was an independent collateral promise, consistent with the asset-sale terms. Id. at 450–452.

Several features supported the Lees. The written instrument governed a corporate asset sale; the oral promise benefited particular individuals who held only half the corporation's ownership. Comparable employment or consulting arrangements for particular shareholders often stood apart from a corporate transaction. The parties' identities and the promises' subjects therefore differed. The long relationship between Harold and Yogman, whose authority was unchallenged, made reliance on a handshake plausible. The written negotiations were conducted by other officers, who might not have understood the two transactions as one integrated bargain. Barth's memorandum described Harold's desire for another distributorship, apparently without knowing what Yogman had already promised. Id. at 452 & n.4.

The asset-sale agreement also lacked a customary integration clause despite substantial boilerplate. Finally, the oral promise did not change the money consideration paid to the corporation: it addressed relocation of the individual Lees, while the writing addressed the corporation's assets. The court accordingly upheld reception of the evidence and denial of Rule 50(b) relief on this ground. This was a contextual integration decision, not a rule that any inducement omitted from a writing is enforceable. Id. at 452–453 & n.5.

## Definiteness and the acceptable-location condition

Seagram argued that missing purchase price, profitability, and sales volume prevented enforcement. The jury's credited evidence supplied objective content: the business was to approximate half Capitol City's value and profit potential, with a comparable investment, and industry practice valued a distributorship at book value plus three times the previous year's after-tax net profit. Direct testimony, the reference transaction, and industry evidence made the obligation reasonably ascertainable. Requiring performance within a reasonable time was permissible, particularly because Seagram knew the Lees needed to reinvest within one year to avoid adverse tax consequences. Evidence of Seagram's procurement of Carter's distributorship and Harold's earlier purchase also supported the meaning of providing a business. Id. at 453–454 & nn.6–7.

Seagram separately asserted that the Lees' ability to deem a location unacceptable made the promise illusory. Their consideration, however, was agreement to the asset sale and franchise assignment, which they had already performed. Their discretion was also constrained by good faith and evidence of acceptable performance. They had not promised to buy any particular business, so Seagram could not force acceptance. But a tender of reasonable performance would discharge Seagram's obligation even if the Lees refused it. They could not prolong the obligation by unreasonably rejecting a qualifying distributorship. Id. at 454.

## Damages and precise judgment

The charge directed the jury to compensate gains prevented and losses sustained, while deducting benefits from the freed capital and services. Accountant Ernest Sommers used half Capitol City's last fiscal year's pre-tax profits, supported by an upward five-year trend, to calculate a 14.508-percent return, and compared the Lees' subsequent bond return of 7.977 percent. The 6.531-percent difference represented $83,800 annually. Ten years produced $838,000, discounted to $549,000 present value. The jury's $407,850 award was lower. These were the expert's estimates and the jury's award respectively, not an appellate finding of a guaranteed ten-year franchise. Id. at 454–455.

The evidence addressed the bargain the jury found: provision of a distributorship approximately half Capitol City's cost and worth. The Lees did not have to identify a specific distributor then willing to sell. Seagram could arrange a voluntary sale or acquire and hold a business for them. Prospective profits could support contract damages, including for a business not yet operating, where an established comparator provided a reasonable basis. Seagram offered no better comparable-business evidence; its breach had made exact proof harder, so it bore the uncertainty its conduct created. Disputes about particular figures presented jury questions rather than erasing all proof. Id. at 455–456.

The court **affirmed the judgment**, including the $407,850 award and denial of post-verdict relief. The holding does not dispense with proof that breach caused loss; it distinguishes uncertainty about the amount of established loss from absence of evidentiary support. Id. at 456.
