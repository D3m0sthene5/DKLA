# Securities and Exchange Commission v. Jarkesy
144 S. Ct. 2117 (2024), No. 22-859 | Legislation and the Regulatory State | Civil penalties, juries, and the public-rights exception

## Securities enforcement moved inside the agency

The Securities Act of 1933, Securities Exchange Act of 1934, and Investment Advisers Act of 1940 regulate different parts of the securities market, but the antifraud provisions relevant here all address material misrepresentation or concealment. The SEC can enforce those laws through litigation in federal court or administrative proceedings inside the agency. The choice affects the decision-maker and the procedural protections. A federal trial supplies an Article III judge, ordinary discovery and evidentiary rules, and a jury when required. In an administrative proceeding, the Commission or its delegate decides facts and law, SEC practice rules govern, and later judicial review of supported factual findings is deferential. Majority slip op. pp. 1-4.

Before the 2010 Dodd-Frank Act, civil penalties against persons in respondents' position had to be obtained in federal court. Dodd-Frank expanded the SEC's administrative penalty authority to match its court authority. This case concerns that choice of forum for **civil penalties for securities fraud**, rather than every agency adjudication or every SEC remedy. Id. pp. 3-4, 6-7.

Between 2007 and 2010, George Jarkesy launched two investment funds that raised about $24 million from 120 accredited investors. His firm, Patriot28, served as investment adviser. The SEC alleged that they misrepresented investment strategies, the identity of the funds' auditor and prime broker, and fund values used to calculate management fees. The full dissent adds the particulars: promised auditing and prime-broker arrangements did not materialize, an asserted fifty-percent investment in life-insurance policies was below twenty percent, and alleged overvaluation increased fees. These describe the agency's allegations and determinations, not an independent Supreme Court trial of fraud liability. Majority pp. 4-5; Sotomayor dissent pp. 2-3.

The SEC began administrative enforcement in 2013. An ALJ issued an initial decision in 2014, and the Commission reviewed it and entered its final order in 2020. It imposed a $300,000 civil penalty, ordered cessation of violations and disgorgement, and barred Jarkesy from certain securities activities. The dissent identifies Patriot28's disgorgement amount as $685,000. The penalty, rather than a decision about the independent validity of each other remedy, is the constitutional focus here. Majority p. 5; dissent p. 3.

## The Fifth Circuit's three grounds and the Court's single ground

A divided Fifth Circuit panel granted respondents' petition for review and vacated the SEC order. It concluded that the administrative adjudication violated their Seventh Amendment jury right, that Congress had insufficiently guided the SEC's choice between agency and court proceedings under the nondelegation doctrine, and that two layers of for-cause removal protection for ALJs violated separation of powers. Judge Davis dissented. After denial of rehearing en banc, the Supreme Court granted certiorari. Majority pp. 5-6.

Chief Justice Roberts organized the Supreme Court's analysis in two steps. First, did these claims implicate the Seventh Amendment? Second, if they did, could the public-rights exception nevertheless permit administrative resolution without a jury? The Court answered yes to the first and no to the second. That resolved the case, so it **did not reach nondelegation or ALJ removal protection**. Its affirmance does not make all three Fifth Circuit rationales Supreme Court holdings. Id. pp. 6-7, 27.

## Why this action was legal in nature

The Seventh Amendment's preservation of jury trial is not confined to causes of action bearing the precise forms known at ratification. It extends to statutory claims that are legal in nature, as opposed to equity or admiralty matters. Under *Tull* and *Granfinanciera*, the court considers the cause of action's common-law analogue and the nature of the remedy; the remedy is the more important factor. Roberts also traces the jury's role in colonial resistance and the founding to explain why expediency cannot casually displace the guarantee. Id. pp. 7-9.

The civil penalties here were punitive and deterrent rather than solely restorative. Statutory factors addressed culpability, recidivism, and deterrence. Successively larger penalty tiers likewise turned on fraud, recklessness, gains, losses, or risk, rather than simply measuring compensation owed to a victim. The SEC was not required to distribute the penalty to injured investors. Monetary relief can be equitable, but these features made this relief legal. Id. pp. 9-11.

The antifraud claims' relationship to common-law fraud reinforced the conclusion. Congress used established fraud terminology, and interpretation of securities law frequently draws on the corresponding common-law principles. Federal securities fraud is not identical to its historical analogue: it is restricted to particular subject matter, uses different proof requirements in some respects, and need not require investor loss for SEC enforcement. The Court required a close relationship rather than identical elements. Id. pp. 11-13.

## Why public rights did not remove the jury requirement

The Court recognizes that Congress can assign some public-rights matters to the political branches for initial determination outside Article III. Roberts discusses established categories such as revenue collection, aspects of customs and immigration, public lands, tribal relations, and public benefits, including patent rights. He does not claim to provide an exhaustive definition that resolves every difficult public/private distinction. The presumption favors Article III courts, however, and the legal basis for an exception requires attention; neither agency convenience nor Congress's assignment alone decides the question. Id. pp. 13-18 & nn.1-2.

*Granfinanciera* supplies the central analogy. There, Congress's designation of fraudulent-conveyance actions as core bankruptcy proceedings did not eliminate a jury right. Such actions resembled traditional legal suits and could be brought separately, unlike the coordinated priority claims integral to disposition of a bankruptcy estate. The substance of the action controlled despite a new regulatory designation. Roberts applied that principle to securities-fraud penalties: their common-law character could not be transformed merely by assigning them to an administrative tribunal. Id. pp. 19-21.

The SEC argued that Congress created new statutory obligations and remedies enforceable by the Government in its sovereign capacity. It relied especially on *Atlas Roofing*. The majority rejected statutory novelty and governmental participation as sufficient by themselves. Here the Government regulated transactions between private actors in an existing market through claims modeled on common-law fraud and a punitive legal remedy. The identity of the prosecuting party did not change that substance. Id. pp. 21-22.

## The competing readings of Atlas Roofing

The majority distinguished *Atlas Roofing*, which upheld administrative assessment of occupational-safety penalties. The OSH Act imposed new, detailed safety requirements rather than restating a traditional common-law cause of action. In Roberts's account, its claims were novel in a way these antifraud claims were not. *Atlas Roofing* therefore did not control. Nor could generalized advantages of efficient enforcement replace the constitutional protections. Id. pp. 22-27.

The Court expressly declined to decide respondents' suggestion that later cases had effectively overruled *Atlas Roofing*. Footnote 3 discusses that suggestion without adopting it; footnote 4 collects scholarly criticism while disclaiming endorsement of the various criticisms. The holding distinguishes the precedent for this securities-fraud case. It should not be stated as an express overruling of *Atlas Roofing* or elimination of all administrative civil penalties. Id. pp. 22-26 & nn.3-4.

## Gorsuch and Thomas: three protections working together

Justice Gorsuch, joined by Thomas, concurred and emphasized Article III and Fifth Amendment due process alongside the jury guarantee. In his analysis, each independently supported the result: traditional common-law claims require an independent judge, a jury when applicable, and customary fair judicial process. He described the administrative proceedings' discovery limits, evidentiary differences, delays, and internal access to confidential appellate memoranda as reasons these safeguards mattered. These details and the concurrence's evaluation of them are separately attributed; they are not additional holdings silently added to Roberts's opinion. Gorsuch concurrence pp. 1-5, 11-12.

Gorsuch compared expansion of agency adjudication with colonial vice-admiralty practices and read public rights as a narrow category supported by settled history. He criticized the movement from *Crowell* through *Atlas Roofing* and subsequent balancing approaches, arguing that efficiency could not displace individual constitutional protections. He defended the majority's narrower interpretation of *Atlas Roofing* as consistent with other precedent, rather than treating all statutory obligations as public rights. Both concurrence footnotes were read, including its discussion of administrative judges and its challenge to the dissent's distinction between civil and criminal proceedings. Id. pp. 6-28 & nn.1-2.

## Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson: sovereign enforcement is public

Justice Sotomayor's dissent, joined by Kagan and Jackson, would have upheld the administrative scheme. For her, the controlling initial question was whether Congress could assign this governmental enforcement claim outside Article III. If that assignment was permissible, the Seventh Amendment presented no independent requirement that an administrative factfinder use a jury. She viewed claims belonging to the Government in its sovereign capacity as the public-rights doctrine's central category. A statutory enforcement right protecting the public market was different from a private investor's damages claim. Sotomayor dissent pp. 4-8, 15-16, 25-29.

Her interpretation of *Murray's Lessee*, *Stranahan*, *Atlas Roofing*, and the broader line of public-rights cases emphasized Congress's established authority to create obligations and permit administrative penalties. *Tull* concerned a claim already filed in federal court; *Granfinanciera* concerned private parties and distinguished that setting from government sovereign enforcement. In her view, neither displaced *Atlas Roofing*'s treatment of governmental civil penalties. Nor did parallels with common-law fraud erase the federal scheme's distinct obligations, prophylactic operation without individual injury, and public enforcement purpose. Id. pp. 9-16, 22-32.

The dissent disputed the majority's historically defined categories and its characterization of her position as an efficiency exception. It also rejected the notion that the Government's mere appearance as a party was enough: footnote 9 excludes criminal prosecutions and common-law claims brought in a proprietary capacity. Footnote 4 leaves unpresented due-process objections for a suitable case; respondents had not advanced such a challenge here. Its thirteen notes, including these limitations, form part of the complete dissent. Id. pp. 8-9 n.4, 17-21, 27-28 n.9.

Sotomayor finally stressed precedent, congressional reliance, and potential effects on numerous penalty schemes. Some agencies could seek penalties only administratively, so broad application could remove their enforcement route unless Congress changed the statute. She regarded the Court's restriction on Congress as itself a separation-of-powers problem. Those are the dissent's predicted consequences and institutional objections, not a holding that every cited agency's statute was invalidated in this case. Id. pp. 32-38.

## Judgment, votes, and source coverage

The Court **affirmed the Fifth Circuit and remanded for further proceedings**, holding respondents entitled to a jury trial in an Article III court on these securities-fraud civil-penalty claims. The judgment was 6-3. Roberts's opinion was joined by Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett. Gorsuch and Thomas also joined the concurrence; Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson dissented. The result removed the agency order through affirmance of its vacatur; it did not adjudicate that respondents had committed no fraud or prevent the SEC from pursuing the claims in the constitutionally proper forum. Majority p. 27; official disposition and opinion roster.
