# Croker v. New York Trust Co.
245 N.Y. 17, 156 N.E. 81 (1927) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
On March 29, 1927, the New York Court of Appeals reversed judgments enforcing an oral promise for two third-party beneficiaries and granted a new trial, with costs to abide the event. Judge Pound wrote; Chief Judge Cardozo and Judges Crane, Andrews, Kellogg, and O'Brien concurred, and Judge Lehman did not vote. The court approved the promisee's resort to equity but found his testimony incompetent under the then-governing deceased-person evidence statute. Approval of the equitable cause of action was therefore not affirmance of the existing recovery. (17, 19–24.)

## Facts and procedure
Richard Croker, Jr. alleged an oral agreement with his father. In exchange for the son's promise to transfer his share of his mother's estate to his brother Howard, the father promised, after notice of its amount, to pay an equal sum to Howard and another equal sum to the son's sister Ethel. The son established that his one-fourth share was $76,191.15 and that he paid it to Howard. Each promised share, including interest, was computed at $114,587.29. The son obtained a total recovery of $229,174.58 for the two beneficiaries in an equity action against the father's estate administrator, with Howard and Ethel also before the court. The father's promise was established through the plaintiff son's testimony about their transaction. (19–20.)

The Appellate Division unanimously affirmed the Special Term judgment. The estate administrator appealed by permission. Under the review rules applicable to that unanimous affirmance, no factual question remained for the Court of Appeals. The administrator instead challenged both the promisee's right to maintain the equitable action and the legal competency of his testimony under Civil Practice Act § 347. The beneficiaries defended a contract supported by valuable consideration and argued that the son testified for their benefit, rather than in his own financial interest. (17–20.)

## Promisee enforcement for beneficiaries
The administrator accepted that Howard and Ethel could potentially enforce a contract made for their benefit but denied that the promisee could enforce their rights in equity. The court rejected that restriction. The father's contract was with the plaintiff son, who had parted with a substantial sum in reliance on the father's undertaking. The son had a legitimate interest in insisting on fulfillment even though payment was to go to others. His ordinary damages remedy was inadequate because nonpayment to the beneficiaries caused him no personal pecuniary loss; his own legal injury was merely technical. Equity could bring all interested parties before the court and enforce the promisor's obligation. Otherwise this promisee would be without an adequate remedy despite furnishing the consideration. (19–20.)

That reasoning supports the supplied Restatement discussion of promisee enforcement when specific performance is otherwise appropriate. It does not mean every third-party contract automatically receives equitable enforcement or that beneficiaries lose their own rights. Here the obstacle lay in proving the promise competently, not in the identity of the person seeking enforcement. (20; C&M printed 1174.)

## Evidence holding and its limits
Section 347 retained a deceased-person exception to the general abolition of common-law witness disqualification. Its purpose was to prevent an interested survivor from establishing a personal transaction for himself, or those deriving an interest from him, when death prevented the other participant from contradicting or explaining it. The court recognized the policy's cost to potentially meritorious claims but applied the retained statutory rule. (20–21.)

Merely being named as a party, feeling strongly about the dispute, or favoring a relative's success was not invariably a disqualifying interest. The relevant question was whether the witness could gain or lose by the outcome, including indirect legal effects of the record. The son had no direct interest in the beneficiaries' recoveries, but he could be charged costs if his own action failed. That possible liability was sufficient financial interest. The lower courts had regarded costs in equity as too discretionary or contingent; the Court of Appeals found the common-law rule did not recognize that distinction. Thus the son was a person interested in the event and could not supply the testimony establishing his personal transaction with the deceased father. (21–23.)

A separate possible ground of disqualification was whether the beneficiaries derived their title or interest from, through, or under the son who provided consideration. The opinion surveyed conflicting precedents: some treated the promisee as only a medium, rather than an assignor, while others used a broader derivation rule. Later insurance-assignment authority had somewhat weakened the broad earlier approach. The court did not definitively resolve which line should govern because the costs-based interest already decided competency. Its discussion of that alternate ground should not be converted into an additional necessary holding. (23–24.)

## Exact disposition and source scope
The judgments were reversed and a new trial granted, with costs to abide the event. The existing $229,174.58 award therefore did not survive this appeal; the court did not enter a final judgment that the alleged promise could never be proved or enforced.
