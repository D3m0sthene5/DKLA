# United States v. Wood

**295 F.2d 772 (5th Cir. 1961), No. 19237; October 27, 1961.** United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit. Judge Rives wrote for himself and Judge Brown; Judge Cameron dissented. Rehearing denied November 30, 1961, as noted in the report caption. Civil Procedure. Assigned: CivPro.pdf, physical page 626, printed page 1240, the developed Wood account in Note 2 on temporary restraining orders.

## Facts and urgent procedural setting

The United States sued Mississippi officials under the Civil Rights Act of 1957 to prevent the imminent prosecution of John Hardy. Hardy, a Black Tennessee resident and Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee participant, had organized classes helping Black citizens of Walthall County learn how to register to vote. The government’s affidavits described a county with about 2,490 Black persons of voting age, none registered, while a substantial majority of approximately 4,530 white persons of voting age were registered. After the registration school opened, several Black residents applied. (774–775.)

On September 7, 1961, Hardy accompanied Edith Peters and Lucius Wilson to registrar John Q. Wood’s office. According to the government’s affidavits, Wood refused to register anyone, retrieved a revolver, ordered Hardy out, and struck him on the head as he left. Sheriff Edd Craft then arrested Hardy for disturbing the peace. Statements attributed to the sheriff and other officials linked their hostility to Hardy’s registration activity and presence in Mississippi. No Black residents applied for registration after the incident. These were the government’s allegations and affidavit account, not findings reached after a completed evidentiary trial. (775–776.)

Hardy’s prosecution was scheduled for September 22. The United States filed its federal action September 20 and sought a temporary restraining order. Defendants did not submit counter-affidavits at the next day’s hearing, asserting insufficient notice and time. The district court denied relief at 5 p.m. September 21 and refused certification for a discretionary interlocutory appeal. Government counsel then sought relief from Judge Rives. A Mississippi assistant attorney general agreed to continue the prosecution while the appeal proceeded, making an immediate appellate stay unnecessary. Rives expedited the appeal and allowed defendants time to obtain counter-affidavits, subject to whether considering them would be legally permissible. (774–775.)

That last qualification mattered. Once the court accepted appellate jurisdiction, the majority treated the appeal as review of the district-court record and declined to consider the new affidavits. Note 3 explained that such material might instead have been considered under the alternative All Writs Act request. Cameron disagreed with the treatment of those materials. (775 n. 3; 788.)

## Parties’ positions and questions

The government did not seek to vindicate Hardy’s own right to register: he was not eligible to register in Walthall County. Nor did it assert that he could not receive a fair criminal trial. Its claim concerned eligible Black county residents, whom an allegedly retaliatory prosecution could deter from registering even if Hardy eventually won acquittal. Their voting rights were not at issue in his criminal trial, and his remedies would not remedy their intimidation. (780–781.)

Defendants sought dismissal of the appeal because denial of a temporary restraining order ordinarily is not appealable. They also invoked the Anti-Injunction Act and the traditional reluctance of federal equity courts to interfere with state prosecutions. The questions were whether the denial could be treated as final under § 1291 in these unusual circumstances and whether the district court could refuse interim protection without hearing the properly asserted federal claim. (776–780.)

## Appealability: practical finality

The majority acknowledged the ordinary nonappealability of temporary-restraining-order decisions. It did not announce that every denial is appealable under the interlocutory-injunction provision. Instead, the government’s first claim sought to prevent a specific, imminent prosecution from inflicting the alleged intimidation. Once that prosecution occurred, delayed appellate review could no longer supply the preventive relief sought. In practical effect, denying temporary relief disposed of that claim before its merits could be heard. (777–778.)

Drawing on the practical construction of finality in Cohen and related decisions, the court held that an otherwise nonappealable order determining substantial rights that would be irreparably lost through delayed review could be reviewed immediately under § 1291. It therefore treated this denial as an appealable final decision. Note 5 explained why denying temporary protection could be effectively final even though granting it would merely preserve the controversy for later decision. (777–778 & nn. 5–6.)

## Federal enforcement and equitable relief

The Anti-Injunction Act, § 2283, did not bar this action. Under Leiter Minerals, the majority understood the statutory prohibition as inapplicable to a suit brought by the United States. It rejected limiting that principle to federal property claims or civil state proceedings. The federal government’s protection of voting rights implicated a national public interest, as United States v. Raines confirmed; these were not merely private interests repackaged without statutory authority. The court found it unnecessary to decide whether the Civil Rights Act independently satisfied the Act’s express-authorization or aid-of-jurisdiction exceptions. (778–779 & n. 7.)

Removing the statutory bar did not alone settle whether an injunction was proper. The district court had relied on Douglas v. City of Jeannette and discretion to avoid interfering with a state criminal proceeding. The majority found that reasoning inadequate here. Section 1971 prohibited voting intimidation and authorized the Attorney General to seek preventive relief. Its statutory language and legislative history encompassed using criminal processes to interfere with constitutional rights. The pleaded acts, affidavits, and alleged deterrence therefore stated a claim warranting consideration. (780–782.)

Section 1971(d) also directed district courts to exercise jurisdiction without requiring exhaustion of other remedies. Congress had rejected an amendment making that exercise permissive. The majority read the provision as requiring an opportunity to hear a properly asserted claim, rather than allowing its effective dismissal on the temporary-relief motion. It expressly distinguished that duty from a direction to grant every requested injunction or to decide ultimate merits in the government’s favor. (783.)

Traditional comity concerns carried different weight in this authorized federal enforcement action. The government represented an important public interest, and the allegedly intimidated voters could not obtain relief through Hardy’s trial. Intimidation was the very conduct for which Congress supplied preventive relief. Waiting until it had accomplished its purpose would deprive that remedy of practical value. In contrast, briefly postponing a misdemeanor prosecution would impose little injury. (781–785.)

## Cameron’s dissent

Cameron denied that the temporary-restraining-order refusal was appealable and rejected the majority’s interpretation of § 2283. He believed Douglas’s restraints on interference with state prosecutions remained binding, citing subsequent Supreme Court decisions and disputing the majority’s reliance on circuit precedent suggesting modification. (785–787.)

He also criticized the government’s timing as creating its own emergency and objected to the majority’s portrayal of state officials on the basis of the government’s one-sided submissions. He emphasized that defendants supplied affidavits within the timetable Rives had allowed and that the government had not moved to strike them. (785 n. 1; 787–788.)

The dissent went beyond those procedural arguments. It disparaged Hardy as an outside agitator and defended leaving racial relationships to local communities, reproducing Cameron’s earlier criticism of federal civil-rights intervention. Those statements are Cameron’s historical rationale and characterizations; they are neither the majority’s factual findings nor an adopted rule limiting voting-rights enforcement. (787–789.)

## Exact judgment and significance

The court **reversed and remanded**. It required a full hearing on the preliminary-injunction request or ultimate claim, with examination and cross-examination of witnesses. Unless officials further agreed to postpone Hardy’s prosecution at least through the preliminary-injunction determination, the district court was to issue the temporary restraining order. The majority expressly disclaimed any determination of ultimate merits. (785.)

The assigned account accurately highlights § 1291 and the practical loss of preventive relief. The complete opinion supplies the limits: the government’s statutory claim, harm to eligible voters distinct from Hardy’s defense, record restrictions, and conditional direction to preserve the claim for a hearing. Wood illustrates practical finality in its particular setting, not a general entitlement to appeal every refused temporary order.
