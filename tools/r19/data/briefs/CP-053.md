# Mason v. American Emery Wheel Works

**Civil Procedure · CP-053 · 241 F.2d 906 (1st Cir. 1957).** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, PDF pp. 278–279, printed pp. 518–521. Chief Judge Magruder wrote the court's opinion; Judge Hartigan concurred separately. Judge Woodbury also sat on the panel.

## The injury, evidence, and dismissal

Whit Mason, a Mississippi citizen, sued American Emery Wheel Works, a Rhode Island corporation, in federal district court in Rhode Island. He alleged that the manufacturer negligently manufactured, inspected, and tested an emery wheel for a bench grinder, exposing lawful users to serious injury. On February 10, 1953, while Mason was using the grinder in the course of his employment with T. H. Pearce Company in Mississippi, the wheel shattered and injured him. The appeal did not establish those allegations as a final liability finding. (Full opinion, opening two factual paragraphs.)

Mason's trial evidence traced the wheel through several companies. The manufacturer sold it to Hoover, which attached it to a grinder, labeled the grinder as manufactured by Miller Falls, and shipped it in a crate. Without being unpacked, the grinder passed from Hoover to Miller Falls to Komp Equipment and finally to Mason's employer. The employer unpacked and installed it at Mason's bench; his evidence tended to show ordinary, proper use when the wheel exploded. This distribution chain explained why Mason lacked a direct contractual relationship with the wheel manufacturer. (Paragraph beginning “The case came up for trial.”)

The company denied both negligence and that it had made the particular wheel. It also asserted failure to state a claim and absence of a duty because Mason lacked privity of contract. Although the complaint did not itself expressly say there was no privity, the trial record presented the remote-user issue. At the close of Mason's evidence, the defendant moved orally under the then-applicable Rule 41(b). The district court dismissed, and Mason appealed. The First Circuit described the proceeding as a jury trial; this historical use of Rule 41(b) should not be silently replaced with a modern procedural characterization. (Paragraphs beginning “On the face,” “The answer,” and “At the conclusion.”)

## The Erie problem

The district court applied Mississippi law to the manufacturer's tort duty because the injury occurred there. The appellate court accepted that choice for this case. The harder question was how to ascertain Mississippi law when its highest court's older decision rejected liability without privity but its later statements suggested a different approach. This was a question of determining the relevant state's law, not permission to create a preferred federal tort rule.

In Ford Motor Co. v. Myers (1928), the Mississippi Supreme Court had denied recovery to a remote truck buyer injured by a manufacturing defect discoverable through reasonable inspection. The district court reluctantly treated that ruling as binding despite its severity and the contrary trend elsewhere. According to Magruder, Ford was the sole Mississippi authority mentioned in the district court's explanation. Hartigan's concurrence added that counsel told the appellate court the judge actually had both Ford and the later Du Pont decision before him. Those statements concern the court's expressed reliance and the material before it respectively, and should not be conflated. (Majority, paragraph beginning “Since the injury”; concurrence, first paragraph.)

## Why the First Circuit predicted a change

Magruder described the transformation associated with MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co. The older rule generally denied a manufacturer's duty to third parties without contractual relationships, then created exceptions for such things as drugs, explosives, and firearms. The modern approach understood those exceptions as illustrations of a more general duty to avoid unreasonable risks to foreseeable users. Restatement of Torts § 395 reflected that approach. The full opinion's account of the older federal common-law authorities explains the substantive shift; it does not revive Swift v. Tyson's federal general common law. (Paragraphs beginning “At a time when the federal courts,” and “MacPherson.”)

The appellate court acknowledged an important limit: had Mississippi recently reconsidered Ford and deliberately adhered to it, the federal courts would have had to follow that declaration. But the absence of express overruling did not necessarily freeze state law forever. A decision could become less persuasive through later qualifications, accumulated exceptions, and changes in the state's own jurisprudence. The widespread modern trend supplied context, but the most important predictive evidence was a recent Mississippi Supreme Court opinion. (Paragraphs beginning “MacPherson” and “Of course.”)

In E. I. Du Pont de Nemours & Co. v. Ladner (1954), the Mississippi court resolved the particular dispute on another ground, so it did not have to overrule Ford. Nevertheless, it carefully discussed the modern trend and apparently approved authorities recognizing recovery by people without direct contractual relations with a manufacturer, including for property damage as well as personal injury. Mason stressed that language. Magruder inferred that, when squarely presented with the issue, Mississippi's highest court would reconsider Ford and adopt the modern doctrine. (Final substantive majority paragraph.)

Thus the court was making a prediction about how the state tribunal would decide, supported by recent state-court statements and the surrounding doctrinal development. It did not formally overrule Ford on behalf of Mississippi, and its reasoning does not grant federal judges a general license to disregard state holdings because another rule seems better.

## Hartigan's concurrence and the exact result

Hartigan joined the court's opinion but emphasized the difficulty of the task. Ford was an actual controlling Mississippi holding, whereas the newer Du Pont discussion was dicta. Indeed, the official Ford report showed that the Mississippi court had considered and rejected MacPherson, making Ford more than an accidental failure to notice the modern approach. Hartigan still thought the prediction sound because the later dicta were sufficiently clear and the old rule sufficiently outdated. His warning was that determining when considered dicta can outweigh an earlier holding does not admit an easy general formula. (Complete concurrence.)

**The court vacated the district court's dismissal order and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion.** Mason could continue his suit without the categorical lack-of-privity bar the district court had applied. The manufacturer had not yet been found negligent, identified conclusively as the maker of the wheel, or ordered to pay damages. There was no dissent; Hartigan's writing concurred in the court's opinion, rather than merely its judgment. (Final majority paragraph and concurrence opening.)
