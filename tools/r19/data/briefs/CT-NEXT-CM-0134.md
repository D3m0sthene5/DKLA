# McKenna v. Vernon
258 Pa. 18, 101 A. 919 (1917) | Contracts

## Theatre construction and certificate requirements
On January 20, 1914, McKenna agreed to build a moving-picture theatre at 1526–28 Cumberland Street in Philadelphia for $7,750 under attached plans and specifications. Work was to proceed under an architect’s direction and payments required the architect’s certificates. The agreement provided payment for eighty percent of work in place as construction progressed and contemplated payment after completion. A March 24 enlargement agreement added $1,000 and extended the completion deadline from April 20 to May 11. The total agreed compensation thus became $8,750. 258 Pa. at 20–21.

Vernon paid $6,000 during construction. McKenna sued August 28 for the remaining $2,750 with interest from June 30. Vernon alleged inferior and substituted materials, defective workmanship requiring costly correction, and late completion, for which he demanded a $283.35 penalty offset. A jury awarded McKenna $2,500. Vernon appealed the refusals of a compulsory nonsuit, binding instructions, and judgment notwithstanding the verdict, along with evidentiary and charge objections. Id. at 19, 21.

## The owner’s argument and the architect’s evidence
The assignments centered on the asserted absence of any right to sue without a final architect’s certificate of compliance. Vernon treated that certificate as an unfulfilled condition requiring judgment in his favor. McKenna relied on the actual work, authorized changes, the architect’s satisfaction, and the owner’s conduct during construction. Id. at 19–22.

The architect testified that McKenna had substantially complied with the contractual requirements. Variations from the specifications were authorized and directed by him. He had not issued a completion certificate because none had been requested. The judicial account should be distinguished from the reported counsel argument suggesting an owner-requested withholding: the court’s operative factual explanation was the absence of a request. Vernon had observed the work almost daily without objecting to its performance or materials. Id. at 22.

## Repeated payment without certification supported waiver
The court found no special distinction in the contract between the certificate attached to final payment and certificates attached to progress payments. All required certificates, but six of the seven payments were made without even asking for one. The provision protected the owner; his constant and repeated disregard of it prevented reliance on the missing certificate as a complete defense to payment for honestly and substantially performed work. Id. at 21–22.

Waiver was therefore inferred from the actual course of payment, reinforced by the architect’s substantive approval and the owner’s continuing observation without protest. The court did not treat the certificate as irrelevant in every building contract or say that every isolated accommodation waives all future conditions. Its reasoning addressed repeated disregard of the same condition throughout this particular performance and an attempt to insist on it only at the final balance. Id. at 22.

## Disposition and source scope
The Supreme Court held that nonsuit and binding instructions were properly refused; on the case’s stated theory, the remaining assignments failed for the same reason. It affirmed the $2,500 judgment. Stewart wrote; Brown, Mestrezat, Stewart, Frazer, and Walling are listed before the case, with no separate opinion or numerical division reported. Id. at 19, 22.
