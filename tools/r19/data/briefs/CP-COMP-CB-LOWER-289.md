# Shaw v. Toshiba America Information Systems, Inc.

**91 F. Supp. 2d 942 (E.D. Tex. 2000) — January 28, 2000 — Civil Procedure**

District Judge Heartfield certified an opt-out settlement class, approved the approximately $2.1 billion settlement package, and approved $147.5 million in class counsel’s fees. The decision also approved expenses, representative payments, and compensation for objectors’ counsel, while revoking one objector’s lawyer’s admission for inappropriate conduct. This is the final-settlement decision, distinct from the earlier 1999 summary-judgment rulings discussed as procedural history. (942, 945–946, 973–976.)

## The alleged defect and the path to settlement

Ethan Shaw and Clive D. Moon filed suit on March 5, 1999, initially against Toshiba America Information Systems and NEC Electronics; additional Toshiba and NEC entities were later added. They alleged defective microcode in floppy-diskette controllers. The controller was supposed to signal overruns or underruns during a read or write operation. The alleged condition prevented a signal when the problem occurred only on the last byte of a sector, potentially causing undetected data loss, corruption, or system failure. Toshiba denied that its laptops were defective, that a user had suffered data loss from the controller, and that plaintiffs were entitled to relief. The settlement imposed no admission of liability. (945–946 & nn.2–3, 977–978 findings 10–25, 980 finding 36.)

The claims invoked the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1030, and warranty law. After extensive briefing, the court denied partial-summary-judgment motions in August 1999 and ordered mediation. Discovery continued alongside the mediation: millions of pages, Japanese-language records requiring translation, extensive electronic information, and depositions under an accelerated schedule. The parties announced settlement on October 28. The court conditionally certified a class, preliminarily approved the agreement, protected its jurisdiction against competing litigation, and set a January 19, 2000 fairness hearing. The present opinion followed that hearing. These steps supplied an informed basis for compromise, not a final trial determination of defect or liability. (945–946, 960, 969, 980, 987 findings 95–98.)

## Class boundaries and the benefits provided

The settlement covered qualifying United States citizens or residents, including businesses, governments, and other legal entities, who owned or leased Toshiba laptop or notebook computers. Findings 26–27 excluded Librettos, which lacked internal floppy drives, and computers acquired after November 8, 1999 bearing serial numbers ending in “U,” indicating the hardware fix. The owners collectively held about five million units, about three million under manufacturer warranty. Lease terms determined whether a lessor or lessee received benefits; disputed entitlement went to the claims administrator and court. Serial numbers supplied objective membership and warranty information, without requiring prior warranty registration. (953, 978–979 findings 26–31.)

Relief depended on purchase timing and warranty status. Qualifying units purchased new on or after March 5, 1998 generated cash payments varying with purchase date. Units under warranty on or after March 5, 1999 qualified for a software solution and a $225 Toshiba-product coupon. An owner dissatisfied with the patch could obtain a hardware solution or external-drive option and a $200 coupon, returning the $225 coupon or paying the $25 difference. Other members could obtain the patch and a $100 coupon; some members qualified for both cash and warranty relief. Toshiba also undertook to eliminate the condition in future production and maintain a free patch. The settlement reserved consequential-damage claims except damage to the physical floppy diskette itself. (979–981 findings 32, 38–41.)

The coupons were transferable, aggregatable, and usable on discounted as well as full-priced Toshiba products. Those features supported the court’s finding of economic value, but their Toshiba-only restriction distinguished them from cash and reduced their value somewhat. Objectors helped extend redemption from six months to one year. The opinion did not treat the whole package as an immediate cash distribution to each owner. (961, 983–984 findings 66–68.)

## Why the court certified the class

Settlement did not dispense with Rule 23’s certification requirements. Applying the then-governing framework and *Amchem*, the court examined numerosity, commonality, typicality, adequacy, predominance, and superiority. The main certification was under Rule 23(b)(3). The court additionally said the injunctive and warranty claims, with Rule 23(a) satisfied, were sufficient for (b)(2) treatment. (951–959 & n.13.)

The nationwide owners of millions of computers were too numerous for practical joinder. Whether the same controller problem existed, violated federal law, or breached materially similar warranties presented common questions; Shaw and Moon invoked the same legal and remedial theories as absent owners. They had participated actively and had no conflicting interests, while experienced counsel had prosecuted the case vigorously. (954–955.)

Predominance rested on the single alleged source of economic injury and common liability questions. Individual damages could vary without defeating common treatment. The court found relevant warranty principles sufficiently uniform across states and distinguished latent-injury, personal-injury, and choice-of-law difficulties in *Amchem* and *Castano*. Consequential-damage claims were reserved, avoiding future-injury conflicts in this settlement. Its reasoning was the court’s historical application to this record, not a declaration that every defective-product claim automatically qualifies for class treatment. (955–958.)

Many owners had claims worth less than the cost of individual litigation. Repeating technical discovery and expert testimony in thousands of suits would waste resources, whereas one forum could resolve common issues and avoid inconsistent outcomes. Those practical considerations supported superiority. (958–959, 984–985 findings 70, 76–78.)

## Settlement fairness and the residual charity

The court examined six fairness factors: fraud or collusion; complexity, expense, and duration; litigation progress and discovery; prospects of success; possible recovery; and the views of counsel, representatives, and members. It found arm’s-length bargaining after contested litigation and extensive discovery. Technical uncertainty, a relatively new statutory theory, possible limits to repair-and-replacement remedies, and difficulty collecting against Japanese assets supported compromise. Fewer than thirty objections were filed. Benefits varied by objective age and warranty criteria, which the court found did not create conflicting subclasses. (959–961.)

The distinction among valuation measures matters. The approximately $2.1 billion figure represented aggregate face-value benefits. For the fee analysis the court conservatively valued the common fund at $1 billion to $1.1 billion; it also discussed a $1.4 billion median net-present-value estimate. These figures should not be collapsed into one cash fund. Toshiba had to establish a $597.5 million cash fund for class claims. (972, 981 finding 42, 986 findings 81–83.)

Only cash remaining after class claims would go to a charity buying Toshiba computers for schools, churches, nonprofit organizations, libraries, hospitals, and poor recipients. No remainder would revert to Toshiba. The court connected this residual use to addressing the digital divide and improving computer skills in the labor force needed by class-member businesses. It rejected reversion’s incentive to discourage claims, extra fees’ corresponding disincentive, and additional payments that could create a windfall beyond computer value. The charity’s benefits followed satisfaction of claims, rather than replacing the promised individual remedies. (961, 981 finding 43, 984–985 findings 72–74, 78.)

## Fees, objections, and exact disposition

Rule 23 required the court to scrutinize fees as well as substantive relief. Although Heartfield favored the percentage method, he acknowledged uncertainty in Fifth Circuit practice and tested the request under lodestar/Johnson, percentage, and hybrid approaches. The first yielded a $140–160 million range; applying fifteen percent to the conservatively valued fund yielded $150–165 million, as did the hybrid method. The requested $147.5 million fee plus up to $3 million in litigation expenses totaled $150.5 million, within all three ranges. Each representative received $25,000. Toshiba’s fee payment was independent of the class cash fund, and the court also recognized reimbursement for post-notice advice and administration oversight under the agreement. (961–973 & n.16, 981 findings 44–46.)

Counsel whose objections helped double coupon validity received $6 million, payable by class counsel and Toshiba as agreed without reducing class benefits. In contrast, the court questioned documents submitted for purported objector Robert Demyanovich, including inconsistent contact and purchase information. It admonished Lawrence Schonbrun, revoked his pro hac vice admission, and imposed specified advance written-motion and verified-client-information requirements for a future appearance in the district. These rulings should not be generalized into rejection of all objections: the court considered them and acknowledged tangible improvements obtained by objectors. (973–975 & n.19.)

The court certified the class, found the settlement fair, adequate, and reasonable, found the fees fair, just, and reasonable, and formally approved the agreement. Its appended 126 findings were adopted, with associated conclusions and orders incorporated by reference; all reproduced findings were read. There is no panel vote or separate judicial writing. (975–976 & n.20, 992.)
