# Washington Mutual Finance Group, LLC v. Bailey
364 F.3d 260 (5th Cir. 2004) | Contracts

## Court and result
On March 19, 2004, the Fifth Circuit reversed the refusal to compel arbitration and remanded for entry of an order compelling arbitration for every party to the action. It also reversed dismissal of the finance company's federal action. Judge E. Grady Jolly wrote for the panel of Judges Garwood, Jolly, and Clement; no separate writing or dissent is published. The decision enforces the forum agreements and does not decide whether the underlying insurance practices were wrongful. (364 F.3d at 260, 262, 268.)

## Transactions and competing proceedings
Six borrowers—John Phinizee, Willie Curry, Beulah Tate, Violet Smith, John Bailey, and Helen Spellman—obtained loans from Washington Mutual Finance or its predecessors and bought associated insurance from four insurers. Each signed an agreement to arbitrate disputes with the lender. The borrowers and John Phinizee's wife, Miriah, later sued the lender and insurers in Mississippi state court, primarily alleging that they had been sold and charged for insurance they neither needed nor wanted. Those are allegations of the underlying lawsuit, not findings made by this appellate court. (Id. at 262.)

The lender responded with separate federal actions under FAA § 4 seeking arbitration and a stay of the state cases. The insurers intervened, and the federal district court consolidated the actions. It found the six borrowers illiterate and found that the lender had not specifically told them they were signing arbitration agreements. Treating that combination as procedural unconscionability, it denied the motion to compel and the insurers' summary-judgment motion, and granted the borrowers' motion to dismiss. It also refused to compel Miriah because she had not signed an arbitration agreement. (Id. at 262–263.)

The lender and insurers challenged both the Mississippi-law analysis and the handling of facts outside the pleadings without conversion to summary judgment or adequate discovery. They separately contended that ordinary contract principles bound Miriah despite her absent signature. Her claimed signatures on loan and insurance papers were disputed; the absence of her signature on the arbitration agreement was undisputed. (Id. at 262–263 & nn. 1–3.)

## Applicable law and the limited issue
The court reviewed the § 4 determination de novo. Its inquiry first asked whether the parties agreed to arbitrate and then whether a federal statute or policy made their claims nonarbitrable. No contrary federal statute or policy was asserted here. The FAA was to give arbitration agreements the force of other contracts, and both sides agreed that Mississippi contract law governed formation and validity. The principal issue was whether the arbitration agreements the six borrowers admittedly signed were enforceable. (Id. at 263–264.)

Mississippi recognized procedural and substantive unconscionability. The borrowers' challenge concerned the process of formation, rather than the content of the terms. The court identified knowledge, voluntariness, print, legal language, sophistication, bargaining power, and opportunity to study or inquire as relevant considerations. But there was no coercion allegation, and print, complexity, and relative bargaining power were not disputed issues in this appeal. The district court's grounds were illiteracy and the absence of specific oral disclosure. This matters: the opinion does not declare every procedural-unconscionability defense unavailable. (Id. at 264.)

## Illiteracy and disclosure
The Fifth Circuit concluded that Mississippi precedent did not make inability to read sufficient to defeat a signed agreement. State decisions charged signers with knowledge of the documents they executed and did not permit avoidance simply because they had neither read the document nor had it read to them. An illiterate signer consequently had to obtain assistance reading it; illiteracy did not shift that affirmative responsibility to the other contracting party. The court accordingly rejected both the illiteracy ground and the lender's failure to give a specific oral explanation after learning the borrowers could not read. (Id. at 264–265.)

This was a legal conclusion even assuming illiteracy established. Footnote 3 acknowledges that the district court appeared to use material outside the pleadings, but leaves the conversion-and-discovery objection unresolved because proof of illiteracy would not alter the enforceability conclusion. The court reversed dismissal because its premise of an unenforceable agreement had failed; it did not resolve the procedural objection independently. (Id. at 263 n. 3, 266.)

## Alleged fraudulent description
The borrowers also argued that they had informed the lender they could not read and had asked what they were signing. Describing the documents as insurance and finance papers, they argued, misrepresented the arbitration agreement and induced their signatures. The court gave two reasons for rejection. First, the record showed general inquiries about the package of documents and a general response that was not plainly misleading; it showed no question specifically directed to the arbitration agreement. Second, any inaccurate impression would have been corrected by compliance with the duty to read or obtain a reading. The arbitration agreement was a separate document with a prominent capitalized alternative-dispute-resolution heading, and none of these borrowers obtained a reading. (Id. at 265–266.)

Footnote 4 is an important limit on the lender's argument. The lender claimed fraudulent inducement belonged before an arbitrator, but the court distinguished a challenge to the entire contract from a defense directed specifically to the arbitration agreement. This particular challenge concerned making the arbitration agreement and was therefore one the federal court could consider. The court rejected the defense on its merits, rather than sending the formation question away unanswered. (Id. at 266 n. 4.)

## Miriah Phinizee and equitable estoppel
Miriah had obtained no loan or insurance herself. Whether she co-signed her husband's loan and insurance papers remained disputed, but all her claims depended on rights arising from those transactions. The court applied equitable estoppel: a claimant could not seek benefits from a transaction while disavowing its associated arbitration burden solely because she had not signed that agreement. This was not a rule making marriage alone sufficient to bind a spouse; the dependence of her claims on the contractual transactions supplied the reason. She did not respond to the estoppel arguments in her appellate brief. (Id. at 267–268 & nn. 5, 7.)

Footnote 6 expressly applies federal substantive arbitrability law to this nonsignatory question, while recognizing uncertainty about the boundary between federal and state law and an example applying state law. It reasoned that this estoppel question did not present a state-law issue of formation or validity. The court also said there was no reason to expect a different result under Mississippi law, although Mississippi courts had not expressly applied estoppel in this context. That is the opinion's 2004 choice-of-law reasoning; it should not be recast as a current certification that every nonsignatory issue is governed by federal law. (Id. at 267–268 n. 6.)
