# Haeger v. Goodyear Tire & Rubber Co.

**2018 WL 1182551 (D. Ariz. Mar. 7, 2018), No. CV-05-02046-PHX-GMS, Doc. 1186.** United States District Court for the District of Arizona; District Judge G. Murray Snow. Civil Procedure. Assigned account: CivPro.pdf, physical page 500, printed page 977, the remand discussion immediately before the printed 978 marker.

## Facts and procedural setting

The Haegers sued Goodyear in 2005 on a products-liability theory. The case settled in 2010. Almost a year later, information concerning Goodyear’s withholding of internal testing led to sanctions proceedings. The district court previously found a corporate strategy to conceal test results, including testimony by a corporate representative denying that relevant results existed. This March 2018 order accepts the earlier misconduct findings and addresses what remained after appellate proceedings; it does not retry the underlying products claim. (Order pp. 1–2.)

The plaintiffs requested reimbursement of their litigation expenses. Goodyear maintained that any compensatory sanction must cover expenses caused by the misconduct. The district court originally awarded essentially all the requested fees, reasoning that the misconduct was exceptionally serious and that a narrower causal calculation was difficult. That original approach, recounted as procedural history, is not the governing rule adopted in this remand order. (Pp. 2–3.)

Goodyear had examined the plaintiffs’ billing entries and assigned several objection codes, including one for a lack of causation. Its causation objections totaled $722,406.52 against a request approaching $2.8 million. The district court told the parties to address an alternative award confined to expenses directly attributable to sanctionable conduct. This contingent award would avoid additional proceedings if an appellate court required the narrower standard. The court ultimately deducted the entire amount of Goodyear’s identified causation objections. Goodyear subsequently argued on appeal that other entries also lacked the necessary connection. (Pp. 3–4.)

The Ninth Circuit upheld the broader award, but the Supreme Court required a compensatory, but-for causal connection between the misconduct and the shifted fees. It also left the district court to consider whether Goodyear had waived further challenges to the contingent award. If there was no waiver, a fresh causal assessment would be necessary. The March 2018 order therefore addresses client responsibility, the continuing role of the lawyers, and preservation of particular fee objections. (Pp. 4–5.)

## Positions and questions

Goodyear disputed responsibility for its attorneys’ acts and wanted another opportunity to challenge whether the remaining fees were caused by misconduct. The Haegers maintained that Goodyear was responsible for conduct undertaken on its behalf and that it had already received every specific causation deduction it requested below. The principal preservation question was whether maintaining the legal position that only incremental fees could be recovered also preserved objections to additional individual billing entries. (Pp. 6–10.)

The lawyers’ participation raised a different question. Plaintiffs had settled with Basil Musnuff and Fennemore Craig and later represented that they no longer sought further relief from those participants. The court had to determine whether continued participation by Musnuff, Graeme Hancock, and Fennemore Craig was needed once Goodyear itself was responsible. (Pp. 7–8.)

## Rules and reasoning

Applying Arizona agency principles, the court treated a principal as responsible for acts undertaken by its agents within the scope of their work and in furtherance of the principal’s business. The attorney-client relationship did not make Goodyear a passive stranger to the misconduct. Associate general counsel Ms. Okey retained final approval over discovery decisions, knew about the bad-faith conduct, and participated in decisions central to the concealment. Earlier findings established Goodyear’s direct involvement as well as the lawyers’ activity on its behalf. On these facts, the company was vicariously liable for the attorneys’ acts. The holding is tied to the agency relationship and the existing misconduct findings, rather than an unqualified proposition that every client necessarily bears every sanction imposed on counsel. (Pp. 6–7.)

The settlement and plaintiffs’ later representations also mattered. A September 2016 covenant resolved claims against Musnuff and Fennemore Craig while preserving claims against Goodyear. In July 2017, plaintiffs’ counsel stated that the law firms’ matters were resolved and their further participation was unnecessary. Once Goodyear was held responsible, the court excused Musnuff, Hancock, and Fennemore Craig from further participation. This was a ruling about these proceedings and their resolution; it did not announce immunity for attorneys generally. (Pp. 7–8.)

On waiver, the court distinguished two issues that Goodyear’s position tended to combine. One was the governing legal measure: incremental fees caused by wrongdoing, rather than all fees incurred after a particular point. The other was which specific billing entries failed that measure. Goodyear had preserved its legal argument, but that did not automatically preserve additional entry-specific objections it had never presented to the district court. (Pp. 8–9.)

Goodyear’s own detailed coding showed that it understood how to identify particular causation objections. The district court warned the parties that it would make an alternative award limited to directly caused expenses and invited their guidance. Goodyear supplied no additional causation objections at that point, and the contingent award granted all $722,406.52 of its existing objections. A reservation in its earlier briefing preserved the challenge to awarding all fees instead of incremental fees. It did not reserve a second opportunity to select more incremental fees for exclusion. (P. 9.)

The additional billing challenges appeared on appeal rather than in the district-court proceedings over the contingent award. The court applied the ordinary rule against considering arguments first raised on appeal and found the distinction emphasized by Judge M. Smith’s earlier dissent persuasive. Winning the legal standard in the Supreme Court did not entitle Goodyear to redo the individual objections it could have made under its own original understanding of that standard. Allowing that sequence would reward strategic withholding of objections followed by a claim of error after an unfavorable result. (Pp. 9–10.)

## Exact disposition and significance

The court concluded that Goodyear was vicariously liable for its attorneys’ acts and had waived additional causation objections to the contingent sanctions award. It then ordered the parties to address the **remaining amount** of the award against Goodyear and any other remaining issues, setting a March 13, 2018 status conference. This March 7 order did not itself enter a new final judgment fixing a fresh $2 million award. (P. 11.)

The assigned account accurately identifies waiver and vicarious responsibility as the remand determinations. Its description of an alternative $2 million award is shorthand for the sanctions history; the actual order preserves further proceedings concerning the remaining amount. The case illustrates why preserving a legal rule and preserving its application to particular items are separate procedural tasks. A party can obtain correction of the governing standard yet lose objections that it did not timely present under that very standard.
