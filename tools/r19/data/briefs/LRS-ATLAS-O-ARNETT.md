# Arnett v. Kennedy
416 U.S. 134 (1974) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Judgment and division of the Court

The Supreme Court reversed a three-judge district court judgment that invalidated the federal civil-service discharge scheme for lack of a prior trial-type hearing and for vagueness in regulating employees' speech. It remanded for further proceedings. Five Justices sustained the discharge procedures, while six rejected the facial vagueness and overbreadth challenge. The decision did not establish as a majority rule that Congress could attach whatever procedures it wished to a statutory entitlement. Rehnquist's theory that the benefit and its procedural limitations were inseparable had three votes; six Justices rejected that approach. (163–171, 185, 202–203, 206–212.)

Rehnquist announced the judgment, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Stewart. Powell, joined by Blackmun, concurred on the speech issue and in the procedural result through independent constitutional balancing. White agreed on the speech issue and accepted limited preliminary process followed by a full hearing, but would preserve Kennedy's reinstatement and backpay because the initial decisionmaker was the official Kennedy had allegedly slandered. Douglas dissented separately and joined Marshall's dissent with Brennan. Marshall would affirm both grounds of the district court's judgment. (136, 164, 171, 202–206.)

## Facts and competing accounts

Wayne Kennedy was a nonprobationary competitive-service employee working as a field representative in the Chicago Regional Office of the Office of Economic Opportunity. Regional Director Wendell Verduin issued a proposed adverse-action notice on February 18, 1972, charging misconduct in five incidents during November and December 1971. The central allegation was that Kennedy had publicly accused Verduin and an assistant of offering a community-action representative a $100,000 OEO grant in exchange for a statement against Kennedy and another employee. The notice alleged that Kennedy made that accusation without proof and with reckless disregard of available facts, harming the officials' authority and the office's functioning. The alleged offer, its description as a bribe, and Kennedy's supposed recklessness were disputed matters, not facts adjudicated by the Supreme Court. (137–140, 175–176, 196–197.)

Kennedy was advised that he could answer orally and in writing, submit affidavits, and inspect the supporting material; a copy of that material was attached to the notice. His lawyer responded by demanding an impartial trial-type proceeding with confrontation, witnesses, a record, and findings, and asserting that the charged conversations were protected speech. The response called the conversations inaccurately reported without supplying an alternative factual account. Kennedy did not personally appear to contest the charges. On March 20, Verduin sustained them and notified Kennedy that removal would take effect on March 27. He advised Kennedy of the right to appeal to OEO or the Civil Service Commission. (137–139 & n.2, 175–176 & n.5.)

Both sides submitted affidavits describing markedly different events. Because the district court granted Kennedy summary judgment, Rehnquist treated the material particulars in the government's notice as true for purposes of deciding the legal issues. The question was whether the procedures and removal standard would remain unconstitutional even on that version of the facts. The Court's reversal therefore should not be restated as a factual finding that Kennedy fabricated the bribery charge or that no protected criticism occurred. (139–140, 162–163.)

## Litigation and governing procedures

Kennedy sued on March 27, 1972, seeking declaratory and injunctive relief. The initial complaint sought a full hearing before removal and challenged retaliation for speech. A single district judge referred the procedural constitutional question to a three-judge court and dismissed the individual speech claim pending administrative exhaustion. Kennedy amended to assert a facial vagueness and overbreadth challenge, adding eighteen plaintiffs whose participation was largely nominal. Class certification was deferred. The three-judge court granted summary judgment, ordered reinstatement with backpay, required constitutionally sufficient proceedings for any future removal, and enjoined enforcement of the Act and rules as regulations of competitive employees' speech. Kennedy's Commission appeal was delayed by the litigation and then terminated following his court-ordered reinstatement. The Supreme Court decided the government's direct appeal on April 16, 1974. (134, 136 n.1, 138–139 & nn.3–4, 176–177.)

The Lloyd-La Follette Act, then codified at 5 U.S.C. § 7501, permitted removal or unpaid suspension only for cause promoting service efficiency. It required written charges and reasons, reasonable time for a written answer with affidavits, and an early written decision, but left examination of witnesses or a hearing to the removing official's discretion. Regulations enlarged those protections: at least thirty days' advance notice, detailed reasons and access to supporting evidence, oral and written response, notice of which charges were sustained, and appeal rights. An oral response was an opportunity to persuade, not a formal trial. (140–145 & nn.5–13.)

A full evidentiary hearing was available on appeal through the agency or Commission, including testimony under oath and cross-examination. The lengthy reproduced regulations addressed participation, witnesses, disclosure of accepted documents, transcripts or agreed summaries, and protections against interference with witnesses. Agency and Commission appeals were not processed concurrently; a direct Commission appeal waived agency appeal, whereas an agency appeal could be followed by Commission review. OEO typically held its trial-type hearing after actual removal. A successfully reinstated employee received backpay, less interim earnings. These protections arose from the statutory and regulatory scheme together, including the Veterans' Preference Act and executive extension of its protections, rather than solely from the original Act's text. (144–148 & nn.12–16.)

## Rehnquist plurality: the benefit and its statutory limits

Rehnquist traced civil-service protections from patronage through the Pendleton Act, McKinley's just-cause rule, and the 1912 Lloyd-La Follette legislation. Congress had expanded employee security while deliberately avoiding a mandatory elaborate removal trial. Applying Roth's proposition that independent law defines property interests, he accepted a statutory expectancy of continued employment for cause but regarded its dimensions as including Congress's prescribed method for determining cause. Separating substantive tenure from those procedures would give the employee a right Congress expressly withheld in the same enactment. (148–154 & nn.17–20.)

He invoked decisions disfavoring retention of a statute's benefits while attacking vital conditions, acknowledging that this principle had been unevenly applied. It added weight to his conclusion that an employee must accept the limited statutory enforcement mechanism with the benefit it protected. The resulting procedural entitlement did not require a full adversary hearing before discharge. He distinguished welfare termination, garnishment, replevin, and driver's-license cases both by the different interests at stake and by the procedural limitations he considered part of this employment interest. (152–155.)

In a separate liberty analysis, Rehnquist accepted that unsupported dishonesty charges accompanying discharge might wrongfully damage reputation. The purpose of the relevant hearing was to permit name clearing. A post-removal administrative appeal could provide that opportunity, and the cited delays, including more than three months in many appeals, did not independently invalidate it. His property analysis made it unnecessary to decide the constitutional dimension of Verduin's personal involvement. In note 21 he rejected White's statutory neutrality requirement: supervisors ordinarily participated in employment conflicts, an agency head's involvement could require an outside official, and Congress intended relatively simple procedures. (155–158 & nn.21–23.)

## Powell and Blackmun: constitutional process independent of legislative grace

Powell rejected the plurality's premise. A nonprobationary employee dischargeable only for cause had a legitimate claim of entitlement under Roth and Sindermann. The legislature could choose whether to confer such an interest, but could not, once it did so, define away constitutionally necessary safeguards. Otherwise it could authorize deprivation of any statutory property without notice or a hearing. Procedural due process was a constitutional guarantee whose adequacy had to be judged independently of the legislature's chosen mechanism. (164–167 & nn.1–3.)

Powell nevertheless sustained the procedures through balancing. Government needed to maintain employee efficiency, discipline, and workplace functioning, and to remove disruptive employees promptly. A prior trial would impose expense, delay, and disincentives to justified discharge. The employee faced a serious interruption of income, but reinstatement and backpay were available, and he might have independent resources, private employment, or welfare. Powell distinguished that situation from the immediate subsistence threat in Goldberg. He regarded thirty days' notice, access to the evidence, oral and written response, affidavits, and a later full hearing as reducing initial error sufficiently to make the accommodation reasonable. His notes rejected paid suspension as an adequate answer to all government costs and deterrence concerns. (167–170 & n.4.)

He also rejected White's requirement of an impartial initial decisionmaker, finding neither a statutory mandate nor a constitutional necessity at that stage. An impartial official at the full post-removal hearing was sufficient; the supervisor often knew the relevant facts best, and educating a new initial decisionmaker could turn removal into a preliminary trial. His final note applied the same balance to the liberty claim. (170–171 & nn.5–6.)

## White: preliminary screening, but not by this accused supervisor

White agreed with Powell that substantive property law and constitutionally adequate procedures were distinct. His extended analysis traced hearings in private-property takings, professional licensing, judicial attachments, public employment, and security-related adjudication. Some contexts allowed an immediate preliminary deprivation to avoid destruction, dissipation, or public danger, but still required a hearing before deprivation became final. Public origin of the interest was no reason to eliminate that safeguard. For-cause employment differed from employment terminable at pleasure. He counted six Justices rejecting Rehnquist's inseparability theory and noted that the older Bailey approach resting on rights versus privileges had been undermined. (177–186 & nn.6–7.)

Timing required a separate balance. White considered risk of initial error, effects of temporary loss, delay before final review, danger from advance notice, and uncompensated losses to the government or an opposing party. Studies showed significant reversal rates and often lengthy personnel appeals. The employee could not abscond with his job; paid suspension could protect the workplace, although government incurred costs. White concluded that the Act's advance notice and opportunity for written presentation met minimum preliminary requirements. A full trial with cross-examination need not occur then: the initial proceeding screened probable cause, while the appeal resolved the facts finally. Goldberg's unusually severe deprivation justified more exacting initial procedures there; public employees had at least possible alternative support. (186–196 & nn.8–11, 200–202.)

His decisive objection concerned Verduin. The regional director's own reputation was at issue in the alleged slander, yet he assembled the charges and decided to discharge their author. White did not demand that every supervisor recuse merely because of ordinary workplace disagreements. This direct personal involvement made the risk and appearance of bias intolerable. Through constitutional avoidance he would construe the statute, which was silent on neutrality, to require transfer of the initial decision to a qualified impartial official in these circumstances. He would affirm reinstatement and backpay on that narrower basis while reversing the facial speech injunction. That was his separate position, rejected by the five who sustained the procedural result. (196–200 & nn.12–13, 202–203.)

## Facial speech challenge and dissents

Rehnquist's speech analysis, accepted by Powell, Blackmun, and White, sustained the service-efficiency standard against vagueness and overbreadth. It was a general employment protection applicable to many different occupations, not a criminal code. Congress did not have to choose between exhaustive specification and no tenure protection. Existing employment principles, the personnel manual, and access to agency counsel supplied context. Pickering recognized that government as employer had interests in employee speech different from its authority over citizens generally. The plurality construed cause to exclude constitutionally protected speech while reaching speech improperly impairing the employing agency's efficiency and reputation. On the government's assumed factual version, satisfactory proof of the reckless defamatory accusations could justify removal; the facial challenge did not establish Kennedy's individual speech defense. (158–163 & nn.24–25.)

Douglas's separate dissent rejected balancing away speech on a matter of public concern merely because it offended supervisors or disrupted harmony. He warned that bureaucratic evaluation could reward obedience and internal conformity rather than useful work. His notes addressed judicial recusal after personal attacks, the distinction between institutional compliance and actual performance, and impermissible employment discrimination. He viewed Kennedy as punished for speaking about a public issue, and also joined Marshall's fuller constitutional objections. (203–206 & nn.1–3.)

Marshall, joined by Douglas and Brennan, would affirm both district court grounds. He regarded continued employment, retirement rights, salary progression, and insurance as substantial property; removal could also stigmatize the employee and obstruct other work. Disputed testimonial accusations required confrontation, cross-examination, defense witnesses, and an independent decisionmaker. A hearing months later could not prevent hardship while income stopped. He cited significant reversal rates, delays, Kennedy's debts and lost insurance, and his resort to public assistance despite a $16,000 salary. Future backpay could not undo lost housing, education, or family stability. Welfare eligibility was neither immediate nor guaranteed, and could require exhausting assets. (206–223 & nn.1–23.)

Marshall challenged the asserted efficiency necessity. Nine agencies already afforded prior evidentiary hearings; an Administrative Conference study recommended them and suggested scheduling rather than hearing length caused much delay. A hearing could occur within the existing thirty-day notice period, and administrative leave or reassignment could contain genuine disruption. A single adequate earlier hearing might reduce later proceedings. Verduin's combined roles illustrated why an informal right of reply was inadequate. (224–227 & nn.24–29.)

On speech, Marshall argued that the broad efficiency formula could deter truthful exposure of government misconduct, conduct the legislation was partly intended to protect. Merely declaring protected speech exempt did not give employees usable guidance or cure chilling effects on people other than the litigant. His final notes proposed more definite distinctions based on official versus private speech, truth and reasonable belief, legitimate public concern, and confidential information, drawing on existing agency precedent. He would sustain the facial vagueness and overbreadth ruling. (227–231 & nn.30–32.)
