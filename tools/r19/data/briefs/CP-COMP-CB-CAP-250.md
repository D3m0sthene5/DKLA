# Williams v. Holland

**Court of Common Pleas, June 12, 1833 — 10 Bing. 112; assigned parallel citation 131 Eng. Rep. 848.** Civil Procedure. Assigned: CivPro PDF physical page 303, printed 570–571, the Williams account and quotation. Pinpoints below refer to the original Bingham report, printed 112–118, in the verified 1834 volume X.

Williams permits an action on the case for an injury caused by the defendant’s negligence even when the defendant’s own act immediately produces the collision. Tindal, C.J., distinguished a decision allowing trespass from a decision requiring trespass exclusively. Because the jury had found negligence as the plaintiff alleged, and precedent did not forbid case in that situation, the court discharged the defendant’s rule to replace the plaintiff’s verdict with a nonsuit. The £12 verdict consequently stood. (115–118.)

## Facts and procedural setting

The plaintiff possessed a horse and cart in which his son and servant, John Williams, and his infant daughter, Mary Ann, were riding. The declaration alleged that the defendant controlled and drove a gig and horse carelessly and improperly into the plaintiff’s cart, breaking the cart, injuring the children, depriving the plaintiff of his son’s services, and occasioning medical expenses. The defendant pleaded not guilty. These were the pleaded bases of the plaintiff’s claim; the later jury finding established negligence rather than a willful assault. (112–113, 115.)

At trial before Tindal, the cart was standing beside a road twenty-four feet wide, with its near wheel on the footway, when a gig racing another gig collided with it. There is an internal error in the original report: the narrative at 113 identifies the racing driver as the “Plaintiff,” although the declaration, the runaway-horse defense, and Tindal’s judicial account at 115 consistently treat the defendant as the allegedly negligent gig driver. The original image itself contains that word; it is not merely an OCR substitution. The court’s actual analysis supplies the relevant attribution. (113, 115.)

The defendant argued that his horse had run away with him. The trial judge submitted whether the collision resulted from accident or from the defendant’s negligence and carelessness. The jury chose negligence and returned a verdict for the plaintiff with £12 damages. The defendant also objected that the plaintiff had selected case when the immediate impact required trespass. Tindal reserved that legal question, and Bompas obtained a rule nisi to set aside the verdict and enter a nonsuit. The later hearing therefore concerned the validity of the form of action after the jury’s finding, rather than a new determination of which driver had been careless. (113–115.)

## Competing positions

Jones, resisting the rule for the plaintiff, proposed three categories: an immediate and willful injury required trespass; an immediate injury caused by negligence could support trespass or case; and an indirect injury required case. His collected authorities included Day, Ogle, Leame, and Moreton. Bompas argued for a stricter distinction: whenever the complained-of act was immediate, trespass was the sole remedy, whether the conduct was willful or negligent. The disagreement was therefore about exclusivity, not whether trespass could sometimes address negligent direct injury. (114–115.)

## Holding and reasoning

Tindal began with the congruence between pleading and proof. The declaration made negligence in driving the defendant’s own gig the ground of action, and the jury found injury resulting from that negligence. Negligence was properly a subject of case. To displace that otherwise appropriate remedy, the defendant had to identify a positive rule or sufficiently controlling authority barring it. The court did not dismiss forms of action as meaningless technicalities: Tindal expressly recognized that settled forms adapted to different grievances contributed to certainty in administering justice. But examination of the cited decisions did not establish the asserted prohibition. (115–116.)

Leame v. Bray was the defendant’s principal authority because it involved a similar highway collision. Tindal explained that Leame established that trespass could be maintained; it did not establish that case could not. That distinction prevents a permissible remedy from becoming an exclusive remedy without supporting reasoning. Savignac v. Roome, involving a servant’s willful driving, depended instead on the proposition that a master could not be sued for that willful act of the servant. It did not resolve the present claim based on the defendant’s own negligence. (116.)

Day v. Edwards required further procedural differentiation. Day arose on a special demurrer to language describing furious, negligent, and improper driving. Its court could consider the legal construction of the declaration. Williams arose after a jury had affirmatively found negligence as the ground of injury. Tindal consequently treated the pleading decision as differently situated, rather than silently overruling it. Rogers v. Imbleton and Ogle v. Barnes, meanwhile, supported the availability of case in the circumstances before the court. (116–117.)

Moreton v. Hardern supplied the decisive rule: when carelessness and negligence cause an injury, the plaintiff may make that negligence the ground of an action on the case even if the defendant’s immediate act caused the harm. The defendant attempted to confine Moreton to the need to sue present and absent joint proprietors together, because trespass could not reach the absent proprietors. Tindal rejected that reading as both inconsistent with Moreton’s reasoning and unsound. The form of action against the proprietor who was present should not change simply because another proprietor’s servant happened to be on the box. The court adopted the rule allowing case for negligent immediate injury, while expressly retaining its qualification that the act must not be willful. (117–118.)

## Judgment, assigned treatment, and limits

The reported disposition is **“Rule discharged.”** The court denied the request to set aside the £12 plaintiff’s verdict and enter a nonsuit. Tindal delivered the reported judicial opinion; the source supplies no separate opinion or numerical vote. Counsel’s arguments and the report’s twenty lettered references were read in full, including the additional Leame reference at 115. (114–118.)

The assignment presents Williams as settling the availability of case for negligent immediate injury, and the full opinion supports that formulation with the express willfulness limitation. Its quotation reads “jury [sic] was so occasioned”; the actual continuation at 116 says “injury.” That is an assigned transcription difference, separate from the original report’s mistaken party designation at 113. The assignment’s compressed quotation also omits much of the reasoning distinguishing procedural posture and joint proprietors. Both the entire assigned page and the complete original judicial report were compared.
