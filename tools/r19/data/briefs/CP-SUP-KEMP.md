# Kemp v. United States
596 U.S. 528 (2022) | Civil Procedure

## The two deadlines that produced the dispute
Dexter Kemp received a 420-month sentence after a federal jury convicted him of drug and gun offenses in 2011. He and seven codefendants appealed, and the Eleventh Circuit affirmed their convictions and sentences in November 2013. Kemp neither sought rehearing nor petitioned the Supreme Court for certiorari. Two codefendants did seek rehearing, which the Eleventh Circuit denied in May 2014. That difference became important because the deadline for a motion under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 ordinarily runs for one year from finality of the conviction, and finality for a defendant who does not seek certiorari ordinarily occurs when the time for seeking it expires. 596 U.S. at 530–531.

Kemp filed his § 2255 motion in April 2015. The District Court treated his conviction as final in February 2014, ninety days after the original appellate judgment, making the motion more than two months late. It dismissed the motion in September 2016. Kemp did not appeal that dismissal. In June 2018, he instead asked the court to reopen the § 2255 proceedings under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 60(b)(6). He argued that Supreme Court Rule 13.3 postponed the certiorari clock until all parties' rehearing petitions had been resolved. Counting ninety days from the May 2014 denial would make his conviction final in August 2014 and his April 2015 motion timely. Id. at 531–532.

The District Court rejected that argument and alternatively held the reopening motion untimely. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed on the second ground. Although it thought the original § 2255 motion appeared timely, the asserted error was a judicial mistake of law falling under Rule 60(b)(1). The June 2018 motion came almost two years after the September 2016 dismissal, beyond that provision's one-year maximum. Thus Kemp's problem was not merely whether his original collateral attack had been timely; he also needed a timely procedural means of reopening the judgment dismissing it. Id. at 532.

## The question presented and the competing readings
Rule 60(b)(1) authorizes relief for mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Rule 60(c)(1) requires a motion under that provision within a reasonable time and no more than one year after entry of the judgment or order. Rule 60(b)(6), the residual ground for other reasons justifying relief, has the reasonable-time requirement without that additional one-year ceiling. But subsection (6) is available only when subsections (1) through (5) do not apply, and it requires extraordinary circumstances. A litigant cannot choose the residual provision simply because the deadline for the applicable specific provision has passed. Id. at 533.

The Court granted review to resolve a circuit disagreement about whether mistake under Rule 60(b)(1) includes a judge's error of law. Kemp argued that it covered only factual errors by someone other than the judge, leaving his challenge within subsection (6). The Government defended application of subsection (1), but proposed a narrower rule covering only obvious judicial errors, such as a failure to apply unambiguous law to established facts. The Court rejected both restrictions: judicial mistakes of law fall within subsection (1) whether or not they are obvious. Id. at 532–535 & n.1.

## Thomas's reasoning from text and amendment history
The unqualified word mistake did not distinguish law from fact. Dictionaries contemporaneous with the Rule's 1938 adoption and 1946 revision included errors of judgment and misunderstandings, while legal usage expressly included mistakes of law and fact. The drafters knew how to specify a mistake of fact if they wanted that narrower category. They did not do so. Id. at 534.

The amendment history also answered whose error the provision covered. The original Rule referred to a party's mistake through the word “his.” The 1946 amendment removed that limitation. For the majority, deleting the party-specific qualifier opened the provision to judicial mistakes as well. That textual change, together with the ordinary and legal meanings of mistake, made Kemp's attempted restriction untenable. Id. at 535.

The Government's obvious-error restriction likewise lacked textual support. Historical equity practice did not establish that the modern Rule incorporated that limitation. The proposed distinction would also require courts to decide not only whether a legal ruling was mistaken but how apparent its error was, creating an additional line-drawing problem the Rule did not require. Id. at 535–536.

## Why the neighboring provisions did not justify a narrower rule
Kemp invoked the surrounding grounds in subsection (1), arguing that inadvertence, surprise, and excusable neglect concerned nonjudicial, nonlegal mistakes. The Court answered that excusable neglect had long included misunderstandings of legal rules, and courts had also recognized judicial inadvertence. The neighboring terms therefore did not support the limitation Kemp sought. Id. at 536.

Nor did possible overlap with other parts of Rule 60 decide the case. Rule 60(a) addresses clerical mistakes and certain omissions, while subsection (b)(1) uses a broader term. Some overlap would exist even if subsection (b)(1) covered only factual errors. The same response applied to Rule 60(b)(4) and (5): factual as well as legal errors might implicate those provisions. Courts could use established distinctions and ordinary interpretive principles, including the preference for the specific over the general, if a genuine conflict arose. The Court did not announce that every claim invoking those other provisions must be forced into subsection (1). Id. at 537.

Kemp also warned that litigants could evade the shorter deadlines for Rule 59(e) motions or appeals by recasting ordinary legal challenges as Rule 60 motions. His own reading would allow similar attempts through subsection (6), so the concern did not establish a different meaning of mistake. More practically, the one-year ceiling is not an entitlement to wait one year: every Rule 60(b) motion must still be filed within a reasonable time. The Court noted appellate decisions rejecting delayed challenges that should have been raised sooner, but did not define reasonable time in this case. Id. at 537–538.

Finally, the Court rejected the claim that the borrowed state procedural language carried a settled meaning excluding legal errors. State interpretations had differed, and an early federal treatise understood mistake to include legal error. The history of replacing bills of review did not reserve all judicial legal errors for subsection (6); removal of the party-specific qualifier explained why subsection (1) could now address them. Id. at 538–539.

## Separate writings and the boundary of the holding
Justice Sotomayor joined the Court and separately emphasized two limits. Existing decisions still permit subsection (6) relief in extraordinary circumstances, including changes in controlling law. And the Court had not changed the established, fact-dependent reasonable-time requirement. Her concurrence therefore distinguished correction of an error in applying existing law from the different problem of a later change in law. Id. at 540.

The majority's footnote 2 expressly reserved whether a decision rendered erroneous by subsequent legal or factual developments also qualifies as a mistake under subsection (1). That reservation matters: the case concerns alleged misapplication of controlling law to facts already in the record, and does not resolve every possible relationship between subsections (1) and (6). Id. at 535 n.2. The assigned supplement relocates this judicial footnote to its appended notes.

Justice Gorsuch dissented. He would dismiss certiorari as improvidently granted, stressing the unusual conjunction of circumstances necessary for the choice between subsections to matter. He criticized the Court for adopting the all-judicial-errors position that neither party advocated and relying heavily on the deleted pronoun. In his view, balancing finality against error correction should proceed through the Rules Enabling Act's rulemaking process, where the experience of judges and practitioners could inform the policy choice. He did not supply a controlling alternative holding granting Kemp relief. Id. at 540–542.

## Judgment and procedural significance
Justice Thomas wrote for eight Justices: Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Breyer, Alito, Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh, and Barrett joined him. Gorsuch alone dissented. The Court affirmed the Eleventh Circuit. Kemp's motion alleged a judicial mistake of law within Rule 60(b)(1), and its filing more than one year after the challenged dismissal made it untimely. The Court did not reopen the § 2255 proceeding or decide the merits of Kemp's challenge to his conviction. Id. at 530, 539.

The case makes identifying the ground for postjudgment relief consequential. Calling a motion a subsection (6) motion does not remove the deadline attached to the mistake actually alleged. At the same time, recognizing legal mistakes under subsection (1) does not automatically establish entitlement to reopening: the applicable deadline, the reasonable-time requirement, and the grounds for relief still must be satisfied.
