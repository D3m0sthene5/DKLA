# Bernstein v. Meech
130 N.Y. 354, 29 N.E. 255 (1891) | Contracts

## Contract, correspondence, and performance

On August 4, 1887, Bernstein agreed to supply the Jarbeau Comedy Company for four performances at Buffalo’s Academy of Music on December 22–24. Meech and the other defendants agreed to furnish the theater; Bernstein would receive half the gross receipts. Eight days later Bernstein sent a proposed replacement contract demanding sixty percent and wrote that he could not think of playing for less. On August 13 the defendants returned that paper unsigned, explaining that they already had his signed contract and needed no other agreement for the appearances. This reply, rather than the proposed increase standing alone, became central to the case. (130 N.Y. at 356–357.)

On November 17 Bernstein’s agent sent advertising clippings from Chicago and asked the defendants to publicize Miss Jarbeau’s engagement, or supply newspaper contacts so he could arrange publicity himself. The November 28 reply expressed surprise: the defendants said they had understood Bernstein’s August letter to mean that the company would not come and could no longer arrange the dates. That letter was forwarded to Buffalo and reached the agent only on December 17 or 18, when he arrived to prepare advertising and make arrangements. Bernstein and his company then reached Buffalo in time and were ready to perform. The defendants had engaged the James Brown Potter Company for the same dates and refused them the theater. The appellate opinion describes these preparations and readiness as supported by the evidence; this was not a case in which Bernstein merely abandoned the engagement after demanding more money. (Id. at 357, 359.)

## Proceedings and competing positions

Bernstein sued for breach and obtained a jury verdict. The trial court submitted whether the defendants had been relieved of their contractual obligation and refused to direct a verdict for them. The General Term of the Supreme Court in the Fifth Judicial Department affirmed, in an order dated October 1, 1889. The defendants appealed to the Court of Appeals. Bradley wrote its December 22, 1891 opinion. All concurred except Potter, who did not vote; the report contains no separate writing. (Id. at 354–355, 358, 360.)

The defendants argued that Bernstein’s insistence on sixty percent was an unequivocal refusal to perform, releasing them and requiring them to find another use for the theater to avoid loss. They also argued that his company salaries, printing, hotel expenses, and other proved expenditures were not recoverable contract damages. Their reported argument separately challenged an instruction allowing a reasonable hotel bill rather than the bill actually incurred. Bernstein maintained that the contract and defendants’ breach had been established and that the trial’s damages approach fit the evidence. The published judicial reasoning does not itemize the expenses or state a dollar verdict, so the counsel’s list should not be treated as the court’s determination of every individual item. (Id. at 355–356, 359–360.)

## Keeping the contract alive binds both sides

The court recognized the general principle that an executory contract’s renunciation may allow the other party to act on a breach before performance becomes due. But it rejected the defendants’ claim that Bernstein’s letter necessarily ended this contract as a matter of law. Whether the correspondence and the parties’ understanding justified treating it as a renunciation was properly placed before the jury. The defendants themselves had requested submission of that issue, and the court found that the instructions already given covered it adequately; refusing additional instructions therefore was not error. (Id. at 358.)

More decisively, even if the defendants initially could have treated Bernstein’s letter as ending the agreement, their answer elected to preserve it. By rejecting the replacement paper because an existing signed contract already governed the engagement, they retained their contractual rights rather than accepting a termination. That election operated on both parties. Bernstein remained liable if he failed to perform, and the defendants equally remained obliged to furnish the theater when performance came due. They could not preserve a right to insist on his promised performance while treating their own corresponding duty as discharged. Consequently, the trial court properly refused a directed verdict for the defendants. (Id. at 357–359.)

The holding depends on that affirmative response to the proposed change, together with the correspondence and trial evidence. It does not declare that every refusal to accept changed terms necessarily prevents reliance on a repudiation in every setting. Here the defendants expressly said the original agreement was sufficient, and Bernstein subsequently made preparations and tendered the company’s performance on that basis. (Id. at 358–359.)

## Proven preparation expenses despite uncertain profits

The ordinary recovery would be the contract’s value to Bernstein: the profits remaining from his fifty-percent share of receipts after performance expenses. But the receipts that the four performances would have generated were speculative and could not be established by probative evidence. Those profits therefore were not recoverable. The defendants argued that this left only nominal damages. The court rejected that conclusion because it ignored a separately proved loss caused by their breach. Bernstein’s agent had prepared for the engagement, his troupe arrived ready to perform, and the expenses incurred for that performance were established by evidence. (Id. at 359.)

The jury could find that the expenditures supporting its recovery were legitimately incurred for performance, were within the parties’ contemplation when they contracted, and became a loss through the theater’s refusal to admit the company. The court would not assume that Bernstein would have lost those amounts even had the defendants performed. It thus allowed the expenses legitimately and essentially incurred for the contemplated performance, although the expected profit itself could not be proved. The reasoning does not award speculative profits under an expenses label, and it does not dispense with proof that the expenditures constituted a performance-related loss caused by the breach. (Id. at 359–360.)

## Judgment and assigned account

The Court of Appeals affirmed the judgment for Bernstein. It did not order a new trial or direct a newly quantified award. The textbook’s “Jarbeau’s Case” problem presents the demand for a larger percentage and asks about the performance expenses; the complete opinion supplies the critical August 13 election, the agent’s later communications, the jury posture, and the basis for affirmance. A dollar remittitur appearing above Bernstein’s caption on the original report’s first page belongs to the preceding case and is not Bernstein’s award. (Id. at 354–355, 360.)
