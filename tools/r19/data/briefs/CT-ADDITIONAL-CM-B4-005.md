# Simeone v. First Bank National Ass’n
73 F.3d 184 (8th Cir. 1996) | Contracts

## Court, transaction, and litigation history

Judge Ross wrote the Eighth Circuit’s January 3, 1996 opinion for a panel with Judges Beam and Murphy. The court reviewed damages after an earlier appeal had established breach. It affirmed in part, reversed a duplicative incidental award, and remanded; the result was not an unqualified affirmance of the original judgment. (73 F.3d 184, 186–187, 190–191.)

First Bank repossessed several unusually rare 1920s–1930s Mercedes-Benz cars and thousands of scarce parts from debtor Gohlike. One car and some parts were also claimed by the Quante estate, whose interest the bank did not acknowledge but agreed to buy for $50,000. On October 26, 1985, the bank contracted to sell its repossessed assets to collector Frederick Simeone for $400,000, with another $50,000 for the estate’s claimed car and parts in the same agreement. He paid a ten-percent deposit. The distinctive collection included a unique 1929 SS Roadster and a 1928 SSK previously owned by Arthur Conan Doyle’s son. (186.)

On the November 4 closing date, Gohlike obtained a temporary restraining order preventing the collateral sale. The bank refused Simeone’s tender. It negotiated instead with Gohlike and neighbor Torseth, agreeing to a slightly higher purchase price and dismissal of Gohlike’s $13 million due-process suit against the bank. Believing a contractual condition relieved it of the Simeone obligation, the bank sold to Torseth’s corporation, SMB. SMB ultimately resold the assets for $1,114,960, including $470,000 Simeone paid for the SS Roadster alone. The bank returned his deposit with interest. (186–187.)

The district court initially granted the sellers summary judgment for failure of a condition precedent. The earlier Eighth Circuit decision, 971 F.2d 103 (1992), vacated that ruling and held the bank and estate breached; it remanded other claims and damages. Before the 1994 trial, Simeone dismissed the estate with prejudice. The district court ruled that the bank’s conduct was not fraud but allowed that claim to reach the jury as a precaution against a later reversal necessitating another trial. The jury returned $1 on that dismissed claim and $2,405,000 contract damages: $585,000 compensatory, $225,000 incidental, and $1,595,000 consequential, plus prejudgment interest. The conditional fraud verdict must not be confused with an operative judicial finding of fraud. The court denied the bank’s new-trial, amendment, and remittitur requests. (187 & n. 1.)

## Market measure and foreseeable appreciation

Under Minnesota’s UCC § 2-713, seller-breach damages ordinarily use the difference between market price when the buyer learns of breach and contract price, with allowable incidental and consequential losses. The bank urged a market for repossessed goods in foreclosure sales, rather than collectible automobiles. The court accepted the collector market and expert valuation because these cars and parts were rare or unique; the UCC commentary permitted opinion evidence when scarcity prevented an ordinary market quotation. The jury valued the bank assets at $885,000 and estate assets at $150,000, a $1,035,000 total. Subtracting the $450,000 contract price produced the affirmed $585,000 award. (187–188 & n. 1.)

The consequential award reflected expert estimates of appreciation two years after breach, less the breach-time market value. Under § 2-715(2)(a), such recovery required losses arising from requirements the seller had reason to know when contracting and which could not reasonably be prevented. The bank argued Simeone described himself as a collector rather than a reseller, making this appreciation-related loss unforeseeable. But its officer knew collectors might trade to improve collections, Simeone testified that he disclosed possible trade or resale, and the quantity of parts supported an inference of resale or assembly into more valuable property. Foreseeability was a factual question, and the jury’s finding stood. (188.)

The expert testimony also supplied a reasonable basis for estimating the loss. It used knowledge of the collector market, scarcity, desirability, appreciation, and comparable prices. Minnesota barred speculative losses, but did not demand mathematical precision once loss and a reasonable basis for approximation were shown. The court therefore upheld the $1,595,000 consequential component. (189.)

## Cover did not require a financially unreasonable repurchase

The bank argued that Simeone should have bought the collection from SMB to mitigate. Reasonable cover depends on good faith and the circumstances when the buyer acts; hindsight that a cheaper or more effective method existed does not determine the issue. The seller bears the burden of proving unreasonable failure to avoid loss. An injured buyer need not take impractical measures, incur disproportionate expense, or act beyond available financial means. (188–189.)

The court rejected a mandatory repurchase of the whole collection for over $1 million, roughly $700,000 more than the original obligation as the opinion described it. The bank did not establish that Simeone had those resources or that such cover was reasonable. He had actually covered in part by paying $470,000 for one Roadster, more than the original combined $450,000 price for the collection. The holding concerns this record’s unreasonable expenditure demand, rather than a categorical rule against cover after a price increase. (189.)

## The incidental award and remaining issues

The $225,000 incidental award failed independently. Simeone proposed that it represented the $470,000 Roadster price less a $250,000 contractual allocation, plus $5,000 associated with dismissing SMB from litigation. The court distinguished a cover-price differential under § 2-712 from incidental charges, expenses, and commissions under § 2-715(1). To the extent the award compensated the increased purchase price, it duplicated compensation already provided through the compensatory and consequential awards. With no other evidence supporting incidental damages, the entire $225,000 award was reversed. The court did not reclassify and add it as another cover award. (189–190.)

First Bank remained responsible for the estate-claimed car. Its own contract expressly made disposition of all conveyed assets enforceable between bank and buyer, including that car, and placed the decision about being precluded from performance with the bank. The estate did not decide to reject Simeone’s tender or negotiate the substituted sale. Dismissing the estate therefore did not relieve the bank of its own promise. (190.)

Prejudgment interest remained available on the revised award. The court relied on the 1984 statutory amendment allowing interest despite difficulty in initially determining damages; it also considered the expert and bank market assessments adequate under the earlier ascertainability approach. Disagreement about exact value did not justify denying compensation for lost use of money. (190–191.)

## Disposition and source comparison

The court affirmed the $585,000 compensatory and $1,595,000 consequential components, reversed the $225,000 incidental component, and remanded for proceedings consistent with the opinion, with prejudgment interest on the revised damages. The surviving components total $2,180,000 before interest; that arithmetic is not a separately quoted final district-court judgment. No separate writing is reproduced. The earlier 1992 liability decision is procedural history, not an additional judgment represented as fully read for this brief. (188–191.)
