# Solite Corp. v. United States Environmental Protection Agency

952 F.2d 473 (D.C. Cir. 1991), rehearing denied Feb. 26, 1992 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, posture, and result

A unanimous panel of Ruth Bader Ginsburg, Douglas H. Ginsburg, and Randolph issued a per curiam opinion on December 31, 1991, resolving consolidated petitions for review of EPA’s September 1989 and January 1990 mineral-processing waste rules. The court upheld the rules principally, but required further agency proceedings concerning lightweight aggregate residuals and Du Pont’s chloride-ilmenite process. It also remanded the Bevill mixture rule and its treatment-permit corollary following the separate invalidation of the underlying Subtitle C mixture rule. There was no separate opinion. A February 26, 1992 order denied EPA’s rehearing petition concerning those latter remands. (476–477, 500.)

## Statutory setting and regulatory chronology

RCRA’s Subtitle C established a comprehensive hazardous-waste regulatory system. EPA’s 1978 proposal had contemplated a special category of large-volume, relatively low-hazard wastes potentially unsuitable for ordinary Subtitle C controls. Its May 1980 final rules abandoned that category. Congress then enacted the Bevill Amendment, temporarily withholding Subtitle C regulation of waste from the extraction, beneficiation, and processing of ores and minerals while requiring a detailed study, report to Congress, and ensuing regulatory determination. The exemption was temporary and accompanied by affirmative agency responsibilities. (477–479 & nn. 1–5.)

EPA initially accommodated industry through a broad, expressly provisional interpretation covering exploration, mining, milling, smelting, and refining. It missed the statutory study deadline. Litigation produced separate schedules for extraction/beneficiation wastes and processing wastes. In EDF II, an earlier circuit decision, the court rejected EPA’s continued overbroad processing interpretation and directed the agency to identify the high-volume, low-hazard special wastes intended by Congress. Solite concerns the rulemaking implementing that direction, rather than an unrestricted reconsideration of whether every mining-related waste should qualify. (478–480.)

An October 1988 proposal used a 50,000-metric-ton annual per-facility threshold. An April 1989 proposal separated solid and liquid streams, retained 50,000 tons for solids, proposed 1.5 million for liquids, and introduced low-hazard screening. The September 1989 final rule used newer survey data and selected thresholds of 45,000 tons for solids and one million for liquids. It also adopted toxicity/mobility and corrosivity screens. A further September proposal and January 1990 rule completed the designation of twenty processing wastes for continued temporary exemption. Denial of Bevill status did not itself make a waste subject to Subtitle C: it also had to possess a relevant hazardous characteristic. (480–483; 484 n. 7.)

Companies, trade associations, and Baltimore challenged these determinations. Their objections covered the agency’s statutory authority to narrow the exemption, the adequacy of notice and access to supporting data, methodological choices, and particular waste streams. The court applied the then-governing Chevron framework to statutory interpretation and arbitrary-and-capricious review to the agency’s explanations and technical choices. The statutory high-volume and low-hazard concepts lacked precise congressional measurements, leaving EPA room to adopt reasonable implementing standards consistent with EDF II. (481–483.)

## High-volume standards and the assigned disclosure rule

Petitioners argued that rigid thresholds improperly replaced the comprehensive, multifactor study Congress required. The court distinguished screening for entry into the Bevill category from the later study and regulatory determination for wastes actually within it. Congress had not required the agency to conduct the same multifactor inquiry twice. EPA reasonably used waste volumes already managed under Subtitle C as evidence of the technical feasibility of applying those controls. (483–484.)

The assigned passage quotes the court’s rule that meaningful notice includes access to technical studies and data supporting a proposal. APA § 553 requires a description of the proposed subjects and an opportunity to submit views; withholding the technical basis until that opportunity has passed can constitute serious procedural error. That proposition did not produce a wholesale defeat for EPA here. The court also recognized that supplementary data may expand or confirm an existing proposal and answer deficiencies without reopening comment, absent prejudice. (484–485.)

EPA replaced the older Biennial Report with the newer TSDR Survey when finalizing its thresholds. Petitioners called this a last-minute substitution, but identified no inaccuracies in the replacement data or concealment by the agency. The methodology remained substantially constant, and the more complete data addressed earlier industry objections while confirming the basic assessment. Accordingly, this general substitution satisfied notice and comment. The court’s conclusion depends on what the new information did and what meaningful opportunity had already existed; it is not permission to withhold any technical material labeled supplementary. (484–485.)

EPA also adequately defended its use of land-disposal and wastewater-treatment facilities, inclusion of commercial management facilities, attention to the largest streams rather than typical volumes, and separate solid/liquid thresholds. These choices tracked technical feasibility. A 95th-percentile cutoff permitted a five-percent overlap with volumes already managed under Subtitle C, a reasonably explained choice between competing recommendations. Footnote 9 acknowledges a troubling discrepancy over whether some comparison data remained partially aggregated. Even assuming petitioners’ analysis, the agency’s conscientious disaggregation effort and reasonable stream-specific approach did not make the criterion arbitrary. (485–488; 487–488 n. 9.)

## Low hazard and other general objections

The court rejected the claim that volume alone defined special wastes. Low hazard appeared in the relevant regulatory history and EDF II’s construction of congressional intent. The earlier EDF I holding—that hazardous extraction and beneficiation wastes need not automatically be regulated under Subtitle C—did not prohibit hazard screening to determine which processing wastes entered Bevill’s protected category. Screening and final regulatory treatment served different functions. (488–490.)

EPA could use its developing Method 1312 leaching test and a relaxed corrosivity range because it explained their technical basis and checked initial results through other methods. Scientific certainty was not required before any regulatory judgment could be made. A narrower issue concerning a liquid-waste toxicity test equivalent to the ordinary RCRA test was unnecessary to resolve: the two affected wastes were independently excluded on other grounds. (489–490 & n. 10.)

The court sustained a rule restricting the exemption to processing feedstocks containing less than fifty percent scrap, including designated in-process materials. It reasonably distinguished primary from secondary processing, notwithstanding effects on recycling incentives. EPA also reasonably treated Bevill designation as a one-time inquiry rather than an exemption continually expanding to future streams. And EPA could interpret the Simpson Amendment’s authority to relax Subtitle C requirements as covering the same special-waste category. The competing legislative-history argument was insufficient to establish a clearly mandated broader interpretation; committee reports and the provisional character of the earlier agency interpretation undercut it. (490–493.)

## Mixtures and treatment permits

EPA had applied its Subtitle C mixture rule to mixtures of Bevill and non-Bevill wastes and required a treatment permit for the mixing operation. In Shell Oil, the court had separately vacated the underlying mixture rule on procedural grounds. Solite therefore remanded the derivative Bevill rule without deciding its validity. EPA could repromulgate and justify it through a valid underlying rule or an independent explanation. The treatment-permit corollary was likewise remanded, with future review available if the agency reinstated or otherwise retained it. This disposition was not a merits ruling that all such mixtures must be exempt or that treatment permits are categorically unauthorized. (493–494.)

The rehearing order rejected EPA’s attempt to distinguish mixtures unaffected by Shell Oil because it had not advanced that alternative position in its original briefing. The agency remained free to draw appropriate distinctions on remand. The same reasoning governed the permit requirement. (500.)

## Particular waste streams and the two affirmative grounds for relief

Du Pont’s chloride-ilmenite process produced iron chloride gas during an initial beneficiation step and titanium tetrachloride during a subsequent processing step in the same vessel. The iron chloride was later condensed and removed as acid waste. EPA classified the waste as processing waste, emphasizing physical inseparability and similarities to conventional chloride operations. It never explained why physical coexistence outweighed the temporal distinction or why the stated similarities controlled. Imperfect regulatory categories still required a rational policy justification for choosing between them. The court remanded for an adequate explanation or reconsideration, rather than itself awarding Du Pont Bevill coverage. SCM’s requested similar relief was denied because it had not presented its argument to the agency and the processes were not obviously identical. (494–495.)

For chrome tailings, EPA could distinguish treated low-hazard tailings from untreated landfill residues. Recognition of treated material in response to comments did not require an additional opportunity merely because other parties’ arguments failed. Untreated landfill waste had no showing of low hazard, and future regeneration did not satisfy the agency’s sustained one-time volume inquiry. For lead wastewater, EPA reasonably measured separate streams as generated, rather than combining process wastewater and acid-plant blowdown as managed. Data from two of the industry’s five plants also reasonably supported the low-hazard determination. These challenges failed. (495–498.)

Lightweight aggregate air-pollution-control dust/sludge presented the contrasting notice failure. EPA initially estimated average annual per-facility volume at 134,000 tons, above the 45,000-ton solid-waste threshold, and proposed retaining the waste. Audubon questioned the usable responses from only six of twenty-eight facilities. EPA answered by using other survey questions and new inferential calculations, reducing its estimate to 15,813 tons. Solite received an explanatory memorandum on January 11, 1990, one day before the Administrator signed the final rule. (498–499 & nn. 11–12.)

Unlike the general TSDR substitution, this revision changed the methodology and the relevant data without exposing those changes to meaningful comment. The proposal had not signaled reconsideration of this volume determination. One day’s informal notice did not provide a practical opportunity to respond, and the possibility that commenters criticized the original estimate did not put everyone on notice of the new method. Parties need not monitor all other comments to discover a change the agency itself should disclose. The court required reconsideration after notice and comment and did not reach the substantive correctness of the reduced estimate. (499–500 & nn. 13–14.)

## Exact disposition and source scope

EPA’s rules survived principally. The lightweight aggregate determination was vacated for inadequate notice and comment; the Du Pont classification was remanded for a reasoned explanation or reconsideration; the mixture and treatment-permit matters were remanded without a merits decision. Rehearing concerning the latter two matters was denied. The contrast between confirming data and a new methodology is central to the assigned quotation’s practical meaning. (477, 500.)
