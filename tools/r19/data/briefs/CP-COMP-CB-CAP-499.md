# United States v. Nixon

**418 U.S. 683 (1974) — Supreme Court of the United States, July 24, 1974.** Chief Justice Burger wrote for all eight participating Justices; Justice Rehnquist did not participate. Civil Procedure: appellate finality, criminal subpoenas, justiciability, and executive privilege. The assigned account is CivPro PDF 620, printed 1228–1229, Note 3, within the surrounding discussion of immediate appeal.

## Facts and procedural course

The Watergate prosecutions made confidential Presidential conversations potentially important trial evidence. On March 1, 1974, a federal grand jury indicted seven individuals, including former Attorney General John Mitchell and White House aides Haldeman and Ehrlichman, for offenses including conspiracy to obstruct justice. The grand jury also identified President Nixon as an unindicted coconspirator. Colson subsequently pleaded guilty to a different charge and was no longer a defendant in this prosecution. These circumstances supplied the context for an evidentiary dispute; the Supreme Court did not decide that the grand jury could lawfully identify the President in that manner. **687 nn.2–4.**

At Special Prosecutor Leon Jaworski’s request, the district court issued an April 18 subpoena under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 17(c), requiring tapes and documents concerning precisely identified conversations and meetings. White House logs identified dates, places, participants, and timing. On April 30, Nixon publicly released edited transcripts of 43 conversations, including portions of 20 subpoenaed conversations. His May 1 special appearance nevertheless sought to quash the subpoena, invoking executive privilege. **687–689 & nn.5–6.**

The district court rejected the request on May 20 and ordered delivery of the original recordings and materials for examination in camera, with copies and an index. The impending criminal trial, scheduled for September 9, made delay consequential. Nixon appealed and sought mandamus. Jaworski obtained certiorari before judgment in the court of appeals. Nixon separately obtained review of the district court’s refusal to expunge the grand jury’s reference to him. The Supreme Court heard argument on July 8. **689–690.**

## Issues and positions

The Court first examined the availability of immediate judicial review. Nixon disputed the existence of a justiciable controversy between two members of the Executive Branch, the subpoena’s evidentiary foundation, and the judiciary’s authority to override an absolute Presidential privilege. He maintained that separation of powers and the need for candid confidential advice protected Presidential communications from compulsory judicial process. The prosecutor argued that the governing delegation authorized him to contest the privilege and that specific, necessary evidence for an ongoing criminal trial outweighed a generalized confidentiality claim. The Court addressed those issues separately rather than treating the President’s constitutional status as a complete answer to every procedural objection. **690–706.**

## Immediate appeal without contempt

Ordinarily, an order refusing to quash a subpoena is not a final decision under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. The recipient must resist compliance, suffer contempt, and appeal the contempt judgment. This prevents routine evidentiary disputes from interrupting litigation. The Court recognized an exception associated with a subpoena directed to a third party unlikely to risk contempt to protect another person’s interests. **690–692.**

The President’s position presented a different but comparably compelling reason to permit review. Requiring him to disobey a judicial order solely to create an appealable contempt proceeding would produce an unnecessary constitutional confrontation. Insisting on that step would delay resolution of a dispute affecting an approaching criminal trial. The Court therefore treated this particular order as appealable under § 1291, making the case properly pending in the court of appeals and eligible for certiorari before judgment under § 1254. It did not decide whether the parties’ additional jurisdictional theories independently supported review. **691–692 & n.7.**

The assigned Note 3 accurately emphasizes that Nixon had not refused compliance and had not been held in contempt. Its concluding question about whether the facts were unique is editorial. The decision supplies a carefully justified exceptional route to review; it does not make every interlocutory subpoena or privilege ruling immediately appealable.

## Why an intrabranch disagreement was justiciable

The Court rejected the claim that organizational labels alone made the dispute nonjusticiable. Congress had vested criminal litigation authority in the Attorney General, who delegated authority to the Special Prosecutor through an operative regulation. That regulation authorized the prosecutor to seek evidence, contest assertions of executive privilege, and exercise substantial independence. It also imposed special removal protections. **692–696 & nn.8–10.**

The Executive could potentially amend or revoke the regulation, but had not done so. While it remained in force, it bound the Executive Branch. A dispute between the prosecutor exercising that delegated authority and the President asserting privilege therefore presented concrete adversity over evidence in a pending criminal case. This was the kind of legal controversy courts traditionally adjudicated. The conclusion depended on the actual delegation and its continuing legal effect, not a general principle that every internal executive disagreement belongs in court. **695–697.**

## The subpoena satisfied Rule 17(c)

Rule 17(c) provides a means to obtain evidentiary material before trial, not a general criminal discovery device. The Court identified the central requirements as relevance, admissibility, and specificity. The district court’s framework also considered whether diligent efforts could obtain the material elsewhere, whether advance production was necessary for trial preparation and avoidance of delay, and whether the request was made in good faith rather than as a fishing expedition. **697–700.**

The prosecutor’s showing met those standards. It identified particular conversations instead of demanding an undefined mass of Presidential records. Participants’ testimony and the conversations’ relationship to the alleged conspiracy supplied a rational basis for relevance. The material could contain admissible party statements or coconspirator declarations, subject to the requirements governing those uses. Its potential importance and the time needed to analyze recordings supported advance examination. Mere impeachment ordinarily would not justify pretrial production, but the showing included other evidentiary uses. **700–702 & nn.13–14.**

The Court did not pronounce every recording admissible before anyone examined it. It upheld the district judge’s carefully supported decision to require production for screening. Nor did it resolve whether a lower Rule 17(c) standard might apply to subpoenas issued to third parties, because the prosecutor satisfied the ordinary standard. **699 n.12, 702.**

## Executive privilege: recognized but qualified

The Court acknowledged a constitutionally grounded presumption protecting Presidential communications. Candid advice requires room for confidential deliberation; the Constitution’s failure to enumerate the privilege expressly did not eliminate it. These interests warranted substantial judicial respect. **705–708 & nn.15–17.**

But the President could not conclusively determine the scope of a privilege asserted against a court’s demand for evidence. Determining legal claims was part of the judicial function. An absolute privilege based solely on separation of powers or generalized confidentiality would also interfere with the courts’ constitutional responsibility to administer criminal justice. The Court distinguished confidentiality in ordinary Presidential deliberations from a claim concerning military, diplomatic, or sensitive national-security secrets, which was not asserted here. **703–711.**

The competing interest was concrete: evidence demonstrably relevant to a criminal trial. The Fifth and Sixth Amendments and the judicial obligation to determine guilt fairly gave that need constitutional importance. Allowing a generalized confidentiality claim to defeat a specific, demonstrated evidentiary need would seriously impair criminal adjudication. The Court concluded that the need shown here outweighed the President’s generalized interest in secrecy. **711–713.**

That balance was expressly limited. Note 19 reserved civil litigation, congressional demands for information, and preservation of state secrets. This opinion consequently does not itself supply the complete rule for those different disputes. **712 n.19.**

## Judgment, safeguards, and limits

The Court affirmed the district court’s production order in No. 73-1766 and directed that the mandate issue forthwith. Production meant delivery to the district judge for confidential inspection, followed by selection of relevant and admissible evidence. It did not authorize immediate public disclosure of all recordings. The judge was to protect unrelated or inadmissible Presidential communications, excise material that did not belong in the trial, and return excised material under seal to its lawful custodian. The judge could seek counsel’s assistance in camera concerning proposed excisions without abandoning those protections. **713–716 & n.21.**

In the companion No. 73-1834, the Court dismissed the President’s cross-petition as improvidently granted. It thus left unresolved his challenge to the grand jury’s authority to name him an unindicted coconspirator. His request to transmit that grand jury material was denied. **687 n.2.** There were no separate opinions. The unanimity was among the eight participating Justices, with Rehnquist taking no part. **685, 716.**

The decision connects procedural accommodation with substantive accountability. The Court avoided forcing a President into contempt to obtain review, recognized a substantial confidentiality privilege, and nonetheless enforced a sufficiently justified criminal subpoena under protective judicial supervision. Each step matters: special appellate access did not imply immunity from evidence production, and rejection of absolute privilege did not eliminate confidential screening.
