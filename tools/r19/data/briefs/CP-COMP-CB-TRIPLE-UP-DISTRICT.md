# Triple Up Limited v. Youku Tudou Inc. — District Court

**Civil Procedure · CP-COMP-CB-TRIPLE-UP-DISTRICT**  
**235 F. Supp. 3d 15 (D.D.C. 2017)** · January 24, 2017 · District Judge Randolph D. Moss  
**Assigned:** CivPro.pdf, physical PDF page 126, printed pages 202–203, Note 2. This brief concerns the district decision; the assigned appellate affirmance is a distinct stage.

## Concrete dispute and procedural posture

Triple Up, a Seychelles corporation, claimed exclusive United States performance rights to three Taiwanese films: *Sleeping Youth*, *Sorry, I Love You*, and *Squirrel Suicide Incident*. It alleged that Youku, a Cayman Islands corporation principally operating in China, made the films available to American viewers without permission. Youku operated Mandarin-language video platforms used by roughly 400 million unique visitors monthly. Less than one percent of its traffic originated in the United States; the precise American share was unknown. Some videos were uploaded by Youku, others by users. Youku used territorial geoblocking for its own uploads but not for user uploads. Its principal revenue came from advertisements, with subscriptions and pay-per-view services supplying a smaller share. 235 F. Supp. 3d at 18–20.

The specific American viewing alleged was by Triple Up’s own attorney in Washington, D.C., in August and December 2015. One stream displayed an English-language University of Phoenix advertisement; the other advertisements concerned Mandarin-language video games. Triple Up inferred from website features that Youku itself uploaded the films. Youku disputed that characterization: it asserted Chinese licenses and geoblocking for two films and attributed the remaining upload to a user. The court did not decide that infringement dispute. After receiving a January 17, 2016 notice, Youku removed every identified version within twenty-four hours; the complaint alleged no subsequent availability. Id. at 19–20.

Triple Up filed suit on February 1, 2016, asserting copyright public-performance and reproduction/distribution theories, direct and secondary liability, a Lanham Act claim, and common-law unfair competition. Certain additional theories had been withdrawn. Youku moved under Rule 12(b)(2), alternatively Rule 12(b)(6). Although it had no American offices or employees and did not market its services here, Triple Up invoked its New York Stock Exchange listing, New York process agent, advertising relationships, American content licenses, and an American antipiracy software contract. The question was whether those contacts authorized adjudication of these particular claims, not whether Youku had any American connections whatsoever. Id. at 19–22.

## Jurisdictional framework and issues reserved

Triple Up advanced specific jurisdiction under D.C.’s transacting-business provision and, alternatively, Rule 4(k)(2), which permits jurisdiction for qualifying federal claims when the defendant is not subject to jurisdiction in any state’s courts of general jurisdiction. Youku did not identify another state where it conceded jurisdiction. The judge proceeded using national contacts under Rule 4(k)(2) and the Fifth Amendment: if even the United States as a whole supplied insufficient contacts, the narrower District of Columbia theory necessarily failed. Id. at 21–23 & nn. 1–4.

Two reservations matter. Note 1 did not finally decide whether the transacting-business provision covered these tort claims. Note 2 acknowledged a possible New York consent argument arising from Youku’s designated agent and accepted service; the parties had not litigated its validity. The opinion therefore did not hold that the agent could never create consent jurisdiction. Nor did Triple Up press general jurisdiction. The decisive inquiry was defendant-created minimum contacts connected with the claims, followed, if satisfied, by constitutional reasonableness. Id. at 22–24.

## Why the asserted contacts failed

**Access and geoblocking.** A globally accessible website did not itself establish purposeful American activity. Triple Up’s novel argument was that Youku’s ability to exclude American viewers made its failure to do so a deliberate decision to serve them. The court rejected that inference on this record. Purposeful availment ordinarily requires affirmative conduct directed toward a forum, not merely failure to prevent access from it. Otherwise any website operator capable of filtering locations could become suable everywhere it did not block. The court acknowledged developing technology and doctrine; it did not announce that geoblocking could never matter in a future case. Id. at 23–26.

**Advertising and claim connection.** The judge expressly avoided deciding whether hosting English-language, geographically targeted advertisements amounted to purposeful availment. The claims failed the separate relatedness requirement even assuming such availment. The opinion rejected a merely discernible relationship as insufficient and required at least some causal connection. Advertisements did not cause the alleged unauthorized availability: removing them would leave the asserted infringement unchanged. Id. at 26–28 & nn. 6–8. This is the court’s historical 2017 relatedness analysis, not a statement that later Supreme Court law universally demands strict causation. Note 7 separately distinguished a local court’s interpretation of federal due process from binding interpretations of D.C. law.

**Interactivity and corporate relationships.** The opportunity to register accounts, search, and watch videos did not establish claim-related forum transactions. A website’s technical interactivity was an imperfect proxy for deliberate business activity. Triple Up did not develop a claim-related VIP-subscription theory. Stock-exchange listing and reporting obligations had no connection with the films. Neither did contracts with American advertising agencies, software providers, or content producers establish the necessary connection merely because the counterparties were American. Id. at 28–29. Note 5 preserved the distinction between earlier general-jurisdiction reasoning and the narrower significance of actual website transactions.

**Effects theory.** The opinion discussed *Calder* but held that Triple Up had waived an effects-test argument by not presenting it. Its alternative observations also undermined such a theory: no American business injury was alleged by this Seychelles plaintiff, the works were Taiwanese and apparently Mandarin-language, and the sole alleged American viewer was its attorney. Note 10 identified the films’ language as an inference from the parties’ submissions, rather than an express record fact. The court did not finally decide a properly developed, factually different effects theory. Id. at 29–30 & nn. 9–10.

## Why discovery did not save the action

Triple Up sought seven categories of jurisdictional discovery, including advertising, revenues, geoblocking, VIP subscribers, servers, contracts, and stock-exchange matters. Most would illuminate contacts already found unrelated to the claims, rather than cure their missing connection. Evidence of concerted efforts to attract American viewers might conceivably bear on an effects theory, but that theory was waived and lacked a good-faith factual basis here. Id. at 30–31 & n. 11.

The server request required separate treatment. Youku’s declaration did not affirmatively rule out every American server, and the judge accepted that claim-related American operations might matter. But Triple Up supplied no reason to believe such servers existed: it relied on a hunch despite Youku’s principal Chinese operation and absence of American offices or employees. Discovery requires a plausible factual lead, not an unrestricted search for a jurisdictional theory. The denial therefore did not establish a universal rule that server location is irrelevant. Id. at 31.

## Exact result and assigned significance

The court granted Rule 12(b)(2) dismissal and dismissed the action. It denied jurisdictional discovery and denied the alternative Rule 12(b)(6) motion as moot. That is a jurisdictional disposition, not a finding that Youku’s conduct was lawful or that Triple Up lacked substantive intellectual-property rights. A separate order issued concurrently; this source is the complete memorandum opinion stating its disposition. Id. at 31–32.

The casebook uses this dispute to test whether technological control over access amounts to forum targeting. The complete opinion adds distinct relatedness, waiver, and discovery grounds. Its eleven judicial notes have been read, including the consent and statutory reservations. There is no separate judicial writing. The later appellate judgment receives its own brief because it adds remedial standing and examines the appellate record rather than simply adopting every district proposition.
