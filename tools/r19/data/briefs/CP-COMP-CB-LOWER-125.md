# Grey v. Hayes-Sammons Chemical Co.

**310 F.2d 291 (5th Cir. 1962) — November 7, 1962 — Civil Procedure**

Judge Wisdom wrote the panel opinion; Chief Judge Tuttle and Judge Rives were the other members. No separate writing is reported. The court reversed a judgment notwithstanding the verdict and directed entry of judgment for the cotton grower in accordance with the jury’s $8,750 verdict. The decision combines an Erie prediction about Mississippi negligence law with a separate agency basis satisfying privity for the warranty claim.

## Facts and procedural history

F. O. Grey farmed two cotton tracts in Yazoo County, Mississippi, in 1957: a 45.6-acre tract and a 19-acre tract separated by woods. He regularly sprayed his cotton with insecticides manufactured by Hayes-Sammons, a Texas company, purchased through T. A. Quinn Chemical & Fertilizer Company in sealed five-gallon cans. He generally used tractor-mounted equipment and water from Potato Hill Bayou. One drumload covered roughly twenty-five acres; the first two loads covered the larger tract and the northern quarter of the smaller tract, with a third partial load completing the job. (292–293.)

Late in July, blooms blackened, squares reddened and burst, and leaves grew abnormally. Severe damage was uniform across the land covered by the first two drumloads, while the remainder was only slightly damaged. Hayes-Sammons’s division manager, Andrew White, and an entomologist agreed that the symptoms indicated a growth-hormone herbicide such as 2-4D or 2-4-5T. They found no evidence of contamination in the bayou or of airborne herbicide damage to surrounding vegetation. Laboratories could not test the empty insecticide cans for past herbicide content. (293–294.)

Alternative contamination remained an issue. Grey shared the spraying equipment with his brother and testified that he was sure his brother used weed killer. The manufacturer described careful separation of packaged herbicides from its insecticide manufacturing operations and reported no other similar complaints that year. Grey said White promised to pay his loss if he cooperated and kept the matter quiet; White described the promise as conditional on finding that the company’s insecticide caused the damage. No amount was agreed upon. (294.)

Grey sued in state court for $11,423.75, alleging warranty, negligence, and a settlement agreement. Hayes-Sammons removed the action. The district judge withheld settlement from the jury because of inadequate consideration and indefiniteness, refused a res ipsa loquitur instruction, and gave warranty instructions without expressly addressing privity. Although portions of the charge could be understood as referring to negligence, the appellate court characterized the submitted controversy essentially as causation. The jury awarded $8,750. On the manufacturer’s motion, the judge entered judgment notwithstanding the verdict: the label’s disclaimer supposedly shifted all risk to Grey, and Mississippi warranty law ordinarily did not protect consumers lacking privity outside the food-product exception. (292–293 & nn.1–3.)

## Issues and rules

Did Mississippi’s older privity decisions require judgment for the manufacturer? Had Grey actually pursued negligence, and could res ipsa support that theory? Independently, did the distributor’s relationship to the manufacturer satisfy warranty privity? Did the can’s disclaimer cover a foreign contaminant, and was circumstantial causation evidence sufficient to sustain the verdict?

Mississippi law controlled the substantive questions. The Fifth Circuit had to decide as a Mississippi court would, considering both the state’s prior holdings and later indications from its highest court. The prediction did not rest solely on a federal preference for a different rule. (294–297.)

## Reasoning and holdings

**The old negligence-privity requirement no longer accurately predicted Mississippi law.** The court distinguished warranty from negligence. Existing Mississippi authority required contractual privity for warranty claims, subject to exceptions; negligence law had also formerly required it. In Ford Motor Co. v. Myers, Mississippi had expressly declined to follow MacPherson’s manufacturer-duty approach. But subsequent developments mattered. In E. I. Du Pont de Nemours & Co. v. Ladner, the Mississippi Supreme Court had resolved a property-damage claim on causation while deliberately discussing approvingly the modern principle permitting recovery by remote consumers for a manufacturer’s negligence. (294–296.)

The First Circuit’s Mason decision had treated that discussion, together with the overwhelming modern trend, as signaling that Mississippi would abandon Ford’s rule. Grey agreed. It acknowledged the unusual nature of preferring recent considered dicta to an older contrary holding, but concluded that Ladner’s strong language and chosen authorities put litigants on notice that Ford no longer represented the state’s negligence rule. Cases concerning defendants who had not manufactured the product did not defeat that inference: a manufacturer controlled production and packaging and was primarily responsible for its labeling and advertising. Thus a manufacturer’s breach of due care could support recovery without privity. (296–297 & nn.9–10.)

**Negligence was pleaded and pursued.** The complaint alleged that the product was contaminated when manufactured, packaged, and sealed. Counsel’s opening expressly argued negligent packaging, the defense introduced evidence denying negligent manufacture, and Grey requested a negligence-based res ipsa instruction. Hayes-Sammons therefore could not characterize negligence as an unasserted theory. The court further concluded that, subject to the plaintiff’s burden and a proper evidentiary predicate, sealed-container contamination could support res ipsa under Mississippi principles. It relied on analogous contaminated beverage cases and the California insecticide decision Burr. That reasoning did not eliminate the plaintiff’s proof burden or establish negligence simply from crop damage. (297–299 & nn.11–12.)

**Warranty privity was satisfied through agency, rather than abolished.** Quinn received a 7½-percent sales commission and a $300 monthly salary, sold only Hayes-Sammons insecticides, and held the products under a written consignment agreement. The manufacturer retained title until withdrawal for sale, fixed prices and territory, paid some advertising expenses, required segregation of its goods, and could inspect them. Quinn bore customer-credit risk, leased its warehouse, and paid its expenses, but these circumstances did not outweigh the overall sales relationship. Mississippi’s Anticich decision supported treating a distributor as a selling agent despite the agreement’s terminology. Quinn acted sufficiently as the manufacturer’s agent to establish privity with Grey for warranty purposes. The negligence holding consequently should not be restated as a universal abolition of Mississippi warranty privity. (299–300.)

**The label did not disclaim contamination liability.** Mississippi recognized disclaimers, but their language had to clearly identify the warranties excluded and ambiguities were construed against the seller. Hayes-Sammons listed the ingredients and expressly directed use against cotton insects. Its nonwarranty clause addressed use and handling, assigning the buyer those risks. Read strictly and with the ingredient description, it warned about hazards of the described insecticide; it did not authorize an undisclosed foreign herbicide that destroyed the product’s merchantability and fitness. Burr and Diamond Alkali supported distinguishing application hazards from an impurity inconsistent with the product’s description. The district court therefore erred in treating the clause as a complete defense. (300–302 & n.13.)

**The physical damage pattern supplied adequate causation evidence.** Direct evidence of contamination inside the manufacturer’s plant was unavailable, but circumstantial proof could support the verdict. If residue left by Grey’s brother had contaminated the equipment, its concentration should have diminished after the first drumload. Uniform severe damage across the first two loads’ coverage instead supported an inference that the second can replenished the herbicide concentration. Slight damage after the third load fit dilution. The court held that the jury could logically infer contamination of the insecticide despite the competing evidence. (302–303.)

## Judgment and assigned significance

The court expressly did not reach whether an enforceable settlement existed. A new trial would serve no useful purpose: the original instructions favored the defendant, the judgment notwithstanding the verdict rested on legal errors, and substantial evidence supported the verdict. It reversed and directed judgment for Grey consistent with the $8,750 award. (303.)
