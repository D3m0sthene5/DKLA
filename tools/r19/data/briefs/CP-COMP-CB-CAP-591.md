# Wilko v. Swan

**346 U.S. 427 (1953) — Supreme Court of the United States, December 7, 1953.** Justice Reed delivered the Court’s opinion. Justice Jackson concurred separately; Justice Frankfurter dissented, joined by Justice Minton. The judgment was 7–2. Civil Procedure: predispute arbitration, statutory nonwaiver, and judicial enforcement of investor protections. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 704, the Wilko account across printed 1399–1400. This brief explains the historical 1953 decision rather than certifying its rule as current law.

## Facts and procedural posture

Wilko sued members of the brokerage firm Hayden, Stone & Co. for damages under §12(2) of the Securities Act of 1933. He alleged that on January 17, 1951, he purchased 1,600 shares of Air Associates common stock after false representations about a proposed merger with Borg Warner and the supposedly favorable price of the shares. According to his complaint, the brokers concealed that an Air Associates director and counsel, Haven B. Page, was selling his own shares, including the shares Wilko purchased. Page was a defendant in the action but not a party to the Supreme Court review. Wilko alleged that he sold about two weeks later at a loss. These were allegations supporting the damages action, not fraud findings reached by the Supreme Court. (428–429, nn.1–3.)

Wilko had signed margin agreements covering controversies arising from the account. Their arbitration provisions applied broadly to future disputes, selected New York arbitration law, and permitted arbitration before specified exchanges or organizations. The agreement reproduced in note 15 called for at least three arbitrators. The brokers moved to stay the lawsuit under §3 of the Federal Arbitration Act before answering. They asserted willingness to arbitrate and relied on Wilko’s failure to pursue that procedure. No arbitration or arbitral award had occurred. (429–430; 432, n.15.)

The district court refused the stay, reasoning that the Securities Act’s nonwaiver provision invalidated the agreement insofar as it required arbitration of the statutory claim. The Second Circuit reversed. It considered arbitration compatible with preservation of the substantive statutory rights and regarded judicial review as an adequate safeguard against an arbitrator’s failure to apply the statute. The Supreme Court granted review and reversed the appellate judgment. It resolved the enforceability of the advance agreement, not whether the alleged misrepresentations occurred or what damages were recoverable. (429–430, nn.4–7; 438.)

## Statutory issue and holding

The question was whether an agreement made before a controversy arose could require arbitration of a §12(2) Securities Act claim despite §14, which voided any condition, stipulation, or provision binding a securities purchaser to waive compliance with a provision of the Act. The Court held that the protected provisions included the investor’s right to select a judicial forum. On its historical construction of the two statutes, the predispute agreement was invalid as applied to the Securities Act controversy. (430–435, nn.5–7, 14–19; 438.)

The parties did not dispute that the transaction fell within the Arbitration Act’s coverage. That statute ordinarily supported enforcement of arbitration agreements and a stay where the controversy was referable to arbitration. Reed recognized the congressional aim of overcoming judicial hostility to arbitration and obtaining prompt, economical resolution of commercial disputes. The Securities Act, however, addressed an additional concern: buyers’ informational disadvantages and the difficulty of enforcing obligations arising from misleading securities transactions. The Court had to accommodate both policies rather than assume that the Arbitration Act answered the question by itself. (430–433, nn.8–14.)

Section 12(2) gave the purchaser a special civil remedy for a materially misleading prospectus or oral communication. Its liability and defense provisions required attention to the purchaser’s knowledge and to the seller’s showing of lack of knowledge and exercise of reasonable care. Section 22 broadened the available judicial forums, allowed federal jurisdiction without the ordinary amount restriction, supplied advantageous venue and service rules, and prohibited removal of covered state-court actions. Reed treated these procedural protections as part of the statutory design, not as dispensable details outside §14. (428, n.1; 431–434, nn.9, 13–19.)

## Why the Court treated arbitration as a prohibited waiver

The brokers argued that arbitration changed the forum without changing the governing law. The agreement expressly preserved obligations under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, and the Court accepted that arbitrators would also have to apply the relevant Securities Act of 1933 obligations. The objection therefore was not that the contract formally erased every substantive right or shifted the statutory burden of proof. The Court instead examined the practical effectiveness of those rights in a different decision-making process. Its extended note 18 discusses the 1933 and 1934 statutes and explains why the agreement’s express reference to one did not make the other inapplicable. (432–434, nn.15–18.)

Reed emphasized the timing of the agreement. Before a violation or controversy arose, the buyer could not adequately assess the disadvantages of surrendering the statutory choice of courts. Although particular buyers and sellers might bargain on equal terms, Congress had legislated for the unequal access to information typically separating investors from issuers and dealers. Giving up the judicial forum at that early point surrendered an advantage Congress had supplied to help offset that inequality. (434–435.)

The Court also distinguished relatively straightforward commercial arbitration from adjudication of the securities claim. Determining materiality, knowledge, reasonable care, and the applicable burden of proof required legal judgments as well as factual findings. Arbitrators could decide without judicial instruction, without explaining their reasons, and without preserving a complete record. Those features could make it difficult for a court to determine whether the statutory protections had actually been applied. (435–436, nn.20–23.)

The Arbitration Act’s vacatur grounds were limited. Note 22 reproduced the grounds involving corruption, partiality, procedural misconduct, and excess or imperfect execution of arbitral powers. The Court distinguished ordinary errors of legal interpretation from manifest disregard and stressed the absence of ordinary merits review of legal interpretations in unrestricted submissions such as the margin agreements. It also contrasted English provisions permitting judicial determination of legal questions. These observations concerned the adequacy of the anticipated procedure; they were not findings about a completed award. (436–437, nn.22–26.)

Reed drew an analogy to *Boyd v. Grand Trunk Western Railroad*, which invalidated a restriction on an employee’s statutory choice of venue. Even though that statute spoke in terms of liability, the choice of forum was sufficiently important to the statutory protection that contracting it away frustrated Congress’s purpose. Wilko did not require the Court to reach the full extent of Boyd’s treatment of agreements after liability arose. The Court expressly confined its decision to the advance restriction before a controversy existed. (437–438, nn.27–31.)

## Jackson’s separate concurrence

Jackson agreed with the conclusion that the Securities Act prohibited waiver of the judicial remedy through an agreement made before the controversy. He believed the parties could agree to arbitrate after a controversy arose. He also declined to endorse an unnecessary conclusion that the Arbitration Act precluded any judicial remedy for an arbitrator’s statutory interpretation error. Because no arbitration had taken place, the Court did not need to settle the precise scope of review of an actual award. His concurrence reinforces both the temporal limit and the distinction between enforceability of this agreement and review of a completed arbitration. (438–439.)

## Frankfurter’s dissent, joined by Minton

Frankfurter accepted that arbitration would be prohibited if it inherently denied the purchaser the statutory protections or left no effective judicial means to ensure them. He disputed that the record established either premise. The available New York arbitration system, operating under federal judicial supervision, gave no demonstrated reason to assume the purchaser would lose the rights §12(2) supplied. His sole footnote detailed the American Arbitration Association’s selection process, including party objections and preferences among qualified potential arbitrators. (439, dissent n.*.)

The dissent emphasized arbitration’s potential advantages in speed, economy, and effective enforcement. Arbitrators remained bound by the Securities Act. If violation of those duties justified vacatur, appropriate means of judicial scrutiny—some record or opinion revealing compliance—should be implied rather than declared unavailable. Frankfurter also stressed that this record did not show Wilko had no practical choice but to accept an unconscionable provision; the SEC, appearing as amicus, did not assert that particular coercion theory. He would have affirmed the Second Circuit. These were arguments against the Court’s statutory inference, not findings that every arbitration clause was voluntary or every future award would be lawful. (439–440.)

## Judgment and assigned comparison

The Supreme Court **reversed the Second Circuit’s judgment**. It did not adjudicate the securities claim’s merits, award damages, or review an arbitral award. Its holding addressed the predispute statutory claim, while Jackson expressly separated later agreements and the Court reserved the broader postcontroversy question. (438–440.)
