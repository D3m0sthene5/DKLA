# Imamura v. General Electric Co.

**957 F.3d 98 (1st Cir. 2020). April 24, 2020; accompanying errata correct “amendable” to “amenable” on slip page 21.** Judge Torruella wrote for Judges Torruella, Lynch, and Kayatta; no separate writing is reported. Citations below use the preserved court opinion’s numbered slip pages. Assigned account: CivPro PDF 237, printed 432, Note 6.

## The disaster, the allegations, and Japan’s response

Four individuals and six business entities from Fukushima Prefecture sued General Electric in Massachusetts for property damage and economic harm arising from the 2011 Fukushima nuclear disaster. Their putative classes encompassed homeowners and businesses affected by evacuation and contamination. GE had designed the plant’s six reactors, constructed three of them, and provided designs and expertise for the others. The plaintiffs alleged defective reactors and safety systems, poor siting of emergency equipment, inadequate backup power, and failure to warn about earthquake and tsunami risks. The court expressly described the background as allegations rather than findings, even where the parties did not dispute particular details. It did not adjudicate GE’s negligence. (Slip 3–5 & nn.1–3; 10–12.)

The earthquake-induced tsunami disabled power and cooling systems, followed by explosions and radioactive releases. Japan’s nuclear-damage statute channeled liability to the plant operator, Tokyo Electric Power Company, or TEPCO. The operator was strictly liable, without a damages cap, although claimants still had to establish causation and damages. The opinion described a ten-year limitations period then scheduled to expire in 2021; that is the historical period reported by the court, not a statement of present filing rights. (Slip 5–7.)

Victims could pursue three nonexclusive avenues: sue TEPCO in Japanese courts, submit claims directly to TEPCO, or mediate claims through the publicly established Nuclear Damages Dispute Resolution Center. Courts could hear claims initially or after an unsatisfactory settlement offer. Although the particular nuclear claims lacked an American-style class mechanism, claimants could join cases, and substantial groups had obtained judgments or settlements. The direct and mediated procedures applied compensation guidelines covering property loss, business interruption, and related harms. Mediation had no filing fee and allowed representation or self-representation. Japan had provided significant financial support to TEPCO and the compensation system. The opinion’s reported payment and case totals supplied concrete evidence that these mechanisms operated, rather than merely existing on paper. (Slip 7–10.)

## Proceedings and the narrow appellate challenge

The plaintiffs filed in November 2017 and amended in May 2018. They asserted Massachusetts negligence, product-liability, and property claims and Japanese-law negligence, warning, and economic-loss claims, seeking compensatory and punitive damages. GE invoked the Convention on Supplementary Compensation for Nuclear Damage as a jurisdictional objection, Japanese liability channeling and Massachusetts time bars as merits defenses, and forum non conveniens as an independent ground for dismissal. (Slip 10–13.)

The district court dismissed for forum non conveniens. It assumed jurisdiction for that purpose and found both an adequate Japanese alternative and a strong balance of convenience favoring Japan. The First Circuit likewise left the Convention’s exclusive-jurisdiction question unresolved: under Sinochem, it could sustain the nonmerits forum dismissal without deciding that issue. On appeal the plaintiffs challenged only availability and adequacy. Their failure to contest the district court’s private/public balancing waived any challenge to that separate part of the analysis. (Slip 13 n.4; 14–19 & n.5.)

## Rule and issue

Ordinarily a defendant seeking foreign-forum dismissal must show that the alternative can address the relevant dispute and that the defendant is amenable to process there. A less favorable remedy does not defeat adequacy; the remedy must be so inadequate that it amounts to none. The defendant also must show that convenience and judicial efficiency strongly favor the alternative. The appellate court reviews discretionary assessment deferentially, but reviews legal errors de novo. (Slip 16–19.)

The plaintiffs argued that Japan could not count as an alternative because its law prevented recovery specifically against GE. Even if GE could accept service, the channeling statute made that promise empty. They asserted a right to pursue their chosen defendant and contended that the district court had improperly relied on administrative-compensation precedents. The panel rejected both objections on the particular record. (Slip 19–22.)

## An adequate remedy for the injury need not come from the same defendant

GE supported its assertion of amenability to service through a Japanese-law expert. But the court recognized that ordinary service consent did not alone resolve the channeling issue. Instead, Japan’s coordinated system addressed the same injuries and allowed the plaintiffs to obtain relief from TEPCO. Courts remained open to their damage claims, and many putative class members had already recovered. They supplied no basis to find that compensation effectively nonexistent. (Slip 20–24 & n.6.)

The court did not read Gulf Oil’s reference to two forums in which the defendant is amenable to process as barring every scheme that redirects liability to another party. Its concern was meaningful absence of jurisdiction and relief. Full and fair compensation for the same injury could suffice regardless of which entity ultimately paid. The panel analogized that principle to complete recovery from one of two jointly liable tortfeasors; it did not actually adjudicate GE and TEPCO to be joint tortfeasors. (Slip 24–25.)

The opinion further observed that Massachusetts choice-of-law rules would likely select Japanese substantive law, potentially making the channeling statute relevant even if the American suit continued. It treated that as an inference supporting the forum assessment, not a final choice-of-law judgment. Footnote 7 recognized that the plaintiffs disputed the statute’s extraterritorial application. The court did not resolve that disagreement into a holding that GE necessarily possessed blanket immunity in American courts. (Slip 25–26 & n.7.)

## The administrative remedies and the mandate

The panel also upheld considering direct claims and mediation. It found instructive precedents accepting no-fault or administrative systems that provide a remedy for the injury even when the exact American action cannot be maintained. The plaintiffs did not challenge the district court’s specific adequacy findings about Japan’s administrative compensation mechanisms; they instead tried to distinguish those precedents on the same-defendant jurisdiction issue already rejected. Japanese judicial relief against TEPCO remained available alongside the administrative avenues. Nothing barred these plaintiffs from presenting their own compensation claims through mediation or directly to the operator. (Slip 26–32.)

This is not a categorical ruling that any administrative fund is adequate, or that defendant consent always cures a foreign forum’s deficiencies. The holding depended on operative, accessible remedies for these injuries, their availability to these plaintiffs, and the narrowed appellate arguments. It did not award compensation, establish GE’s liability, decide the Convention’s jurisdictional effect, or independently reassess waived convenience objections. The panel **affirmed dismissal of the amended complaint for forum non conveniens**. (Slip 32.) Independent-review status appears in the accompanying register.
