# King County v. Taxpayers of King County
133 Wash. 2d 584, 949 P.2d 1260 (1997), as amended October 9 and December 23, 1997 | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
The Washington Supreme Court, en banc, affirmed summary judgment validating $336 million in bonds for a new Seattle Mariners stadium. Justice Talmadge wrote for seven justices, joined by Chief Justice Durham and Justices Dolliver, Smith, Guy, Johnson, and Alexander. Justice Sanders dissented, joined by Justice Madsen. The decision was initially issued June 13, 1997; the assigned Pacific Reporter citation contains the amended opinion, replacing the earlier report at 938 P.2d 309. Its consideration ruling is part of a bond-validation action, not a private suit to collect stadium rent. (1261–1267, 1274–1275; editor’s amendment note.)

## Facts and procedural chronology
The Mariners’ owners acquired the team in 1992 to keep it in Seattle. Discussions with King County led to a proposed new stadium. At an October 1995 special session, the legislature enacted the Stadium Act authorizing a county with at least one million residents to use specified taxes for stadium financing. King County enacted Ordinance 12000, created the public facilities district, and imposed authorized taxes. A special .017 percent sales-and-use tax was credited against the ordinary statewide rate, rather than added to it. (1263–1264.)

The district requested $336 million in construction financing in January 1997. The council authorized the bonds through Ordinance 12593 and subsequently provided for their sale through Ordinance 12686. At the time of the court’s decision, the bonds had been sold but proceeds remained in escrow pending release or refund according to the litigation’s outcome. Opponents pursued both court challenges and proposed voting measures. Initiative 16 would require voter approval for county facility debt exceeding $50 million and would operate from January 1, 1997, potentially reaching the stadium bonds retroactively. A separate proposed referendum sought a vote on the bond ordinance. (1263–1264.)

The county brought an action under RCW 7.25 against representative taxpayers to validate the bonds, declare Initiative 16 inapplicable, and establish that the bond ordinance was not subject to referendum. Following cross-motions, the trial court granted county summary judgment, denied the taxpayers’ motion, and entered a declaratory judgment. After reconsideration failed, the taxpayers appealed; the Supreme Court accepted direct review. The dissent criticized the expedited proceedings and restrictions on discovery, but its central objection was the legal standard used to dispose of the gift claim. (1264–1266, 1275–1277.)

## Issues and governing framework
The court adopted three questions for bond validity: whether the government has legislative or constitutional authority to issue the bonds, whether the authorizing statute is constitutional, and whether the financed purpose is public. The parties seeking validation—the county and district—bore the burden of establishing validity. Because validation conclusively resolves matters within its scope, the court addressed the lease’s alleged gift, tax collection, delegation, and the proposed voting measures. Prior litigation had established the stadium’s public purpose, but the particular lease remained subject to a gift challenge. (1265–1266.)

The taxpayers argued that the lease’s return was grossly inadequate, making it an unconstitutional transfer of public value to a private team under article VIII, sections 5 and 7. They also challenged uniformity and disposition of tax revenues, delegation to the district, and refusal to submit the financing measures to voters. The assigned Contracts question concerns the relationship between legally sufficient consideration and the constitutional prohibition on gifts. (1266–1267, 1269–1274; majority n.7.)

## Majority: enforceable promises and public funds
The majority examined consideration and donative intent. It relied on the usual legal-sufficiency approach, which asks whether something supports a promise rather than comparing market values, and warned that routine judicial valuation would unduly interfere with governmental contracting. Its discussion also recognized the qualifications concerning donative intent or grossly inadequate return. It concluded neither was shown here and that the exchange was legally sufficient. The opinion does not simply hold that any private benefit is constitutionally permissible: it distinguishes incidental private benefits from prohibited public gifts and examines the actual obligations. (1266–1269.)

The Mariners promised to play at least ninety percent of their games at the stadium over the bonds’ term, contribute $45 million toward construction-related costs, and share profits after accounting for specified losses. The lease also required $700,000 annual rent, construction cost overruns, parking-facility funding deficiencies, first-class operation and maintenance, major repairs and capital improvements, and insurance protecting the district. (1267.)

The majority rejected claims that these promises were illusory. The $45 million obligation remained meaningful even if paid from revenues the team would otherwise receive. Profit sharing used generally accepted accounting principles and audit rights; a possibility of future breach did not make performance optional under the promise’s terms. Termination provisions depended on unmet district obligations, rather than permitting the team to leave at will. Other lease sections imposed substantial repair obligations, and required insurance protected the district as an additional insured. Thus, the court distinguished an enforceable obligation from doubts about whether the bargain was economically wise. (1268–1269.)

## Other majority holdings
The .017 percent stadium tax did not increase the state sales-tax rate above 6.5 percent because it was a credit. Nor did it release county residents from their share of state-purpose taxation: constructing the stadium served a state purpose. The revenues entered an account in the state treasury, and statutory directions for distributing the earmarked funds satisfied the constitutional objection to unappropriated discretionary spending. The legislature could delegate local implementation, including site and project scope, to a municipal entity under appropriate standards. (1269–1271; majority nn.8–9.)

Initiative 16 could not retroactively invalidate the bond contract or contradict the state’s grant of power to incur debt below 1.5 percent of taxable property value without voter approval. The taxpayers did not dispute that the bonds remained within that limit. The majority held that state law preempted the field and the authorized bond decision was not subject to the proposed initiative or referendum. It treated the initiative’s possible effect as ripe within the comprehensive validation proceeding even though the measure had not yet passed. (1271–1275; majority nn.10–13.)

Majority footnote 14 also rejected revival of a referendum challenge to the earlier policy ordinance. It invoked delay and prior litigation challenging that ordinance. This additional reasoning became a separate focus of the dissent; it should not be mistaken for a holding that the original referendum petition had actually been circulated and defeated at an election. (Majority n.14.)

## Sanders’s dissent
Sanders agreed that legal consideration existed but argued that contract enforceability could not answer whether the public had made a constitutionally forbidden gift. A peppercorn may support a private promise while leaving the public with a profound loss. Constitutional text and history, in his view, required protection against subsidies and examination of adequacy whenever evidence showed donative intent or grossly inadequate return. He emphasized the disjunctive formulation in precedent and argued that the majority’s legal-sufficiency reasoning prevented the factual inquiry those precedents required. (1277–1281; dissent nn.3–7.)

The dissent identified evidence and inferences that, viewed favorably to the taxpayers on summary judgment, warranted trial. Communications emphasized saving the team and improving its profitability. A former district board member described an unfavorable allocation of control and risk. Economic experts criticized the rent, anticipated little or no profit sharing, and questioned the stadium’s residual value after the lease. Sanders considered maintenance, insurance, and other team expenses predominantly costs of operating a publicly provided private business site, rather than an adequate return to the county. His annual-subsidy estimates and predictions were evidence supporting his position, not findings adopted by the majority. (1281–1287; dissent nn.8–11.)

Sanders also disputed using laches and a prior “day in court” to establish the earlier ordinance’s validity. He argued the county had prevented the timely referendum process, had neither pleaded nor proved laches in its appropriate defensive role, and could not profit from its own conduct. A suit by particular taxpayers did not necessarily preclude the rights of the entire defendant class. Although critical of curtailed discovery, he expressly would not reverse on discovery alone because the assembled record already sufficed under the correct substantive standard. He would at minimum require a trial on the gift question, rather than affirm validation on summary judgment. (1275–1277, 1287–1291; dissent nn.2, 12–14.)
