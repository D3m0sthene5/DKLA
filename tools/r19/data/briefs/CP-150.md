# Hart v. American Airlines, Inc.

**Civil Procedure · CP-150 · 61 Misc. 2d 41, 304 N.Y.S.2d 810 (Sup. Ct., N.Y. County 1969) · October 7, 1969**

This reported opinion bears the Hart caption together with Landano and Kirchstein, but decides the latter plaintiffs’ motions. Justice Frank gave New York claimants the benefit of a Texas federal judgment establishing American Airlines’ liability for the same crash, despite their absence from the earlier action. Their cross-motion for summary judgment on liability was granted; the airline’s motion to join liability issues for trial was denied, with damages left for assessment. New York’s Supreme Court here was a trial court, not the state’s highest appellate court.

On November 8, 1965, an American Airlines aircraft traveling from La Guardia to an airport in Covington crashed in Kentucky. Fifty-eight of the sixty-two people aboard died. Numerous suits followed in state and federal courts. The first completed trial, Creasy in the Northern District of Texas, lasted nineteen days and produced a plaintiff’s verdict against the airline. That court applied Kentucky’s wrongful-death statute and substantive negligence law. The judgment was affirmed on appeal. Landano and Kirchstein’s New York actions asserted the same airline-liability issue arising from the crash. American sought to have those issues tried jointly with twenty other cases already grouped under an Appellate Division order. The plaintiffs instead sought summary judgment based on the Texas adjudication. (61 Misc. 2d at 41–42.)

The court applied the New York Court of Appeals’ recent Schwartz formulation: an identical issue must have been necessarily decided and must be decisive in the current action, and the party to be bound must have had a full and fair opportunity to contest it. The airline’s liability for the crash met the identity requirement on the pleadings and papers. The court viewed this common airplane-crash liability question as lacking the participant-specific complications that can arise in a multi-vehicle collision. The airline’s full opportunity to contest liability during the nineteen-day Texas trial was undisputed; the airline bore the burden of establishing the absence of such an opportunity and did not meet it. (Id. at 42–43.)

American principally relied on a previous decision by Justice Quinn in the Hart matter, affirmed without opinion. That ruling had refused to apply New York collateral-estoppel law for nonresident dependents of a deceased nonresident breadwinner with no significant New York contacts. Frank distinguished it. Landano and Kirchstein and their decedents were New York domiciliaries. In his interest analysis, New York had the superior interest in the preclusion question affecting its own residents, while Texas had no legitimate interest in imposing its restrictive preclusion rule upon them. The airline itself acknowledged that the earlier Hart decision was not strictly law of the case for these claimants. (Id. at 43–44.)

The opinion rejected the airline’s full-faith-and-credit objection by distinguishing enforcement of the Texas judgment itself from use of its liability determination in the New York suits. Frank characterized the latter as implementing New York policy against repeatedly trying a fully contested issue and producing inconsistent liability findings on essentially the same facts. This distinction is the court’s historical reasoning, not a statement that interstate judgment recognition generally escapes governing preclusion law merely by calling its use evidentiary. The assigned editors subsequently pose the broader choice-of-preclusion-law question and describe competing approaches. (Id. at 44; assigned Notes following printed page 1359.)

American’s remaining objections also failed. Mutuality had already been abandoned in the controlling New York decisions, so the plaintiffs’ absence from Creasy did not alone prevent their invoking it. The airline argued that Kentucky negligence law was less favorable than New York law, but Frank concluded that a New York trial would likewise apply Kentucky substantive conduct law because the crash occurred there. He distinguished that substantive-law conclusion from using New York’s preclusion doctrine. Conjectures that the Texas verdict reflected prejudice against corporations or sympathy lacked supporting evidence. Nor did the jury’s supposed ignorance of the verdict’s potential consequences for other claimants undermine its decision: whether the airline was negligent did not depend on the eventual number of claims. (61 Misc. 2d at 44–46.)

The plaintiffs’ **summary-judgment cross-motion on liability was granted**, American’s **joint-trial motion was denied**, and an order assessing damages was to be settled. The ruling did not fix individual damage awards or decide every pending crash claim. Frank alone issued the opinion; the full reported text contains no judicial footnotes or separate writing. The assigned principal on printed pages 1357–1359 abbreviates the earlier Hart comparison and some fairness objections. Its value is as a concrete historical example of nonmutual offensive preclusion across judicial systems, with the governing-law reasoning accurately attributed to this 1969 trial-court opinion and no unchecked assertion of present-day status.
