# Ryan, LLC v. Federal Trade Commission
746 F. Supp. 3d 369 (N.D. Tex. Aug. 20, 2024), No. 3:24-CV-00986-E, ECF No. 211 | Contracts

## Rule challenged and procedural history

Ryan challenged the FTC’s April 2024 Non-Compete Clause Rule. The Chamber of Commerce of the United States, Business Roundtable, Texas Association of Business, and Longview Chamber of Commerce intervened on the plaintiff’s side. The rule treated entering or enforcing most employment noncompetes as an unfair method of competition. It barred new agreements even for qualifying senior executives, while allowing their existing agreements to remain enforceable; for other covered workers it also reached existing restrictions. The rule included exceptions for bona fide business sales, previously accrued causes of action, and certain good-faith beliefs about inapplicability. It displaced state permission for prohibited agreements while preserving parallel state restrictions and enforcement authority. Its scheduled effective date was September 4, 2024. (Filed memorandum 4–7 & n. 5.)

Ryan sued on April 23 and amended its complaint May 1, invoking the APA and Declaratory Judgment Act. On July 3, Judge Ada E. Brown granted preliminary protection limited to Ryan and the named intervenors, based on a likely statutory-authority violation, likely arbitrary and capricious action, and irreparable injury. The August 20 decision was a later merits stage: the plaintiffs’ July 19 summary-judgment motions and the FTC’s August 2 cross-motion sought a ruling on the rule’s validity and disputed the appropriate final remedy. The earlier party-limited injunction was not the scope of the August order. (2 & nn. 1–2; 7–9.)

## Claims and governing review

Ryan asserted lack of delegated rulemaking power, unconstitutional delegation, retroactive invalidation, arbitrary and capricious decisionmaking, and unconstitutional insulation of the Commission from presidential control. The intervenors additionally emphasized the statutory meaning of unfair competition, the unsupported breadth of a categorical ban, inadequate consideration of less costly alternatives, and a defective cost-benefit analysis. The FTC answered that Congress authorized both rulemaking and adjudication; that noncompete regulation fit its competition mission; that the delegation had an intelligible principle; and that the record reasonably supported prospective regulation. Its response also disputed the major questions, retroactivity, and removal arguments. These were litigants’ positions, not all separate holdings of the court. (13–14.)

The court applied the ordinary cross-motion summary-judgment framework, examining each motion independently with inferences favoring its opponent. Cross-motions did not themselves eliminate genuine factual disputes. The APA separately required independent resolution of statutory authority and review of the agency’s stated reasons; the court cited Loper Bright’s rejection of Chevron deference. It found two grounds sufficient to grant plaintiffs’ motions: the FTC exceeded its statutory authority and its rule was arbitrary and capricious. Having resolved those grounds, it expressly declined further discussion of the remaining arguments as unnecessary. The opinion therefore is not a holding invalidating the Commission’s removal protections or all congressional delegations to it. (9–16 & n. 6; 26.)

## Why section 6(g) did not authorize this substantive rule

Section 5 of the FTC Act created a scheme for preventing unfair competition through administrative proceedings and cease-and-desist orders. Section 6 listed additional powers, mostly investigative or ministerial, and subsection (g) allowed the Commission to classify corporations and make rules carrying out the statutory subchapter. The court acknowledged that the statute gave the FTC some rulemaking authority, including in relation to competition, but concluded that section 6(g) did not confer substantive legislative rulemaking of the kind exercised here. Its reading distinguished rules governing agency procedure from rules independently imposing binding duties on regulated parties. (3–4; 15–17 & nn. 7–9.)

Text and structure supported that distinction in the court’s view. The asserted substantive authority occupied part of the seventh item in a list of twelve predominantly investigative powers, shared a provision with corporate classification, and did not expressly refer to section 5’s substantive standards. Section 6(g) also supplied no penalty for rule violations. The court relied on the historical convention of pairing legislative rulemaking grants with sanctions, contrasting the penalties attached to section 5 adjudicatory orders. That absence and placement made the provision a housekeeping authorization rather than the sweeping substantive delegation the FTC asserted. (17–19 & n. 10.)

The history likewise did not persuade the court that Congress had made that grant. The FTC had disclaimed substantive rulemaking for its first forty-eight years, asserted it in 1962, and gained the D.C. Circuit’s approval in National Petroleum Refiners. The August opinion acknowledged that precedent and the agency’s subsequent rules, but did not adopt its interpretation. Congress later expressly granted binding rulemaking power for specific subjects and, in the 1975 Magnuson-Moss amendments, for unfair or deceptive acts or practices. The court reasoned that section 18’s preservation of any competition-rulemaking authority did not itself affirmatively create substantive competition-rulemaking power. It likewise rejected the FTC’s argument that the unchanged language and definitions in the 1980 amendments ratified such authority. Congress had to delegate the power; the agency could not assemble it from references that did not make that affirmative grant. (19–22.)

## Why the categorical ban was arbitrary and capricious

The court also found a distinct failure of reasoned decisionmaking. It recognized that arbitrary-and-capricious review was deferential, that judges could not substitute policy preferences, and that an agency need not commission perfect empirical studies. The agency nevertheless had to connect its evidence and rationale to its chosen policy and address important aspects of the problem. The court could review only the reasons the FTC actually supplied. (22–24 & n. 11.)

The nearly universal prohibition, of indefinite duration, was broader than the state approaches addressed by the studies on which the FTC relied. The court found no adequately explained bridge from those varied, fact-specific enforcement settings to a categorical nationwide rule. In its assessment, the record used inconsistent and flawed evidence, overlooked positive benefits and substantial evidence supporting noncompetes, and did not justify targeting virtually all agreements instead of harmful classes of agreements. That was a criticism of the explanation and breadth of the rule, not a finding that every individual noncompete was fair or legally enforceable. (24 & n. 11.)

The FTC also had not adequately justified its rejection of alternatives and treatment of reliance interests. Its stated concern that case-by-case enforcement threatened workers, and its view that other devices could protect employers, did not sufficiently explain why less disruptive distinctions or exceptions should be rejected. The court concluded that the rule fell outside a reasonably explained zone of reasonableness. This independent APA ground would condemn the rule’s actual reasoning even apart from the statutory-authority conclusion. (25 & n. 12.)

## Exact disposition and remedial scope

The court granted Ryan’s and the intervenors’ summary-judgment motions, denied the FTC’s cross-motion, held the rule unlawful, and set it aside under APA section 706(2). It rejected the FTC’s request to restrict relief to the parties: under the Fifth Circuit authorities the court applied, setting aside agency action had nationwide effect. The rule could not be enforced or take effect on September 4 or thereafter. Footnote 14 expressly declined a separate discussion of the parties’ “vacatur” arguments in favor of the statutory instruction to set aside the rule. The operative nationwide set-aside remains clear despite that terminology choice. The original final page contains Judge Brown’s signature; no separate writing or panel vote accompanies this district-court decision. (26–27 & nn. 13–14.)
