Citation
Gunn v. Minton, 568 U.S. 251, 133 S. Ct. 1059, 185 L. Ed. 2d 72 (2013). Supreme Court of the United
States, argued January 16, 2013, decided February 20, 2013. Chief Justice Roberts delivered the opinion of a
unanimous Court. No separate opinions were filed. Casebook pages 347-351. The online judicial text lacks

Parties
Vernon F. Minton was the patent owner, the plaintiff suing his former attorneys for legal malpractice, and
the respondent in the Supreme Court. Jerry W. Gunn and the other petitioners were the attorneys who had
represented Minton in the earlier patent suit and the defendants in the malpractice action. Minton contended
that their failure to make a timely patent-law argument caused his loss. The attorneys disputed that the
omitted argument would have succeeded. Casebook p. 347; full judicial opinion, Part I.
Material Facts
The casebook summarizes the underlying dispute: Minton lost a patent-infringement suit and then alleged
that his lawyers’ failure to raise an experimental-use argument caused the loss. Experimental use was a
claimed exception to the rule preventing a patent when the invention had been on sale more than a year
before the patent application. The malpractice action therefore required a hypothetical inquiry into what
would have happened in the earlier patent case had the attorneys made the argument on time. Casebook pp.
347-349.
The following details come from Part I of the full opinion, which the casebook replaces with its summary.
Minton developed TEXCEN, a securities-trading system, leased it to a brokerage in March 1995, and
applied for a related patent a little more than a year later. The patent issued in January 2000. When he sued
NASD and NASDAQ for infringement, they relied on the earlier lease to argue that the patent was invalid
under the on-sale bar. Minton first raised experimental use in a motion for reconsideration, contending that
the lease had been part of ongoing testing. Full judicial opinion, Part I (omitted from the casebook).
Procedural History
In the original federal patent litigation, the district court held the patent invalid and denied reconsideration.
The Federal Circuit affirmed, treating the experimental-use argument as waived because it had not been
raised on time. Minton then sued his attorneys in Texas state court. The attorneys obtained summary
judgment, a decision without trial, because the court found insufficient evidence that the lease was
experimental. These details appear in the omitted Part I of the full opinion. Full judicial opinion, Part I
(omitted from the casebook).
On appeal in the malpractice case, Minton argued that the state court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction,
meaning authority to decide this category of dispute. He invoked 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), which reserves cases
arising under federal patent law to the federal courts, and sought to begin again in federal court. The
intermediate Texas court rejected that argument and affirmed on the merits. The Texas Supreme Court
reversed on jurisdiction, reasoning that the patent question was substantial and that uniform application of
patent law favored a federal forum. The U.S. Supreme Court granted review. The detailed sequence comes

Issue
Does a state-law legal-malpractice claim fall within exclusive federal patent jurisdiction because deciding
whether the lawyers caused the client’s loss requires resolving a disputed patent-law issue in a hypothetical
reconstruction of earlier litigation?
Rule and Holding
No. Federal jurisdiction over a state-created claim requires a federal issue that is necessarily raised, actually
disputed, substantial to the federal system as a whole, and capable of resolution in federal court without
disrupting the congressionally approved division of responsibility between federal and state courts. All four
conditions must be met. The same arising-under analysis governs the general federal-question statute, §
1331, and the patent statute, § 1338(a), although the latter also excludes state jurisdiction over qualifying
patent claims. Casebook pp. 347-349.
Minton’s malpractice claim necessarily raised an actually disputed federal patent issue. It nevertheless failed
the substantiality and federal-state-balance requirements. Resolving the hypothetical patent question would
not alter the actual patent judgment or control future patent litigation. Section 1338(a) therefore did not
deprive the Texas courts of jurisdiction over the malpractice claim. Casebook pp. 349-351.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the judgment of the Texas Supreme Court and remanded for further
proceedings consistent with its opinion. It resolved the jurisdictional question, not the ultimate merits of
Minton’s malpractice claim. The underlying judgment that his patent was invalid remained unchanged.
Casebook pp. 349-351.
Reasoning
The claim’s source was state malpractice law, not federal patent law. It therefore could reach federal court
only through the limited category of state claims containing a sufficiently important federal issue. The Court
applied Grable’s four requirements separately, rather than treating any necessary federal-law question as
sufficient. Casebook pp. 347-349.
The patent issue was necessary because Texas malpractice law required Minton to establish duty, breach,
causation, and damages. To show that the failure to make an argument caused his injury, he had to show that
the argument would have changed the earlier result. Deciding that hypothetical question required applying
patent law. The issue was also actually disputed: Minton maintained that experimental use would have
defeated the on-sale bar, while the lawyers maintained that the exception did not apply. Casebook pp.
348-349.
The Texas Supreme Court had measured substantiality by the issue’s importance to the parties and its
centrality to the malpractice claim. The Supreme Court rejected that approach because those considerations
already explain why a federal issue is necessary and disputed. Substantiality instead asks whether the
question matters to the federal system as a whole. In Grable, federal tax collection and the reliability of
tax-sale titles supplied that broader interest. Minton’s backward-looking malpractice dispute had no
comparable consequence: even if the state court found that a timely argument should have saved the patent,
the actual patent would remain invalid. Casebook p. 349.

State malpractice judgments would not determine the controlling content of patent law. Federal courts
deciding actual patent disputes are not bound by state courts’ hypothetical patent rulings. Moreover, a state
court reconstructing an earlier federal case would ordinarily follow the federal precedents that would have
governed it. A novel question that arose repeatedly would eventually receive an authoritative federal answer;
one that rarely arose was unlikely to have substantial systemic importance. Casebook pp. 349-350.
Minton argued that the state decision might bind later proceedings involving a related patent through issue
preclusion, the rule that can prevent relitigation of an issue already determined. The Court found that
proposed effect uncertain and unsupported by an example supplied by Minton. Even assuming some
preclusive effect, a consequence confined to the parties and patents before the state court would remain too
fact-specific to establish federal arising-under jurisdiction. Greater federal expertise, or the possibility of a
state court’s error about patent law, did not independently supply jurisdiction. Casebook p. 350.
The balance of judicial responsibilities also favored state jurisdiction. The Court found no substantial federal
interest that required moving this dispute to federal court, while states have a special responsibility for
professional standards and a particularly strong interest in regulating lawyers. Congress’s reservation of
actual patent cases to federal courts did not show an intention to remove ordinary state malpractice claims
merely because they required answering a hypothetical patent question. Casebook pp. 350-351.
Separate Opinions
The Supreme Court’s judgment and opinion were unanimous. No separate Supreme Court opinion was filed.
The dissent described in Part I belonged to the Texas Supreme Court proceedings, not to the U.S. Supreme
Court.
Notes
Narrow reading — interpretation. A malpractice claim alleging that lawyers lost a completed patent case
by failing to raise an argument does not arise under § 1338(a) when deciding the hypothetical argument
cannot change the patent’s actual invalidity and has no sufficiently broad federal consequence. The decision
does not resolve whether these lawyers committed malpractice.
Broad reading — interpretation. A necessary and disputed federal issue within a state claim remains
insufficient without importance to the federal system and compatibility with the intended federal-state
division of work. Gunn makes substantiality a distinct inquiry, not a synonym for the issue’s importance to a
litigant. Its discussion strongly disfavors federal jurisdiction over this type of patent-malpractice claim, but
does not establish that every conceivable malpractice claim containing federal law can never qualify.
