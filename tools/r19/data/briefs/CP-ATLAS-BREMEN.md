# The Bremen v. Zapata Off-Shore Co.

**407 U.S. 1 (1972) · Supreme Court of the United States · June 12, 1972**

## A negotiated towage contract and a storm during the voyage

Zapata, a Houston-based corporation, hired the German company Unterweser in November 1967 to tow its self-elevating drilling rig, *Chaparral*, from Louisiana to a drilling location off Ravenna, Italy. Zapata solicited competing bids; Unterweser was the low bidder. The submitted contract designated the London Court of Justice for any dispute. It also contained provisions disclaiming responsibility for navigation errors and assigning damage to the towed object to its owner. Zapata changed several contract terms but left the forum and exculpatory clauses intact before executing it. Unterweser accepted the changes. 407 U.S. at 2–3 & n.2.

The tug *Bremen* departed Louisiana on January 5, 1968. Four days later, a severe storm in international Gulf waters caused the rig’s raised elevator legs to break off, substantially damaging the rig. Zapata directed the tug to Tampa as the nearest port of refuge. Zapata had elected to self-insure its rigs, so insurance did not compensate this loss. These facts explain both the American forum’s immediate practical connection and the parties’ earlier interest in choosing a predictable forum for a transoceanic voyage. Id. at 3 & n.2.

## American litigation, English litigation, and a defensive limitation proceeding

Zapata sued in admiralty at Tampa on January 12, claiming $3.5 million for negligent towage and breach of contract against Unterweser personally and the tug in rem. The tug was arrested and released after security was furnished. Unterweser sought dismissal for lack of jurisdiction or forum non conveniens, alternatively a stay for litigation in London. It also sued Zapata for breach of contract in the English High Court. English courts rejected Zapata’s jurisdictional challenge and treated the agreed forum as presumptively enforceable absent adequate reasons to depart from it. Id. at 3–5 & nn.3–4.

The American court had not resolved Unterweser’s motion when the six-month deadline to initiate a limitation-of-liability proceeding approached. Unterweser filed that proceeding in Tampa in July 1968, expressly reserving its reliance on the London clause. The limitation court enjoined outside proceedings, and Zapata refiled its claim there. Unterweser later counterclaimed for the contract claim and salvage while maintaining its forum position. Id. at 5 & nn.5–6.

The district court eventually denied dismissal or a stay, following the Fifth Circuit’s traditional hostility toward clauses said to oust judicial jurisdiction. It applied ordinary forum non conveniens balancing, under which the plaintiff’s chosen forum ordinarily controls unless the balance strongly favors the defendant. It later refused to stay the limitation proceeding and restrained further London litigation. It considered Unterweser’s invocation of the Limitation Act an additional reason to require the company to litigate entirely in Tampa. Id. at 6–7 & n.7.

A divided Fifth Circuit panel affirmed, and the en banc court adopted that opinion with six of 14 judges dissenting. The majority emphasized the casualty’s Gulf location, nearby witnesses and repair activity, available German depositions, and England’s lack of a connection apart from the clause. It also feared that English enforcement of the exculpatory clauses would defeat American substantive protections. The record suggested materially smaller limitation exposure under English law. Id. at 7–8 & nn.8–9.

## Holding and exact disposition

Federal courts sitting in admiralty should treat a freely agreed forum-selection clause as prima facie valid and enforce it unless the resisting party establishes adequate grounds to reject it. The lower courts had assigned the clause insufficient weight and improperly made Unterweser prove that London was more convenient. The burden belonged to Zapata to show that enforcement would be unreasonable or unjust, or that the clause was invalid through fraud or overreaching. Id. at 8–10, 15.

The Supreme Court **vacated the Fifth Circuit’s judgment and remanded**, allowing Zapata an opportunity to meet the demanding standard for serious inconvenience. It did not conclusively direct a London merits judgment, determine negligence, or declare that every forum clause is enforceable. The assigned editorial account describes the result loosely as a reversal; the operative judicial disposition is vacatur and remand. Id. at 19–20.

Chief Justice Burger wrote the Court’s opinion, joined in the announced lineup by Brennan, Stewart, White, Marshall, Blackmun, Powell, and Rehnquist. White’s separate statement withheld agreement insofar as the opinion commented on issues remanded for the district court’s initial consideration. Douglas alone dissented and would have affirmed. Thus eight Justices supported the judgment, with White expressly qualifying his agreement on the remand discussion. Id. at 1, 20–24.

## Contractual certainty and the jurisdiction-ouster objection

The majority rejected the premise that domestic courts must retain international commercial disputes merely because they possess jurisdiction and are convenient. Businesses dealing across borders need predictable arrangements; American participation in world commerce cannot depend on insisting that American courts and rules govern every dispute. A sophisticated international transaction may appropriately select a neutral forum with relevant expertise. England supplied such neutrality and extensive admiralty experience. Id. at 8–12 & nn.10–13.

The forum clause did not actually strip the Tampa court of jurisdiction. It raised the distinct question whether that court should exercise its authority beyond enforcing the parties’ legitimate agreed expectations. Describing enforcement as an impermissible ouster concealed the real inquiry. Contractual submission to another court was consistent with established recognition that parties may agree in advance to jurisdictional arrangements. Id. at 10–12.

This particular agreement involved capable companies and a valuable rig crossing many jurisdictions, with numerous possible accident locations and ports of refuge. Choosing London removed uncertainty before any casualty occurred. Tampa’s practical connection resulted from a contingency along the voyage rather than from a previously chosen forum. Zapata had meaningful ability to alter the contract, even though it denied specifically negotiating the forum clause. The majority inferred that forum certainty formed part of the bargain; it did not treat the parties’ affidavits about express discussion as undisputed. Id. at 12–14 & nn.14–16.

## Public policy and serious inconvenience remain possible objections

A clause can be unenforceable if it contradicts a strong forum policy declared by statute or judicial decision. The majority therefore addressed *Bisso*, which disapproved contractual immunity from negligent towage. It regarded that policy as arising from the towage business in American waters and as not controlling this freely negotiated international agreement concerning a foreign party’s conduct in international waters. It did not abolish *Bisso* for domestic towage. Id. at 15–17 & n.17.

The Court likewise did not say that inconvenience is never relevant. In this international transaction, however, foreseeable inconvenience was ordinarily part of the bargained arrangement. Zapata needed to show that litigating in London would be so gravely difficult that it would effectively lose a meaningful day in court. Ordinary imbalance in witness convenience was insufficient. Depositions could serve Zapata abroad just as the lower courts thought they could serve Unterweser in Florida. Id. at 16–19 & nn.18–19.

The majority distinguished the possible concerns raised by two Americans sending an essentially local dispute to a distant foreign forum. Such remoteness could indicate adhesion, an unforeseen controversy, or attempted avoidance of a strong domestic policy. Those examples explain the limits of the reasoning; they were not the factual situation the Court decided. Id. at 17.

## Why the limitation filing did not surrender the agreed forum

Unterweser’s American limitation complaint was a defensive response to Zapata’s decision to disregard the clause while the court left the initial dismissal motion unresolved. The majority considered it imprudent to assume that limitation could safely be pursued later only through a defense. The filing consequently did not preclude reliance on the contractual forum. Footnote 20 explains the risk in the alternative course Zapata suggested. Id. at 19–20 & n.20.

Zapata also argued for the first time in the Supreme Court that the clause was not exclusive or did not cover an in rem action. The Court considered the actual language mandatory and comprehensive. That conclusion concerned this clause’s wording; it did not make every provision naming a court automatically exclusive. Id. at 20 & n.21.

## White’s reservation and Douglas’s dissent

White agreed with the judgment and general opinion but preferred to leave the remanded issues for the district court without the majority’s commentary. His short statement preserves that qualification rather than creating a different enforcement test. Id. at 20.

Douglas emphasized the limitation court’s traditional equitable power to gather a maritime controversy and restrain competing litigation. He thought Unterweser’s own limitation petition subjected it to that full authority. He also considered the English forum clause inseparable from the exculpatory terms and incompatible with *Bisso*’s protection against negligent towage. Even equal bargaining power did not remove the policy of deterring negligence by requiring wrongdoers to pay damages. His extended final asterisk note compared foreign-law clauses and criticized the forum clause as an equivalent way to evade domestic policy. Id. at 20–24 & dissent n.*.

Nearby witnesses, repair work, and available German depositions reinforced Douglas’s view that Tampa was sensible. He would have affirmed the injunction and denial of a stay as a proper exercise of equitable discretion. These objections explain the dissent’s disagreement with the majority’s treatment of international contractual expectations; they did not become the governing holding. Id. at 23–24.
