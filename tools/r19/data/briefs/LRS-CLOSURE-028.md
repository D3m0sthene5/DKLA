# Burnet v. Chicago Portrait Co.
285 U.S. 1 (1932) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and procedural posture

On February 23, 1932, the Supreme Court affirmed the Seventh Circuit’s judgment allowing a domestic corporation credit for its share of income taxes that a foreign subsidiary paid to New South Wales. Chief Justice Hughes wrote the sole reported opinion; no separate writing or numerical vote is recorded. The question was whether New South Wales constituted a “foreign country” for the credit authorized by § 238(e) of the Revenue Act of 1921. The Court held that the phrase included a foreign government competent to impose the tax, although that government was a component of a larger international unit. (4–5, 21.)

## Facts and litigation

Chicago Portrait Company, an Illinois corporation, owned 51 percent of the stock of International Art Company, Ltd., of Sydney, Australia. In determining its federal income tax for 1923, it claimed credit corresponding to income taxes the subsidiary had paid abroad. The Commissioner allowed credit for taxes paid to the Commonwealth of Australia and New Zealand, but denied credit for the tax paid to the State of New South Wales. The resulting deficiency rested on treating that state as outside the statutory category of a foreign country. The Board of Tax Appeals rejected that distinction, and the Seventh Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to decide the disputed statutory classification. (4–5.)

Section 238(a) allowed a domestic corporation, within specified limitations, to credit income, war-profits, and excess-profits taxes paid to a foreign country or a possession of the United States. Section 238(e) addressed dividends from a foreign corporation in which the domestic corporation owned a majority of voting stock. It treated the domestic corporation as having paid a proportionate share of the foreign subsidiary’s tax on the accumulated profits from which the dividends were distributed. The controversy concerned the identity of the taxing government, not an allegation that New South Wales lacked authority to impose the tax. (4–5 n. 1, 21.)

## Parties’ positions and statutory issue

The Commissioner argued that “foreign country” meant an internationally sovereign state, with Treasury regulations permitting an additional category of self-governing colonies. New South Wales was a constituent state, so its tax did not qualify. The government invoked Treasury’s administrative construction and argued that congressional reenactment adopted it. Chicago Portrait maintained that the language encompassed the competent foreign taxing authority and that excluding its payment contradicted the statute’s relief from international double taxation. (2–5, 15–21.)

## Reasoning from language and statutory purpose

Hughes rejected the premise that “country” possessed one technical meaning controlling every statute. It could refer to territory, an international state, or a government exercising authority over a particular area or subject. Earlier decisions applying the word in tariff and deportation statutes used meanings responsive to those statutes’ purposes. The relevant meaning therefore had to emerge from this tax legislation rather than an abstract classification in international law. (5–7 & nn. 2–4.)

The credit’s purpose was to mitigate double taxation and facilitate the foreign enterprises of domestic corporations. That burden arose when a competent foreign government taxed the income; whether it independently constituted an international person did not diminish the burden. The subsidiary-credit provision extended the same relief to a domestic corporation’s share of taxes on profits earned through a controlled foreign corporation. A restriction to internationally sovereign governments lacked a connection to that practical objective. (7–10 & nn. 5–7.)

The legislative history of the tax provisions supported that understanding. Earlier federal statutes permitted deductions for taxes imposed by foreign governments or governmental authorities without making international standing decisive. The Revenue Act of 1918 substituted a credit for the older deduction to afford greater relief and expressly included taxes paid to possessions of the United States. The 1921 statute extended the credit mechanism to the foreign-subsidiary setting. Nothing in that sequence indicated that Congress intended to narrow the covered foreign taxing authorities in the manner asserted by the Commissioner. (10–16 & nn. 8–19.)

The government stressed that comparable state taxes within the United States yielded deductions rather than these credits. Hughes treated that as a difference in domestic tax policy, not proof that Congress used “foreign country” inconsistently across the provisions governing foreign taxation. Nor did changes between taxes paid “to” a government and taxes imposed “by authority” of it establish the proposed sovereign-state restriction. The statutory language and relief it sought to provide remained the central guides. (13–15.)

## Administrative construction and reenactment

The Court recognized that courts ordinarily gave great weight to executive construction of statutes entrusted to the executive department. That consideration had limits: courts were not bound by the department’s interpretation; a construction lacking uniformity and consistency was entitled only to the force of its valid reasons; and an ambiguous regulation offered little help in resolving statutory ambiguity. These qualifications arose from examining the actual Treasury materials, not from denying any relevance to administrative practice. (16.)

An initial Treasury regulation defined foreign taxation by reference to a government’s power to impose the tax and included Canadian governmental taxation. An April 1919 amendment introduced the categories of foreign sovereign states and self-governing colonies while excluding municipalities unless independently sovereign. The Court found no textual or purposive justification for that restriction. Degrees of self-government supplied no useful criterion for the statutory problem; competency to impose the tax did. (16–18 & nn. 20–21.)

Other regulations concerning exemptions and reciprocal treatment listed foreign political units in ways that failed to yield a coherent sovereign-state test. The Court read those materials and the Commissioner’s interpretations together and found uncertainty rather than a clear, consistently applied administrative construction. Consequently, reenactment of the legislation in 1921 did not establish congressional adoption of a definite rule excluding taxes imposed by New South Wales. The later regulations and individual administrative rulings did not cure the absence of sufficient reasons for the restriction. (18–21 & nn. 22–26.)

## Holding, judgment, and limits

The Court held that the statutory phrase embraced a foreign government competent to lay the tax for which credit was sought. Because New South Wales’s authority to impose this income tax was unquestioned, there was no need to determine its constitutional relationship to the Commonwealth of Australia or its international status. The Seventh Circuit’s judgment was affirmed. This was a determination of an actual tax-credit entitlement under the 1921 Act, not solely a general observation about deference. (21.)
