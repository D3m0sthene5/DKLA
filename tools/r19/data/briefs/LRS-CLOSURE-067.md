# Arizona Grocery Co. v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co.
284 U.S. 370 (1932), decided January 4, 1932 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Rate orders followed by a retrospective award

Railroads carried sugar from California to Phoenix under rates that the Interstate Commerce Commission had addressed in successive proceedings. On June 22, 1921, after a complaint and hearing, the Commission found the challenged rate unreasonable to the extent it exceeded 96.5 cents per hundred pounds, including for future transportation. It ordered a rate no higher than that amount. The carriers published a 96-cent rate on September 17, then voluntarily reduced it to 86.5 cents. Other complainants initiated a new challenge on November 3, 1922. While that challenge was pending, the carriers lowered their rate again, to 84 cents, on January 10, 1924. (381–382 & nn. 1–2.)

On February 25, 1925, the Commission prescribed a new future maximum of 71 cents, modifying its earlier order. It found a basis for reparation under the older rate without then awarding it. After further proceedings, including consolidation of Arizona Grocery’s reparation claim, the Commission determined that since July 1, 1922, reasonable rates would have been 73 cents from Northern California and 71 cents from Southern California. It awarded damages measured by the excess of the 86.5- and 84-cent rates over those amounts. Arizona Grocery’s affected shipments ran from February 21, 1923, through February 5, 1925: all occurred while the first prospective order remained effective and before the second order took effect. (382 & nn. 2–3.)

The carriers argued that compliance with the Commission’s prospective rate prescription foreclosed retrospective damages. The Commission answered that a more comprehensive evidentiary record showed its earlier reasonable-rate determination had been erroneous and that substantial justice required correction. When the railroads did not pay, Arizona Grocery obtained a district-court judgment enforcing the award. The Ninth Circuit reversed and entered judgment for the railroads. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to decide the Commission’s statutory authority. (382–383 & n. 4.)

## Carrier-made rates under the original statute

Justice Roberts’s opinion reconstructed the difference between a carrier’s own tariff and a rate prescribed through delegated legislative authority. At common law, a carrier fixed its rates but risked damages if they were unreasonable. The original Interstate Commerce Act required published tariffs and uniform adherence to them, making the published rate the legally collectible rate. But publication alone did not make it substantively lawful: the statute preserved the duty to charge a reasonable rate, and a shipper could pay the required tariff and seek reparation for its unreasonable excess. (383–384 & nn. 5–9.)

The original Act assigned the Commission the factual determination of reasonableness and the award of reparation, leaving courts to enforce an unpaid award. That function concerned past charges and was judicial in character. The Commission initially lacked power to prescribe a specific rate or maximum for the future. A carrier could respond to a finding of unreasonableness by publishing a different rate, potentially requiring another proceeding. Reparation remained appropriate because the carrier, rather than the Commission, had independently chosen the challenged rate. (384–385 & nn. 9–12.)

## The later grant of legislative rate-setting authority

The Hepburn Act and Transportation Act changed the allocation of responsibility. Congress authorized the Commission to prescribe maximum reasonable rates and later specific rates, minimum rates, or maximum-and-minimum boundaries. When the Commission prescribed a reasonable rate for the future, it exercised delegated legislative authority and its directive had statutory force. The majority treated federal delegated rate setting in this respect like the familiar rate-setting power of state commissions. (385–386 & nn. 13–16.)

Arizona Grocery argued that a maximum still left the carrier free to choose the actual rate, so the carrier retained its common-law responsibility to keep that rate reasonable at its peril. The Court rejected that distinction. The Commission’s delegated authority was to prescribe a *reasonable* maximum, not knowingly authorize an unlawful one. The 1921 order expressly declared 96.5 cents reasonable for future service. A conforming rate therefore became both the published legal rate and a lawful, reasonable rate under the order. Setting a permissible boundary was legislative in quality just as setting a single exact rate was. (386–388 & n. 17.)

This did not make every Commission order immune from challenge. The majority’s note 15 explained constitutional review, review for excess of delegated authority, and review for findings lacking evidentiary support. But where a valid prospective directive governed, the Commission could not treat compliance as an actionable wrong by later deciding that it should have selected a different rate. The Court also noted the corresponding position of carriers: a carrier bound by a prescribed rate could seek a new future rate, but could not collect retrospective reparation from shippers on the ground that the earlier prescribed rate was too low. (386 n. 15; 387–388.)

## Dual functions do not permit retroactive repeal

A single proceeding and order could combine adjudication of past carrier-made rates with prescription of future rates. Combining those tasks did not erase their different legal character. In a later reparation proceeding the Commission, acting judicially, had to recognize the continuing prospective rule it had previously promulgated legislatively. It could not retroactively repeal that rule by declaring conforming charges unreasonable. (388–389 & n. 18.)

The Commission’s assertion that res judicata did not control missed the point. The Court did not decide whether an earlier adjudication of past rate reasonableness bound a subsequent adjudication. Estoppel by judgment concerned judicial acts; the present protection followed the validity of a legislative rate prescription. The agency could revise its prospective directive when circumstances required, but could not impose damages for conduct satisfying it while it governed. Nor did administrative inconvenience enlarge statutory authority. The great mass of carrier-made rates remained subject to ordinary reparation proceedings. (389–390.)

## Judgment, dissent, and assignment

The Court held that after a hearing establishing the maximum reasonable rate, the Commission could not use the same or additional evidence about the situation existing when it promulgated that order to impose reparation based on what it later thought it should have decided. It **affirmed the Ninth Circuit’s judgment for the railroads**, leaving Arizona Grocery without its district-court enforcement recovery. (390.)

Justices Holmes and Brandeis stated that they would reverse for the reasons in Judge Hutcheson’s concurrence in *Eagle Cotton Oil Co. v. Southern Railway Co.*, 51 F.2d 443, 445. The decision thus has two identified dissenters, rather than an unqualified unanimous result. (390.)
