# Earle v. Angell

157 Mass. 294, 32 N.E. 164 (1892) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural significance

The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court sustained a nephew’s exceptions to a directed verdict for his aunt’s executor. His testimony could support a finding that the aunt promised payment in exchange for his present promise to attend her funeral, and that she assented to any qualifications he added. The court did not itself find a binding contract or award the money. Holmes wrote the October 21, 1892 opinion; Field, C.J., Holmes, Knowlton, Morton, and Lathrop were present. There is no separate writing or judicial footnote. 294, 296.

## Actual facts and evidence

Benjamin A. Earle sued Charles A. Angell, executor of Mary Dewitt, for $500 allegedly promised during her lifetime. At trial before Maynard, J., Earle testified about a conversation with his aunt in Oxford, Massachusetts, in August 1883. She recalled his journey from the Philadelphia area to his uncle’s funeral in a severe snowstorm, when friends from Boston had been unable to attend. Concerned that few family members remained, she asked him to attend her own funeral if he outlived her, offering $500 and his expenses. His testimony included a more specifically reciprocal formulation: she offered payment if he would agree to come. Earle said he promised on his honor, provided he remained alive, received timely notice, and was able to travel. These were his account of the conversation, not jury findings. 294–295.

Earle also testified that his aunt reminded him of the agreement during a visit following his mother’s funeral in 1885. He renewed his assurance that he would come if able and notified in time. He was less certain of her additional statement that she would leave him $500 in her will. Dewitt died in 1887; Earle attended her funeral because of his promise. Soon after returning, he received a sealed writing in her handwriting, dated August 14, 1883, directing her executor to pay $500 if he attended her funeral. The report does not establish the precise date he received it. 295–296.

The executor offered no evidence. After Earle rested, the trial judge directed a verdict for the executor, and Earle alleged exceptions. The evidentiary question was therefore whether his proof permitted the contract claim to go forward, rather than whether the supreme court should resolve credibility or enter a final recovery. 296.

## Promise for promise and payment after death

Holmes explained that an oral contract to pay money after the promisor’s death could be valid. Payment postponed until death did not, by itself, defeat an otherwise binding lifetime contract. The aunt’s request for a promise to attend, and Earle’s stated promise in return, could provide a bilateral exchange; attendance was a condition of payment under that agreement. 296.

The prior visit to the uncle’s funeral explained the aunt’s feelings and the setting. The court’s contract analysis rested on the present exchange concerning her own funeral, not on treating the earlier visit or flowers alone as fresh consideration. Likewise, the handwritten direction was part of the reported evidence; the opinion did not hold that it independently satisfied requirements for a will or created a testamentary gift. Its short reasoning addressed the possible oral contract. 295–296.

This matters to the assignment’s placement under death of an offeror. If the jury found the reciprocal agreement Holmes described, the aunt’s death would occur after formation, and her estate could owe the contractual payment when the condition was met. The court did not hold that death generally leaves an unaccepted offer open, or that attendance after death necessarily accepts a new unilateral offer. 296.

## Qualified acceptance

The executor’s side suggested that Earle’s response varied from the aunt’s offer because it added conditions concerning notice and ability. Holmes did not disregard those differences as legally irrelevant in every negotiation. He emphasized that the parties spoke face to face and apparently separated in agreement. A jury could find that the aunt assented immediately to any variation. The response could thus lead to agreement on qualified terms instead of terminating the discussion without a contract. That possible finding was enough to defeat the directed verdict. 296.

## Exact disposition and the assigned hypothetical

The court’s order was **“Exceptions sustained.”** It rejected the ruling that Earle’s evidence could not support recovery. It neither awarded $500 nor expenses, and did not conclusively determine that the parties had exchanged enforceable promises. 296.
