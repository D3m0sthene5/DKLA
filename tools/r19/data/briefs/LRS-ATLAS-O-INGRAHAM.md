# Ingraham v. Wright
430 U.S. 651 (1977) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Judgment and questions decided

On April 19, 1977, the Supreme Court affirmed the Fifth Circuit’s en banc judgment rejecting the Eighth Amendment and procedural due-process theories advanced by two students challenging disciplinary paddling in Florida public schools. Justice Powell’s five-Justice opinion held that the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause did not apply to that school discipline and that, although appreciable physical punishment implicated a protected liberty interest, Florida’s preservation of common-law constraints and judicial remedies afforded the process required in this setting. Justice White dissented with Justices Brennan, Marshall, and Stevens. Stevens also wrote separately. (651–653, 664, 672–683, 700–702.)

The result was not a finding that the alleged beatings were reasonable, an award of tort damages, or a decision that extreme punishment could never violate substantive due process. Certiorari was expressly limited to the Eighth Amendment and procedural due process; the Court declined to review the independently presented question whether arbitrary, severe punishment violated substantive due process. Its final order therefore foreclosed relief on the two theories reviewed, while leaving that other constitutional question unresolved. (659 n.12; 679 n.47; 683.)

## Alleged punishments and applicable school rules

James Ingraham and Roosevelt Andrews attended Charles R. Drew Junior High School in Dade County, in eighth and ninth grades respectively. Ingraham alleged that, for being slow to follow instructions, he was held over a table in the principal’s office and struck more than twenty times with a paddle. He suffered a hematoma requiring medical attention and missed school for several days. Andrews described repeated paddlings for minor infractions, including blows to his arms and an occasion leaving him without full use of an arm for a week. Sixteen students testified about practices at Drew, which the evidence suggested were exceptionally harsh. The District Court made no credibility findings: it assumed the testimony credible but concluded that no federal constitutional relief was available. (653–658 & nn.1, 8–11.)

Florida’s then-existing statute prohibited punishment that was degrading or unduly severe and required consultation with the principal or teacher in charge. School Board Policy 5144 authorized paddling when other means of securing cooperation failed, required a determination of necessity and an explanation to the student, and called for another adult’s presence without public shaming. In practice, teachers sometimes paddled on their own authority despite the consultation requirement. The normally described punishment involved one to five blows to the buttocks with a flat wooden paddle and no apparent injury. Policy changes during litigation standardized the paddle, restricted where it could strike, and introduced grade-specific limits and parental notification. The opinion’s sixth note separately reproduces the amended 1976 statutory scheme; that later law should not be silently substituted for the rules governing the alleged October 1970 incidents. (655–657 & nn.5–8.)

The majority’s national survey and common-law discussion are historical descriptions of the situation before the Court in 1977. The Court did not decide that all school systems must use corporal punishment or that every practice then authorized by legislation was constitutionally sound. Its analysis addressed disciplinary paddling within a longstanding privilege to employ reasonable force and the legal consequences of exceeding that privilege. (660–663 & nn.13–28.)

## Litigation and procedural posture

The complaint was filed January 7, 1971, through the students’ parents. Two counts sought individual damages for the paddlings, and a third sought declaratory and injunctive relief for a certified class of Dade County students. The defendants were the principal, assistant principal, assistant to the principal, and superintendent. The School Board’s separate dismissal under the then-applied view of amenability to suit was not before the Supreme Court. (653–654 & nn.1–3.)

After a week of plaintiffs’ evidence, defendants sought dismissal of the equitable count under Rule 41(b) and a ruling that the evidence could not support the damages counts. The parties had agreed that the evidence on the class claim, together with stipulated testimony, would also be considered on a directed-verdict motion for the damages claims. The District Court dismissed all three counts without receiving defendants’ evidence. A Fifth Circuit panel reversed, finding constitutional problems in the severity and procedures; the en banc court rejected that result and affirmed. The Supreme Court reviewed the en banc judgment, rather than treating the vacated panel opinion as the operative disposition. (654 & n.4; 658–659.)

The students urged that a prohibition of barbaric punishment should protect schoolchildren no less than convicted prisoners, and that some opportunity to explain their conduct should precede physical punishment. The school authorities relied on the historical distinction between criminal punishment and school discipline, the common-law privilege, and civil and criminal accountability for abuse. Those competing positions concerned both the reach of constitutional provisions and whether later judicial remedies could supply due process. (664–680.)

## Eighth Amendment: historical criminal context and its limits

Powell connected the Eighth Amendment’s parallel limits on bail, fines, and punishments to the criminal-law functions of government. He traced the language through the English Bill of Rights, Virginia Declaration of Rights, ratification debates, and First Congress. The Court’s prior applications concerned criminal punishment: restrictions on types of punishment, gross disproportionality, and limited restrictions on what could be criminalized. Earlier deportation and coercive civil-contempt cases illustrated the distinction. Evolving standards of decency affected assessment of criminal punishments, not the initial expansion of the Amendment to this educational context. (664–668 & nn.29–36.)

The majority also distinguished the conditions of prisoners from those of children in an open school. Students remained connected to family, friends, other pupils, and teachers who could observe and protest abuse; community supervision and state-law sanctions reinforced those protections. It rejected the argument that the greater formal protections afforded criminals required extension of the Eighth Amendment to ordinary school discipline. This was the majority’s reasoning, not an evidentiary finding that the alleged severe injuries had not happened. (668–671 & nn.37–40.)

The scope was qualified. Note 37 reserved punishment in mental or juvenile institutions and recognized that some formally noncriminal punishments might be sufficiently analogous to criminal ones to require Eighth Amendment scrutiny. The Court’s conclusion was that disciplinary corporal punishment by public school teachers and administrators was outside that Amendment; the pertinent constitutional inquiry was due process. The brief consequently does not convert this holding into a categorical rule about every restraint imposed outside a criminal conviction. (669 n.37; 671–672 n.40.)

## A protected liberty interest, but no prior administrative hearing

The Court separated existence of an interest from the adequacy of procedure. Freedom from unjustified bodily restraint and punishment was part of the historically protected liberty of personal security. Subject to a de minimis qualification, deliberate restraint and appreciable pain imposed by state school authorities implicated that liberty. The case did not principally concern the state-created educational property interest involved in suspension cases; disciplinary paddling was intended to correct conduct without interrupting education. (672–674 & nn.41–44.)

The common-law privilege qualified the protected interest. Reasonable force, evaluated in light of the offense, child, severity, and alternatives, was justified. Excessive force could lead to civil damages, and malicious punishment could bring criminal penalties under Florida law. The Court relied on statutory recognition of those constraints and the parties’ agreement that remedies existed, rather than requiring an already reported Florida recovery. It viewed ordinary punishments as generally moderate, often responding to misconduct directly observed by a teacher, with the open school environment and potential liability reducing the risk of unjustified action. (661–663; 675–678 & nn.45–46.)

Applying Mathews, the majority recognized a substantial interest in avoiding wrongful punishment, but weighed additional safeguards against the low assessed error risk and educational burden. It compared the continued common-law practice to warrantless public arrests on probable cause, whose propriety could be reviewed afterward. It thought advance hearings would divert personnel and teaching time, potentially disrupt authority, and induce abandonment of an otherwise lawful disciplinary option. Procedures could not practicably vary with every contemplated level of severity. The marginal protection did not justify turning a policy choice about discipline into a universal constitutional hearing mandate. (675–682 & nn.47–55.)

The conclusion was expressly tied to corporal punishment as authorized and limited by the common law. Florida’s preserved constraints and remedies satisfied procedural due process; no prior notice and hearing were required on the theories reviewed. The majority did not adjudicate the merits of a possible state tort claim or declare each particular paddling lawful under those constraints. (682–683.)

## White’s dissent, joined by Brennan, Marshall, and Stevens

White rejected restriction of the Eighth Amendment by the label attached to the offense or proceeding. Text prohibited cruel and unusual punishment without saying “criminal,” and school punishment pursued familiar punitive purposes such as deterrence, correction, and retribution. Punishment too barbaric for a serious offender should not become acceptable when applied to a child for lesser misconduct. He did not contend that every moderate paddling violated the Amendment; his objection was exclusion from its protection regardless of severity. Nor did he propose that ordinary discipline receive the entire criminal procedural apparatus. (683–692 & dissent nn.1–9.)

On procedure, White would require at least the informal give-and-take recognized in Goss: notice of the charge, an explanation of the evidence when disputed, and an opportunity for the student’s version. The distinction between suspension and physical pain did not justify omitting those rudimentary safeguards. Remedies aimed at excessive force might leave an innocent student without recovery when a teacher reasonably and in good faith relied on mistaken information. His detailed eleventh note also discussed uncertainty concerning Florida remedies, official immunity, school-board immunity, and limited personal assets. Those are dissenting objections, not holdings that Florida lacked all remedies. (692–696 & dissent nn.10–12.)

More fundamentally, physical pain could not be undone after it had been inflicted. White distinguished a brief warrantless arrest, which initiated a system of prompt judicial controls, from final punishment without a prior opportunity to respond. He disputed the majority’s assessment of insignificance of error and the burden of a brief conversation. His final note reserved the possibility that more severe punishment might require more elaborate protections; the minimum he proposed was not necessarily adequate in every case. He would reverse. (696–700 & dissent nn.13–18.)

## Stevens’s separate dissent

Stevens joined White on both constitutional issues, but emphasized that the Due Process Clause’s wording does not make all postdeprivation remedies constitutionally insufficient. Timing depends on the particular deprivation. Compensation may restore a property loss completely more readily than it can remedy an invasion of bodily freedom. He also suggested, without deciding, that an adequate remedy for damage to reputation might satisfy procedure and support the result of Paul v. Davis on a different rationale. His separate discussion therefore qualified the breadth of an argument for mandatory advance hearings while maintaining that the majority reached the wrong result here. (700–702 & Stevens nn.1–2.)

## Judgment and assigned scope

The Supreme Court’s final judgment was “Affirmed”: the students could not obtain injunctive relief or damages on the Eighth Amendment or procedural due-process theories considered. Substantive due process was outside the certiorari grant. The assigned October-b PDF 37 / printed 466 uses Ingraham as a developed comparison in its Memphis Light account, emphasizing the Court’s reliance on later civil and criminal sanctions. That editorial criticism and comparison do not replace the two issues and limits of the complete judgment. (659 n.12; 679 n.47; 683.)
