# Chase Precast Corp. v. John J. Paonessa Co.
409 Mass. 371, 566 N.E.2d 603 (Mass. February 20, 1991) | Contracts

## Facts and chronology
In 1982 the Massachusetts Department of Public Works contracted with Paonessa to improve two stretches of Route 128. Each project included replacement of a grass median with concrete surfacing and precast concrete barriers. Paonessa placed two orders with Chase for an aggregate 25,800 linear feet of barriers meeting departmental specifications. Work began in spring 1983. Residents protested the loss of the grass median, and about 100 citizens sued on June 6 to stop the barrier installation and other work. Both companies knew of the protests around June 1. Anticipating a change in the projects, Paonessa wrote Chase on June 7 to stop production; Chase stopped upon receipt on June 8. On June 17 the department settled with the citizens, agreeing that no additional barriers would be installed. On June 23 it deleted the permanent-barrier item from the prime contracts. (372–373.)

Chase had already produced approximately half the ordered barriers and delivered most of them. Paonessa paid the contract price for everything produced. Cancellation of the remainder caused Chase no out-of-pocket expense, but deprived it of anticipated profits. The prime contracts expressly allowed the department to eliminate unnecessary items and barred the contractor from claiming anticipated profit. Chase's purchase orders contained no corresponding cancellation clause. (373, 376–377 & n. 6.)

## Proceedings and parties' positions
Chase sued Paonessa for the profit it expected on the unproduced barriers. Paonessa sought indemnification from the Commonwealth if held liable. Following a jury-waived trial, the Superior Court ruled for Paonessa on impossibility grounds and determined that the department had authority to cancel under the prime contracts. Chase and Paonessa cross-appealed. The Appeals Court affirmed, describing the defense more accurately as frustration of purpose. The Supreme Judicial Court granted Chase further appellate review. (371–372 & n. 2.)

Chase's central risk-allocation argument was that the cancellation provision appeared only in the prime contracts: Paonessa had not secured a comparable protection in its own orders to Chase. Chase also claimed that an unrelated Superior Court decision supplied controlling law or became the law of this case because opposing counsel had not objected when it was presented to the trial judge. Paonessa relied on the unforeseen destruction of the projects' need for the remaining barriers and on the commercial circumstances known to the parties. (376–379.)

## Governing rule and holding
The court recognized frustration of purpose as a Massachusetts contract defense and held it applicable here. Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 265, a party's remaining duties are discharged when, without its fault, a supervening event substantially frustrates its principal purpose and the event's nonoccurrence was a basic assumption, unless the language or circumstances allocate that risk to the party. Frustration differs from impossibility because literal performance may remain possible while its expected value is destroyed. The common inquiry is whether an unanticipated circumstance, whose risk should not fairly be imposed on the promisor, makes performance vitally different from what the parties reasonably expected. (373–376.)

Paonessa could physically have bought barriers that the department would no longer use. Frustration, rather than literal impossibility, therefore explained its defense. It bore no responsibility for the department's decision. The decisive issue was whether the parties had allocated this cancellation risk to Paonessa. Given the trial findings and supporting evidence, they had not; Chase could not recover the lost profit. (374 & n. 3, 375–378.)

## Application and treatment of foreseeability
Risk allocation required appraisal of the particular commercial circumstances, not a mechanical inference from silence. The trier of fact asks whether the contingency was reasonably foreseeable as a real possibility and whether omission of an express provision tacitly assigned that variety of risk to the promisor. Chase knew it was supplying barriers for departmental projects. It had supplied the department before, understood the industry's unit-price practice of paying for quantities actually accepted, and was familiar with the department's standard provisions. The judge found that the parties knew anticipated profit was not an element of damage under the public-works contracts. Those circumstances supported awareness of the department's power to alter quantities. (376–377.)

General knowledge of that power did not establish that these parties contemplated elimination of a major portion of a project involving an item as widely used as concrete median barriers. Nor did it establish that Paonessa undertook this extraordinary cancellation risk in its orders to Chase. The trial judge had not expressly found every proposition about contemplation and allocation, but a supported decision imported the findings essential to sustain it. The appellate court treated the record as supporting those implicit findings. (377 & n. 7.)

The court distinguished Chicopee Concrete: the absence of incorporation of a prime-contract approval requirement did not make a subcontractor's actual notice irrelevant. More significantly, it declined to follow John Soley & Sons insofar as that case imposed an inconsistent fixed rule requiring a promisor to provide expressly against cancellation of a prime contract. The court favored examination of all the circumstances and the parties' roles when they had not themselves allocated responsibility. Its discussion recognized that foreseeability alone need not bar relief where the parties did not intend the promisor to bear the event's risk. (377–378 & n. 8.)

## Additional issue and disposition
The unrelated trial-court decision did not become binding law through counsel's failure to object to its submission. Presenting legal authority is not an admission of facts, abandonment of an issue, or a trial judge's binding statement of the issues being tried. The other familiar law-of-the-case principle concerns reconsideration within the same case; it cannot bind an appellate court to a legal ruling made in a separate lower-court action. (378–379.)

The Supreme Judicial Court affirmed the judgment for Paonessa. Lynch wrote for the five-justice court consisting of Liacos, Wilkins, Abrams, Lynch, and Greaney. No separate opinion or dissent is reported. The decision resolves the particular lost-profit claim after payment for all barriers already produced; it does not grant contractors an unrestricted right to cancel supply contracts whenever their customer changes its plans. (379.)
