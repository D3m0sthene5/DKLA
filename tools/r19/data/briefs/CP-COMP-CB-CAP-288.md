# Biggs v. Public Service Coordinated Transport

**Civil Procedure — 280 F.2d 311 (3d Cir. 1960). Decided July 5, 1960; rehearing denied July 25, 1960.** Goodrich wrote for Chief Judge Biggs and Judges Goodrich and Kalodner. No separate writing is reported. Assigned: CivPro physical page 357, printed page 680, Note 2. The preserved reproductions supply the complete judicial text and all five notes; discussion references identify the relevant paragraphs and notes without assuming unverified reporter breaks.

The central pleading question was whether a bus company could treat a qualified general denial as disputing its own New Jersey corporate citizenship. The Third Circuit interpreted the answer in light of counsel's good-faith certification, concluded that this allegation had been admitted, and held that a later certificate confirming it added nothing material. Although obtaining that certificate through an ex parte reopening of the trial record was erroneous, the error did not require reversal. The court also rejected the defendant's challenges to the evidence, trial judge, and jury instructions, and affirmed the plaintiff's judgment.

On April 2, 1956, Eleanor Biggs was a passenger in her own car, driven by Robert Washington, when it collided with the defendant's bus on the Camden Plaza side of the Benjamin Franklin Bridge, below its New Jersey exit. She suffered substantial injuries and sued for personal injuries and property damage. Washington became a third-party defendant and had independent counsel at trial. The jury found the bus company negligent and Washington not negligent; it awarded Biggs $15,000. The defendant appealed, without contesting the amount of damages as such. (Opening factual paragraph; jury-charge discussion.)

Biggs's eight-paragraph complaint alleged that she was a Pennsylvania citizen and the bus company was a New Jersey corporation, with an amount in controversy exceeding the then-applicable $3,000 threshold. It separately alleged the defendant's ownership of the bus, the driver's relationship to the company, the collision and negligence, and damages. The answer admitted ownership and the driver's relationship, asserted insufficient knowledge about the damages paragraphs, and generally denied the remaining allegations. It specifically disputed the amount in controversy. Another defense objected to the company's name, but the parties stipulated to correct it to Public Service Coordinated Transport. (Notes 1–4.)

Approximately three months after trial, the district court reopened the record, on Biggs's motion and without the defendant's participation, to admit a New Jersey Secretary of State certificate showing the defendant's New Jersey citizenship. The defendant characterized that procedure as a violation of the Fifth and Seventh Amendments. The Third Circuit agreed that reopening a case for additional evidence should not occur ex parte at one litigant's request. The court acknowledged discretion to reopen a record in the interest of fairness, but that discretion did not justify the procedure used here. (Certificate and reopening discussion.)

The court nonetheless found the error harmless because the certificate established only facts already admitted through the answer. A fair reading of the general denial did not include the company's own corporate citizenship. Counsel's signature carried the historical Rule 11 certification that counsel had read the pleading and believed there was good ground to support it. The panel would not interpret an experienced lawyer's answer as a denial, made in good faith, that the lawyer's client was a New Jersey corporation. The citizenship allegation therefore stood admitted under then-Rule 8(d), and the later certificate was redundant evidence rather than a new jurisdictional fact. (Pleading and good-faith discussion.)

Footnote 5 strengthens this reasoning by analogy to earlier decisions rejecting a lack-of-knowledge disclaimer concerning such a fact. A party cannot avoid an allegation by professing ignorance of information its own circumstances make available to it. The panel regarded a similar rationale as appropriate for construing this general denial. That does not mean every general denial admits every allegation: this answer specifically disputed the monetary threshold and was interpreted according to its actual language and counsel's good-faith obligations. Nor does the opinion hold that parties may create federal jurisdiction merely by agreement. Its conclusion rests on an admitted fact of corporate citizenship, not a stipulation that jurisdiction exists. The assigned note's question about a court's independent jurisdictional obligation is editorial, rather than an additional question expressly decided by the panel. (Note 5; assigned Note 2.)

The defendant also argued that its negligence had not been proved and that Washington was conclusively negligent. The panel found those claims depended on which witnesses the jury believed. Conflicting testimony does not permit appellate displacement of a verdict unless the credited account is so inherently improbable that no reasonable person could accept it. Here charts explained the busy collision site, both sides developed their versions, and jurors asked questions demonstrating engagement with the evidence. The court found no confusion warranting reversal. (Evidence and credibility discussion.)

The alleged judicial favoritism likewise required review of the whole trial rather than selected comments. The judge sometimes interrupted counsel to let a witness complete an answer, but did so across witnesses and sides. A dispute about a look or gesture could not readily be evaluated on the written record. The panel found the judge impartial and attentive to fairness. Its observation about the limitations of a record without video describes this historical record; it is not a universal modern restriction on documenting judicial conduct. (Trial-judge discussion.)

Finally, the court reviewed the charge as a whole. Some comments were unnecessary or irrelevant, but they did not divert jurors from their task. The judge defined negligence and damages, explained that credibility was for the jury, and submitted separate questions about whether the bus company's or Washington's negligence substantially caused the injuries. The answers supported Biggs and Washington. The jury understood the dispute, and the verdict was considered and not excessive. The court examined other objections but found none required discussion. The exact disposition was **affirmance of the district court's judgment**. (Charge discussion and final paragraph.)
