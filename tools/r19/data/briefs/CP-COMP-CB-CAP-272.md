# Hearns v. San Bernardino Police Department

**530 F.3d 1124 (9th Cir. 2008) · July 1, 2008 · Civil Procedure**

The court vacated dismissal of a lengthy but intelligible employment-discrimination complaint and required reinstatement of a separate retaliation action. Length alone did not justify Rule 8 dismissal where the allegations clearly identified the claims and defendants. Judge Hart wrote for himself and Judge Paez; Judge Kleinfeld concurred in restoring the second lawsuit but dissented from restoring the first. The assigned account appears in CivPro physical page 340, printed page 647, in the first note on form of pleadings. Its citation “30 F.3d 1124” omits the leading 5, and its caption misspells San Bernardino; the actual published citation and court caption above supply the corrections.

Kimberlyn Hearns, an African-American male police officer, filed his first case in December 2003 alleging racial discrimination and retaliation by the Police Department and ten unnamed defendants. It asserted seventeen federal and state claims, including civil-rights statutes and Title VII. The original complaint occupied eighty-one pages. The defendants sought dismissal under Rule 8 and, alternatively, dismissal of some claims under Rule 12(b)(6). The district court dismissed without prejudice for violation of Rule 8, gave eighteen days to amend, and did not reach the substantive Rule 12 grounds. **Official slip pages 7877–7878.**

After no timely amendment, the court issued a show-cause order. Counsel asserted lack of receipt of the relevant orders, and the court allowed another eighteen days. The resulting sixty-eight-page amended complaint retained the same seventeen claims; much of the apparent shortening resulted from narrower margins. The court dismissed it with prejudice for failure to comply with the earlier Rule 8 order, stating that less drastic measures would not be effective. That February 17, 2005 dismissal order was docketed without a separate Rule 58 judgment. **7878–7879.**

A successor lawyer sought Rule 60(b)(6) relief based on former counsel's alleged misconduct, or an extension of the appeal deadline. The court denied Rule 60 relief but granted a ten-day extension. Hearns appealed four days later. The defendants appealed both the extension and the denial of reconsideration. Meanwhile, Hearns had filed a second suit alleging retaliation for bringing the first. The district judge consolidated the actions and later directed closure of the entire matter on the assumption that it was all before the appellate court. Hearns separately appealed closure of the second case, which the Department did not oppose. **7879–7881.**

The two defendants' appeals did not require adjudication of whether counsel's conduct justified an extension. The panel identified an overlooked Rule 58 issue: without a separate judgment, the first dismissal became final after the prescribed 150-day period, on July 18, 2005. The 150th day itself fell on Sunday, July 17. Hearns's August 5 notice was therefore within the ensuing thirty-day appeal period, so no extension was needed. The majority's second note supplies an alternative tolling analysis under the rules governing Rule 59 or Rule 60 motions. It also treated the parties' conduct as waiving the pertinent entry requirement for the August order. Both defendants' appeals were dismissed as moot. **7879 note 1; 7881 note 2–7882.**

On the first lawsuit, the panel reviewed the Rule 41(b) dismissal for abuse of discretion. A district court necessarily abuses discretion when it rests on an error of law, making the validity of the initial Rule 8 dismissal material. The amended complaint's four opening pages identified parties, forty-two pages provided factual history, and twenty-two pages set out enumerated claims with the relevant defendants and legal grounds. Apart from the hostile-workplace claim, no claim exceeded nine paragraphs. The defendants' appellate objection was excessive factual detail, rather than inability to understand which theories ran against which defendants. The majority regarded the pleadings as organized and intelligible. **7882–7883.**

The majority distinguished *McHenry*, *Nevijel*, and other upheld dismissals involving confusing, conclusory, redundant, or unintelligible pleadings and failures to correct them. Those cases did not establish that excessive length alone violated Rule 8. Here, unnecessary detail did not disguise the complaint's substance. A defendant could identify and respond to the claims without first reconstructing a coherent lawsuit out of an incoherent pleading. The court therefore held that neither the original nor the amended complaint violated Rule 8 on the asserted basis. **7883–7887.**

The majority also emphasized alternatives to dismissal with prejudice. The district judge could strike surplusage, including many of the long background allegations, or excuse the defendants from answering those paragraphs. The harsh sanction should account for the preference for adjudication on the merits and whether it punished the client for counsel's conduct. The majority concluded that imposing dismissal with prejudice rather than a less drastic alternative was an abuse of discretion. It did not decide the merits of the Rule 60(b) claim of attorney misconduct, and it expressly allowed the district court to address the defendants' previously unresolved Rule 12(b)(6) grounds on remand. Restoration of the pleading was therefore not a merits judgment on the discrimination claims. **7887–7888.**

The second lawsuit required separate treatment. It also lacked a separate Rule 58 judgment, but the parties treated its closure order as final and Hearns's notice was timely. Because the Department did not oppose this appeal, the panel remanded with instructions to reinstate that case. The exact concluding directions were: **vacate and remand the first-case dismissal in No. 05-56214; remand with instructions to reinstate the second case in No. 05-56306; dismiss Nos. 05-56272 and 05-56324 as moot**. The first-case result was 2–1; restoration of the second was agreed by all three judges. **7888–7889.**

Kleinfeld disputed both the majority's factual evaluation and its deference to the district judge. He viewed the eighty-one-page pleading as confusing, needlessly rhetorical, and laden with irrelevant allegations. He contrasted it with the nine-page, thirty-nine-averment complaint in the second lawsuit, demonstrating that Hearns could plead concisely. He emphasized repeated opportunities to amend, narrower margins instead of meaningful revision, and counsel's affidavit that Hearns resisted shortening the pleading. In his view, the district court had already considered alternatives, and the majority shifted the plaintiff's responsibility to court and defense lawyers. **7889–7895.**

His broader objection concerned the costs of prolix pleading: a good-faith answer requires investigating each averment, discovery and trial can become needlessly expensive, and uncertain claims complicate the future scope of preclusion. Time devoted to reconstructing one party's pleading also delays other litigants. Citing circuit precedent and the then-existing illustrative pleading forms, he would defer to the first dismissal while agreeing that the second complaint should proceed. Those assessments belong to his separate opinion, not to the majority's description of the complaints. **7894–7900; all twenty-six dissent notes.**
