2. Dyer v. National By-Products, Inc.
380 N.W.2d 732 (Iowa 1986). The Supreme Court of Iowa decided the case on January 15, 1986. Justice Schultz wrote
for the court.
Parties and procedural history
Dale Warren Dyer was the plaintiff and appellant. National By-Products, Inc., his employer, was the defendant and
appellee. Dyer alleged breach of an oral contract for lifetime employment. The employer denied making that promise
and obtained summary judgment.
The district court gave two reasons for summary judgment. It concluded that Dyer had made no reciprocal promise to
work for life and that he had surrendered no viable claim because workers’ compensation was apparently his
exclusive remedy. Dyer confined his appeal to the second rationale. The Iowa Supreme Court reversed and remanded
after holding that invalidity of the surrendered claim did not establish lack of consideration as a matter of law.
Procedural caution
The opinion did not decide whether the employer promised lifetime employment, whether Dyer honestly believed
he had a tort claim, or whether an enforceable contract ultimately existed. The opinion also did not decide the
district court’s separate ‘reciprocal promise’ rationale. It is inaccurate to say either that the first rationale was
affirmed or that Dyer won enforcement of a lifetime contract.
Material facts
Dyer lost his right foot in a job-related accident on October 29, 1981. The employer kept him on leave at full pay until
August 16, 1982, when he returned to his former position as a foreman. The employer laid him off indefinitely on
March 11, 1983.
Dyer alleged that he believed in good faith that he had a personal-injury claim against the employer and that he
surrendered the claim in exchange for lifetime employment. The employer specifically denied offering a lifetime job.
For purposes of analyzing the consideration issue, the court assumed that the tort claims against the employer and
coemployees were clearly invalid. The court did not decide whether Dyer actually held an honest belief in their
possible validity.
Question presented and holding
The question was whether good-faith forbearance from a claim that later proves invalid and unfounded can constitute
consideration. The court answered yes. Under the approach stated in Restatement (Second) § 74(1), an invalid claim
can support a settlement when the claim was objectively doubtful because of uncertainty about law or fact, or when
the surrendering party honestly believed the claim might fairly be determined valid.
Because a genuine factual dispute remained about Dyer’s good faith, the invalidity of the tort claim did not entitle the
employer to summary judgment on the consideration issue. The court therefore reversed and remanded.

Why objective weakness still matters
Section 74(1)(b) does not convert a bare assertion of belief into sufficient consideration. If invalidity was obvious, that
obviousness can show that the claimant knew the claim was baseless. Dyer therefore treated the legal weakness of
the claim as relevant evidence of subjective honesty. The court quoted the proposition that reasonable grounds are
generally needed before a fact finder will be persuaded that the belief was honestly held.
Reasonable grounds perform an evidentiary function in the court’s analysis. They help prove or disprove good faith. A
brief should avoid rewriting the rule as an independent requirement that every surrendered claim possess objective
merit, because the court expressly rejected that stricter rule.
Policy
The court favored settlements because parties should be able to trade uncertain claims for negotiated peace. If a
party could reopen every settlement by litigating the surrendered claim’s merits, settlement would lose much of its
value. The good-faith requirement prevents parties from manufacturing nuisance claims solely to extract
concessions.
What Dyer does not establish
 •  The decision does not hold that Dyer’s tort claim was valid. The court assumed the opposite for its analysis.
•  The decision does not hold that Dyer acted in good faith. It identifies that question as a disputed fact for later
 proceedings.
•  The decision does not establish that the employer made the alleged promise. National By-Products denied making
 it.
•  The decision does not enforce lifetime employment. It reverses summary judgment on one consideration issue
 and returns the case for further proceedings.
The exam rule from Dyer
When consideration consists of surrendering a claim, analyze § 74 instead of saying only that forbearance is legal
detriment. State whether the claim was objectively doubtful, whether the claimant honestly believed it might be
valid, and what evidence bears on that belief.
