# OWBR LLC v. Clear Channel Communications, Inc.
266 F. Supp. 2d 1214 (D. Hawai‘i 2003) | Contracts

## Court, motions, and result
District Judge Kay decided the cross-motions on February 5, 2003. He granted the resort's motion on rejection of SFX's force majeure defense and on the difficulty-of-proof component of liquidated damages, but left reasonableness and the amount of damages unresolved. He granted Clear Channel Communications' motion because that named company was not a contracting party and was not bound through agency. SFX's motion was denied. The resort's evidence-striking motion was denied as moot and, alternatively, on admissibility grounds. This interlocutory decision did not award the approximately $626,000 sought in the complaint or finally enforce the damages clause. (266 F. Supp. 2d at 1214–1217, 1229–1233.)

## The room commitment and cancellation
On November 19, 2000, Outrigger Wailea Resort contracted with “Urban Network—SFX Multimedia Group” to host Power Jam 2002, a music-industry conference scheduled February 13–17, 2002. The resort drafted the agreement, signed by its sales and marketing director and by Miller London for the other side. It held 2,270 room nights in six categories at rates between $235 and $900. Cancellation zero to thirty days before arrival required one hundred percent of total guest-room revenue plus applicable taxes. (Id. at 1215–1216.)

The force majeure provision identified acts of God, war, government regulation, terrorism, disaster, specified strikes, civil disorder, transportation curtailment, and other emergencies beyond the parties' control, making contractual performance inadvisable, illegal, or impossible. Either party could cancel for such reasons on written notice. London's January 16, 2002 cancellation letter cited September 11 together with fragile consumer economies and withdrawn sponsor and participant commitments. SFX described only 150 of 500 rooms booked, three secured by credit card, and thirty-eight participating companies compared with 102 the previous year. The resort sued March 4, seeking $625,912.65 in liquidated damages after crediting a $10,000 deposit. The parties disputed excuse, the proper corporate obligor, and damages. (Id. at 1216–1217.)

## The named parent was not the contracting entity
The resort relied on acquisition announcements, a trade-magazine account, and later website descriptions to characterize the signatory organization as a division of Clear Channel Communications. The evidence instead distinguished that named company from Clear Channel Entertainment, the business name of another corporation in the ownership chain. A general-counsel declaration and Delaware formation documents showed SFX Multimedia was a separate entity, not a division of the named parent. Later public descriptions neither established the corporate organization at contracting nor showed reliance in making the agreement. (Id. at 1218–1220 & nn.2–4.)

There was no demonstrated actual authority to bind Clear Channel Communications. Nor did the record support apparent authority: London denied representing that he bound that company, and the resort's own signatory disclaimed awareness of such representations. The resort did not establish reliance on a representation of agency. The court therefore denied the resort's motion on parent liability and granted that parent's motion. Corporate ownership and branding did not, on this record, make the named parent the contractual promisor. (Id. at 1220, 1229.)

## “Inadvisable” required objective contractual interpretation
The parties agreed that the attacks were a force majeure event of a kind described in the provision; they disagreed about their effect on this conference five months later. SFX emphasized fear, travel disruption, economic weakness, and poor registrations. The resort argued that cancellation resulted from economic downturn rather than an objective impediment to performance in Maui. Under Hawai‘i law, the court construed ordinary contractual language as a legal question using an objective standard. The word inadvisable could not be detached from the clause's risk-allocation function. Construing ambiguity against the drafter was inapplicable to these equally sophisticated commercial parties with equal bargaining power. (Id. at 1220–1222 & nn.5–7.)

The court used Restatement section 261 and impracticability decisions to inform construction, while acknowledging that the Restatement did not specifically address force majeure clauses. Ordinary market shifts, financial inability, or increased expense did not usually discharge a promise. A severe change could qualify, but economic undesirability alone did not become an excuse merely because it followed an event named in the clause. This agreement did not expressly allocate the risk of low attendance or withdrawn commitments to the resort. (Id. at 1222–1224 & nn.8–11.)

SFX's evidence made cancellation economically sensible, but did not establish an objective reason that travel to Maui in February was inadvisable. The record contained no specific terrorism threat to Maui or relevant air travel. Substantial travel and convention attendance continued; general fear and tourism decline did not prove the necessary effect on performance. The conference's relocation from Palm Springs to Maui also increased attendees' cost and inconvenience. The court considered rather than struck defendants' evidence, but found it insufficient. It observed that an event scheduled immediately after September 11 would present a stronger excuse, without deciding such a case. Nor could a single attack excuse performance indefinitely. The resort won summary adjudication on this defense. (Id. at 1223–1225 & nn.9–14.)

## Liquidated damages remained partly open
Under Hawai‘i law and the parties' accepted Restatement section 356 approach, enforceability depended on a reasonable relation to anticipated or actual loss and difficulty of proving that loss. The resort established difficulty: a conference would generate not just room profit but uncertain profits from food, beverages, spa services, recreation, shops, and other ancillary purchases. Defendants' simpler room-based calculations did not address all those sources. That component favored enforceability. (Id. at 1225–1227 & nn.15–17.)

Reasonableness remained genuinely disputed. The clause used revenue, not profit, and the experts offered sharply different loss estimates: at least $410,645 excluding taxes for the resort versus $29,000–$38,000 for defendants. They disputed room margins, rebooking credits, and whether replacement local guests merely shifted revenue from another period. They also disputed rooms allegedly released in December and January and whether a January 10 letter canceled the event earlier or merely adjusted the room block. That date could affect the applicable cancellation tier, while released rooms could affect the relation between attrition and cancellation clauses. The court did not resolve those questions through summary judgment. Note 16 separately addressed whether transient-accommodations tax belonged in damages when no accommodations had been furnished. (Id. at 1226–1229 & nn.16–21.)

## Evidence and exact concluding orders
The motion to strike portions of expert reports and declarations was moot because even considering them the force majeure defense failed. Alternatively, the court found the experts sufficiently qualified and their sources adequate; it treated attendee fear evidence under the then-existing-state-of-mind exception and other witness opinions as based on personal perceptions and experience. The report mislabels Rule 56(e) as a Federal Rule of Evidence in its discussion; it is the summary-judgment provision being quoted. The court denied SFX's motions on both force majeure and liquidated damages and left the remaining factual damages issues for further proceedings. No separate writing appears. (Id. at 1229–1233.)
