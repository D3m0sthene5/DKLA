# Foster v. Colorado Radio Corporation

381 F.2d 222 (10th Cir. July 6, 1967) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B1-070

The Tenth Circuit affirmed the seller's judgment with a ten-percent reduction in the contract-price/resale-price damages, from $15,750 to $14,175. The radio-station transaction included both non-goods and goods; Article 2 governed the movable office equipment and furnishings even though they were a small part of the business sold. Because the seller gave no notice of the private resale, it could not use the stipulated resale-price remedy for that goods portion. The non-goods remedy remained available, and the separate unchallenged $500 attorney-fee award was not reduced by the court's specified calculation. Chief Judge Murrah wrote for the panel with Hickey and District Judge Christensen; there is no separate opinion. (381 F.2d at 224–228.)

## The buyer failed to take the steps needed to complete the acquisition

Colorado Radio brought a diversity action after Rosemary Foster failed to complete her agreement to acquire certain assets of a New Mexico radio station. The agreement referred to in the seller's demand was dated August 28, 1964. It required Foster to make satisfactory arrangements to assume two notes and to apply to the Federal Communications Commission for transfer of the station's operating license. Both parties were to act diligently and cooperate in the license transfer. A provision quoted in note 2 would release the parties if transfer permission was not obtained within nine months of filing without the specified outstanding protest or litigation. The court distinguished that transfer condition from Foster's affirmative duty to start the application process. (Id. at 224–225 & n.2.)

Foster argued that failure to make satisfactory note arrangements meant a condition precedent to her purchase duty never occurred, so she had not breached. The agreement placed responsibility for those arrangements on her, however, and the evidence fell far short of reasonable diligence for one note. A party cannot use a condition's nonoccurrence to avoid liability when her own inaction caused it. The court sustained the breach finding on that basis. (Id. at 224.)

She also invoked New Mexico § 50-7-3, reproduced in note 1, which required a performance demand before certain contracts without a fixed performance time could become money demands. Colorado Radio's November 18 telegram, about two weeks before suit, explained that the application was still not ready despite the seller's materials having been ready in September. It demanded diligence and said its attorneys considered five more days reasonable. Two later telephone calls asked whether and when Foster would file. She conceded a filing demand but argued that the statute also required a separate demand for payment of the price. (Id. at 224–225 & nn.1–2.)

The court rejected that additional requirement. The statute's purpose was to give a final opportunity to perform before suit. License transfer was a condition precedent to purchase, but Foster had not even filed. Demanding the first step toward orderly performance satisfied the statutory purpose in this setting. The court did not treat the uncompleted FCC transfer as excusing her failure to perform her own application obligation. (Id. at 225.)

## The stipulated remedy and the private resale

Shortly after filing suit, Colorado Radio privately resold the station to Walton. The trial court found that the two transactions covered the same items. At trial the parties stipulated that, apart from incidental damages, the measure was the difference between the resale price and contract price. The trial judge calculated $15,750 under that formula and added $500 attorney's fees as incidental damages. Foster did not challenge that fee award. (Id. at 225.)

The seller admitted that it had not given notice of its intended private resale. Foster relied on the New Mexico versions of UCC §§ 2-706(1) and (3), which permitted recovery of the contract-price/resale-price difference for a qualifying resale but required reasonable notification of a private resale. The seller did not contend that the stipulation barred consideration of the notice objection. The court consequently read the stipulation as specifying the measure if the resale was legally proper, rather than waiving the statutory prerequisite. (Id. at 225 & n.3.)

## Article 2 governed the goods sold within the broader asset transaction

The trial court had excluded the transaction entirely from the Code. The appellate court agreed that the evidence supported treating the license, goodwill, real estate, studios, and transmission equipment as nonmovables and therefore non-goods under the applicable definitions. That was a determination about the assets and evidence in this record, not a categorical declaration that all transmission equipment in every sale is immovable. Colorado Radio conceded that the office equipment and furnishings were movables. (Id. at 225–226 & n.4.)

It nevertheless argued that those goods were incidental to the main purpose of transferring a going concern. The court rejected that exemption. Article 2's transaction-in-goods language supplied no basis to disregard a conceded sale of goods solely because non-goods predominated in the larger bargain. The office assets made up only five to ten percent of the price, but that proportion did not remove their sale from the statute. Conversely, a business could consist almost entirely of goods apart from goodwill; treating the business-transfer purpose as conclusive would defeat the Code's intended reach. (Id. at 226 & n.5.)

The seller's beauty-services analogy also failed. In Epstein, a product was applied while supplying a beauty service, and the earlier court treated the transaction as a service rather than a goods sale for warranty purposes. Foster involved sale of a group of assets, some goods and some non-goods, rather than the supply of a service. The court distinguished that setting instead of announcing that all mixed goods-and-services contracts must always be divided. (Id. at 226.)

The Foster contract could therefore be viewed in two parts. For the goods, absent notification foreclosed § 2-706's remedy. Because the parties had stipulated to that specific price-difference measure, the breach regarding goods was unremediable in this case. That conclusion depended on both the notice failure and the stipulation; it does not hold that every seller omitting resale notice necessarily loses every possible Article 2 damages remedy. For the non-goods, notice was not mandatory, the resale price's reasonableness was unchallenged, and the agreed measure remained available. (Id. at 226–227.)

Taking the uncontradicted valuation testimony most favorably to Foster, the court used ten percent as the goods' share and reasonably attributed ten percent of the damages to refusal to buy those goods. It reduced the $15,750 award to $14,175, rather than reversing the entire judgment or requiring another allocation trial. (Id. at 226–227.)

## The remaining damages challenges failed

Foster sought to go behind the stipulated net-price comparison and ask whether the seller was actually worse off without her performance. The court declined to let that late theory subvert an in-court stipulation entered with open eyes. The calculation was supported by the record. Note 6 addresses indirect objections: although the Walton writing contained a lease provision, uncontroverted extrinsic evidence showed it was a mistake and the real estate had actually been sold; similar evidence supported deducting precomputed interest to determine Walton's net price. Those supported findings meant the comparison was not based on an uncorrected mismatch between a sale and a lease. (Id. at 227 & n.6.)

She finally contended that the $5,000 good-faith escrow provision limited liability. The trial judge found ambiguity in the contract provision and used the separate escrow agreement to determine intent. That agreement expressly said payment of the fund did not limit damages or other remedies. Foster's objection that the document had not been attached to the complaint under New Mexico Rule 9(k) failed because this was a damages suit founded on the purchase contract, not a suit founded on the escrow agreement. The escrow instrument was admissible to clarify the intended exclusivity of the remedy, and the finding that the fund was not exclusive was correct. (Id. at 227–228 & nn.7–9.)

The final disposition was affirmance as modified. Breach liability survived, the principal price-difference award was reduced to $14,175, and no objection to the $500 incidental fee award was sustained. The court ordered no new trial or remand for a different damages theory. (Id. at 225, 227–228.)
