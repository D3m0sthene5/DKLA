# Garden State Plaza Corp. v. S. S. Kresge Co.
78 N.J. Super. 485, 189 A.2d 448 (App. Div. 1963) | Contracts

## Court and procedural result

The New Jersey Superior Court, Appellate Division, decided the appeal on March 21, 1963. Judge Conford, S.J.A.D., delivered the court's opinion on a panel with Judges Gaulkin and Kilkenny; no dissent or separate writing was reported. The landlord appealed a judgment favoring the tenant after a bench trial. The appellate court reversed and remanded for a new trial because relevant interpretive evidence had improperly been excluded. It did not finally select the landlord's or tenant's construction of the lease. (78 N.J. Super. at 485, 488, 491–495, 503.)

## Lease and competing constructions

Garden State leased shopping-center premises in Paramus to Kresge on May 11, 1956, for approximately twenty-five years. The premises included about 16,000 square feet on the mall level and 15,000 below it. In addition to rent, § 4.3 allocated common-area maintenance costs, principally parking maintenance, proportionately among tenants. For that allocation, mall-level space counted at full weight and space above or below it at half weight. A rider, § 4.3(a), capped Kresge's common-area charge at twenty-five cents per square foot of demised floor space for the first seven fiscal years if the two levels were substantially equal in area; later it substituted three-eighths of one percent of gross sales. Unpaid amounts attributable to the cap could be carried forward, subject to the applicable later cap. (Id. at 489–490.)

The parties disagreed about whether the rider's maximum retained the base clause's weighting. Kresge contended that basement space carried a twelve-and-a-half-cent maximum while mall space carried twenty-five cents. Garden State contended that twenty-five cents applied to every square foot, pointing to the lease's definition of floor space and arguing that weighting allocated maintenance costs rather than set an individual tenant's cap. The parties agreed that the modest difference in the two areas did not defeat the rider's substantially-equal-area condition. (Id. at 490–491.)

A further provision, § 17.23, stated that there were no affecting oral agreements and that the lease superseded previous negotiations and agreements. It then went further: those materials were not to be used to interpret or construe the lease. The trial judge excluded the offered negotiation materials both because of that final restriction and because the written provisions appeared unambiguous. Nevertheless, the judge adopted Kresge's weighted-cap construction and entered judgment in its favor. Garden State appealed. (Id. at 491, 500.)

## Excluded evidence and appellate posture

Kresge sought to support its construction with an early memorandum of agreement estimating a mall-level charge and half that amount for basement space; written comments on a later lease draft, proposing a ceiling and expressly computing basement charges at half the mall rate; an April 9, 1956 telegram said to show that common-area charges were no longer open; and testimony from two officers. The comments contained notations whose timing and communication to Garden State were unclear. The court required those circumstances to be clarified on retrial rather than treating the notation as proof of mutual assent. (Id. at 492–494.)

Although Kresge had won below, it could defend that result on alternative grounds involving evidence that the trial court had excluded. Garden State objected to the appellate court's request for supplemental briefing on admissibility and the exclusionary clause. The court answered that Kresge had contested exclusion below and in its initial appellate argument. Supplemental consideration exercised the court's discretion to reach a correct result on applicable law and competent evidence; it did not introduce an entirely new claim against the landlord. The clause also directly implicated the judiciary's function. (Id. at 494–495.)

## Interpretation before the parol-evidence bar

The court distinguished determining the integrated writing's meaning from altering an agreement whose meaning is already established. The parol-evidence rule protects integrated substantive terms against contradictory earlier agreements. It does not categorically exclude circumstances offered to determine what the written terms mean in the first place. Facial clarity on a first reading is therefore not a sufficient reason to exclude relevant interpretive evidence. (Id. at 496–497.)

There remained a substantive limit: the proposed interpretation had to be one the words could bear. Negotiations could not give an integrated writing a meaning wholly alien to its language. Kresge's interpretation met the permissible-meaning threshold when the rider was read with the allocation provision, although the court doubted that it necessarily was the most natural construction without the negotiations or that it ultimately would prevail. The memorandum and comments could support an inference that Garden State knew Kresge expected a half-rate basement cap. The landlord's objections to their persuasive effect concerned weight, not their categorical competency. Final interpretation required a fuller record. (Id. at 497–499.)

The court rejected wholesale exclusion but did not order admission of every item. The early memorandum and written comments were permissible interpretive evidence. The telegram's materiality had been inadequately explained, so its admissibility was left to the trial judge on retrial. Mere subjective understanding was different: Hollister's proposed testimony about his own uncommunicated interpretation was inadmissible. Kresge itself conceded that uncommunicated office notations or comments had no probative value. The decision therefore does not authorize a party to replace a written bargain with any privately held intention. (Id. at 495–496, 499–500.)

## Public policy and the attempted interpretation restriction

The ordinary integration portion of § 17.23 was essentially equivalent to the parol-evidence rule and did not defeat the court's interpretive approach. Its final prohibition on using negotiations for interpretation was the objectionable part. Enforcing it would remove a basic adjudicative tool when both signatories disputed the writing's meaning. (Id. at 500.)

The court examined competing treatments of agreements about evidence. It noted that insurance precedents sometimes rested more on unfair substantive restrictions than on a universal ban on contracting about proof. It also discussed limits on stipulations that distort a court's judicial task and contrasted an agreement admitting useful evidence with one depriving the court of relevant facts. It found the latter concern persuasive here: a party asking the court to enforce its contract could not require interpretation on a deliberately incomplete evidentiary basis contrary to the state's interpretive precedents. Expressly confining its holding to this situation, it invalidated the relied-on portion of § 17.23 as against public policy. It did not invalidate the lease as a whole or announce that every agreement altering evidentiary arrangements is void. (Id. at 501–503.)

## Disposition and assignment

The judgment was reversed and the case remanded for a new trial consistent with those evidentiary principles. There was no final declaration of the basement rate or monetary recovery. The casebook's Variation by Contract account quotes the public-policy holding; the complete opinion supplies its express limitation, the surviving integration provision, and the distinctions among particular kinds of evidence. Its questions about California law and choice of New York law are editorial hypotheticals, not rulings of this New Jersey court. (Id. at 503; C&M printed 556, PDF 249.)
