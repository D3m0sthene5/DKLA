# Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co.

**342 U.S. 437 (1952) · Supreme Court of the United States · March 3, 1952**

## A Philippine corporation directed from Ohio during wartime

Idonah Slade Perkins, who did not reside in Ohio, brought two Ohio actions against Benguet Consolidated Mining Company and other defendants after litigation elsewhere. Benguet was organized under Philippine law and owned profitable gold and silver mines there. Perkins sought approximately $68,400 in dividends allegedly owed to her as a stockholder and $2.5 million in damages, principally for the company’s alleged failure to issue certificates for 120,000 shares. Neither claim arose in Ohio or related to the company’s Ohio activities. The Court did not determine whether those stock claims were valid. 342 U.S. at 438–439 & n.1.

The company’s Ohio presence arose from an unusual wartime displacement. Japanese occupation halted its Philippine mining operations. Its president, also its general manager and principal stockholder, returned to his Ohio home and directed the company’s limited continuing business there. He kept corporate files, handled business correspondence, issued salary checks to himself and two secretaries, maintained substantial corporate bank balances, and held directors’ meetings at his office or home. An Ohio bank served as stock transfer agent. From Ohio he supervised plans to rehabilitate the Philippine properties and dispatched funds for machinery. The company mined no Ohio property, but its corporate affairs during this period were substantially managed there. Id. at 447–448.

The president was served in Ohio while acting for the company. Thus the case involved actual notice to its responsible representative, rather than service on a public official without authority to receive process for this type of claim. Id. at 439–440.

## The Ohio decisions and the Supreme Court’s task

The trial court quashed service on the company. Ohio’s intermediate appellate court and supreme court affirmed. The company had neither obtained a foreign-corporation license nor appointed a designated statutory agent in Ohio, but those omissions did not themselves prevent it from conducting business or being sued under Ohio law. The lower courts’ analysis implicated both the state’s jurisdictional rules and federal due process. Id. at 439–440 & n.2.

The difficulty was determining which ground controlled the Ohio Supreme Court’s judgment. Under Ohio’s reporting practice, its syllabus represented the court’s approved law, while an accompanying opinion could reflect only its author’s views on points not included in the syllabus. The syllabus denied jurisdiction over this unrelated claim without clearly identifying whether Ohio law or the Fourteenth Amendment required that result. The accompanying opinion relied unequivocally on a federal constitutional prohibition. The U.S. Supreme Court therefore faced a risk that Ohio had declined jurisdiction because it mistakenly believed federal law left no choice. Id. at 441–443 & nn.3–4.

## Holding: federal due process permitted, but did not require, jurisdiction

Justice Burton’s opinion held that the corporation’s Ohio activities were sufficiently substantial to make jurisdiction over these unrelated claims constitutionally permissible. Federal due process did not prohibit Ohio from entertaining them. But neither did it compel Ohio to provide that forum. Ohio remained free to choose a narrower jurisdictional policy under its own law. Id. at 440–441, 446–449.

That second proposition mattered to the result. The Constitution did not require equivalent treatment simply because Ohio allowed a properly served nonresident natural person to be sued on an out-of-state cause of action. Nor did due process independently require Ohio to open its courts to every properly notified foreign corporation. The Court decided the federal boundary, leaving Ohio’s affirmative choice to its courts. Id. at 440–441.

The Court distinguished older decisions involving attempted service on public officials without sufficient authority to bind the foreign corporation in an unrelated action. Those cases involved inadequate service and notice. Their broader assumptions about the constitutional limits of corporate jurisdiction had also been modified by *International Shoe*. Here, actual service on the president resolved the notice concern, while the company’s own substantial Ohio activities resolved the separate constitutional fairness concern. Id. at 443–445.

## Why unrelated claims could be heard on these facts

*International Shoe* required attention to the quality and nature of the defendant’s activities and whether jurisdiction was fair and reasonable. Corporate licensing criteria could help identify relevant business activity, but did not conclusively answer the constitutional question. Corporate management, banking, correspondence, salary payments, stock administration, and directors’ meetings were meaningful activities even though the company’s productive mines remained elsewhere. Id. at 445–447 & nn.5–6.

The case went beyond jurisdiction over obligations arising from local activities. The Court examined the whole Ohio operation and found continuous, systematic supervision of the necessarily limited wartime business. Given that concentration of corporate management, due process allowed Ohio to adjudicate causes of action entirely distinct from the Ohio activities. The holding rested on those combined circumstances, rather than a rule that an officer’s presence or a few local purchases alone establishes jurisdiction over every corporate dispute. Id. at 447–448.

## Separate positions and the exact judgment

The Court vacated Ohio’s judgment and remanded for further proceedings in light of its opinion. It did not order Ohio to take jurisdiction or enter judgment for Perkins on her stock claims. The remand removed a possible mistaken constitutional restriction so Ohio could determine the state-law question for itself. Id. at 448–449 & n.7.

Justice Black concurred in the result without writing separately. Justice Minton dissented, joined by Chief Justice Vinson. Minton agreed that the constitutional premise in the accompanying Ohio opinion was erroneous, but considered the Ohio syllabus a clear, adequate, independent state ground. In his view, the Court could not review the judgment merely because one judge had supplied an erroneous federal explanation supporting it. He regarded the majority’s clarification as an advisory opinion and would have dismissed the writ as improvidently granted. The result was 7–2, with six Justices joining Burton’s opinion and Black agreeing only in the result. Id. at 449–450.
