# Turken v. Gordon
223 Ariz. 342, 224 P.3d 158 (2010) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
The Arizona Supreme Court unanimously clarified the Gift Clause’s consideration requirement but applied its clarification only to transactions after January 25, 2010. It therefore affirmed dismissal of the taxpayers’ Gift Clause claim, vacated the court of appeals’ opinion, and remanded to that court to consider other constitutional claims it had not reached. Vice Chief Justice Hurwitz wrote; Chief Justice Berch and Justices Ryan, Bales, and Pelander concurred. The court considered the agreement quite likely unconstitutional under its clarified approach but did not make the factual valuation necessary for a definitive conclusion or invalidate this transaction under that clarification. (¶¶1, 43–50.)

## Facts and chronology
CityNorth was planned as a major commercial development in Phoenix’s Desert Ridge community, with offices, hotels, residences, garages, and more than a million square feet of retail space. Developer NPP CityNorth said it could not complete the proposed project without assistance. Phoenix feared that the retail component and anticipated sales-tax revenue would be lost, potentially to Scottsdale. Its council authorized a Parking Space Development and Use Agreement after required findings that expected revenue exceeded the incentive and the project otherwise would not locate there in the same time, place, or manner. A consultant independently verified those findings before execution. (¶¶2–5.)

For forty-five years, NPP would reserve 2,980 garage spaces for nonexclusive public use and 200 spaces exclusively for commuting-program users. Phoenix’s payments would begin only after construction of the garages and at least 1.02 million square feet of retail space. Payments equaled half of specified privilege taxes generated at the development, capped at $97.4 million, over no more than eleven years and three months. Footnote 1 lists the covered construction and business taxes. The agreement did not, however, covenant that NPP would construct the retail component; it expressly described that construction as a condition precedent to city payment. Nor did it contractually promise any particular tax revenue. (¶5; n.1; ¶¶36–39.)

In August 2007, Phoenix taxpayers and business owners sued to enjoin payments, invoking the Gift Clause, Equal Privileges and Immunities Clause, and Special Laws Clause. The superior court granted defendants summary judgment. It found a public purpose and counted anticipated tax revenues as part of the consideration. The court of appeals reversed on the Gift Clause as to nonexclusive parking, using an additional inquiry about undue promotion of private interests, while leaving the other constitutional arguments unresolved. Phoenix and NPP obtained Supreme Court review. (¶¶6–9; n.2.)

## Issues and governing test
Article 9, section 7 prohibits government credit or subsidy to private entities. The Supreme Court adhered to Wistuber’s two requirements for contractual expenditures: a public purpose and consideration whose value is not so disproportionately low compared with the public payment that the transaction becomes an unconstitutional subsidy. It rejected an additional test based on whether the private party was the primary beneficiary and public benefits merely incidental. That approach had appeared in a prior dissent rather than the controlling test. (¶¶15–22.)

Public purpose and contractual consideration perform different work. Courts give significant deference to elected officials on public purpose, which may include indirect benefits and respond to changing social conditions. But public purpose alone does not prevent a government from subsidizing a seller by overpaying for the promised goods or services. Ordinary private contract law generally avoids comparing exchanged values; the Gift Clause requires the additional proportionality inquiry to protect public funds. Footnote 4 confines this consideration analysis to contractual expenditures, distinguishing noncontractual assistance to the needy. (¶¶10–18, 22, 28, 30–35; nn.3–4.)

## Public purpose
No party disputed that providing parking served a public purpose or that Phoenix could build its own garage. The city could instead obtain spaces in NPP’s garages. The court also refused categorically to exclude indirect goals such as expanding the tax base, encouraging denser development, reducing pollution, and increasing employment. Those goals could support the public-purpose inquiry even though they would not necessarily count as contractual consideration. The council had not unquestionably abused its discretion, so the first prong was satisfied. Footnote 5 distinguishes this Gift Clause inquiry from the separate constitutional requirement for a public use when property is taken by eminent domain. (¶¶23–29; n.5.)

## Consideration and the bargain’s value
Consideration consists of performance or a return promise bargained for in exchange. The court required an objective valuation of what NPP actually promised Phoenix. Anticipated benefits that were not part of that promised performance could not be added as consideration simply because the project might produce them. The court illustrated the distinction with sewer repairs: avoiding disease establishes a public purpose, but it does not permit paying millions for repairs available for thousands by valuing all the avoided public harm as the contractor’s consideration. (¶¶30–35.)

Here, NPP promised parking access. The retail-construction requirement merely determined when Phoenix owed payment and was expressly not a developer covenant. Tax obligations arose from generally applicable law, not a contractual promise to create revenue. Accordingly, increased tax receipts and broader development benefits could not inflate the consideration for the parking agreement. (¶¶36–39.)

Phoenix argued that the generated taxes would offset its expenditures and that the independent findings required by the tax-incentive statute established compliance. The court rejected both shortcuts. Taxes become public money upon collection, so their expenditure remains subject to the Gift Clause. Statutory compliance and constitutional adequacy are separate requirements: the statute asks whether anticipated revenue exceeds the incentive, while the constitutional comparison concerns promised contractual value. Footnote 6 notes then-recent legislation restricting some municipal retail-relocation incentives, without deciding the present agreement under that separate rule. (¶¶40–41; n.6.)

Turken conceded that $97.4 million might be fair for forty-five years of exclusive use of all 3,180 spaces. But only 200 were exclusive; customers could fill the other 2,980 precisely when other members of the public wanted them. The court strongly doubted that the nonexclusive access approached the public payment in value. Still, the superior court had never separately valued the parking, and appellate intuition could not substitute for findings. Ordinarily that would have required remand for valuation. (¶¶42–43.)

## Prospective application and exact disposition
The court chose a different course because prior language had encouraged a widespread misunderstanding of the consideration inquiry. References to public benefits and a broad “panoptic” view might have led governments to count indirect benefits despite the actual earlier holdings focusing on directly promised obligations. The trial judge and other municipalities had relied on that understanding. Although the decision overruled no prior holding, the court limited its clarification to later transactions to avoid inequitable retroactive consequences. (¶¶44–49.)

Consequently, the taxpayers did not obtain relief on their Gift Clause claim. The court affirmed its dismissal, vacated the court of appeals’ opinion, and remanded only for that court’s first consideration of the remaining constitutional arguments. Turken’s Supreme Court fee request was untimely even assuming a potentially successful party could receive fees for establishing an important legal principle; the court of appeals could consider fees on remand. This limited disposition must be kept separate from the decision’s prospective rule. (¶50; n.7.)
