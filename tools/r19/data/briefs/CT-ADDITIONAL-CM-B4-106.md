# Matter of Associated General Contractors, New York State Chapter, Inc. and Savin Brothers, Inc.
36 N.Y.2d 957, 335 N.E.2d 859 (1975) | Contracts

## Court, vote, and result
New York’s Court of Appeals affirmed, with costs, an order confirming a $104,400 arbitration award on June 16, 1975. Judges Jones, Wachtler, Fuchsberg, and Cooke joined the per curiam opinion. Chief Judge Breitel dissented, joined by Judges Jasen and Gabrielli. The 4–3 decision concerned the judicial treatment of an award already made under a broad arbitration agreement, rather than a court’s initial calculation of contractual damages. (957–959, 964.)

## Agreement, breach, and proceedings
Associated General Contractors was a multi-employer collective-bargaining association. Each member agreed to arbitrate the association’s damage claims for alleged violations of membership obligations. The damage formula required an award of at least three times the daily liquidated damages specified in each of the member’s heavy and highway construction contracts in the geographic area of the applicable labor contract, for each day of the violation. (958.)

Breitel’s dissent supplies further chronology. Savin had belonged since 1958 and appointed the association its exclusive bargaining agent. The designation allowed additional damages in the arbitrators’ discretion and lasted while Savin remained a member. Withdrawal required notice at least 30 days before negotiations began, and notice could not be given during negotiations. In 1972 Savin was building an arterial highway for the State in Albany. The Teamsters agreement expired March 31, and the next day a strike began, continuing until June 2. During the negotiations Savin independently signed a one-year union agreement on May 5. Associated demanded arbitration on May 15; Savin announced an immediate resignation May 17. These details are reported in the dissent, rather than separately found in a new trial conducted by the Court of Appeals. (959–960.)

The arbitrators found a 58-day violation and awarded $104,400. They expressly deemed the liquidated-damages clause reasonable, necessary, and not a penalty. Associated moved to confirm; Savin cross-moved to vacate, arguing that enforcement of a penalty violated public policy. Special Term confirmed and refused vacatur. The Appellate Division affirmed although its majority and dissenters agreed the provision imposed a penalty. The lower appellate judges therefore disagreed about enforcement, not about their own classification of the clause. (958–960.)

## Issue and majority rule
Was the judicial policy against contractual penalties strong enough, in these circumstances, to overturn the arbitrators’ contrary determination? The majority held that Savin, having selected arbitration, was bound by the arbitrators’ resolution of the legal issue presented to them, including their determination that the damage clause was enforceable. Ordinary questions of law submitted to arbitrators could not be reopened simply because a court reached another legal conclusion. The court did not itself recalculate probable loss or adopt the arbitrators’ penalty analysis as a generally applicable contract-law test. (958–959.)

## Majority’s public-policy reasoning
The majority acknowledged both the policy favoring peaceful arbitration in collective bargaining and the policy disfavoring penalties. It emphasized the broad arbitration clause, the collective-bargaining context, and the absence of third-person interests transcending the parties’ own concerns. Under those circumstances it found no public-policy question of sufficient magnitude to warrant judicial intrusion. The holding thus preserved a role for sufficiently weighty public-policy objections while rejecting this particular challenge. It should not be restated as permission to arbitrate every illegal bargain into validity, or as a finding that the association proved $104,400 of actual loss. (959.)

## Breitel’s dissent
Breitel would have reversed and vacated the award. In his view the provision was plainly a penalty: damage to the association from separate bargaining had no ascertainable relationship to damages Savin might owe owners under its various construction contracts. Multiplication by three, with additional damages also available, intensified the discrepancy. He distinguished compensatory agreed damages for losses difficult to measure from disproportionate payments used to secure performance. The policy against the latter protected against oppression and harsh unequal bargains. (959–962.)

The dissent accepted that ordinary factual or legal error would not ordinarily invalidate an award. Precisely because such errors were insulated from correction, however, arbitrators could not be permitted to enforce an agreement violating strong public policy. That limit was different from appellate reconsideration of the merits of an ordinary legal question. Breitel rejected any distinction under which privately selected adjudicators could impose supposedly less severe penalties without external review: a penalty was disproportionate by definition, and its severity could not cure its objectionable character. (962–963.)

He also discussed the risks of abusive or collusive relationships among trade associations, unions, and arbitrators. Those observations explained his policy concern; they were not findings that these parties had engaged in a corrupt arrangement. He acknowledged the benefits of arbitration and collective bargaining and suggested that statutory regulation or labor-board supervision could legitimately govern reasonable agreed remedies where actual proof was difficult. He nevertheless concluded the controlling provision violated public policy and voted to reverse. (963.)

## Assigned significance and reading scope
The assigned note asks whether a penalty objection would defeat an arbitration award under a hypothetical lease clause and cites this case for the binding effect of the arbitrators’ determination. The actual majority provides the necessary qualifications: the clause was broad, the dispute arose in collective bargaining, and no transcendent third-party interest was implicated. The full dissent makes clear that the public-policy boundary was sharply contested. The exact disposition was affirmance with costs, preserving confirmation of the existing award. (959, 964.)
