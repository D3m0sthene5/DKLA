# Courtney Shoe Co. v. E. W. Curd & Son
142 Ky. 219, 134 S.W. 146 (Ky. Feb. 14, 1911) | Contracts

## Decision and transaction

Chief Justice Hobson’s opinion reversed a retailer’s jury judgment on a counterclaim for undelivered sample shoes. An acknowledgment saying that an order would receive prompt and careful attention did not accept it. The wholesaler had promptly rejected the salesman’s unauthorized sample sale internally; the eight-day delay in conveying that rejection to the customer did not turn the acknowledgment into a contract. The published report supplies one opinion, with no separate writing, judicial footnote, or numerical voting lineup. (142 Ky. at 219–224.)

Courtney Shoe was a St. Louis wholesaler, and E. W. Curd & Son were merchants in Cave City, Kentucky. On Sunday, August22,1909, the retailers gave traveling salesman W. B. Yater two orders: $49.15 of stock shoes and $1,772.35 of sample shoes, for shipment as soon as possible. The orders were dated August21 because the actual transaction occurred on a Sunday. When the papers reached the wholesaler August23, it mailed a card acknowledging receipt, promising prompt and careful attention, thanking the customer, and expressing hope for future business. It shipped the stock shoes but rejected the sample order on the ground that Yater lacked authority to sell those samples. (Id. at 220.)

The rejection first went to Yater with directions to return the order to the customer. Yater sent it back and asked Courtney Shoe to communicate directly. The resulting August31 rejection letter told Curd that the salesman had lacked authority and that the wholesaler could not accept the sample order. Curd’s proof emphasized that acknowledgment cards were often the only notice customers received before learning of shipment, and that the stock-shoe order had received no other prior acceptance notice. Courtney Shoe’s evidence described an internal routine: acknowledge receipt first, submit the order to the approving officer next, and dispatch the result through the salesman. The approving officer had rejected the sample order on the day it arrived. (Id. at 220–221.)

## Claim, procedure, and arguments

Curd refused to pay for the delivered stock shoes, so Courtney Shoe sued for their price. Curd counterclaimed that the wholesaler had accepted the sample order and then breached it, claiming $1,083.20 damages. A jury awarded Curd $250 on that counterclaim, the trial court entered judgment, and Courtney Shoe appealed. The trial court had submitted the intended effect of the acknowledgment card to the jury. The appellate issue was whether the evidence permitted the jury to treat that card as acceptance rather than having its legal effect determined by the court. (Id. at 220–221.)

Curd relied on the acknowledgment language, the merchants’ experience of receiving such cards before shipments, and the delay before the express rejection. Courtney Shoe relied on the card’s ordinary meaning, the salesman’s restricted authority, the immediate internal rejection, and the routine explaining the later customer notification. Curd’s position would make the initial receipt card bind the wholesaler before its home office actually approved the samples. (Id. at 221–223.)

## Rule and reasoning

The court started with the division between interpreting a writing and resolving admissible explanatory evidence. Ordinary words were to have their ordinary meaning unless technical language, an established trade usage, or context gave them a different sense. There were no technical words or proof that a trade usage gave the acknowledgment a peculiar meaning. Its construction was consequently for the court, not a question of the sender’s unexpressed intention for the jury. The evidence that cards commonly preceded shipments did not establish an altered linguistic usage making a promise to consider every order an acceptance of it. (Id. at 221–222.)

A promise of attention was a promise to consider the proposal, rather than assent to supply the goods. The opinion adopted that distinction from Manier v. Appling and compared responses concerning bills and paper orders. Adding “careful” to “prompt” did not change the operative sense. Nor did conventional thanks and polite hopes for future orders provide an otherwise absent commitment. Courtney Shoe had fulfilled the promise to consider the order by examining and rejecting it. (Id. at 221–223.)

The court distinguished precedents in which acknowledgments were followed by conduct recognizing an agreement. In Jordon v. Patterson and Bauman v. McManus, acceptance rested substantially on subsequent performance or correspondence, rather than the receipt card alone. Those decisions therefore did not establish that all similar acknowledgment wording bound the merchant immediately. Here, the salesman’s sample order remained subject to the house’s approval; he had no authority to bind it to sell the samples at the large discount proposed. The house repudiated his action promptly when the order reached it. The additional time before the direct letter resulted from sending the rejection through the salesman and his return of it, rather than leaving an accepted order unperformed. (Id. at 222–223.)

The court explained the practical consequence for both sides: the buyer could countermand before acceptance, while the seller could not revoke an actual acceptance without the buyer’s consent. Where there had been no formal acceptance, shipment ordinarily could accept an order and end the buyer’s right to countermand. That principle did not convert delivery of the separate $49.15 stock order into acceptance of the rejected sample order. In the absence of conduct establishing acceptance of the latter, the acknowledgment retained its ordinary limited meaning. (Id. at 220–221, 223–224.)

## Exact disposition and significance

There was no acceptance of the sample-shoe order. The trial court should have instructed the jury peremptorily to find for Courtney Shoe for the amount of its price claim. The appellate court reversed and remanded for proceedings consistent with that conclusion; it did not itself enter a rendered final money judgment. The $1,083.20 was the retailer’s asserted counterclaim loss, while $250 was the jury award overturned on appeal. (Id. at 220, 224.)

The casebook uses this decision to test whether a courteous acknowledgment constitutes acceptance. The complete opinion adds the separate stock order, the restricted salesman authority, the actual internal rejection chronology, the attempted reliance on commercial practice, and the court-versus-jury interpretation issue. It supports a contextual distinction between considering an offer and accepting one; it does not establish that no acknowledgment can ever be accompanied by effective acceptance through conduct. This account describes the historical adjudication and does not certify current statutory or common-law treatment. (C&M physical PDF265–266, printed220–221; 142 Ky. at 220–224.)
