# Northeastern Florida Chapter of the Associated General Contractors of America v. City of Jacksonville
508 U.S. 656 (1993) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## The contracting program and the association’s claim

Jacksonville’s 1984 ordinance required approximately 10% of annual city-contract expenditures to be reserved for qualifying Minority Business Enterprises. An enterprise needed at least 51% qualifying minority or female ownership. The historical ordinance’s categories included several racial or ethnic groups and people with disabilities. Once designated, projects were reserved exclusively for qualifying firms; the code also allowed waiver or reduction of the target in specified circumstances. These were the ordinance’s classifications, not the Court’s definition of which groups constitutionally warrant preferences. (658–659.)

The local Associated General Contractors chapter represented people and firms in construction. Most members did not qualify for the program. Its April 4, 1989 § 1983 complaint against the city and mayor alleged that many members regularly bid on and performed city construction contracts and would have bid on reserved contracts but for the ordinance’s restrictions. It sought declaratory and injunctive relief, claiming the scheme violated equal protection on its face and as applied. The alleged injury was an unequal opportunity to compete, not an assertion that a particular member necessarily would have won a contract. (659.)

## Litigation and the intervening replacement ordinance

The District Court first restrained and then preliminarily enjoined the program. The Eleventh Circuit reversed the preliminary injunction for failure to demonstrate irreparable injury. Chief Judge Tjoflat concurred in that result on a standing theory: no specific contract was alleged that would have gone to a nonqualifying bidder but for the program. Meanwhile both sides moved for summary judgment. The city’s motion challenged the merits, not standing. On May 31, 1990 the District Court entered summary judgment for the association under the equal protection criteria of Croson. The Eleventh Circuit again reversed course below, vacating that judgment and directing dismissal without prejudice because no association member was shown to have been a successful bidder absent the preference. It did not decide the constitutional merits. (659–660 & n. 1.)

After the Supreme Court granted certiorari, the city repealed the ordinance on October 27, 1992 and replaced it effective the next day. The replacement covered black- and female-owned firms, used participation goals from 5% to 16%, and provided five implementation methods chosen project by project. One, the Sheltered Market Plan, retained exclusive competition for qualifying businesses. Other methods included required subcontracting, direct negotiation, bid preferences, and an impact-points system. The Court denied a motion to dismiss as moot and reconsidered that contention in the merits opinion. (660–661 & n. 2.)

## Holding and judgment

On June 14, 1993, Justice Thomas’s opinion for Rehnquist, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter held the case live and the pleaded competitive injury sufficient for standing. The Court **reversed the Eleventh Circuit and remanded for further proceedings**. O’Connor, joined by Blackmun, dissented on mootness and would not reach standing. Thus the judgment was 7–2; it did not determine that either version of Jacksonville’s preferences actually violated equal protection. (656–657, 663–669.)

The majority applied voluntary-cessation principles. In its view, the city had already continued the alleged injury through a replacement ordinance: preferences, especially the Sheltered Market Plan, still disadvantaged the association’s members in the same fundamental manner. A lesser degree of disadvantage did not necessarily moot that challenge. Mesquite did not require threatened reenactment of an identical statute. The majority distinguished statutory-change precedents where sufficiently substantial changes eliminated the challenged conduct. This was an assessment of these ordinances, not a holding that every repeal leaves litigation alive. (661–663 & n. 3.)

## Why lost opportunity can establish injury

The Court restated Article III’s injury, causation, and redressability requirements. It rejected the appellate demand for proof that a member would have won a contract. Turner, Quinn, and Clements recognized obstacles to being considered for public office without requiring proof of eventual appointment or election. Bakke similarly identified exclusion from competition for all medical-school places as an injury independent of proof of admission without the special program. Note 4 explains why later developments in Supreme Court review of state judgments did not render Bakke’s federal-standing discussion irrelevant. (663–665 & n. 4.)

For an equal protection challenge of this kind, the concrete injury is the government’s barrier to equal consideration. A challenger must demonstrate that it is able and ready to bid and that the allegedly discriminatory policy prevents bidding on equal terms. It need not show ultimate entitlement to the benefit. With that definition of injury, the ordinance causes the disadvantage, and a decree discontinuing the program would redress it. This does not dispense with readiness to participate or license suits by any observer opposed to preferences. (666 & n. 5.)

Warth did not require a different result. There the construction association complained about inability to obtain variances and permits, rather than unequal eligibility to apply, and did not allege a member’s current project or application. The Court acknowledged some tension but emphasized the present association’s allegations of regular bidding and willingness to bid on the reserved work. Even on an alternative reading of Warth, that distinction supplied the necessary immediacy. Warth did not make a showing of certain success indispensable. (666–668.)

The allegations here had not been challenged through a factual summary-judgment motion and were assumed true. That procedural premise mattered: the Court did not find that every association member had proved readiness through evidentiary proceedings. No party had raised a separate failure of associational-standing prerequisites, and the existing record gave the Court no basis to do so itself. The resulting holding addressed the erroneous standing ground for dismissal, not all possible future issues. (668–669 & n. 6.)

## O’Connor’s dissent, joined by Blackmun

The dissent would vacate the appellate judgment and permit the association to challenge the replacement ordinance in the District Court. Only prospective relief was requested against a law that no longer existed, and the association had not amended its pleadings to attack the new law. O’Connor agreed that merely cosmetic amendments cannot defeat review, but thought these changes substantial enough to present a different controversy. The replacement followed a commissioned study and public hearings, adopted findings about effects of discrimination, narrowed eligible groups, imposed a ten-year expiration, varied targets, and offered alternative methods whose actual use was uncertain. She expressly did not decide the replacement’s constitutionality. (669–675.)

She read Mesquite as exceptional because the city there had announced an intention to restore the disputed language and had previously altered and reinstated another provision during litigation. Jacksonville’s replacement effort instead suggested an attempt to address alleged legal defects. Without evidence that the old law would return, she thought the usual statutory-change cases required caution against an advisory ruling. Her disagreement was principally about the continuity and identity of the live controversy; she did not reject the majority’s competitive-injury rule on its merits. (675–678.)
