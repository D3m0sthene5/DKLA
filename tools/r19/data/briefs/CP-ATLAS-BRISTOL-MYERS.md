# Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court of California, San Francisco County

**582 U.S. 255 (2017) · Supreme Court of the United States · June 19, 2017**

## A nationwide drug controversy assembled in California

Eight complaints brought in California state court alleged that Plavix, Bristol-Myers Squibb’s prescription blood thinner, had injured the plaintiffs. The official opinion identifies 86 California residents and 592 residents of 33 other States, totaling 678 plaintiffs. They asserted 13 California-law claims, including products liability, negligent misrepresentation, and misleading advertising. The allegations were not judicial findings about the drug’s safety or the company’s liability. The nonresident plaintiffs did not allege that they obtained Plavix through California physicians or another California source, suffered injury there, or received treatment there. 582 U.S. at 258–259.

BMS was incorporated in Delaware and headquartered in New York, with substantial operations in New York and New Jersey. California nevertheless contained five research and laboratory facilities with approximately 160 employees, about 250 sales representatives, and a small governmental advocacy office. Between 2006 and 2012, BMS sold nearly 187 million Plavix pills in California for more than $900 million. The company had not developed Plavix, created its marketing strategy, manufactured or packaged it, or sought its regulatory approval in California; those activities occurred in New York or New Jersey. Thus, substantial California business existed, but the disputed claims concerned prescriptions, use, and injuries elsewhere. Id. at 258–259.

McKesson, a California-based distributor, was also a defendant below. Plaintiffs relied on BMS’s contract with that company to distribute Plavix nationwide. But they did not allege that BMS performed relevant acts jointly with McKesson in California or was derivatively liable for McKesson’s California conduct. Nor did they identify evidence showing how or by whom their own pills reached the dispensing pharmacies. These omissions mattered to the asserted connection between the particular claims and the forum. Id. at 268; see also id. at 270 (Sotomayor, J., dissenting).

## How the case moved from general to specific jurisdiction

BMS moved to quash service on the nonresidents’ claims. The superior court denied the motion, initially relying on general jurisdiction from the company’s extensive California operations. The state court of appeal denied mandamus relief, but after *Daimler* the California Supreme Court required reconsideration. The court of appeal then rejected general jurisdiction while sustaining specific jurisdiction. Id. at 259–260.

The California Supreme Court unanimously agreed that general jurisdiction was absent but divided over specific jurisdiction. Its majority used a sliding scale: extensive forum contacts permitted a less direct connection between the forum activity and each claim. It considered the nonresidents’ claims similar to those of California residents, involving the same allegedly defective product and misleading promotion, and relied additionally on California research unrelated to Plavix. Three state justices dissented, reasoning that similarity could not make out-of-state injuries arise from California sales and marketing. Id. at 260–261.

The federal issue was whether this exercise of state-court jurisdiction complied with the Fourteenth Amendment. California’s jurisdictional statute reached the constitutional limit, as the Court’s first note explains. BMS’s amenability to the resident plaintiffs’ claims was uncontested; the disputed issue was authority over the nonresidents’ separate claims. Id. at 260–261 & n.1, 271.

## Holding, exact judgment, and vote

California lacked specific personal jurisdiction over the nonresident plaintiffs’ claims on this record. Extensive unrelated forum business could not replace the missing affiliation between California and those particular claims. The Supreme Court **reversed the California Supreme Court and remanded** for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. It did not decide the underlying products-liability merits. Id. at 264–265, 269.

Justice Alito wrote for eight Justices: Roberts, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer, Kagan, and Gorsuch joined. Justice Sotomayor dissented. There were no other separate writings. Id. at 257, 269–279.

## Why the sliding scale failed

General jurisdiction ordinarily permits adjudication of any claim against a defendant at home in the forum. Specific jurisdiction permits a narrower set of claims arising out of or relating to the defendant’s forum contacts. It requires an affiliation between the forum and the underlying controversy. The distinction would collapse if a corporation’s extensive unrelated business relaxed the necessary claim connection until otherwise unrelated lawsuits became actionable there. Id. at 261–264.

The Court therefore rejected California’s sliding scale as an unsupported substitute for general jurisdiction. The nonresidents were not prescribed Plavix, did not buy or ingest it, and were not injured in California. The similar experiences of resident plaintiffs did not supply the missing relationship for others. Nor did unrelated California research do so. A defendant’s relationship with third parties cannot, standing alone, establish the required defendant–forum–controversy connection. Id. at 264–265.

The Court viewed *Walden* as illustrating the same limit. There, the plaintiffs’ connections to Nevada and foreseeable harm there did not convert a Georgia-centered episode into a Nevada-linked controversy. Here, the connection was still weaker because the disputed plaintiffs were neither California residents nor allegedly harmed there. The decision concerns the missing link on these facts; it does not formulate a comprehensive rule requiring strict causation between every forum act and every claim. Id. at 265–266; compare id. at 277 n.3 (Sotomayor, J., dissenting).

Convenience did not cure the defect. The majority treated due process as protecting defendants from a State’s coercive authority as well as from burdensome travel or litigation. Interstate federalism could limit that authority even when litigation would be convenient and the forum had some interest. It thus rejected the proposition that efficiency and minimal practical burden were sufficient by themselves. Id. at 263.

## Why the other precedents and distributor did not supply the link

Plaintiffs relied on *Keeton*, where a nonresident could pursue nationwide libel damages in New Hampshire. The Court distinguished that single claim: distribution in New Hampshire allegedly caused injury there, and the forum could adjudicate the full scope of a claim already connected to it. The nonresident Plavix claims lacked any such California injury. The distinction did not make plaintiff residency a categorical prerequisite to specific jurisdiction. Id. at 266.

*Shutts* addressed different due-process rights: those of absent nonresident plaintiff class members. The defendant there had not challenged jurisdiction over itself. The Court would not infer an unannounced holding on defendant jurisdiction from a decision protecting plaintiffs. Its notes explain the defendant’s standing to assert class members’ rights because of judgment-preclusion consequences and identify BMS’s speculation about why the earlier defendant had not asserted its own rights. Id. at 266–267 & nn.2–3.

The McKesson contract also fell short. Jurisdictional requirements had to be satisfied separately for BMS. A California distributor’s involvement, without the alleged joint conduct, derivative liability, or connection to plaintiffs’ own purchases described above, could not establish jurisdiction over BMS merely through the distributor’s location. The Court did not decide a case with materially different allegations connecting that relationship to the injuries. Id. at 268.

## Alternative forums and questions left open

The majority observed that resident and nonresident plaintiffs could consolidate claims where BMS was subject to general jurisdiction, including New York or Delaware as BMS conceded. Plaintiffs from the same State could probably proceed together in their home State. Those statements answered concerns about available forums; they did not approve every contemplated future action without examining its jurisdictional facts. Id. at 268–269.

Because this was a state-court Fourteenth Amendment case, the Court expressly left open whether the Fifth Amendment imposed the same restrictions on personal jurisdiction in federal court. Sotomayor separately observed that the case did not confront a nationwide class action brought by an injured forum plaintiff representing unnamed class members. Her observation identifies the case’s limits rather than resolving that distinct question for the majority. Id. at 269; id. at 278 n.4 (dissenting).

## Sotomayor’s competing account of relatedness and fairness

Sotomayor accepted, for this case, the absence of general jurisdiction while retaining her criticism of *Daimler*. She described three specific-jurisdiction inquiries: purposeful availment, claims arising out of or relating to forum conduct, and reasonableness. BMS plainly sought California’s pharmaceutical market. In her view, materially identical nationwide advertising and distribution also linked these claims to its California conduct. This was not like an unrelated slip on the sidewalk outside its New York headquarters. Id. at 271–273 & dissent n.2.

She considered consolidated litigation reasonable. BMS already had to defend materially identical resident claims; dividing the litigation among many States could increase its burdens. Shared counsel and discovery helped plaintiffs with small individual claims obtain effective relief, while California had an interest in efficiently adjudicating residents’ claims and regulating BMS and McKesson. Her first note explains that residence was shorthand: the important distinction here concerned where injury occurred. Id. at 270 n.1, 273–274.

She disputed the majority’s use of *Walden*, which she understood as addressing purposeful availment, a requirement BMS already met. She read *Keeton* as permitting liability for a nationwide course of conduct in a forum the defendant deliberately exploited. She also considered the majority’s federalism concern insufficient where the affected States shared an interest in that same nationwide activity. Id. at 275–277.

Finally, she anticipated barriers to aggregating small claims, especially against multiple defendants with different corporate homes or foreign corporations without an American home. Her third note distinguished this decision from a strict causal rule that BMS urged; her fourth noted the unaddressed class-action question. These were dissenting criticisms and qualifications, not independent majority holdings eliminating every form of nationwide litigation. Id. at 277–279 & dissent nn.3–4.

## Assigned-source reconciliation

The separately identified editorial account occupies PDF pages 113–114, printed pages 173–175, ending before the *Ford* caption. Both original scans and the complete account were read, along with all three majority notes and all four dissent notes in the full 25-page official report.

Two factual differences should not be silently imported from the editorial account. It says 88 California plaintiffs; the official majority and dissent identify **86**, with 592 other plaintiffs. It describes more than $1 billion in California Plavix sales; the official majority says more than **$900 million**, and the dissent describes nearly $1 billion. This brief uses the actual judicial report’s figures. Id. at 259, 270, 273. It states the historical 2017 decision without certifying subsequent treatment.
