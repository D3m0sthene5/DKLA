# McMillan v. Smith
363 S.W.2d 437 (Tex. 1962) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
The Texas Supreme Court, through Justice Greenhill, decided the ranch purchasers’ specific-performance suit on November 14, 1962. The reporter records denial of rehearing on January 23, 1963. The court rejected the purchasers’ claimed deductions but held that their deficient original tender did not defeat equitable relief in these circumstances. It affirmed reversal of the sellers’ trial-court judgment, displaced the intermediate court’s general new-trial remand, and substituted a conditional remand requiring payment of the correct cash amount within thirty days after the Supreme Court opinion became final. The report contains no separate Supreme Court writing or numerical vote. (437–438, 441–444.)

## Agreement and disputed tender
On August 24, 1959, W. W. McMillan, Arthur Mandell, and Herman Wright agreed to sell the McMillan Ranch in Brazoria County to R. E. Smith and Roy Hofheinz. The $525,000 consideration assumed 1,184 surface acres; shortages or excesses would adjust the price at $443.42 an acre. If the parcel contained fewer than 1,000 acres, the buyers could cancel and recover their $25,000 earnest money. The sellers promised an abstract showing good and merchantable title and a general warranty deed. Paragraph 6 required an opportunity to cure objections and, upon failure, permitted the buyers to cancel, seek specific performance, or take the defective title and recover reasonable costs of curing it. Paragraph 7 addressed closing and charges for title costs. (438–441.)

The buyers’ lawyer raised twenty-five title objections. Many were cleared, but in February 1960 the sellers refused to address several and said they could not clear others. The Supreme Court therefore treated the sellers as already in default. On February 29, the buyers elected to take title subject to unresolved objections. Their proposed deed demanded a general warranty covering the entire ranch, including an alleged 36.293-acre vacancy of unpatented school land; it excepted specified canal and county-road claims from the warranty. The buyers would assume $152,874.43 in secured debt and tender the remaining purchase consideration after deductions. The contested deductions totaled $27,898.12. (439–440.)

That total included $1,500 in anticipated legal and other expenses for quitclaim deeds, a school-district succession certificate, and possible litigation. It also included the per-acre value of the alleged vacancy, despite the demand for a warranty covering that land, and $10,305.08 for estimated costs of removing three utility easements. The buyers did not know what removing those easements would cost and estimated it at the contractual per-acre figure. Their demand thus combined a warranty of the affected land with deductions resembling exclusion of that land from the purchase. On March 18, the sellers refused the demanded deed and purported to rescind because the tender was insufficient. (439–440.)

## Procedural history and positions
After the buyers presented their evidence in a nonjury trial, both sides rested. The sellers moved for judgment on the assertedly undisputed inadequacy of the tender. The trial court ordered the buyers to take nothing and declared the contract void. The Court of Civil Appeals reversed and remanded for a new trial, but its three judges did not share one rationale: Justice Werlein wrote the principal opinion; Justice Coleman agreed only with reversal and remand and disagreed on major issues; Chief Justice Bell would have affirmed because he regarded the tender as insufficient. Both sides sought Supreme Court review. That intermediate judgment, reported at 352 S.W.2d 872, is procedural background here rather than a separately reviewed full opinion. (438.)

The buyers maintained that their deductions were authorized by the contract. In court they deposited $336,038.10, alleged that they were ready, willing, and able to perform, and offered to deposit or pay any further amount the judge found due. Their solvency was unquestioned. The sellers insisted that insufficient tender foreclosed specific performance and entitled them to rescission. The buyers also urged that they should not have to pay for defective title and then litigate later to recover the cost of work that the sellers should have performed. (440, 442–443.)

## Contract construction: the deductions were not allowed
The court first determined the correct contractual obligation, rather than excusing the disputed deductions through equity. Neither paragraph 6 nor paragraph 7 authorized deducting speculative future attorneys’ fees and court costs. The buyers might never need the contemplated litigation; even a contractual allowance for anticipated costs could not support this uncertain estimate on the record. Paragraph 7 addressed actual closing expenses where good title was conveyed. The election to take defective title under paragraph 6 preserved a later right to recover established curative costs, not an immediate right to deduct an unincurred estimate. (441.)

The alleged vacancy presented alternative choices. The buyers could exclude the acreage and abate the price, cancel for the failure to furnish good title, or take all the land owned or claimed by the sellers under a general warranty while retaining rights to curative costs and warranty relief. They chose the third course. They could not also subtract the price as if they had chosen the first. The same reasoning governed the utility easements: accepting and demanding a warranty of the encumbered acreage required payment, with contractual curative-cost and warranty rights preserved. The court acknowledged an authority suggesting that specific performance and warranty damages could be combined in one action, but no such damages claim had been pleaded or presented here. (441–442.)

## Equity: an inadequate original tender did not end the suit
The decisive distinction was between finding the original tender deficient and treating that deficiency as an absolute bar to an equitable remedy. Courts of equity had not historically applied the rigid tender rules of an action at law. Where a vendor failed or refused to cure title defects so that a pre-suit tender would be useless, a purchaser’s failure to make that tender could be excused when the purchaser tendered performance in the equitable pleading. The court found support in treatises and its earlier decision in Burford v. Pounders. (442–443.)

The particular circumstances mattered. The sellers had defaulted before the buyers’ tender. Numerous title defects, negotiations, and a difficult contract made the exact amount uncertain; even the intermediate judges differed over its construction. The buyers tendered the amount they believed due, placed cash in court, and offered unequivocally to supply whatever further sum the court required. This permitted the judge to protect the sellers’ right to full payment while enforcing the buyers’ right to a conveyance. Accordingly, the original deficiency did not preclude specific performance, and it did not entitle the defaulting sellers to rescission. This was a conditional equitable solution, not a holding that the buyers’ deductions were correct or that a promise to pay later always substitutes for proper tender. (442–443.)

## Exact disposition and significance of the assignment
The intermediate court had relied on excluded evidence concerning the vacancy, the buyers’ lawyer’s good-faith objections, and possible waiver of insufficient tender. The Supreme Court’s analysis assumed the vacancy, the lawyer’s good faith, and the original tender’s inadequacy. Those exclusions therefore did not justify a general new trial. Instead, the buyers had to supply the withheld $27,898.12, increasing the cash registry payment from $336,038.10 to $363,936.22, separately from their assumption of the secured debt. If they paid that total within thirty days after the Supreme Court opinion became final, the trial court was to grant specific performance through the February 29 deed. Otherwise it was to enter judgment for the sellers. Curative-cost recovery, general-warranty rights, and rights to any after-acquired title remained protected. (443–444.)

The assigned note, printed page 836, uses the case to explain how an equitable order can secure the defendant’s receipt of the plaintiff’s performance. Its short description of uncertainty over an aggregate per-acre price omits the central dispute over title-cost deductions and incompatible contractual elections. The complete opinion supplies those details and the conditional deadline; it does not award immediate unconditional specific performance.
