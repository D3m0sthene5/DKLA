# Anheuser-Busch, Inc. v. John Labatt Limited

**Civil Procedure — 89 F.3d 1339 (8th Cir. 1996). Decided July 8, 1996, Nos. 95-2877 and 95-2879; rehearing denied August 12, 1996.** Bowman wrote for Chief Judge Richard S. Arnold, Bowman, and District Judge John B. Jones, sitting by designation. No separate writing is reported. Assigned: CivPro physical pages 348–349, printed pages 663–664, Prayer for Relief Note 1. Judicial pinpoints use the preserved nineteen-page court-issued opinion.

The textbook selects this case to examine notice of a demand for punitive damages. The jury awarded Anheuser-Busch one dollar in compensatory damages and $5 million in punitive damages for Labatt's injurious falsehoods. The district court set aside the punitive award because Anheuser-Busch had not adequately disclosed its demand, and the Eighth Circuit upheld that decision. The complete appellate judgment also resolved trademark, contract, and other commercial claims and required declaratory relief that the district court had refused. (Slip 4–5, 16–19.)

Both companies were brewers and distributors. In 1993, Labatt began selling an ice beer in Canada after Molson announced its own product. Labatt then negotiated with Anheuser-Busch about bringing Labatt's beer to the United States. The negotiations involved confidential marketing and process information, protected by two agreements. Anheuser-Busch also investigated developing its own brewing process and repeatedly communicated that possibility to Labatt's president. A prototype was produced in late July; on August 4, Anheuser-Busch announced it would market its own beer. Equipment needed to duplicate Labatt's process might take six months to a year to obtain, while its own process permitted earlier entry. Anheuser-Busch began United States sales in October, followed shortly by Labatt. These facts were recounted in the light favorable to the verdict, rather than as an endorsement of every disputed assertion. (2–3.)

Labatt's November 30 infringement accusation prompted Anheuser-Busch to seek a declaration that terms such as ice beer and ice brewed were not Labatt's protected trademarks. It also asserted federal false-advertising and Missouri injurious-falsehood claims. Labatt counterclaimed under trademark and unfair-competition provisions, tortious interference, injurious falsehood, dilution, and a confidentiality agreement. A nineteen-day trial produced a finding that the ice terms were not Labatt trademarks and verdicts rejecting Labatt's submitted counterclaims. Labatt prevailed against Anheuser-Busch's false-advertising claim, but Anheuser-Busch prevailed on injurious falsehood. Both parties appealed after posttrial motions. (3–5.)

The court reviewed denial of judgment as a matter of law de novo, while reviewing denial of a new trial for abuse of discretion. It sustained the district court's decision to let Anheuser-Busch present first, despite Labatt's burden on the trademark issue: both parties bore burdens on distinct claims, and Anheuser-Busch had initiated the suit. The allocation was within the trial court's discretion. (5–6.)

Labatt argued that faulty instructions and irrelevant evidence caused the jury to reject its trademark rights. The panel found that the instructions and special verdict permitted a finding of past infringement even if a term later became generic; the jury was not forced to conflate those issues. The general burden instruction and identification of the genericness issue also adequately presented the competing burdens. Evidence of foreign, trade, and other uses became relevant after Labatt asserted in its opening that it had invented the term ice beer. Surveys and earlier generic uses supported the verdict. Some evidence showed consumers understood ice as a beer category rather than a source identifier. The panel therefore declined to displace the jury's finding that the terms were not trademarks. Footnotes 1 and 2 explain the distinctiveness categories and Labatt's unsuccessful theory that Anheuser-Busch's conduct had made its marks generic. (7–10 & nn.1–2.)

The court also rejected the claim that Anheuser-Busch had dishonestly prolonged negotiations or intentionally interfered with Labatt's business prospects. The credited testimony supported parallel consideration of licensing and independent development, communicated to Labatt, with a final decision after the prototype. Trademark-dependent unfair-competition, false-advertising, and dilution theories failed with Labatt's failure to establish protected marks. Footnote 3 explains the apparent dependency of Labatt's own injurious-falsehood argument. (10–11, 15–16 & n.3.)

The verdict for Labatt on federal false advertising and against it on state-law injurious falsehood could be reconciled because the claims had different elements. The jury could find foreseeable reliance on a false statement without finding actual deception or a tendency to deceive a substantial part of the intended audience. Labatt's advertising slogan claimed that beer other than Labatt's was not ice beer; the jury could infer that this disparaged competing brewers and caused loss. The court denied Labatt's motion to strike the cross-appellant's reply discussion of verdict consistency, which responded to Labatt's argument about punitive damages. It nevertheless refused to consider a newly raised waiver argument that Labatt had no opportunity to answer. (11–13.)

The confidentiality agreement presented a different dispute. Labatt read it as creating distinct nonuse and confidentiality duties, with exceptions applying only to confidentiality. Anheuser-Busch read nonuse as part of the single confidentiality duty, subject to the same exceptions for public, lawfully obtained, or previously held information. The court agreed that the agreement was ambiguous and that resolving the ambiguity was for the jury under proper, unchallenged instructions. Its verdict for Anheuser-Busch remained intact. (13–15.)

On Anheuser-Busch's appeal, the court separated a declaration from an injunction. The verdicts entitled it to a declaration that Labatt lacked protected rights in the ice terms and that Anheuser-Busch had not infringed or unlawfully competed with respect to those rights. No countervailing equity justified denying that declaration. An injunction, however, required irreparable harm and inadequacy of legal remedies. The one-dollar compensatory award, Labatt's success against the false-advertising claim, and Anheuser-Busch's market performance permitted refusal of injunctive relief. Winning relevant claims did not mechanically establish every requested equitable remedy. (16–17.)

Punitive damages likewise required a separate analysis. Missouri law governed the reduction or elimination of punitive damages on the Missouri injurious-falsehood claim, with the trial judge's discretion reviewed for abuse. Anheuser-Busch had not mentioned punitive damages in its pleadings or interrogatory answers. It first raised them in supplemental instructions one week before trial. The finding of inadequate notice was not clearly erroneous. Rule 15(b) did not save the demand: that rule's treatment of unpleaded issues tried by consent did not apply where Labatt expressly resisted submission of punitive damages through written and preserved oral objections. Trying the issue over objection was not consent or waiver. (17–19.)

The exact judgment was **affirmed except for the omission of declaratory relief, with a remand to amend the judgment to include an appropriate declaration**. The punitive award remained set aside; the nominal compensation and refusal of an injunction remained. The assigned question connects this result to Rules 8(a)(3), 9(g), and 54(c), which the opinion did not cite in its punitive analysis. Those are editorial prompts, not additional holdings. The court's actual procedural rationale expressly considered Rule 15(b), notice, and the preserved objection. (19; assigned Note 1.)
