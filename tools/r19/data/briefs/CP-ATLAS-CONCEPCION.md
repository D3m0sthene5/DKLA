# AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion

**563 U.S. 333 (2011) · Supreme Court of the United States · April 27, 2011**  
**Civil Procedure; shared Contracts assignment · Federal Arbitration Act, class waivers, and unconscionability**

## Facts and contractual arrangement

Vincent and Liza Concepcion purchased cellular telephone service in February 2002 from Cingular, which AT&T acquired in 2005 and renamed in 2007. Their dispute concerned phones advertised as free: they were charged $30.22 in sales tax calculated from the phones’ retail value. They alleged false advertising and fraud, rather than a failure to supply the promised telephone service. Their agreement required arbitration of disputes and prohibited either party from proceeding as a class member or representative. It also prohibited an arbitrator from consolidating claims of more than one person. The controlling version resulted from AT&T’s December 2006 revision under its contractual power to amend. 336–337 & nn.1–2.

Those provisions came with unusually favorable procedures for an individual claimant. A customer first submitted a one-page notice; after 30 days without resolution, the customer could demand arbitration. AT&T paid the costs of nonfrivolous claims, proceedings occurred in the customer’s billing county, and claims of $10,000 or less could proceed in person, by telephone, or on documents at the customer’s choice. Small-claims court remained available. Arbitrators could grant individual relief, including injunctions and presumably punitive damages. AT&T could not seek reimbursement of its attorney’s fees. If an award exceeded its last written settlement offer, the claimant received a minimum of $7,500 plus twice the attorney’s fees. The minimum increased to $10,000 in 2009, but the Court’s analysis concerned the $7,500 arrangement. 337 & n.3.

The details matter because the case did not simply ask whether consumers should receive some forum. The lower courts thought this agreement provided meaningful individual recovery, yet held that the elimination of class proceedings could undermine deterrence of many small injuries.

## Procedure and competing arguments

The Concepcions filed a federal action in the Southern District of California in March 2006. It was consolidated with a putative class action. AT&T moved to compel arbitration in March 2008. The district court denied the motion under California’s *Discover Bank* rule, despite finding the individual procedures favorable and the likely recovery potentially better than participation in a class. The Ninth Circuit affirmed. It treated California’s rule as an application of ordinary unconscionability rather than a discriminatory rule against arbitration. 337–338.

*Discover Bank* treated a class waiver as unconscionable when it appeared in a consumer contract of adhesion, disputes predictably involved small damages, and the stronger party allegedly pursued a scheme to cheat many consumers of individually small amounts. California regarded such a waiver as an effective exemption from responsibility for fraud or willful injury. The rule applied to litigation waivers as well as arbitration waivers. 340; 357–359.

The Concepcions relied on §2 of the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA), which preserves grounds existing at law or in equity for revocation of any contract. Because California applied the same principle to agreements governing litigation and arbitration, they argued that the defense fell within that saving clause. AT&T argued that forcing the availability of class proceedings nevertheless obstructed the federal statute’s protection of arbitration according to the parties’ agreed terms. 341–344.

## Issue, holding, and judgment

The issue was whether the FAA preempted California’s *Discover Bank* rule when that rule prevented enforcement of an agreement requiring individual arbitration. The Court held that it did. Requiring class arbitration through that rule interfered with attributes of arbitration protected by the FAA, even though the state characterized its rule as ordinary unconscionability and applied it to class litigation waivers too. The Court **reversed the Ninth Circuit and remanded** for proceedings consistent with its opinion. It did not decide the merits of the consumers’ false-advertising claims. 343–344, 352.

Justice Scalia wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy, Thomas, and Alito. The decision was **5–4**. Thomas also concurred separately; Breyer dissented with Ginsburg, Sotomayor, and Kagan. 334, 352–357.

## Majority reasoning

The Court began with §2’s equal-treatment principle: arbitration agreements may be invalidated by generally applicable defenses such as fraud, duress, and unconscionability, but not by defenses directed only at arbitration or deriving their meaning from its existence. That principle did not end the inquiry. A nominally general defense could obstruct the FAA when applied to demand procedures inconsistent with arbitration. The majority illustrated the problem with hypothetical rules requiring judicially supervised discovery, ordinary evidence rules, or a jury. Applying such requirements to litigation as well would not prevent them from undermining arbitration. 339–343.

The saving clause therefore could not preserve a state rule that defeated the federal statute’s objectives. Sections 2, 3, and 4 emphasized enforcing the agreement according to its terms. The parties could choose specialized decisionmakers, confidentiality, restricted issues, and streamlined procedures. Enforcing their contractual choices and preserving arbitration’s efficiency worked together here; the majority did not treat speed as permission to disregard the contract. 343–346.

The Court considered *Discover Bank*’s limitations weak in practice. Consumer contracts were commonly adhesive, the small-damages condition was flexible, and the wrongdoing condition required only an allegation. Although individual proceedings remained formally available, lawyers’ incentives to pursue class claims and companies’ exposure to duplicative proceedings made class arbitration the practical consequence of the rule. Note 6 preserved states’ ability to address adhesion concerns, for example through disclosure of waiver provisions, so long as their measures did not conflict with the FAA or frustrate enforcement according to agreed terms. 346–347 & n.6.

The majority then explained why imposed class arbitration differed fundamentally from bilateral arbitration:

- **Complexity and delay:** Certification, representativeness, typicality, and class discovery add threshold disputes before an arbitrator reaches the merits. The Court contrasted short consumer arbitrations with class arbitrations that had produced no final merits award in the cited AAA data. 348–349.
- **Procedural protections for absent people:** Binding absent members requires safeguards such as adequate representation, notice, an opportunity to be heard, and opt-out rights. Those protections bring greater formality and third-party concerns into an ordinarily private process. The Court doubted Congress intended to entrust their implementation to arbitrators in proceedings not contemplated in 1925. 349–350.
- **Aggregate risk with restricted review:** Errors in a small individual arbitration have limited consequences. Aggregating thousands of claims can create enormous exposure and settlement pressure, while FAA §10 principally addresses corruption, misconduct, or excess of arbitral authority, rather than ordinary legal mistakes. The combination could make companies unwilling to arbitrate at all. 350–351 & n.8.

The Court did not hold that parties were forbidden to agree to class arbitration. Its distinction concerned **consent versus a state-imposed change** to the agreed procedure. The comparison was between the bilateral arbitration the parties selected and the class arbitration state law effectively required. Note 7 expressly rejected the dissent’s proposed comparison with class litigation as the controlling one. 347–348, 349 n.7, 351.

Finally, the majority answered the argument that class proceedings were necessary for claims too small to pursue individually. That policy could not justify procedures inconsistent with the FAA. It also considered these particular terms likely to make customers whole and to encourage meritorious individual claims. The contingent $7,500 payment and fee provision were important to that factual answer; the opinion did not announce that every small claim is economically feasible in every individual arbitration agreement. 351–352.

## Thomas’s concurrence

Thomas reluctantly joined the majority to provide lower courts a controlling interpretation, while retaining his objections to purposes-and-objectives preemption. He proposed a narrower textual interpretation: read with §4’s reference to the making of an arbitration agreement, §2’s word “revocation” should preserve defenses concerning formation, such as fraud, duress, or mutual mistake. Public-policy objections to an otherwise properly formed contract would not qualify. He acknowledged that this interpretation had not been fully developed by the parties and deserved further briefing in an appropriate case. 352–355.

On that approach, *Discover Bank* still failed because it condemned class waivers as exculpatory on public-policy grounds, rather than finding that the agreement was not properly made. His extended starred note addressed seemingly broader language in earlier decisions and explained why he viewed their statements as dicta or their recognized defenses as formation-related. This formation-only theory was **Thomas’s separate explanation**, not a substitute for the majority’s general-defense and obstacle analysis. 355–357 & starred note.

## Breyer’s dissent

Breyer viewed California’s rule as precisely the kind of nondiscriminatory contract defense §2 preserved. It invalidated some class waivers under ordinary principles, rather than categorically forbidding every waiver or singling out arbitration. The FAA’s central purpose was placing arbitration agreements on the same footing as other contracts, not guaranteeing every possible efficiency advantage. 357–361.

The dissent challenged the majority’s baseline. If California invalidated class waivers in litigation too, businesses’ relevant choice was between class arbitration and class litigation, not between class and individual arbitration. Class arbitration could be faster than class litigation and more efficient than thousands of separate proceedings. Nor did arbitration of large commercial disputes support treating high stakes as inherently incompatible with arbitration. Breyer considered the statutory history and established class-arbitration practice inconsistent with the majority’s assumption that bilateral proceedings were an indispensable feature. 361–364.

For small claims, the dissent also questioned the apparent generosity of AT&T’s terms. The contingent minimum would not be payable if AT&T offered the claim’s full face value, leaving little incentive to secure legal assistance for a $30.22 dispute. California could reasonably view aggregation as necessary to prevent systematic small injuries from becoming practically unenforceable. Its equal treatment of arbitration and litigation, together with the saving clause and federalism considerations, should have led the Court to uphold the state rule. 365–367.
