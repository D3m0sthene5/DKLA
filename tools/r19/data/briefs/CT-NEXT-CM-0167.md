# Sumerel v. Goodyear Tire & Rubber Co.
232 P.3d 128 (Colo. App. May 28, 2009) | Contracts

## Facts and the computational dispute
Homeowners and a trustee recovered damages in a products-liability suit involving defective Goodyear hose in their heating systems. A 2002 jury awarded approximately $1.3 million against Goodyear to the plaintiffs and two other entities, including repair and replacement, diminution in value, and other repair-related costs and losses. For the last category, Goodyear’s responsibility was 36% for the Berzins and Dickes and 48% for the Sumerels and Kaufman. A prior appeal upheld that category and required prejudgment interest on it, remanding to determine accrual dates from the existing record. (232 P.3d 130–131.)

The attorneys agreed readily on accrual dates but could not reconcile their computations. Goodyear’s lead lawyer Thomasch had expressly explained to plaintiffs’ lead lawyer Maywhort that his calculations used the jury’s fault percentages. Later, Goodyear’s co-counsel Brooks told plaintiffs’ associate Gray that his total was approximately $2.7 million. Gray said this exceeded his own estimate by about six figures, but did not share his calculations. Brooks suggested the difference might concern Kaufman’s postjudgment interest; Gray responded tentatively that it might. (Id. 130–131.)

On November 2, 2006, Brooks emailed charts stating they contained figures Goodyear believed appropriate and requesting review followed by discussion. Plaintiffs’ counsel immediately recognized that the charts mistakenly attributed 100% of the “other costs and losses” to Goodyear, even though they correctly applied the fault percentages to other categories. The resulting overstatement exceeded $550,000. They did not alert Brooks. Maywhort instead telephoned and faxed an “acceptance” to Thomasch, who had not prepared the charts; neither communication went to Brooks. Counsel later discussed using a satisfaction of judgment rather than a release. On November 16, Brooks sent an expressly provisional draft, discovered the error before anyone signed, immediately notified Gray, and provided corrected charts and satisfaction. Plaintiffs insisted on the larger amount. (Id. 131–132.)

## Posture and arguments
The district court enforced the purported settlement. Goodyear appealed, arguing both that the email was not an offer and that any resulting contract was avoidable for unilateral mistake. It meanwhile paid, and plaintiffs accepted, the undisputed amount without prejudice to the disputed additional payment. The relevant facts were largely undisputed; the trial court assumed Goodyear’s version where a dispute existed and ruled on documents and affidavits. The appellate court therefore reviewed formation de novo rather than deferring to trial findings. (Id. 132–133.)

Plaintiffs argued that the email and charts supplied an offer they accepted; they construed the extra damages as Goodyear accepting full responsibility or “sweetening” the bargain. They also relied on subsequent silence and the draft satisfaction as confirming or ratifying an agreement, and argued Goodyear assumed the calculation risk by proceeding despite its acknowledged uncertainty.

## Formation holding and reasoning
The court held there was no offer capable of acceptance. Under Restatement § 24, an offer must reasonably invite assent that will conclude the bargain. Section 26 excludes preliminary proposals where the recipient knows or should know a further manifestation of assent is contemplated. Definite settlement language must be read with qualifications and surrounding communications. (Id. 133–134.)

Here liability and accrual dates were already settled; counsel were reconciling arithmetic rather than bargaining over a new dollar compromise. The email qualified the figures as what Goodyear believed appropriate and requested further discussion. That request invited a return call, not concluding assent. Plaintiffs could not convert an exchange intended to locate a discrepancy into an additional settlement offer. Their explanations for the error were contradicted by the jury allocations, prior explicit communications, and the charts’ inconsistent treatment of different damage categories. The obvious error also required inquiry before an attempted acceptance; the court treated the circumstances as a paradigmatic attempt to seize an offer manifestly too favorable to have been intended. (Id. 133–135.)

Subsequent conduct did not cure the formation defect. Ratification cannot give contractual significance to an act that was a nullity initially. Even as evidence of original intent, the silence was weak: plaintiffs sent acceptance to a different lawyer in a different firm, and Brooks was awaiting the requested discussion, with no reason to assume plaintiffs still disputed the arithmetic. Repetition of the numbers in an unsigned discussion draft likewise did not make the original tentative communication an offer. (Id. 135.)

## Alternative unilateral-mistake holding
Independently, the court held that any agreement would be avoidable. Its discussion uses Powder Horn and the Restatement §§ 153–154 framework while expressly observing that Colorado had not yet expressly adopted those two sections, although Powder Horn cited § 153 approvingly. Under that framework, a material mistake about a basic assumption permits avoidance if the mistaken party does not bear its risk and either enforcement would be unconscionable or the other party knew, should have known, or caused the mistake. Risk can arise through agreement, consciously treating limited knowledge as sufficient, or reasonable judicial allocation. (Id. 135–137.)

Plaintiffs admitted knowledge or reason to know of the error. Enforcing a windfall exceeding $550,000 above the amounts determined by the jury was oppressive and unconscionable, while giving plaintiffs their actual entitlement caused no substantial hardship. Goodyear acted in good faith and did not choose to close a bargain in conscious ignorance: Brooks circulated the first calculations to obtain further review precisely because a discrepancy remained. That inquiry was inconsistent with consciously accepting insufficient knowledge as final. (Id. 136–137.)

Poly Trucking did not require enforcement. That case involved repeated releases omitting individual doctors and no evidence that the opposing party knew they were meant to be included. Here the mathematical error was obvious and known. Even the requirement of inequitable conduct used in Poly Trucking’s reformation discussion supported Goodyear, because deliberately exploiting this known error to secure a windfall was inequitable. The court’s contract holdings do not themselves adjudicate a separate professional-disciplinary charge. (Id. 137–138.)

## Judgment, panel, and scope
Judge Gabriel wrote; Judges Russel and J. Jones concurred, producing a unanimous three-judge decision without separate writings or judicial footnotes. The enforcement order was reversed. Remand had the sole purpose of permitting a satisfaction of judgment for the amounts already paid, not recalculation of an additional settlement award. The remaining appellate contention was unnecessary to reach. Rehearing was denied July 9, 2009; the report records certiorari dismissed September 28, 2009. (Id. 138.)
