# In re Petition of Sheila Roberts Ford

**Course:** Civil Procedure
**ID:** CP-094
**Review:** Complete source comparison; reporter and assigned-page locators corrected. Independent source review passed.

## Citation

170 F.R.D. 504 (M.D. Ala. 1997), Miscellaneous No. 1984, decided January 21, 1997. United States District Court for the Middle District of Alabama; Chief Judge Myron H. Thompson. The decision addresses a verified petition under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 27(a) to take a deposition before an action was filed. Casebook pp. 900–903; source PDF pp. 463–464 (principal excerpt and following notes); p. 465 begins Fassett.

## Parties

Sheila Roberts Ford, administratrix of the estate of Fred William Roberts, was the petitioner. Elmore County Sheriff Bill Franklin was the proposed deponent and appeared through counsel to oppose the petition. Ford expected to bring a federal action arising from Roberts’s fatal shooting by Elmore County law-enforcement officers and identified Franklin and deputies as potential adverse parties.

## Material Facts

Fred William Roberts was shot and killed by Elmore County law-enforcement officers on November 8, 1996. Ford did not know which officer shot him or whether the shooting was justified. She sought Franklin’s deposition because he had been the commanding officer and was expected to identify the officers involved and describe the circumstances. Ford asserted that an early deposition would preserve an accurate account before memories faded or publicity distorted them. The court found no evidence, however, that Franklin’s testimony faced imminent loss through illness, departure, or another comparable circumstance; in substance Ford wanted information needed to identify defendants and learn what had happened. 170 F.R.D. at 505–07; CB 900–902.

## Procedural History

Ford filed a verified Rule 27 petition on November 15, 1996 and later amended it. At a December 13 hearing Franklin opposed the request as outside Rule 27, while Ford argued she could not determine whom to sue or the basic facts without his testimony. The district court denied the amended petition and taxed costs against Ford. 170 F.R.D. at 505–06, 509; CB 900–903.

## Issue

May a prospective federal plaintiff use Rule 27(a) to depose a witness before filing suit when the purpose is to discover the underlying facts and identify proper defendants, rather than to preserve testimony that is in danger of being lost?

## Rule and Holding

No. Rule 27 authorizes perpetuation of testimony before an action, meaning preservation of evidence that might otherwise be lost before it can be used. It does not create a general device for pre-complaint discovery or investigation. Because Ford sought to uncover what happened and whom to sue, and did not show that Franklin’s testimony was in danger of loss, the requested deposition fell outside the rule. 170 F.R.D. at 506–08; CB 901–903.

## Judgment

The verified petition, as amended, was denied. Costs were taxed against Ford. 170 F.R.D. at 509; CB 903.

## Reasoning

The court began with the text of Rule 27(a). The rule permits a verified petition by a person seeking to perpetuate testimony, and permits an examination when perpetuation may prevent a failure or delay of justice. Reading that language according to its ordinary meaning, the court treated perpetuation as preservation from loss, rather than discovery of unknown evidence. Ford’s request was investigative: she wanted Franklin to reveal the identity of the shooter and the circumstances of the shooting. Nothing showed that his testimony was about to disappear. 170 F.R.D. at 506–07; CB 901–902. Ford argued that Rule 27 should be read together with Rule 11 because Rule 11 requires evidentiary support, or a reasonable expectation of obtaining such support after investigation or discovery, before factual contentions are filed. The court rejected that proposed use. Rule 11 does not expand Rule 27 beyond preservation of evidence. The court acknowledged the practical difficulty: without ordinary discovery Ford had difficulty learning facts needed to frame a complaint, while Rule 11 and qualified-immunity doctrine made unsupported allegations hazardous. That difficulty did not authorize the court to rewrite Rule 27. 170 F.R.D. at 507–09; CB 902–903.

## Separate Opinions

None. This was a district-court memorandum opinion by Chief Judge Thompson.

## Notes

The case draws a sharp distinction between preserving known testimony and using a deposition to investigate whether a claim exists or who should be sued. Its importance in the discovery sequence is temporal: ordinary discovery generally follows commencement of an action and the Rule 26(f) process, while Rule 27 is a narrow preservation mechanism rather than an alternative route around those prerequisites.

The court’s discussion of Ford’s predicament explains the practical stakes but does not alter the holding. The denial rests on the limited function assigned to Rule 27, not on a conclusion that Ford’s contemplated civil-rights claim lacked merit.
