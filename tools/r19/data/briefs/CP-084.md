# Jeub v. B/G Foods, Inc.

**Civil Procedure · CP-084**  
2 F.R.D. 238 (D. Minn. 1942) · January 8, 1942 · District Judge Nordbye

## Facts and procedural posture

Restaurant patrons alleged that B/G Foods served contaminated ham that made them sick. Their complaints invoked Minnesota statutes prohibiting the sale of unwholesome or poisonous food. B/G Foods alleged that it bought the ham the preceding day in a sealed can bearing Swift's “Premium Ham” label, handled it without negligence, and that any contamination resulted entirely from Swift's conduct. B/G therefore asserted a right to reimbursement or indemnity for any recovery the patrons obtained against it. These allegations supplied the possible basis for liability over; neither contamination nor either company's liability had yet been determined. 2 F.R.D. at 239.

Before answering the patrons' complaints, B/G obtained an ex parte order adding Swift as a third-party defendant. The patrons refused to amend their complaints to assert direct claims against Swift. Swift moved to vacate its addition in the six actions addressed together by the opinion: Jeub, three Stack actions, Mack, and Watson.

Swift argued that Rule 14 created only procedure, not substantive liability. Under Minnesota law, B/G had neither paid a judgment nor otherwise incurred a loss, so it could not yet enforce a contribution or indemnity claim. With no direct claim from the patrons and no presently enforceable claim from B/G, Swift maintained that it should not be kept in the litigation. 2 F.R.D. at 240.

## Issue and holding

Could Rule 14 bring a potential indemnitor or contributor into the original action before the defending party suffered the loss necessary for a separate money-recovery action? The court held that it could. The governing rule expressly covered someone who **was or might be liable**. Accelerating determination of a contingent claim did not have to accelerate the substantive right to receive payment.

The court agreed with Swift's starting premise: Minnesota law controlled any right B/G possessed against Swift, and federal third-party procedure could not override that substantive law. But the premise did not require a second lawsuit. The parties could determine their respective responsibilities concurrently, while conditioning enforcement on the loss or payment state law required. 2 F.R.D. at 240–41.

## Reasoning and protection of substantive rights

The full opinion relied on *Burris v. American Chicle Co.*, where a judgment against an indemnitor could be entered but execution stayed until the indemnitee satisfied the plaintiff's judgment. It also cited *People of Illinois v. Maryland Casualty Co.*, which treated Rule 14's reference to possible liability as permitting impleader before the indemnified loss occurred. These decisions illustrated the distinction between establishing a conditional obligation and collecting money before that obligation became enforceable. 2 F.R.D. at 240.

Swift also invoked the limitation on contribution for intentional wrongdoing or acts the contributor knowingly performed unlawfully. The court refused to presume such culpability on the pleadings. If B/G's allegations were established, its potential violation of the food statutes could be technical rather than intentional or knowing. That response did not establish B/G's eventual right to contribution. The court expressly left open whether B/G had a right to contribution, indemnity, or neither. A difference between the patrons' theory against the restaurant and the restaurant's theory against Swift likewise did not make impleader improper. The question of B/G's insurance was immaterial to this motion. 2 F.R.D. at 240–41.

One jury could determine the underlying injury and the related claim over, avoiding the sequence of an original suit followed by a separate contribution or indemnity suit. The patrons' refusal to sue Swift directly did not eliminate B/G's possible derivative claim. Swift's payment protection could be preserved by staying any contribution or indemnity judgment until B/G paid or satisfied the patrons' judgment. On the showing made, concurrent determination therefore served efficiency without identifying prejudice requiring Swift's removal.

## Exact order and significance

The court **denied Swift's motion to vacate the third-party order in each case** and allowed Swift twenty days after filing of the order to answer. It reserved Swift's exception. Nordbye was the sole deciding judge; no separate writing or judicial footnotes appear in the report. The order neither entered damages nor finally resolved the patrons' claims or B/G's claim over. 2 F.R.D. at 241.

The important procedural lesson is that a contingent derivative claim can be heard before it becomes independently collectible, provided the ultimate relief respects its substantive conditions. Rule 14 did not itself create an entitlement to contribution or indemnity. The historical rule quoted in the full opinion also contained language permitting liability to the plaintiff; readers should distinguish that quoted version from later amendments rather than silently treating it as current text.
