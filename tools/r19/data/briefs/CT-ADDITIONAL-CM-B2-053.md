# Laidlaw v. Organ
15 U.S. (2 Wheat.) 178 (1817) | Contracts

## Court, issue, and disposition

Chief Justice Marshall delivered the Supreme Court's short opinion on March 15, 1817, as dated on original reporter page 195. February 20 on page 184 marks argument, although CAP's case metadata assigns that date to the decision. The Court concluded that Organ was not bound to disclose the extrinsic market intelligence at issue where the means of obtaining it were equally accessible. It nevertheless reversed the judgment in his favor because the trial court had instructed the jury absolutely to find for him. Whether he had practiced an imposition upon the seller had to go to the jury. The mandate remanded to the Louisiana district court with directions for a venire facias de novo—a new jury trial. No separate opinion or numerical voting lineup appears in the complete report. (184, 195.)

## Tobacco sale and news of peace

Organ claimed to have purchased 111 hogsheads of tobacco from Peter Laidlaw & Co. The bill of parcels, dated February 18, 1815, listed 120,715 pounds and a price of $7,544.69. He alleged delivery and possession, followed by Laidlaw's forcible repossession on February 20. His petition sought the tobacco and damages and stated that he had tendered the agreed purchase money in bills of exchange. To prevent removal or disposal, he also sought sequestration in the marshal's hands pending judgment. These petition allegations must be distinguished from the later bill of exceptions describing the transaction. (178–179.)

The bill of exceptions recounted that Livingston, White, and Shepherd brought news of the Treaty of Ghent from the British fleet on the night of February 18. White made the news public in a handbill about 8 a.m. on Sunday, February 19. Shepherd's brother, who had a one-third interest in the purchase's profits, told Organ the news that morning. Organ then approached Francis Girault, a member of Laidlaw's firm with whom he had bargained the previous evening, soon after sunrise and before Girault knew the news. The purchase was made and the bill delivered between 8 and 9 a.m. The report thus preserves a February 18 document date alongside testimony locating completion the next morning; this brief does not silently erase the distinction. (182–183.)

Girault asked whether there was news likely to increase the tobacco's value. The statement of the case said there was no evidence that Organ asserted or suggested anything calculated to make Girault believe no such news existed. Counsel for the sellers characterized Organ as silent in response and argued that silence amounted to deception. The news reportedly increased tobacco's value by thirty to fifty percent. On February 20 Girault promised to supply an invoice rather than objecting to the sale. (183–184, 188–190.)

## District court proceedings

The marshal sequestered the tobacco, and the court required Organ to provide a $1,000 indemnity bond. Laidlaw & Co. disclaimed any ownership, while Boorman & Johnston intervened, asserting that the tobacco actually belonged to them and disputing Organ's claim under the alleged sale. At the April 20, 1815 trial, Girault was offered as a witness. The claimants argued that Laidlaw's firm had acted as commission merchants, customarily selling in their own names for principals. The district court sustained Organ's objection to Girault's competency. That ruling was preserved, but Marshall's later opinion did not separately decide the witness question. (179–182.)

The judge instructed the jury to find for Organ. Its verdict awarded him the tobacco without damages, payable according to the contract. The court ordered delivery upon deposit of endorsed bills of exchange for the purchase price and awarded costs. A writ of error was allowed on April 29, 1815, and the deposited bills were later taken from court by the opposing parties. CAP's native text misreads the writ date as 1845; the original page clearly reads 1815. The Supreme Court's reversal set aside this compelled favorable result rather than affirming Organ's ultimate right to the goods. (181, 184–185, 195.)

## Arguments and the narrow judicial rule

Ingersoll, for the parties challenging the judgment, argued that withholding material information in response to a direct inquiry could constitute fraud without an express falsehood. He contended that the parties were dealing on unequal informational terms and that the jury, rather than the judge, should determine whether there was fraud. He also contested Girault's exclusion and the requirement for payment in bills of exchange. His reply described the transaction as circumvention despite the absence of proof of a positive misleading assertion. These positions were counsel's arguments, not separate findings by the Supreme Court. (184–191, 194.)

Key, for Organ, distinguished moral obligations from legally enforceable duties and defended informational advantage acquired through diligence or good fortune. He argued that the seller could have obtained the same intelligence, that silence might be understood either way, and that the seller failed to insist on an answer. He also defended the witness exclusion and the form of the district court's relief. Marshall did not expressly adopt each of these arguments, especially the asserted waiver through failure to insist. (191–194.)

Marshall's actual opinion on page 195 separates nondisclosure of extrinsic information from conduct tending to impose upon the other party. He rejected a disclosure obligation in the circumstances presented, explaining the difficulty of properly limiting a contrary rule where information was equally accessible. Yet both parties had to avoid misleading words or conduct. The absolute instruction was erroneous because the jury should decide whether Organ had practiced an imposition. Superior knowledge alone therefore did not establish fraud, while the Court also did not declare the entire transaction free of fraud as a matter of law. The remand left that factual question open. (195.)

## Report annotations and assigned account

The original report's extensive apparatus includes a note on sequestration, authority references, a lengthy translated discussion of Pothier's sale doctrine and the distinction between conscience and civil enforcement, and a note on intervention and witness competency. These materials were all read, including continuations across pages, but are reporter annotations surrounding counsel's submissions, not additional pages of Marshall's opinion or separately adopted Court holdings. In particular, the reporter's discussion of Girault's continued status as a party should not be presented as a Supreme Court resolution of the preserved witness objection. (179–180, 185–190, 191–193.)

The complete assigned concealment account at C&M volume 351–700, PDF page 201, printed page 508, was read in native text and the original scan. It supplies the blockade context and characterizes Marshall's disclosure statement as dictum. The brief preserves the actual page-195 rule, its equally-accessible-information qualification, and the separate jury question; it does not treat the assignment's abbreviated seller-rescission description as the complete procedural history.
