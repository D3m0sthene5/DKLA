# Security Stove & Manufacturing Co. v. American Railway Express Co.
227 Mo. App. 175, 51 S.W.2d 572 (1932) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Missouri’s Kansas City Court of Appeals decided the case on May 23, 1932. Judge Bland wrote the opinion, and all concurred; no separate writing appears. Following a bench trial, the carrier appealed a judgment reimbursing the manufacturer’s wasted exhibition expenses. The appellate court affirmed. Its reasoning allowed definite expenditures frustrated by a delay of which the carrier had notice, rather than requiring proof of speculative profits from exhibiting a new product. Pinpoints below refer to the complete Missouri Appeals report. (175–176, 180–184.)

## Shipment and frustrated exhibition
Security manufactured a furnace with a special combination oil and gas burner and wanted to show it at the American Gas Association convention in Atlantic City during the week beginning October 11, 1926. Its president hoped to interest the Henry L. Dougherty Company in the product; the shipment was primarily for demonstration, not for a sale at the convention. Security reserved a booth, but by that time it was too late to ship by freight. (176.)

On September 18, Security wrote American Railway Express explaining the exhibit and the need for arrival no later than October 8. The carrier’s representative visited and noted that October 4 shipment would allow timely delivery. Security wrote again on October 1 stressing the deadline and arranging collection for October 2, earlier than required. The carrier collected twenty-one numbered packages, each labeled to resist loss of its sticker. (176–177.)

Security’s president arrived about October 11 and found the shipment at the booth, but the gas manifold was missing. That component controlled gas flow and was the exhibit’s most important part; a replacement could not be obtained there. The missing package was located in the carrier’s St. Louis “over and short” department. Although the carrier said it would arrive on Wednesday, October 13, it had not arrived when the convention closed on Thursday. The president left. The carrier’s witness testified that delivery at the Atlantic City hotel occurred October 16; the president described the package’s return approximately a week after he reached Kansas City. Ordinary transit took three or four days. Security had paid the regular rate and requested no special route, train, rate, or expedited preference. (177–178.)

## Claim and carrier’s defenses
Security sought its express charges to Atlantic City, return freight, railway and Pullman fares, hotel charges, compensation for its president’s time, another employee’s wages, and booth rental. The opinion’s opening describes a $801.50 principal judgment plus interest, totaling $1,000. Its later itemization totals $801.51; that one-cent reporting inconsistency is preserved rather than silently converted into a new judgment. (176, 179.)

The carrier argued first that the suit enforced a special promise to deliver on a fixed date, prohibited by federal interstate-commerce law because it would discriminate among shippers. It also contended that only lost profits were recoverable and that none had been proved. Finally, it argued that the exhibition expenses would have been incurred even with proper delivery, so refunding them improperly restored the position before contracting rather than the position performance would have produced. (179–182.)

## Ordinary carriage obligation and notice of special loss
The court accepted the historical federal rule against preferential contracts promising special transportation within a specified time. But it read the unchallenged petition liberally as alleging failure to deliver within a reasonable time. The October 8 date demonstrated both the ordinary time allowed for carriage and the carrier’s knowledge of the shipment’s purpose. The actual arrangement demanded nothing that another shipper could not obtain on the same terms. It therefore did not create an unlawful special advantage. (179–180.)

Ordinarily, delay damages for goods could be measured by the difference in their market value between timely and actual delivery. Here that measure missed the known exhibition purpose. Where a carrier receives sufficient notice of peculiar circumstances producing unusual losses from delay, it can be responsible for the real loss contemplated in that setting. American Railway Express knew the importance of timely arrival and the use of the furnace. The numbered package’s placement in the over-and-short department indicated mistake or negligence, and the carrier made no effort to explain either that handling or the failure to forward it promptly after discovery. The court described negligence as practically conceded. (180–181.)

## Definite wasted expenses rather than conjectural profits
The record contained no claim or evidence of lost profits. Security had not shipped the furnace to sell it or compete for a prize. The court distinguished authorities involving an unsuccessful prize entry or insufficient proof of sales: those cases did not make profits the exclusive possible measure for a demonstration shipment. Nor did the carrier establish that this exhibition would have been entirely worthless. (181–184.)

The court recognized the general performance-based principle of compensation but rejected the proposition that it invariably barred recovery of expenses that proper performance would also have required. Breach had deprived Security of the purpose for which those expenses were incurred. Without reimbursement of its definite expenditures, it would receive no substantial compensation for its inability to exhibit. The opinion used authorities involving business preparation and other difficult-to-value benefits to explain why a practical approximation of real damage could be preferable to a supposedly certain rule that excluded the actual loss. It did not award the conjectural value of a later Dougherty contract or any presumed sales. (182–184.)

The booth rental presented an additional timing issue: Security had already incurred it before making the shipping agreement. The court nevertheless allowed it in these circumstances. Security arranged the exhibit in contemplation of being able to call on the carrier’s common-law obligation to accept and transport goods with reasonable dispatch. Timely delivery would have made that already-incurred expense useful; the known-purpose breach wasted it. The reasoning is therefore tied to the carrier relationship, disclosure, and the expenditure’s connection to the exhibition, rather than a universal statement that every precontract cost becomes recoverable reliance. (184.)

## Disposition and source distinctions
The court affirmed the judgment, with all concurring. (184.)
