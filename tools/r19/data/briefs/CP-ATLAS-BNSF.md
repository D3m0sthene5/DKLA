# BNSF Railway Co. v. Tyrrell

**581 U.S. 402 (2017) · Supreme Court of the United States · May 30, 2017**

## Two employment-injury suits brought away from the alleged injuries

Robert Nelson, a North Dakota resident, sued BNSF in a Montana state court in March 2011 under the Federal Employers’ Liability Act. He alleged knee injuries suffered while working as a fuel-truck driver. In May 2014, Kelli Tyrrell, appointed in South Dakota to administer her husband Brent’s estate, brought another Montana FELA suit. She alleged that exposure to carcinogenic chemicals during his BNSF employment had caused fatal kidney cancer. Neither plaintiff alleged an injury arising from or related to work in Montana. The record indicated that neither injured worker appeared ever to have worked for BNSF there. These were employment-injury allegations; the Supreme Court did not adjudicate their merits. 581 U.S. at 404–406.

BNSF was incorporated in Delaware and had its principal place of business in Texas. It operated in 28 States. Montana nevertheless contained a considerable part of its physical operations: 2,061 miles of track and approximately 2,100 employees. Those figures represented about six percent of its total track and less than five percent of its workforce. Montana generated less than ten percent of its revenue and contained one of its 24 automotive facilities. The dispute concerned whether this substantial business permitted Montana to hear claims unrelated to that business. Id. at 406–407.

## Conflicting trial decisions and Montana’s two jurisdictional theories

BNSF moved to dismiss both suits for lack of personal jurisdiction. The trial court granted the motion in Nelson’s case and denied it in Tyrrell’s. The Montana Supreme Court consolidated the matters and upheld general jurisdiction on two grounds. It read 45 U.S.C. § 56 as authorizing state-court personal jurisdiction over a railroad doing business in the State. It also held that BNSF was a person found within Montana under the State’s Rule 4(b)(1). Its employees and track satisfied those descriptions. The court distinguished *Daimler* because that case involved neither a railroad nor a FELA claim. Justice McKinnon dissented, distinguishing venue and subject-matter jurisdiction from personal jurisdiction and concluding that *Daimler* controlled. Id. at 407–408.

The Supreme Court therefore addressed two separate questions: whether FELA supplied the asserted personal-jurisdiction authority, and whether the alternative exercise of Montana jurisdiction satisfied Fourteenth Amendment due process. A statutory conclusion alone would not dispose of the state-law ground. Id. at 407–408, 412.

## Holding, judgment, and voting alignment

Section 56 did not confer personal jurisdiction over railroads. Its relevant provisions addressed federal venue and concurrent state and federal subject-matter jurisdiction. Montana’s alternative exercise of general jurisdiction was unconstitutional because BNSF was not essentially at home there and the claims had no connection with its Montana operations. The Court **reversed the Montana Supreme Court and remanded both cases** for proceedings consistent with its opinion. It expressly left the plaintiffs’ separate consent argument unresolved. Id. at 405–406, 412–415.

Justice Ginsburg wrote for eight Justices: Roberts, Kennedy, Thomas, Breyer, Alito, Kagan, and Gorsuch joined her. Justice Sotomayor joined Parts I and II but dissented from Part III and the judgment. Thus, the statutory interpretation received unanimous agreement; the constitutional application and resulting judgment divided the Court eight to one. Id. at 404, 415–416.

## Why FELA’s jurisdictional language did not establish personal jurisdiction

The first relevant sentence of § 56 allowed a federal action in the district of the defendant’s residence, where the cause arose, or where the defendant was doing business when suit began. That language identifies permissible locations for suit. The Court had already interpreted it as expanding venue beyond the older general venue provision. Congress commonly describes venue through language stating where an action may be brought; it ordinarily addresses personal jurisdiction through provisions authorizing service of process. This distinction explained why a business’s presence could make a district an appropriate venue without automatically making every court there competent to adjudicate every claim against it. Id. at 408–409. Footnote 1 clarifies that the Court’s shorthand excludes § 56’s actual first sentence, which imposed a time limit and was irrelevant here. Id. at 408 n.1.

Nelson and Tyrrell argued that legislative reports referred to earlier dismissals for want of jurisdiction and that Congress had addressed both venue and personal jurisdiction. The Court rejected the inference. It had long understood the predecessor statute as governing venue, and the Senate Report expressly described the new provision in venue terms. Reading the predecessor as a personal-jurisdiction grant would also have produced the implausible result that a diversity plaintiff could compel a defendant to litigate in the plaintiff’s home district despite no affiliation between that district and the defendant or controversy. Id. at 409–410 & n.2.

The second relevant sentence made federal jurisdiction concurrent with that of state courts. Concurrent jurisdiction is a familiar description of shared **subject-matter** competence. Congress added it against the background of a Connecticut decision treating FELA claims as exclusively federal. The sentence confirmed that state courts could hear this type of claim; it did not dispense with the separate requirement of authority over the particular defendant. Plaintiffs’ proposed interpretation also depended on a personal-jurisdiction grant in the preceding sentence that was not actually there. Id. at 410–411.

The Court separately examined four precedents invoked by Montana. *Terte* concerned an asserted burden on interstate commerce; *Kepner* and *Miles* concerned injunctions against residents pursuing FELA litigation elsewhere; *Pope* concerned whether federal transfer legislation changed the state-court injunction rule. None decided the personal-jurisdiction question presented here. Most also preceded *International Shoe*’s transformation of jurisdictional doctrine. Permission to pursue an action, venue, interference with interstate commerce, and personal jurisdiction therefore could not be treated as interchangeable holdings. Id. at 411–412.

## Why substantial Montana business did not establish an at-home relationship

BNSF did not dispute that Montana considered it found within the State under its own procedural rule. The remaining question was constitutional. Because neither plaintiff claimed an injury connected to Montana employment, the Court addressed general jurisdiction, which permits adjudication of unrelated claims. The ordinary corporate homes are the place of incorporation and principal place of business. An exceptional corporation may be at home elsewhere, as illustrated by *Perkins*, where wartime relocation made Ohio the center of the Philippine company’s activities. Id. at 412–413.

The Court rejected Montana’s proposed distinction between ordinary businesses and FELA railroads. The due-process restriction applies to state-court assertions of general jurisdiction over nonresident defendants regardless of the enterprise or claim involved. FELA’s venue provision did not create an alternative constitutional home wherever a railroad did business. Id. at 414 & n.3.

The Montana track and employees demonstrated extensive operations, but the inquiry required an appraisal of BNSF’s activities as a whole. A multistate corporation does not become at home in every State with substantial operations. BNSF’s Montana activity could support specific jurisdiction over claims related to that activity; it did not support these unrelated claims through general jurisdiction. The Court did not announce a universal numerical ceiling on miles, employees, or revenue. Its conclusion concerned the character of this corporation’s relationship to Montana under the at-home standard. Id. at 413–414.

Footnote 4 answered Sotomayor’s reliance on *International Shoe*. That case’s in-state activities generated the liabilities sued upon. It therefore did not require a comparison of corporate activities across States to determine authority over unrelated claims. Here, by contrast, the injuries had no relationship to events or their principal impact in Montana. The same note explained why deciding Montana’s alternative constitutional ground was proper: the state court had expressly decided that ground. Id. at 414–415 n.4.

## Sotomayor’s disagreement over doctrine and procedure

Sotomayor agreed that FELA did not confer personal jurisdiction and that being a railroad did not answer the due-process question. She nonetheless maintained her objection to *Daimler*’s restrictive at-home approach. In her view, *International Shoe* called for a contextual assessment of whether continuous corporate operations were substantial enough to justify adjudicating unrelated claims. A corporation receiving substantial benefits in the forum could fairly bear corresponding litigation burdens. Id. at 415–417.

She criticized the comparative-contacts approach for favoring large corporations: extensive operations elsewhere could prevent general jurisdiction despite significant local activity. She considered the quality and quantity of local contacts more important than their percentage of nationwide activity. Her notes addressed commentary on the doctrinal change, disputed the majority’s reading of *International Shoe*, and observed that *Perkins* and *Helicopteros* had not conducted the comparative analysis used here. These were arguments in her separate opinion, rather than rules adopted by the Court. Id. at 417–418 & separate-opinion nn.1–3.

Even accepting the Court’s standard, Sotomayor would have remanded for Montana to apply it through a fact-intensive inquiry. She thought the majority reduced *Daimler*’s exceptional-case reservation to circumstances virtually identical to *Perkins*, then applied that narrow approach itself. Her final note identified recent cases illustrating the usual remand practice when a lower court applies the wrong standard. She dissented from the judgment, rather than concluding that plaintiffs had already established jurisdiction under the correct test. Id. at 418–419 & separate-opinion n.4.

## Scope of the assigned account and the unresolved consent issue

The assigned casebook’s separately captioned Note 2 begins on PDF page 111 and continues through page 112, printed pages 170–172. It reproduces BNSF’s operational figures, the majority’s at-home analysis, and Sotomayor’s comparative-contacts criticism. It is an edited account rather than the complete opinion. The full report supplies the FELA statutory analysis, procedural history, all four majority notes, and all four separate-opinion notes.

Plaintiffs also argued that BNSF had consented to Montana jurisdiction. Because the Montana Supreme Court had not addressed that contention, the Supreme Court declined to decide it. The decision consequently rejects the two grounds actually decided below while preserving the consent question for further proceedings. This brief states the historical 2017 decision; it does not certify later treatment or resolve that reserved issue. 581 U.S. at 415.
