# Oneal v. Colton Consolidated School District No. 306
16 Wash. App. 488, 557 P.2d 11 (1976) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Washington’s Court of Appeals, Division Three, decided this employment-contract appeal on December 8, 1976. Chief Judge McInturff wrote, joined by Judges Green and Munson. The court reversed the superior court’s approval of the teacher’s discharge and ordered that discharge expunged from his employment record, but affirmed denial of accumulated sick-leave benefits. Each side bore its own costs and attorney fees. The teacher therefore prevailed on the adverse employment entry, rather than on a claim for payment of the leave he had accumulated. (488–489, 492–493.)

## Facts and procedure
Stanley Oneal signed an employment contract in April 1974 to teach during the 1974–75 school year beginning August 29. Diabetes had caused his eyesight to deteriorate over several years. During the summer after the preceding school year he realized he could no longer perform his teaching duties. On July 24 he asked to be released after using his 27½ days of accumulated sick leave. He did not report for work at the beginning of the term, although the district had not accepted his conditional resignation. The district refused his offer September 20; he renewed his insistence on sick leave September 25. On October 3 it discharged him with notice of probable cause, and also accepted his resignation while refusing leave payment. The appellate court described that combination of resignation and discharge as confusing. (489–490.)

Oneal appealed directly to superior court under the school-employment statute and sought damages including sick leave. That court upheld the district. The appellate question was how the contractual relationship actually ended: through resignation, through disciplinary discharge, or through operation of law. General contract principles governed that question. (489–490 & n. 1.)

## Findings supporting impossibility
The trial court found that Oneal’s diabetic eyesight had deteriorated and that before July 24 he believed he could not perform the next year’s duties. Its memorandum clarified that this was actual physical incapacity, rather than a teacher’s unsupported preference to withdraw. The appellate court read the findings in light of that memorandum and accepted them as substantially supported. A retirement-system official testified that a medical report found Oneal disabled from teaching and eligible for disability benefits. A fellow teacher described his need to hold papers close, write large letters, and replace the ordinary grade book with a more readable record. Older reading textbooks compounded the difficulty. His wife corroborated his frustration trying to prepare summer lesson plans. (490–491 & n. 2.)

## Holding and reasoning
The duty to teach was discharged by operation of law through impossibility before performance was due. The court’s conception of impossibility included impracticability from extreme and unreasonable difficulty, expense, injury, or loss; it was not limited to an act that no person could physically accomplish under any conditions. Here the supported finding of greatly impaired eyesight satisfied the doctrine for this teacher’s personal performance. (491 & nn. 3–4.)

The superior court had also concluded that Oneal breached and that probable cause justified his discharge. Those conclusions could not survive the discharge of his underlying contractual duty. Once law excused that duty, failure to teach was not a breach of an enforceable teaching obligation. The district consequently lacked probable or sufficient cause under the cited school-employment statute for the adverse discharge entry. The appellate court ordered the entry expunged; it did not compel him to resume teaching or decide the remaining assignments of error. (491, 493.)

## Why sick leave was nevertheless denied
Excuse operated on both sides of the exchange. Oneal’s complete failure to perform, though legally excused, also discharged the district’s duties under this contract, including its sick-leave payment provision. Because the contract was completely discharged before August 29, the employment relationship had ended before the period for which he sought leave. The statute provided that accumulated leave not taken when a person retires or ceases school employment was not compensable, subject to its specified retirement-service-credit treatment. The court therefore affirmed denial of the claimed leave payment. The brief does not equate the acknowledged eligibility for disability benefits with an adjudicated right to receive contractual sick pay after this employment ceased. (492 & n. 5.)

The statutory fee award was discretionary. Requiring each side to bear its own fees and costs below did not establish abuse of discretion. The same approach on appeal was warranted because each side prevailed on a major issue and lost on another. No damages amount was awarded by this decision. (492–493 & nn. 6–7.)
