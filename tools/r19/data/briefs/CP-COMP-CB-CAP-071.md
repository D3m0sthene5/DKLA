# McDonald v. Mabee

**Civil Procedure · CP-COMP-CB-CAP-071 · 243 U.S. 90 (1917) · March 6, 1917**

McDonald held a prior personal money judgment void where the only service was local newspaper publication after the defendant had permanently left Texas intending to live elsewhere. The unusual posture matters: the **defendant relied on the plaintiff's earlier judgment as a bar to a second suit on the same note**, while the plaintiff denied that earlier judgment's personal effect. The Court **reversed the Texas Supreme Court's judgment**. It did not decide the merits of the note or announce a universal prohibition on substitute service for every absent domiciliary. (243 U.S. at 90–93.)

McDonald sued Mabee on a promissory note. Mabee's material defense was that McDonald had already obtained a Texas judgment on the same note. That earlier judgment purported both to bind Mabee personally and to foreclose a lien securing the note. The validity of the personal component, rather than every possible consequence of the lien foreclosure, was the question before the Court. (Id. at 90–91.)

When the first suit began, Mabee was technically domiciled in Texas but had left intending to establish a home elsewhere. His family still lived in Texas. He returned briefly and later established a Missouri domicile. Publication in a local newspaper, once weekly for four successive weeks after his final departure, was the only service. He never appeared. The Texas Supreme Court held that publication satisfied state law and that the earlier judgment was a valid personal judgment, rejecting McDonald's federal due process objection to giving it that effect. (Id. at 91.)

Justice Holmes began with the historical foundation of jurisdiction in physical power. That power need not be maintained throughout a proceeding properly begun, and appearance can substitute for personal service. The opinion acknowledged possible extensions beyond service on the person or appearance, while emphasizing natural justice and actual fair play. A legal fiction about domicile could not displace the facts relevant to whether a court had acquired jurisdiction. (Id. at 91.)

Publication could not ordinarily support a personal judgment against a nonresident. Some language in the earlier decisions suggested a broader proposition about absent persons, but Holmes expressly confined the decision to the facts presented. Mabee's technical Texas domicile at the start did not make a newspaper advertisement sufficient after he had left intending not to return. The Court reasoned that, when personal service was dispensed with, the substitute most likely to reach the defendant was the minimum appropriate to substantial justice. (Id. at 92.)

The opinion cautiously reserved a different possibility. Because Mabee's family remained in Texas and he was still technically domiciled there when suit began, service at his last and usual abode might perhaps have been adequate. The Court did not decide that point, and called even that proposed extension of jurisdiction an extreme. Neither a holding that abode service would definitely suffice nor a holding that it could never suffice can be attributed to this decision. The facts supporting publication in this case were inadequate. (Id.)

The Court also rejected a distinction between domestic and interstate effectiveness. An ordinary personal money judgment entered without constitutionally adequate service was ineffective in its rendering state as well as elsewhere. The opinion reserved possible differences for decrees concerning status or its incidents. The present judgment's personal force could not survive simply because the later suit was also in Texas. (Id. at 92–93.)

Nor did the identity of the party asserting invalidity change the result. The usual jurisdictional challenge would come from a defendant resisting enforcement of a judgment against him. Here Mabee invoked the earlier judgment defensively, and McDonald—the party who had obtained it—challenged its personal effect. Judgments impose reciprocal obligations. If the prior personal judgment was invalid when asserted by its successful plaintiff, it was equally invalid as a defendant's bar to the original cause of action. It was void, not merely voidable. The Court distinguished a cited decision involving an absent defendant who intended to return to the state. (Id. at 93.)

Holmes wrote the Court's opinion, and no separate writing, judicial footnotes, or numerical vote statement is reported. The exact disposition is “Judgment reversed,” without an express remand in the reported final line. The textbook's developed account occupies CivPro PDF pages 147–148, printed page 245, following Mullane; Mullane's reproduced reasoning on PDF page 147 also cites McDonald. The short textbook account correctly emphasizes the inadequacy of publication, while the complete opinion adds the prior-judgment defense, its reciprocal effect, and the carefully reserved abode-service possibility. Questions elsewhere on these pages about other forms of notice belong to their respective cases and are not additional McDonald holdings.
