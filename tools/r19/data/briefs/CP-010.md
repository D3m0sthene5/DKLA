Lavender v. Kurn
Citation
Lavender v. Kurn, 327 U.S. 645, 66 S. Ct. 740, 90 L. Ed. 916 (1946). Supreme Court of the United States;
argued March 6-7, 1946; decided March 25, 1946. Justice Murphy delivered the Court’s opinion. The Chief
Justice and Justice Frankfurter concurred in the result; Justice Reed dissented; Justice Jackson did not
participate. The casebook excerpt begins at page 63 and ends at page 67. The exact dates and Jackson’s
nonparticipation come from the full report rather than the excerpt. Full judicial opinion.
Parties
Lavender was the administrator of the estate of L. E. Haney, meaning the representative authorized to pursue
the deceased worker’s claim. He was the plaintiff below and the petitioner seeking Supreme Court review.
The respondents were the trustees of the St. Louis-San Francisco Railway Company, called Frisco in the
opinion, and the Illinois Central Railroad Company. The estate alleged that their negligence caused Haney’s
death and sought damages under the Federal Employers’ Liability Act, a federal statute allowing covered
railroad workers to recover for injuries caused in whole or in part by a carrier’s negligence. 327 U.S. at
646-47.
Material Facts
Haney worked as a switch-tender in the railroad yards near Memphis’s Grand Central Station. Illinois
Central owned the station and employed him, and Frisco paid part of his wages for switching services. On
the very dark evening of December 21, 1939, he opened a switch so a Frisco passenger train could back
toward the station at approximately eight to ten miles an hour. The train cleared the switch, but the switch
stayed open and the signals remained red. Haney was found unconscious north of the tracks and died from a
fractured skull. No known eyewitness saw the fatal blow. 327 U.S. at 647-48.
The estate’s theory was that the end of a loosely hanging mail hook on the train struck the back of Haney’s
head. Witnesses gave conflicting measurements of the hook’s possible outward swing and of a nearby
mound of cinders and dirt. Using the evidence most favorable to the estate, the mound could have raised
Haney into the hook’s path while he stood near the mail car. The location and shape of the head wound were
consistent with that possibility. A doctor testified that a fast-moving object on a backing train could have
caused the fracture, but also acknowledged that a person wielding a pipe or club could have caused it. These
were competing evidentiary possibilities, not eyewitness findings. Id. at 648-49.
The railroads argued that Haney had been murdered. They relied on measurements and photographs
suggesting that the hook could not reach him, the darkness, and the presence of people riding freight trains in
the area. His pistol was found beneath him, and his wallet was later found without money. Other evidence
weakened the murder theory: there was no sign of a struggle, no other weapon was found, his watch and ring
remained on him, and testimony suggested that the pistol had fallen from his clothing. The position of his
body and blood away from his head also left room for disagreement about where he had first been struck. Id.
at 648-50.
A Frisco foreman repeated an unknown switchman’s statement that something protruding from the train was
thought to have struck Haney. The switchman had not witnessed the incident. The railroads objected that
this was hearsay, an out-of-court statement offered to prove what it asserted. The trial judge admitted it over
that objection. Id. at 650-51.

Procedural History
The case was tried in the Circuit Court of the City of St. Louis, Missouri; the full report supplies that court’s
name, which the excerpt omits. The jury awarded the estate $30,000, and judgment was entered on the
verdict. The jury was instructed that Frisco could be liable for negligently allowing an object to protrude
from the train and Illinois Central could be liable for negligently maintaining an unsafe workplace through
uneven ground and inadequate lighting. The latter instruction did not require a finding that the train struck
Haney. 327 U.S. at 646-47, 651.
The Supreme Court of Missouri reversed. Although it acknowledged that the mail-hook inference could be
drawn, it deemed that inference speculation and found no substantial evidence that either the hook or the
workplace conditions caused the death. It also held the switchman’s reported statement inadmissible under
the res gestae doctrine, a doctrine allowing certain statements connected closely with an event. The United
States Supreme Court granted certiorari, discretionary review of the lower court’s judgment. Id. at 647,
651-52.
Issue
Could the Missouri Supreme Court overturn the jury’s negligence verdict by choosing the murder theory or
the railroads’ measurements over the estate’s evidence, when the record supplied a reasonable evidentiary
basis for the jury’s conclusion? Did the disputed hearsay ruling require a different result?
Rule and Holding
The Court held that sufficient evidence supported the verdict against both railroads. Once the record
disclosed a reasonable basis for the jury’s inference of negligence and causation, the reviewing court could
not reweigh conflicting evidence or substitute its preferred inference. The Court stated: “Only when there is
a complete absence of probative facts to support the conclusion reached does a reversible error appear.”
Probative facts are facts having a tendency to prove the disputed conclusion. The Court’s application of this
rule concerned appellate review of this verdict under the Federal Employers’ Liability Act. 327 U.S. at
652-54.
The Court did not decide whether the challenged hearsay was properly admitted. The remaining evidence
independently supplied adequate support for the verdict, so that evidentiary question was unnecessary to its
disposition. Id. at 654.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Missouri Supreme Court’s judgment and remanded for any further
proceedings consistent with its opinion. It rejected the state court’s stated basis for setting aside the jury’s
verdict; it did not conduct a new trial or make a fresh determination of how Haney died. 327 U.S. at 654.
Reasoning
The mail-hook theory rested on identifiable evidence. The hook’s range of movement, the possible location
and height of the mound, Haney’s height, the wound, and evidence about his duties together permitted the
inference that the hook struck him. The record did not have to establish every intermediate event by direct
observation. The Court treated the combined circumstantial evidence as enough to let the jury resolve the
dispute. Circumstantial evidence proves an event through surrounding facts rather than an eyewitness
account of the event itself. 327 U.S. at 648-49, 652.

The body’s final location did not make the estate’s theory unreasonable. Haney could have moved after
being struck and then fallen; blood found away from his head supported that possibility. The Court did not
find that this movement actually occurred. It explained why the final resting place did not eliminate a
reasonable inference that the train had caused the injury. Id. at 652.
Evidence supporting murder or the physical impossibility of contact remained evidence the jury could
evaluate. It did not authorize the appellate court to retry the factual dispute after the jury selected a
reasonably supported account. The Missouri court’s error was to use conflicting evidence as a reason to
substitute its judgment on witness credibility and competing inferences. Id. at 652-53.
The Court distinguished an unsupported verdict from a verdict requiring inference. When fair-minded
people can draw different conclusions from disputed facts, the finder of fact necessarily chooses among
possibilities. That choice does not become impermissible speculation merely because another conclusion is
plausible. Once an evidentiary basis appears, appellate disagreement about which conclusion is more
persuasive does not itself establish reversible error. Id. at 653.
Illinois Central’s liability also had support independent of the appellate court’s preferred account. Darkness
and high, uneven ground were undisputed, and Illinois Central controlled the workplace. The jury could
reasonably view those conditions as unsafe and as contributing to the death on the mail-hook theory. The
opinion therefore sustained the evidentiary foundation for both defendants’ liability rather than discussing
the moving train alone. Id. at 653.
Finally, the Court separated evidentiary sufficiency from the challenged admission ruling. Trial judges
ordinarily exercise discretion over admissibility in these actions, but the Court did not decide whether that
discretion had been misused here. Because the verdict had adequate support without the switchman’s
statement, it left the hearsay question unresolved. Id. at 654.
Separate Opinions
The report records that the Chief Justice and Justice Frankfurter concurred in the result and Justice Reed
dissented, but supplies no separate written reasoning for those positions. Justice Jackson took no part in the
consideration or decision; that participation statement is omitted from the casebook excerpt. No reasons for
these positions are inferred. 327 U.S. at 654.
Notes
Narrow reading — interpretation. In this railroad-negligence action, specific physical and circumstantial
evidence supported the jury’s account of the fatal injury. An appellate court could not overturn the verdict
because the defendants offered a plausible competing account or conflicting measurements. The result does
not establish as historical fact that a mail hook struck Haney. 327 U.S. at 652-54.
Broad reading — interpretation. The opinion protects the distinction between testing whether a verdict
has an evidentiary foundation and deciding which side’s evidence is more convincing. Its language favors
substantial deference to a jury’s reasonable inferences, while expressly preserving reversal where probative
support is completely absent. The casebook’s following note asks how this language fits a decision
involving equally supported inconsistent inferences; that question is an editorial comparison, not a holding
extending this opinion to every procedural setting. Id. at 653; casebook pp. 67-68.
