# Frigaliment Importing Co., Ltd. v. B.N.S. International Sales Corp.
190 F.Supp. 116 (S.D.N.Y. 1960) | Contracts | Ambiguous description, trade usage, and burden of persuasion

## Two contracts and a disputed class of poultry

Frigaliment, a Swiss corporation, bought poultry from B.N.S., a New York sales corporation, under two contracts dated May 2, 1957. The first called for 75,000 pounds of birds weighing 2½–3 pounds at $33 per hundredweight and 25,000 pounds of birds weighing 1½–2 pounds at $36.50 per hundredweight. The description specified fresh-frozen, Grade A, government-inspected, eviscerated U.S. chicken, individually Cryovac-wrapped and export-packed, with shipment scheduled May 10, free alongside ship in New York. The second reduced the heavier quantity to 50,000 pounds, priced the smaller birds at $37 per hundredweight, and scheduled shipment May 30. (190 F.Supp. at 117.)

The first shipment was short, with its balance sent May 17. On May 28, after arrival in Switzerland, Frigaliment discovered that the heavier birds were mature stewing chickens rather than young broilers or fryers. Many packages explicitly identified that type. Frigaliment protested. The second shipment nevertheless left May 29 and again included mature heavier birds; B.N.S. stopped its transportation at Rotterdam. Frigaliment sued for breach of the statutory warranty that the goods correspond to their description, arguing that “chicken” meant young birds suitable for frying or broiling. B.N.S. contended that it included any chicken meeting the stipulated quality and weight requirements. (Id. at 117–118.)

Circuit Judge Friendly adjudicated the action in the district court. He applied New York law because the parties apparently agreed it governed, notwithstanding the Swiss acceptance. The complete opinion's first footnote also notes a Produce Exchange arbitration clause and treats the parties' failure to use that remedy as agreement to eliminate it. The court's opinion supplies trial findings and conclusions; this is not an appellate decision or a motion-stage ruling. (Id. at 117–118, 121; n.1.)

## Wording and the negotiations did not prove the narrow meaning

Dictionaries recognized both proposed meanings, so the word alone was ambiguous. Frigaliment argued that the smaller size necessarily consisted of young birds and that the same description therefore restricted the heavier birds too. Friendly found the inference unpersuasive: one general commodity description could cover different varieties in different sizes. The government-inspection and grading language also provided possible support for the seller, rather than conclusively resolving the dispute for the buyer. (Id. at 117–118.)

Negotiations took place in New York between B.N.S. secretary Ernest Bauer and Stovicek, who was visiting for the Czechoslovak government at the World Trade Fair. Stovicek showed Bauer Frigaliment's April 26 cable seeking heavier chickens at up to 33 cents per pound. Bauer checked market prices and accepted, and later cables supplied additional quantities. Frigaliment emphasized that the predominantly German cables retained the English word “chicken,” although the German general term included broilers and stewing birds. It argued that the seller's German-speaking officers should have recognized the deliberate narrower English usage. (Id. at 118.)

But Bauer testified that he asked what type was wanted and was told any kind, then specifically asked whether the cable meant the broader German term and received confirmation. Frigaliment challenged consistency with his deposition and Stovicek's authority. The full opinion, beyond the assigned abridgment, explains why both attacks failed. Friendly's review found no asserted deposition inconsistency. Even if Stovicek could not change prices or delivery dates, Frigaliment could not invoke its cable as a definition while rejecting the interpretive response of the intermediary to whom it entrusted that cable. Leaving an intermediary blank on the printed forms did not negate his role; the blank concerned identifying a commission recipient. (Id. at 118–119.)

## Conflicting usage evidence did not bind a newcomer

B.N.S. had only entered the poultry trade in 1957. Under the authorities the court applied, a party outside the relevant trade had to know a usage actually or be chargeable with knowledge because the practice was sufficiently established and widely known. There was no proof of actual knowledge of the alleged narrow usage; the seller believed the contrary. The buyer therefore had to demonstrate a longstanding, notorious, sufficiently universal and reasonable practice supporting an inference that the parties contracted with reference to it. (Id. at 119.)

The buyer produced testimony from Strasser, a buyer for Swiss cooperatives, Niesielowski, an officer of a supplier, and Dates of the Urner-Barry reporting service, along with publications distinguishing young chicken from fowl. Their evidence had force but weaknesses. Strasser used explicit broiler or fowl terminology in his own purchases. Niesielowski responded to the seller's inquiry for chickens by asking which class was wanted and ultimately supplied fowl, later requesting that its confirmation use stewing-chicken terminology. Those practices weakened a supposed universal reliance on an unqualified narrow description. (Id.)

The seller's witnesses Weininger, Fox, and Sadina supported the inclusive meaning. Government purchasing specifications included mature birds under chicken; industry statistics distinguished young and mature chickens within that general category; Agriculture Department reports specified classes rather than relying on the bare word. This conflicting material prevented the buyer's evidence from proving the binding usage it alleged. The court did not hold that trade usage is irrelevant or that a newcomer's lack of actual knowledge invariably excludes it. (Id. at 119–120.)

## Regulations, economics, and subsequent conduct

The historical Agriculture Department classification listed broilers, roasters, capons, stags, hens or stewing chickens or fowl, and old roosters as chicken classes. The buyer argued that its reference incorporated inspection and grade only, not the definition, and that the trade ignored the regulatory meaning. The seller's witnesses contradicted the latter point. Friendly gave weight to using the regulations as a contextual dictionary, especially because government grading already appeared in the buyer's initial cable. His ultimate formulation was at least an indirect contractual reference, rather than a necessary holding that every cited regulatory detail had been fully incorporated. (Id. at 120–121.)

Price also supported the seller. The agreed 33-cent price for heavier birds was below the then-prevailing 35–37 cents for broilers, while B.N.S. obtained fowl at 30 cents. The buyer's point that 33 cents was closer to a broiler price did not explain why the seller would deliberately incur a loss. The court nevertheless did not conclusively find that Frigaliment actually knew these market implications; its final discussion acknowledged that they might not have been sufficiently communicated to the buyer. (Id.)

Friendly declined to credit the seller's further interpretation argument based on Frigaliment allowing the second shipment after a cable differentiated chickens from broilers. The buyer continued demanding contractual performance and reserving responsibility for costs. That conduct might matter to the disputed New York-versus-European damages measure if liability were established, but the court did not give it weight in construing the bargain. Nor did later European resale under poultry terminology show an invented objection, given the buyer's immediate and consistent protests. The second original footnote confirms that the cables were German while the key English poultry terms were retained. (Id. at 120–121; n.2.)

## Burden and exact judgment

The seller's belief in the broad meaning aligned with a dictionary definition, the regulatory classification, some trade usage, market economics, and the buyer's spokesman's statement. A subjective belief alone would have been insufficient. The court did not need to decide whether the buyer actually meant young birds: Frigaliment bore the burden to prove that the contract used the narrower meaning, and the total record did not carry that burden. This was a failure to establish the asserted warranty breach, rather than a universal judicial definition of chicken or a finding of bad faith by either side. (Id. at 121.)

On December 27, 1960, Friendly directed entry of judgment dismissing the complaint with costs. There was no panel vote, separate opinion, or remand.
