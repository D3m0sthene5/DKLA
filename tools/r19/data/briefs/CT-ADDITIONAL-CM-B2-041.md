# In re Baby Boy L.
144 A.D.2d 674, 534 N.Y.S.2d 706 (1988) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and controversy

The New York Supreme Court, Appellate Division, Second Department decided this adoption appeal on November 28, 1988, by an unsigned memorandum. Presiding Justice Mollen and Justices Thompson, Brown, and Eiber concurred; there are no separate writings. The prospective adoptive parents appealed a July 1, 1988 order of the Richmond County Family Court, Judge Meyer, which, after a hearing, set aside the natural mother’s extrajudicial consent and denied their adoption petition. The appellate court reviewed the order on both the law and the facts, including whether the findings were against the weight of the credible evidence. The proceeding did not concern damages for breach of an ordinary commercial contract. (674–675, 677.)

## Facts and positions

Nicole L., a seventeen-year-old high-school student, gave birth outside marriage on January 12, 1988. Two days later she executed forms consenting to surrender the child for adoption. About ten days after execution, she sought to revoke consent on the ground of duress. The prospective adoptive parents had cared for the infant from birth. Following a hearing, the Family Court found that pressures on Nicole interfered with her ability to give an independent, knowing, and voluntary consent. It also concluded that she did not fully understand the forms and had received patently inadequate legal services. The adoptive parents challenged those conclusions and sought a remittal for a hearing on the child’s best interests under Domestic Relations Law § 115-b. (674–675, 676–677.)

Nicole’s mother had been a primary influence on her decision. She told Nicole that she could not continue residing in her parents’ home if she kept the baby. The appellate court acknowledged Nicole’s misgivings and the emotional difficulty of her choice. But the record also showed other options. Her father supported her and exerted no pressure. Nicole personally contacted the putative father’s mother, who offered to take in and support the child and provide a home for Nicole. She rejected that option because she wanted to remain with her parents. The court therefore treated her choice as deference to her mother’s judgment rather than proof that she had lost the ability to exercise free will. (676.)

## Duress analysis

A parent’s consent to adoption has legal consequences and cannot invariably be withdrawn at will. Relying on Matter of Sarah K. and Matter of Podmore, the court distinguished pressure and persuasion from the kind of force that would support a legal finding of duress. It cited decisions treating family pressure, parental threats, medical or parental advice, and emotional distress as insufficient grounds in their respective circumstances. The opinion did not deny that such factors existed or declare every conceivable threat harmless. It assessed this record and concluded that the mother’s pressure did not make Nicole’s execution of the consent coercive. (675–676.)

The available alternatives mattered. Nicole knew that keeping the baby was an option, had support from her father, and had been offered a different home where she could keep the child. Her desire to stay with her parents made the alternatives painful, but the court did not equate an emotionally difficult decision with legally involuntary assent. Quoting earlier authority, it explained that the law did not require adoption consents to be executed free of the tensions inherent in contemplating surrender of one’s child. Balancing the interests of the persons concerned and society opposed that standard. The conclusion was case-specific: on this record, later regret did not establish duress. (676.)

## Understanding of the forms and legal assistance

The appellate court also rejected the Family Court’s finding that Nicole failed to understand what she signed. It relied on Sarah K.’s statement that neither emotional distress nor mistake supplied a basis for vitiating consent under § 115-b(4). Independently, it found her assertion that she thought she was authorizing temporary foster care contrary to the weight of the credible evidence. The adoption process had been explained to her; she understood the difference between temporary foster care and adoption, read the first page, and knew it referred to adoption. None of the documents mentioned foster care. The court further considered a claimed coerced adoption inconsistent with her assertion that she willingly signed because she believed the placement was temporary. These are appellate assessments of the record, not a universal conclusion that misunderstanding of every consent document is immaterial. (676–677.)

Finally, the court rejected the conclusion that the legal services were patently inadequate and deprived Nicole of a real ability to exercise independent will. The memorandum does not provide a separate detailed factual account of the attorney’s work; the brief cannot supply one. (677.)

## Exact holding and disposition

The Appellate Division reversed on the law and the facts, without costs or disbursements, denied the application to revoke consent, and remitted the proceeding to the Richmond County Family Court for a best-interest hearing on the adoption petition before a different judge. It did not itself grant the adoption or adjudicate the child’s ultimate best interests. The explicit direction to a different judge appears in the decretal paragraph even though the final narrative paragraph describes remittal more generally. (674, 677.)
