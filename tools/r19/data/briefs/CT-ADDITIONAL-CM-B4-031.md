# Evergreen Amusement Corporation v. Milstead
206 Md. 610, 112 A.2d 901 (1955) | Contracts

## Court, procedural posture, and disposition
Judge Hammond delivered the Maryland Court of Appeals’ March 28, 1955 opinion affirming, with costs, a judgment for contractor Harold D. Milstead against the operator of a drive-in theater. The trial court had sat without a jury. It allowed the unpaid contract balance and extras, deducting the cost of unfinished work and delay damages measured by rental value and actual expenses. It rejected Evergreen’s counterclaim for expected profits during the delayed opening. (613–614, 622.)

The panel included Chief Judge Brune and Judges Delaplaine, Collins, Henderson, and Hammond. The report expressly records that Brune dissented in part, but supplies no separate opinion or identification of the particular issue on which he disagreed. The affirmance therefore must not be described as unanimous, nor should a rationale be invented for the noted dissent. (613, 622.)

## Site work and the unforeseen need for outside fill
The written contract required Milstead to clear timber, stumps, and waste; grade the theater site according to plans; construct a north-side drainage ditch; and install two hundred feet of pipe. A clause specified that, at the stated contract price, additional fill would not be substituted for unsuitable material on the site. Both parties initially assumed that moving existing earth would balance the necessary cuts and fills. Their preliminary calculations relied on a contour map whose accuracy was questioned: debris, stumps, and trunks could have hindered its preparation, and the engineer described complete cross-sections as the accurate method. (614–615.)

The assumption failed. Even after approximately eight thousand cubic yards were brought in, the grading was not complete as planned. The parties agreed to leave the north side slightly lower to avoid further fill there. The trial court found that they also agreed to pay Milstead sixty-eight cents per yard for the outside dirt required elsewhere. Evergreen denied making this agreement, but its president’s contemporaneous log tracked pan-loads of imported material, and he approved the contractor’s bill in writing. Those records supported the finding of an additional undertaking. (615.)

Evergreen argued that the promise lacked consideration because the original contract already required Milstead to supply necessary materials. The Court upheld it on two alternative grounds. First, the discovery of expensive outside-fill requirements revealed an unforeseen difficulty and mutual mistake: the imported dirt cost more than the original contract price, and both parties had assumed it would be unnecessary. The Maryland exception to the preexisting-duty rule, as explained in the precedents discussed by the Court, could apply even though precautions might have averted the surprise. (615–616.)

Second, the limitation concerning additional fill either meant what the contractor said it meant or was ambiguous. If it excluded the added expense from the original price, the later promise required payment for additional work. If ambiguous, the experienced owner representatives’ agreement, detailed records, and written bill approval supplied persuasive evidence of the parties’ construction. Conduct could also establish a subsequent modification. Evergreen could not erase that evidence simply because a later shortage of funds made the charge unwelcome. (616–617.)

## Time of performance and harmless exclusion
Evergreen also sought to prove a contemporaneous oral agreement requiring completion in thirty days. The Court found no harmful exclusion even assuming the offered testimony should have been admitted. Both sides’ witnesses treated thirty working days as reasonable, and the trial court used that same assumption. Without an express deadline, the law implied a reasonable time; on the evidence offered, this did not differ materially from the asserted agreement. The opinion did not rest on an unqualified conclusion that the parol evidence rule necessarily barred the testimony. (617.)

In addition, the opening depended on extra work, including the road, for which Evergreen claimed no separately agreed completion time. A reasonable time applied to that work as well. These circumstances prevented the asserted oral deadline from altering the judgment through the exclusion claim advanced on appeal. (617.)

## Why the offered lost-profits proof was rejected
Evergreen maintained that delay from June 1 to mid-August cost $12,500 in profits. It offered a witness who had built and operated most drive-in theaters in the area, sold theater equipment, knew local theater profitability, and knew of a preselection market survey identifying neighborhood demand. He would compare the corresponding months in the theater’s second year with the missed months in its first year and testify that profits would have been the same. The proffer also asserted approximately equivalent weather, population, and competition across those years. These were proposed evidentiary assurances, rather than findings that the missing first-year profits equaled the later receipts. (617–618.)

The Court upheld exclusion. An established business’s operating history could support estimation of lost profits with reasonable certainty, whereas a business not yet operating ordinarily presented speculation. It relied on the Restatement’s requirement of a sufficient basis for estimating profits and its alternative rental-value measure. But the Court expressly declined to announce a flat prohibition on all new-business lost profits. Its conclusion was that recovery had not been permitted in comparable circumstances and the offered comparison did not justify it here. (618–619.)

The Maryland precedents discussed delays in completing a mill, installing an elevator, and other undertakings whose expected returns depended on uncertain operating and market conditions. Evergreen’s federal antitrust authorities generally involved existing enterprises or theaters and all concerned conspiracies restraining trade. The Court viewed their more permissive proof standards as influenced by civil antitrust enforcement; that was this Court’s explanation for distinguishing them, rather than a holding that every federal damages case uses a different certainty rule. (619–620.)

Theater attendance posed additional uncertainty. The Court discussed decisions rejecting estimates tied to an actor’s anticipated appearance or promised film deliveries, notwithstanding experience offered to support them. A newly opened theater would not necessarily draw the patronage it might attract after becoming known through a period of operation. Second-year figures therefore did not establish that the unoperated first season would have yielded the same profits. The Court approved delay compensation based on fair rental value and actual monetary loss instead. (620–621.)

## Substantial performance and unfinished drainage
Evergreen finally argued that any incomplete performance barred the contractor’s recovery. The Court distinguished a deliberate and willful breach from good-faith substantial performance. Strict forfeiture could unfairly benefit an owner retaining labor and materials; where performance was substantial, recovery of the price less allowances for deviations could be appropriate. Whether the departure was substantial and whether it was willful or justified ordinarily belonged to the factfinder. (621.)

The unfinished ditch and pipe represented approximately $1,000 in a job costing more than $13,000. The theater opened without them, and the president said the omission was not noticed until after opening. Milstead testified that he had been told there was no hurry, had gone away intending to return and complete the work, and later feared he would not be paid. He remained willing to finish upon assurance of payment. The Court found no clear error in treating the work as subordinate, the omission as not willful, and the owner as adequately protected by an easily calculated deduction. The entire trial judgment was affirmed; the Court did not award the anticipated $12,500 or order a new hearing on that proffer. (622.)
