# Pomerantz v. Clark

**101 F. Supp. 341 (D. Mass. 1951). November 30, 1951.** District Judge Wyzanski wrote the opinion. The report addresses Pomerantz and eleven companion cases through a common dismissal analysis; no separate writing is reported. Assigned account: CivPro PDF 280, printed 523, before the Nolan comparison.

## The derivative claims and the unmade member demand

Minority policyholders brought twelve companion suits against directors of two insurance companies and persons allegedly involved in improper corporate loans. Wyzanski selected Sidney Winslow’s dismissal motion in Pomerantz as typical. Pomerantz, a New York citizen holding a policy in Massachusetts-organized John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance, sued directors and others for their roles in a $3.5 million loan to Texmass Petroleum and a loan of at least $332,938 to Petroleum Reserve. He alleged insolvency, inadequate security, insufficient investigation, violations of Massachusetts insurance restrictions, below-market interest, and assistance to directors’ relatives and associates. John Hancock was a defendant, but the requested recovery would go to the company. These allegations did not establish that the loans were unlawful. (342–343.)

The complaint asserted that a board demand would be futile because the accused directors constituted an overwhelming majority. It also excused failure to approach the policyholders collectively: management controlled litigation; any suit undertaken at their request would remain in hostile hands; and communicating with millions of scattered policyholders would require a prohibitively expensive proxy contest. The decisive question was whether a minority policyholder could prosecute the company’s claim without first giving an impartial internal member body an opportunity to act. The court did not need to adjudicate the challenged loans or determine damages. (343–344.)

## Governing state law in a federal diversity court

Diversity jurisdiction required a cause of action cognizable under Massachusetts substantive law. The federal derivative-action pleading requirements then located in Rule 23 were an additional procedural prerequisite, not a substitute for the state’s limitations on the underlying right to sue. Wyzanski distinguished substantive entitlement from compliance with a federal rule. The brief describes the rule’s placement and the court’s analysis in 1951, without implying that present Rule 23 has the same structure. (343–344.)

Massachusetts ordinarily required a member first to seek action from nondisqualified directors, and then from nondisqualified members, before bringing a derivative suit. Exceptions addressed immediate action or the absence of an impartial internal corporate forum. Internal decisionmakers were ordinarily better situated to determine not just whether a claim was valid but whether publicly suing served the enterprise’s welfare. The member had voluntarily joined an organization governed through those bodies. Courts would not displace their judgment absent bias, refusal to consider evidence, unreasonable conduct, or other disqualification. (343–344.)

The defendants argued that accused directors could withdraw and leave an impartial quorum. Wyzanski found the Massachusetts rule on that issue less certain and **assumed without deciding** that no board demand was required. The unmade member demand independently defeated the complaint; the opinion did not announce that accusing a majority always excuses board demand. (344.)

## Why the pleaded excuses failed

The first two member-demand excuses incorrectly assumed that the members themselves could not bring a derivative suit without directors’ approval. Massachusetts allowed the member body to do so. If a majority pursued the claim, the objections to a self-appointed minority’s suit would not apply. The expense and dispersion of a large membership also were not, standing alone, recognized grounds for bypassing it. Precedent already involved a large railroad with scattered shareholders. (344.)

The court nevertheless considered stronger arguments that had arisen at the hearing and could potentially be added by amendment. Mutual-insurance policyholders differed practically from stockholders: few knew or exercised voting rights; meeting participation was historically very low; many attendees were employees, agents, or management friends; and advance proxy filing could alert management to organize a larger attendance. Those possible averments might cast doubt on the practical representativeness of member meetings. Wyzanski did not pretend that the practical differences were irrelevant simply because the complaint had pleaded them imperfectly. (344–345.)

But policyholders had voting and derivative rights parallel to shareholders. Formal differences in the insurance business and corporate structure did not create an exception by themselves. Regulatory oversight, if relevant, might reduce rather than expand the need for an individual suit, a question the court did not decide. The real issue was whether the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court would recognize the proposed exception for practical member-meeting deficiencies. (345–346.)

## Predicting state law rather than choosing a preferred philosophy

The assigned quotation describes a federal diversity judge’s task as anticipating the state judges’ views rather than using an independent commission to develop what seems equitable or sound public policy. In the complete opinion, that statement addresses this concrete choice: whether practical difficulties in a large mutual insurer justified abandoning the demand requirement. Wyzanski predicted that Massachusetts would not create the exception on the allegations and proposed amendments presented. He relied on recent and older derivative-suit precedent and the state judiciary’s approach to associational self-government and internal corporate remedies. (345–346.)

He acknowledged a competing philosophy that minority suits can expose wrongdoing, overcome majority inertia, and obtain information unavailable before compulsory discovery. Those considerations might favor a different rule elsewhere, but he concluded that they did not represent Massachusetts law. The court identified missing circumstances that could support a stronger case: alleged personal profits by directors, officers, or members; corrupted or patently irrational member proceedings; or efforts to obtain representative support followed by unreasonable rejection. These were examples supporting his prediction, not findings that the challenged loans were proper or a new exhaustive statutory test. (346–347.)

## Disposition and source qualifications

The court **dismissed the complaint on the merits without leave to amend**. Its opening explains that the same reasoning requires granting dismissal motions in the twelve companion cases. The reported analysis uses Pomerantz as the typical case; it does not provide separate factual narratives or individual final orders for all eleven others. (342, 347.)

The original report’s caption lists twelve docket numbers, including 50-615 and 50-624, while the judicial body identifies the typical Pomerantz motion as Civil Action 50-625. Both spellings are visible in the original scans, so this discrepancy is not merely an OCR error. No unsupported choice between them is made. Likewise, one shared report is not treated as proof of a precise count of separately preserved judgments. The assigned excerpt emphasizes Erie prediction; the complete opinion explains how that approach actually determines the member-demand requirement and the denial of amendment. Independent-review status appears in the accompanying register.
