# Flyers Rights Education Fund, Inc. v. Federal Aviation Administration
864 F.3d 738 (D.C. Cir. 2017) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Petition and the FAA's response

Paul Hudson and Flyers Rights Education Fund petitioned the FAA on August 26, 2015, to regulate minimum commercial-aircraft seat dimensions and spacing. Their materials described declining seat pitch—the distance between corresponding points on successive seats—from an average thirty-five to thirty-one inches, sometimes twenty-eight, and width declining from approximately 18.5 to seventeen inches. They also described increasing passenger height and girth. They claimed cramped seating could impede evacuation, increase deep-vein-thrombosis risks, and cause soreness and other discomfort. They sought size standards, a moratorium on further reductions pending a final rule, and an advisory committee or task force. These were the petition's factual submissions and requested remedies, not judicially prescribed dimensional requirements. (741–743 & n. 1.)

The FAA denied the petition February 1, 2016. Its governing regulations directed attention to immediate safety or security concerns, other priorities, and available resources. It asserted that existing evacuation demonstrations adequately addressed safety, including tests at twenty-eight- and twenty-nine-inch pitch, and that thinner modern seats could provide more space despite shorter pitch. It characterized the medical concerns as unrelated to pitch and thrombosis as rare and no more prevalent in economy than business class. The denial cited no supporting studies and did not challenge the petition's description of smaller seats and larger passengers. When petitioners sought citations, the agency supplied evacuation reports and medical links. The cited evacuation studies did not address how smaller seat dimensions combined with larger passengers affected reaching the exits. (742–743.)

## Review framework and division of the issues

Judge Millett wrote on July 28, 2017, with Judge Pillard joining and Judge Rogers concurring in part and in the judgment. The FAA possessed broad statutory authority to prescribe aircraft and operational safety standards; ensuring rapid evacuation fell centrally within that authority. Public rulemaking petitions were authorized, but a petition did not automatically require the agency to issue a regulation. Judicial review of refusals remained extremely limited and deferential because agencies managed finite resources. The question was whether the refusal reflected reasoned decisionmaking, an adequate explanation, and facts supported by the record. (738, 740–744.)

The court separated emergency-egress concerns from challenges about health and comfort. It agreed that the evacuation response lacked plausible evidentiary support, while rejecting the requested judicial intervention on routine health and physical comfort. Thus the opinion did not hold that every argument advanced by the passenger group justified rulemaking. (744–749.)

## Why the evacuation rationale failed

The petition identified a plausible safety issue: increasingly cramped rows could hinder reaching aisles, create panic, and impede injured passengers. Passenger comments described difficulty leaving seats. FAA regulations required aircraft above forty-four-passenger capacity to be capable of evacuation within ninety seconds. Given that statutory and regulatory setting, the agency needed reasonably to address the identified risk. (744.)

Its evidence did not do so. Studies failing to measure or control for seat pitch, width, and growing passenger size could not establish that those dimensions were irrelevant. Omission of a variable from a test did not prove that variable harmless. Basic physical constraints meant that at some point passengers could become unable to leave their seats; the issue was where that point lay. One study found passenger waist size particularly influential in exit speed, yet the agency did not explain why body size mattered in relatively wide exit rows but not in cramped seating rows. Older studies also could not silently resolve claims based on changing seating and passenger dimensions. (744–745 & n. 2.)

Maximum cabin occupancy was not a sufficient proxy for the problem. Different cabin classes allowed larger first- or business-class seats to coexist with increasingly compressed economy rows; a total passenger limit therefore did not necessarily control individual seating geometry. The claimed successful tests at current dimensions posed an additional defect: none was in the record. (745–746.)

The FAA called those tests proprietary, but offered no reasoned explanation for withholding them entirely and declined sealed or redacted submission. Agencies could protect confidential business information in appropriate circumstances, yet courts could not assess evidence hidden from judicial view. Counsel also could not say whether those tests accounted for larger passengers. A refusal-to-rulemake record could be comparatively sparse, but not empty of the evidence the agency materially relied on and asked the court to trust. Deference could not supply missing support. (746–747 & nn. 3–4.)

The appropriate remedy was remand for an adequately supported response. If the FAA again denied the petition, it had to provide record support addressing the evacuation concerns. The court expressly declined to compel rulemaking: that exceptional remedy was unwarranted while the agency claimed to possess information potentially justifying denial. It likewise did not impose the requested seat dimensions or moratorium itself. (747 & n. 5.)

## Health, comfort, and the concurrence

Petitioners invoked § 40101(a)'s public-service factors, but those provisions applied to the Transportation Secretary, not the FAA. The majority nevertheless recognized that the FAA's safety authority included at least some passenger-health issues. Precedent permitted regulation of emergency medical equipment even where the health condition was not caused by flight. The agency had acknowledged authority over safety and health; its decision was that these particular concerns did not warrant rulemaking then, not that all passenger health lay outside its power. (747–749.)

Evidence indicated thrombosis was rare and not more likely in economy than business class, supporting refusal to regulate seat dimensions on that basis. Soreness, stiffness, and similar commonplace, temporary, non-life-threatening discomfort did not demonstrate error in declining immediate rulemaking. The majority also noted that petitioners appeared to have abandoned a purely comfort-based statutory argument; exclusively comfort concerns in any event lay outside the cited safety mandate. (749 & n. 7.)

Rogers agreed with the evacuation remand and rejection of reliance on § 40101(a), but would not reach the further theory that health was inherently part of § 44701(a)'s safety mandate. She understood that theory as first raised in the reply brief, denying the FAA a fair opportunity to respond. Different statutory bases for health obligations were distinct arguments, rather than refinements of one preserved theory. The majority's note 6 disagreed: it understood the core health-with-safety argument as preserved, with the supporting legal authorities evolving, and emphasized the court's responsibility to apply pertinent precedent. Rogers therefore concurred in the result without joining that additional statutory discussion. (748 n. 6; Rogers concurrence, 749–751.)

## Judgment and assigned scope

The court **granted the petition for review in part and remanded for a properly reasoned disposition of the emergency-egress concerns, otherwise denying review**. There was no order to commence rulemaking and no adjudication of a later FAA response. No dissent or appendix accompanied the opinion. (749.)
