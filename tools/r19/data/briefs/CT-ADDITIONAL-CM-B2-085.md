# Steuart v. McChesney
498 Pa. 45, 444 A.2d 659 (1982) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and holding
The Pennsylvania Supreme Court decided this land-sale dispute on April 30, 1982. Justice Flaherty wrote for the majority; Justice Roberts dissented, joined by Justice Larsen. The seven-member court affirmed the Superior Court's order enforcing a right of first refusal at the market value reflected by the county assessment rolls, rather than the third-party offer or an independently determined market price. The clause was unambiguous in context, and the large difference between the contractual price and current offers did not authorize rewriting the bargain or denying specific performance on the record presented. (45, 47–48, 52–56.)

## Transaction and the competing price formulas
On June 8, 1968, Lepha and James Steuart granted William and Joyce McChesney a right of first refusal over improved farmland. During the Steuarts' lifetimes, obtaining a bona fide purchaser for value would permit the McChesneys to purchase at a value equivalent to the property's market value according to Warren County's assessment rolls. The stipulated valuation date was the date when the Steuarts gave written notice of the bona fide purchaser. James later died, and Lepha became the appellant. (47.)

A broker appraised the property at $50,000 on July 6, 1977. Lepha received bona fide offers of $35,000 on October 10 and $30,000 on October 13. After notice, the McChesneys tendered $7,820. That amount was twice the tax-roll assessment: Warren County's practice was to assess at fifty percent of market value. Lepha refused the tender and brought an equity action seeking cancellation of the right or a construction requiring the third-party offer's price or independent fair market value. The McChesneys sought specific performance for $7,820. (47–48.)

After hearing testimony, the Court of Common Pleas treated twice the assessed value as a protective minimum, not an exclusive pricing formula. It allowed exercise for $35,000, the first bona fide offer. The Superior Court reversed because the writing made assessed market value alone controlling. The Supreme Court affirmed that appellate resolution. Footnote 2 distinguished Bobali, on which Lepha relied: that earlier preemptive right matched a bona fide offer because its particular agreement said so. It did not supply a general rule overriding this agreement's different price language. (48 & n. 2.)

## Rule: clear text controls, with context relevant to clarity
The majority reaffirmed that the intent embodied in a clear written agreement is found in its expressed terms, rather than an unexpressed intention attributed to the parties afterward. It acknowledged criticism of a rigid plain-meaning approach: words can acquire meanings from trade practices, local usage, and surrounding circumstances, and determining whether a writing is clear may require awareness of its context. The opinion thus did not pretend that a judge could identify meaning entirely without knowledge of the transaction. It nevertheless retained the rule that genuinely clear language does not need extrinsic evidence to change its meaning. (48–50.)

The majority gave institutional and practical reasons. A court interprets the contract the parties made, rather than improving it according to its own view of fairness. Carefully chosen writing ordinarily represents the parties' final expressed intentions. Reliance on that expression promotes contractual security and limits later efforts to recast a disappointing bargain through recollections of what supposedly should have been intended. Those policies supported preserving the plain-meaning rule despite the acknowledged importance of context. (50–52.)

Patent ambiguity appears in defective or uncertain language on the instrument's face. Latent ambiguity arises when external facts make apparently clear language uncertain, such as a reference fitting two people or things equally. Neither type can be manufactured by distorting a term or offering an unreasonable alternative construction. The agreement here was not reasonably susceptible to competing meanings on the majority's analysis. (53.)

## Application: the bona fide offer triggered the right but did not set the price
The writing separated the event permitting exercise from the formula determining price. Requiring a bona fide purchaser protected against a sham offer improperly triggering the McChesneys' right. It did not connect the purchase price to the amount offered. The specified price instead depended on market value according to the assessment rolls. The trial court's minimum-price reading replaced equivalence with a not-less-than condition that the parties had not written. Footnote 3 explained the court's treatment of equivalent as equal in the relevant valuation sense. A later difference between the roll-based price and genuine market offers could not justify substituting a new formula through interpretation. (52–54 & n. 3.)

The county's fifty-percent assessment practice explained why $7,820, twice the recorded assessment, represented the roll-based market value; it did not convert the formula into the current third-party offer or a broker's independent appraisal. The majority therefore accepted the Superior Court's result at $7,820, rather than $35,000 or $50,000. It found no ambiguity warranting extrinsic clarification of the expressed price formula. That conclusion should not be converted into a claim that the court forbade all contextual knowledge, since its opinion expressly acknowledged context in identifying clarity and described the assessment practice in its own account. (48–54.)

## Equity and the representation finding
Lepha separately argued that specific performance at the low roll-based value would be inequitable. Under the court's cited precedents, inadequacy of consideration alone does not justify refusing specific performance of a real-estate conveyance; fraud or transaction unfairness must make compelled performance inequitable. Lepha alleged that an attorney employed solely by the McChesneys drafted the agreement and induced her to sign without representation. The majority rejected that allegation because the trial court had expressly found, with ample testimony supporting the finding, that the same attorney represented all parties during preliminary negotiation and drafting. The opinion did not endorse unrepresented bargaining as harmless; it relied on a finding that the claimed lack of representation had not occurred. The Superior Court's order was affirmed. (54–55.)

## Roberts's dissent: the valuation-date clause required a current assessment
Roberts, joined by Larsen, agreed that the writing linked purchase price to market value according to the assessment rolls but disputed the majority's assumption that this necessarily meant the stale figure of $7,820. The draftsman had testified that the parties intentionally omitted a fixed future price to reflect changes in assessed value when the purchase right was exercised. The specified valuation date reinforced the expectation of a current figure. Notice had been delivered on or about October 25, 1977, but the property apparently had not been reassessed since 1972, when the assessment increased by only $405. The recorded assessment was $3,910. (55.)

The dissent invoked the statutory assessor's duty to assess according to actual value and precedents identifying actual value with market value. It regarded specific performance at an outdated valuation as an unfair windfall resulting from the county's apparent failure to maintain accurate assessments. Roberts described bona fide offers of $30,000, $35,000, and $50,000, whereas the majority's factual chronology described $50,000 as the broker's appraisal and identified $35,000 and $30,000 as the received offers. The two accounts should not be silently flattened into a single chronology. (47–48, 55–56.)

The dissent would have remanded to determine the proper assessment as of October 25, 1977, then ordered specific performance at the market value based on that determination. It did not simply adopt the trial court's $35,000 protective-minimum approach. Its proposed remedy preserved the assessment-linked formula but insisted on the current valuation the dissent believed the parties and assessment law required. That position did not command the majority. (56.)
