# Ellis Canning Co. v. International Harvester Co.

**174 Kan. 357, 255 P.2d 658 (1953) · Civil Procedure · CP-076**

After an insurer fully paid a property loss, the insured could not maintain a negligence action in its own name for the insurer's benefit under the Kansas real-party-in-interest statute. The Kansas Supreme Court affirmed the refusal to strike or sustain a demurrer to the defendant's real-party-in-interest defense. In resolving conflicting Kansas precedents, the court expressly overruled one earlier decision and disapproved part of another. The decision determines who could prosecute this fully subrogated claim under the state statute, rather than whether the defendant caused the fire.

Ellis Canning alleged that International Harvester negligently started a fire while servicing its tractor, causing $479.79 in damage. Potomac Insurance insured the loss under a policy containing a subrogation clause and paid the loss in full. Ellis then sued International Harvester in Ellis's own name, expressly for the use and benefit of Potomac. The answer denied each alleged negligent act but admitted the allegations about insurance, the amount of loss, and full payment. Its third paragraph asserted that Potomac was consequently the real party in interest and Ellis lacked the right to maintain the action. Thus, negligence remained disputed, while the procedural issue rested on agreed pleadings about payment and subrogation. (174 Kan. at 357–58.)

Ellis moved to strike the third answer paragraph and demurred to it, arguing that it stated neither a sufficient defense nor facts establishing that Ellis was an improper plaintiff. The trial court denied both challenges. Ellis appealed those rulings. Both parties agreed that the sole issue was whether an insured already reimbursed for its entire loss could nonetheless sue in its own name for the insurer's benefit. This was not an appeal following a factual determination of negligence or an award of damages. (Id. at 358.)

Kansas General Statutes 1949, §60-401 required prosecution in the name of the real party in interest, subject to exceptions the court said were inapplicable here. Full payment mattered because Ellis no longer had a direct interest in recovering compensation for the loss. The subrogated reimbursement claim belonged wholly to Potomac. Merely declaring that the lawsuit was for Potomac's benefit did not make Ellis the holder of that claim or satisfy the statutory direction about the name in which the action must proceed. (Id. at 358–59.)

The difficulty was precedent, rather than factual uncertainty. Earlier Kansas cases, including Railroad Co. v. Insurance Co. and Klingberg, treated the insurer as the sole real party in interest after full reimbursement. But Hume v. McGinnis and City of New York Insurance Co. v. Tice had allowed the insured to sue for the insurer's benefit. Ellis could therefore invoke existing language supporting its chosen plaintiff designation. The court acknowledged that conflict instead of pretending the decisions all stated one consistent rule. (Id. at 359.)

The court chose the rule followed in its more recent Campbell, Hill, and Mehl decisions: full reimbursement leaves the insurer with the entire claim against the alleged wrongdoer, so the insurer may and must bring the action if it is to be maintained. The opinion declined a detailed effort to distinguish or harmonize the inconsistent precedents. It expressly overruled Hume and disapproved whatever in Tice could be understood to permit an insured owner, whose loss had been fully paid and satisfied, to maintain the action for the insurer's benefit. This deliberate choice resolves the doctrinal conflict on which Ellis relied. (Id. at 359–60.)

The judgment was affirmed: the answer's challenged allegations supported the real-party-in-interest defense, and the trial court correctly refused both the motion to strike and demurrer. Justice Parker delivered the court's opinion. The complete report contains no judicial footnotes or separate writing, and gives no numerical vote count. Its brief discussion supplies no ruling on an insurer that pays only part of a loss, no determination of International Harvester's negligence, and no application of later federal substitution provisions. The assigned casebook's subsequent questions about Federal Rule 17 are instructional context rather than holdings of this 1953 Kansas decision. The distinction to retain is between a person who originally suffered an injury and the person who owns the claim after complete contractual subrogation; under the statute applied here, the latter had to prosecute it. (Id. at 360.)
