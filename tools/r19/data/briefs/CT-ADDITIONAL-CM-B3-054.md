# Manuel Wackenheim v. France
Communication No. 854/1999, U.N. Doc. CCPR/C/75/D/854/1999 (2002) | Contracts

## Decision, issue, and result

On July 15, 2002, the United Nations Human Rights Committee adopted views finding no Covenant violation in France's prohibition of the “dwarf tossing” activity in which Manuel Wackenheim had worked. The Committee decided the article 26 discrimination claim on its merits after rejecting other claims as inadmissible. It regarded the relevant differentiation as objectively and reasonably justified by public order and human dignity. Fourteen named members participated; the reproduced views contain no separate opinion or numerical vote. This was an Optional Protocol treaty-body determination, distinct from the Conseil d'État's 1995 national rulings and not an appellate order affirming those judgments. (¶¶ 6.3–7.6 & participation note.)

## Activity and national proceedings

Wackenheim, a French citizen born in 1967 who had dwarfism, began participating in events organized by Fun-Productions in July 1991. Wearing protective equipment, he allowed nightclub customers to throw him short distances onto an air bed. These facts are introduced as submitted by him. On October 25, 1991, the mayor of Morsang-sur-Orge banned a scheduled event. Wackenheim applied for annulment on October 30. A November 27 Interior Ministry circular subsequently instructed prefects to have mayors monitor such events and contemplated bans, including on European Convention article 3 grounds. (¶¶ 1, 2.1–2.3.)

The Versailles administrative court annulled the mayor's order on February 25, 1992. It found no demonstrated threat to local order, peace or health; public disapproval alone did not establish a disturbance. Even assuming an affront to human dignity, it considered particular local circumstances necessary to justify the ban. The commune appealed on April 24, 1992. On October 27, 1995, the Conseil d'État reversed: human dignity formed part of public order, an affront to it could justify municipal police action without special local circumstances, and freedom of employment or trade did not preclude the prohibition. (¶¶ 2.3–2.5.)

A separate Aix-en-Provence prohibition followed a similar course. Wackenheim applied for annulment in March 1992; the Marseille administrative court annulled it in October, but the Conseil d'État reversed on October 27, 1995 on the same grounds. Fun-Productions then ceased organizing the events. Wackenheim wished to continue but said he remained unemployed because no organizer was available. The views also record an earlier European Commission complaint, declared inadmissible on October 16, 1996 for nonexhaustion as to some claims and incompatibility with the Convention as to others. (¶ 2.6 & n. 3.)

## Wackenheim's complaint and France's response

His initial communication was submitted on November 13, 1996. Wackenheim maintained that losing his occupation diminished his dignity, rather than protecting it. He asserted that no work was available for people with dwarfism in France and claimed violations of freedom, employment, private life, an adequate standard of living and nondiscrimination. He invoked Covenant articles 2(1), 5(2), 9(1), 16, 17(1) and 26. Those employment-market assertions were his position, not independently established findings about all French employment opportunities. (Communication heading; ¶ 3 & nn. 1–2.)

France argued that he had suffered neither deprivation of liberty nor loss of legal personality, and that his private-life claim had not been presented to French courts. It characterized paid public tossing as a professional activity rather than protected private life; alternatively, protection of dignity justified any restriction. It also argued that employment and trade freedoms were outside the Covenant, and that article 26 nondiscrimination was confined to rights the Covenant protected. On an alternative merits theory, the distinction between people with and without dwarfism was objective because the activity depended on their physical characteristics, and dignity justified the restriction. France further rejected a freestanding article 5(2) claim and described public morals and respect for persons as legitimate aspects of public order. These were the state's submissions, not all conclusions adopted by the Committee. (¶¶ 4.1–4.6.)

Counsel replied that adding public morals and dignity to the familiar categories of public order, safety and health gave mayors potentially abusive authority to censor harmless activities. Unlike genuinely violent behavior, this consensual activity had not threatened public tranquillity. Counsel again stressed that employment itself was an element of dignity. (¶¶ 5.1–5.3.)

## Admissibility

France had entered a reservation to Optional Protocol article 5(2)(a), but had not invoked it; the Committee therefore found it no obstacle to consideration. The article 9 and 16 claims lacked information grounding a violation and were inadmissible under Optional Protocol article 2. The article 17 private-life claim was inadmissible under article 5(2)(b) because Wackenheim had not complained of such a violation to domestic courts. (¶¶ 6.1–6.4.)

Covenant article 5 concerned general state undertakings and could not supply a self-standing individual communication. That claim was inadmissible under Optional Protocol article 3, although article 5 could inform interpretation of other Covenant provisions. Article 26, however, supplied an autonomous nondiscrimination right rather than merely duplicating article 2. Contrary to France's narrow position, it extended beyond rights otherwise secured by the Covenant. With no further admissibility objection, the Committee admitted the discrimination claim for merits examination. (¶¶ 6.5–6.6.)

## Objective and reasonable differentiation

The Committee asked whether the ban discriminated under article 26. A difference in treatment was not necessarily discrimination: the governing question was whether objective and reasonable grounds justified distinguishing the people covered from those not covered. (¶¶ 7.1–7.3.)

It accepted that only people with dwarfism were covered by the prohibition and reasoned that they were the people capable of being thrown in the activity concerned. That provided an objective basis for the distinction, without a discriminatory purpose. France had demonstrated that the prohibition as applied to Wackenheim was not abusive but necessary to protect public order, involving human dignity considerations compatible with the Covenant. The differentiation therefore rested on objective and reasonable grounds. This was the Committee's assessment of the particular prohibited activity, not a general declaration that all disability-based employment restrictions were permissible. (¶ 7.4.)

Other activities might also be capable of prohibition on similar grounds. But the existence of other unbanned activities did not itself make this ban discriminatory when the criteria were objective and reasonable and Wackenheim had not established discriminatory purpose. The Committee accordingly found no article 26 violation and formally concluded that the facts before it revealed no Covenant violation. It issued no compensation award or direction to reopen the activity. (¶¶ 7.5–7.6.)
