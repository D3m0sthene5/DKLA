Justices White, O’Connor, and Kennedy. Justice Brennan dissented with Justices Marshall and Blackmun;
Justice Stevens separately dissented. Assigned casebook pp. 531–537. Full opinion: Full judicial opinion . The

Parties
David A. Boyle’s father sued as the personal representative of Boyle’s heirs and estate. He was the plaintiff
below and petitioner seeking Supreme Court review. The defendant and respondent was United Technologies
Corporation, whose Sikorsky Division built the military helicopter. The United States purchased the helicopter
but was not a party to this damages action. The suit invoked diversity jurisdiction and Virginia tort law, which
made the asserted displacement by federal common law central to the defense. 487 U.S. at 502–504, 506–507.
Material Facts
On April 27, 1983, Boyle was copiloting a Marine CH-53D helicopter during a training exercise off Virginia
Beach. The helicopter crashed into the ocean. Boyle survived the impact but could not escape and drowned.
His estate presented two distinct theories: Sikorsky had negligently repaired a servo in the automatic
flight-control system, causing the crash, and had defectively designed the copilot’s emergency escape system,
preventing escape after the crash. 487 U.S. at 502–503.
The design claim concerned an outward-opening hatch that water pressure would prevent opening when
submerged, and equipment that obstructed access to its handle. The asserted defect therefore concerned
survival after impact, separate from the asserted repair defect that allegedly caused the aircraft to crash. The
jury returned a general $725,000 verdict, so the verdict itself did not separately identify which theory it
accepted. 487 U.S. at 503.
The procurement specifications and the Government’s approval mattered because the contractor argued that
Virginia law could not impose liability for producing the military design the Government had selected. The
Supreme Court’s discussion of the defense did not independently resolve all disputed facts concerning
approval, conformity, or warnings. Whether the evidence conclusively established those conditions became a
separate issue concerning the court of appeals’ method of review. 487 U.S. at 509–514.
Procedural History
The federal district court submitted both Virginia-law theories to the jury and denied Sikorsky’s motion for
judgment notwithstanding the verdict. The Fourth Circuit reversed and directed entry of judgment for Sikorsky.
It found the repair theory insufficient under Virginia law because the plaintiff had not shown that Sikorsky’s
work, rather than the Navy’s work, caused the alleged malfunction. It rejected the design claim under a federal
military-contractor defense recognized in its companion decision in Tozer. 487 U.S. at 503.
The petition challenged the existence of a judicially created federal defense, the elements the Fourth Circuit
adopted, and the failure to return the defense issue to a jury. The Supreme Court accepted a defense with the
three stated conditions but vacated and remanded because the appellate opinion did not make clear whether it

Issue
May federal common law displace Virginia design-defect liability for military equipment despite the absence
of legislation expressly granting contractors this defense? If so, what federal interest and conflict justify
displacement, what conditions delimit the defense, and could the court of appeals enter judgment without a
new jury trial after adopting a formulation different from the trial instructions? 487 U.S. at 504–514.
Rule and Holding
Federal procurement involves a uniquely federal interest, but that alone does not displace state law.
Displacement requires a significant conflict between the state duty and an identifiable federal policy or interest.
The policy protecting governmental discretionary decisions, reflected in the Federal Tort Claims Act’s
discretionary-function exception, provides the relevant limiting principle for the military-design claim. 487
U.S. at 504–512.
State design-defect liability for military equipment is displaced when the United States approved reasonably
precise specifications, the equipment conformed to those specifications, and the supplier warned the United
States of dangers known to the supplier but unknown to the Government. Government purchasing alone, or
conformity to a stock product ordered by model number, does not automatically establish the necessary federal
design judgment. 487 U.S. at 509–513.
A different appellate formulation of the defense does not itself require a new jury trial. Judgment may be
entered if, under the correct rule, the evidence could not support a reasonable jury verdict for the plaintiff. An
appellate court may not instead decide contested facts for itself. 487 U.S. at 513–514.
Judgment
The Supreme Court vacated the Fourth Circuit’s judgment and remanded for clarification and, if necessary, the
proper sufficiency inquiry. If the Fourth Circuit had meant that no reasonable jury could reject the defense on
the trial evidence under the correct rule, its judgment was to stand; otherwise it had to undertake that inquiry.
The Court neither reinstated the $725,000 award nor finally determined that the estate recovered nothing. 487
U.S. at 514.
Reasoning
The majority located the federal interest by relating procurement to two established areas: the United States’
contractual rights and obligations, and liability arising from the performance of federal officials’ duties.
Although this suit concerned a private contractor’s liability to a third person, liability for following a required
military design would affect the Government’s procurement terms. In the majority’s account, contractors
would either refuse the design or charge more to cover liability. Its reference to official immunity supported the
identification of a federal interest; the Court expressly did not decide whether official immunity itself extended
to contractors. 487 U.S. at 504–507 & n.1.
The next question was whether the particular state-law duty conflicted with that interest. A federal purchase
that specifies cooling capacity without prescribing construction details ordinarily leaves a manufacturer able to
satisfy both its contract and a state safety duty. Conversely, a state duty requiring a different escape-hatch
mechanism may contradict the Government’s specified design. Even that contradiction needs examination:
purchase of an existing helicopter by model number does not demonstrate a considered governmental interest

The Fourth Circuit’s Feres rationale was an inadequate limit. Feres barred service-related tort claims against
the United States, but using it as the contractor-defense boundary would immunize ordinary stock products
bought for service members while failing to protect deliberate military design choices that injure civilians. The
relevant distinction was governmental design discretion, not simply the injured person’s military status. 487
U.S. at 510–511.
The majority instead used the FTCA’s discretionary-function exception as evidence of a federal policy.
Military design can involve tradeoffs among technical performance, combat effectiveness, safety, and other
considerations. State tort liability imposing a different design could subject those choices to judicial
reexamination and pass the resulting financial burden to the Government through procurement prices. The
exception did not directly govern the private defendant; the Court derived a federal-common-law defense from
the policy it perceived in that exception. 487 U.S. at 511–512.
Each condition serves that rationale. Approval of reasonably precise specifications and actual conformity
connect the complained-of feature to governmental consideration, rather than unilateral contractor choice. The
warning condition prevents the defense from rewarding a supplier for withholding superior knowledge of
danger. Officials need that knowledge to exercise the discretion the rule seeks to protect. The standard requires
disclosure of dangers actually known to the contractor and not already known to the Government; it is not a
general immunity from every negligent act associated with government work. 487 U.S. at 512–513.
The Court rejected a proposed rule limiting protection to contractors who had little design participation, or who
warned of risks and alternatives and then obtained clear governmental authorization to proceed. A government
decision can embody policy judgment even when the contractor developed the design. The majority also
reasoned that penalizing contractor participation could discourage useful technical contributions. The accepted
test therefore focuses on approval, conformity, and superior knowledge rather than who first drew the design.
487 U.S. at 513.
The jury issue required a separate analysis. A party has no entitlement to a new trial when the evidence cannot
legally support a verdict under the governing rule. But the statement that Sikorsky had satisfied the defense
could mean either that the evidence was conclusive under that standard or that the appellate court had weighed
the facts itself. The Supreme Court remanded because it could not be certain which operation the Fourth Circuit
had performed. This procedural qualification is essential to the disposition even though the Court endorsed the
defense’s elements. 487 U.S. at 513–514.
Separate Opinions
Justice Brennan, joined by Marshall and Blackmun, would have reversed and reinstated the jury award. He
argued that Erie leaves federal common law in narrow areas and that a private victim’s tort claim against a
private manufacturer is collateral to the Government’s contract. In his reading, precedents concerning federal
contracts did not authorize federal rules for every relationship economically affected by those contracts.
Possible higher prices were an indirect burden, comparable to burdens insufficient to justify displacement in
other cases. 487 U.S. at 515–522 (Brennan, J., dissenting).
Brennan also challenged the official-immunity and Yearsley analogies. An independent contractor differs from
an official exercising statutory authority, and a contractor helping formulate a design differs from an agent
simply implementing governmental directions. The FTCA expressly concerned suits against the United States,
and its retained immunity did not itself confer a defense on a manufacturer. Brennan disputed both the legal

Brennan warned that imprecise scrutiny of governmental approval could protect negligent design after little

question not presented. 487 U.S. at 515–516, 529–530; id. at 512 n.5.
Justice Stevens separately emphasized institutional competence. He distinguished the limited lawmaking
inherent in adjudication from creating a new liability-limiting doctrine that balances a major governmental
program against individual compensation. He regarded Congress as the appropriate institution to make that
choice and cited decisions declining to create new liabilities without legislative aid. His disagreement focused
on who should decide the policy question, rather than proposing different elements for the majority’s defense.
487 U.S. at 531–532 (Stevens, J., dissenting).
Notes
Boyle requires two separate showings before the three defense conditions are applied: a uniquely federal
interest and a significant conflict with state law. Reading it alongside Clearfield explains that federal common
law may displace only the conflicting part of state law; identification of a federal setting does not necessarily
replace the entire state-law field. The federal defense here also arose in a diversity action. Its availability does
not itself establish that every contractor suit belongs in federal court. 487 U.S. at 503–508 & n.3.
The case’s substantive and procedural results must remain distinct. The Court recognized a qualified design
defense and rejected the demand for an automatic new jury trial, yet vacated for clarification of the appellate
court’s treatment of evidence. This brief states the 1988 decision and the materials actually read; it does not
certify every later application or amendment affecting contractors. 487 U.S. at 512–514.
