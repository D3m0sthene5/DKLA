# International Union, UAW v. Brock

**477 U.S. 274 (1986) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 25, 1986.** Justice Marshall wrote for Brennan, Blackmun, Stevens, and O’Connor. White dissented with Chief Justice Burger and Rehnquist; Powell separately dissented. The judgment was 5–4. Assigned in LRS November-b, physical PDF 128–131, printed 1185–1188. This is the trade-benefits and associational-standing case, not another Brock decision involving a different administrative program.

## The federal guideline and individual claims

The Trade Act of 1974 provided federally financed trade readjustment allowances to workers displaced by import competition. The Secretary of Labor certified affected groups, while cooperating state unemployment agencies determined individual eligibility and administered payments as federal agents. Federal funds paid both benefits and administration. Individual eligibility decisions were reviewable through the state procedures specified in §2311(d), but state agencies had to apply the relevant federal law and regulations. (277–278.)

A worker generally needed at least 26 qualifying weeks within the 52 weeks preceding separation. A 1975 Labor Department handbook excluded periods without service, including sick leave, vacation, and leave of absence, as well as military service and certain work for other employers. A 1981 statutory amendment expressly included specified leave periods but applied only to benefits payable for unemployment weeks beginning after September 30, 1981. It therefore did not remove the dispute over earlier claims. (278–279.)

The UAW and eleven members challenged the earlier interpretation. Some members had been denied benefits; others were defending awards against state-agency appeals. They sought a declaration against the Secretary’s interpretation and an injunction requiring him to notify cooperating agencies and direct reconsideration of wrongly denied claims. The federal suit did not ask the district court to calculate and award each worker’s allowance. That division of remedial responsibility was central to the case. (279–280.)

## Lower courts and Supreme Court holding

The district court rejected the Secretary’s argument that state review provisions barred this federal challenge, found the guidelines inconsistent with governing statutes, and ordered corrective instructions and renewed claim processing. The D.C. Circuit reversed without deciding the merits. It held that the union lacked representational standing because injuries and amounts varied among members and concluded that relief could not proceed for individual plaintiffs without joining the state agencies that had denied benefits. (279–281.)

The Supreme Court reversed and remanded. The UAW satisfied the three Hunt conditions for associational standing, and failure to join the cooperating state agencies did not defeat the action. The Court of Appeals still had to consider the merits of the district court’s statutory decision and any properly preserved procedural questions. Thus the Supreme Court did not itself approve the handbook challenge on its merits or award benefits. (282–293.)

## The three standing conditions

An association could represent its members when some members would have standing individually, the protected interests were germane to its purpose, and neither the claim nor requested relief required individual members to participate. The Secretary disputed each condition, but the Court held all satisfied here. (282–283.)

First, some UAW members had a live stake in correction of the guideline. The Secretary attempted to divide claimants into categories—successful claimants uninjured, unsuccessful final judgments precluded, pending claims committed to state review, and unappealed administrative denials final—and argued that no one could bring the challenge federally. The majority responded that this action did not supplant individual eligibility review. Members who had not received the benefits allegedly due and whose claims were not foreclosed by final state judgments retained an interest in challenging the governing federal interpretation. The statutory direction of individual claim review into state channels did not withdraw federal-question jurisdiction over statutory or constitutional attacks on the federal guideline itself. (283–286.)

Second, obtaining unemployment benefits was germane to the union’s purposes. Its constitution identified national social and unemployment insurance as a goal, its leaders had supported the trade-benefit legislation, and Congress itself gave unions a role in requesting group eligibility certification. The suit was not an unrelated organizational project. (286–287.)

Third, neither deciding the legal interpretation nor ordering correction required each worker’s individual participation. State agencies would still examine particular employment histories and eligibility facts before payments. The Court distinguished an association’s attempt to recover individualized damages in Warth: there, establishing the injuries and amounts required member-specific proof in the suit itself. Here, member-specific work occurred later in the appropriate benefit processes. The possibility that successful litigation would ultimately affect different benefit amounts did not convert the federal statutory-interpretation suit into individual damages adjudication. (287–288.)

## Why Rule 23 was not mandatory

The Secretary urged the Court to abandon ordinary associational standing in favor of class actions, subject to Rule 23’s representation safeguards. The majority reaffirmed Hunt. An existing association could bring accumulated expertise, research, financing, and a continuing organizational relationship to litigation, whereas a class could be an ad hoc group connected only by claims. Members often joined an organization to make shared interests more effective, providing some assurance that representation served those interests. (288–290.)

The Court did not dismiss inadequate-representation concerns categorically. A deficient association’s adverse judgment might fail to bind members consistently with due process, and actual evidence could require consideration of protections. But the Secretary had supplied no reason to doubt this union’s representation in this case. The Court also expressly declined to decide whether class treatment would have been proper after the district court’s judgment because associational standing sufficed. (288 n.*, 290.)

## State agencies and joinder

The case challenged the federal guideline, not an adverse individual benefit determination removed into federal court. Accordingly, state procedural rules requiring an agency’s presence on review of its individual decision did not control this different federal action. (290–291.)

Nor did relief depend simply on voluntary cooperation by absent states. Cooperating agencies had agreed to act as federal agents and were required to implement the program uniformly. The Secretary’s directives would bind them; breach of their agreements could have consequences for employer tax credits. Federal reimbursement for benefit and administrative costs also reduced the likelihood of resistance and the claimed prejudice to agencies. Under these circumstances, the agencies were not indispensable under Rule 19(b). A slight possibility that plaintiffs might not achieve every aspect of relief did not justify preventing the suit. (291–293.)

## The two dissents

White, Burger, and Rehnquist read §2311(d) and its history as committing all legal and factual questions concerning already-submitted benefit claims to state review. For them, distinguishing the guideline from its applications made little sense once the guideline had been superseded and only accrued claims remained. White’s notes addressed that timing, distinguished prospective-relief cases, and challenged reliance on Christian, where the relevant jurisdictional issue had not been squarely considered. He recognized that TRA funds were federal and that the Eleventh Amendment did not directly apply; his argument was about the state-review arrangement Congress had incorporated. (293–296 and notes 1–3.)

Powell separately worried that the unknown number of members retaining live claims could be too small to ensure effective representation. Formal germaneness, union resources, and expertise did not necessarily establish a sufficient alignment of interests. A union might litigate for publicity while most members had no stake. His starred note contrasted that concern with the interest alignment required in a typical class action. He would not confer standing on the record before the Court. (296–298.)

The Court reversed the D.C. Circuit and remanded for merits and remaining preserved procedural consideration.
