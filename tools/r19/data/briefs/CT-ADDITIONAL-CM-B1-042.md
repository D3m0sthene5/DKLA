# Mesaros v. United States
845 F.2d 1576 (Fed. Cir. 1988) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Senior Circuit Judge Skelton wrote for a panel consisting of Mayer, Skelton, and Baldwin on May 6, 1988. Mary and Anthony Mesaros filed a putative class action in the Southern District of Georgia seeking damages for nondelivery of commemorative coins or, alternatively, mandamus compelling the government to accept their order and deliver the coins. Thirty-three other persons were named. The district court granted defendants’ motion to dismiss or alternatively for summary judgment in its entirety on April 13, 1987; it did not act on class certification because that issue was moot after judgment. The appeal initially went to the Eleventh Circuit and was transferred to the Federal Circuit on defendants’ motion. (1576–1577.)

The Federal Circuit affirmed. The contract claim failed on summary judgment because the promotional materials solicited offers and the government had not accepted the disputed order. The mandamus claim was properly dismissed for want of jurisdiction because the statute imposed no duty owed to these purchasers to enter a contract in the asserted sequence. Class certification did not require decision in light of those conclusions. There are no separate writings. (1580–1583.)

## Statutory program and promotional materials
Congress’s July 1985 Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Commemorative Coin Act authorized coin sales to help restore the monuments and establish a maintenance endowment, without net cost to the government. It limited production to twenty-five million half-dollar coins, ten million silver dollar coins, and 500,000 gold five-dollar coins. Section 105(c) directed the Secretary to accept prepaid orders before issuance at a reasonable discount reflecting prepayment; another provision authorized discounted bulk sales. (1577–1578 & n.1.)

In November and December 1985 the Mint sent promotional materials to previous customers and collectors, including the plaintiffs. The materials said a reservation received by December 31 would qualify for a pre-issue discount of up to sixteen percent. Payment could be by check, money order, or credit card. Credit-card processing was contracted to Mellon Bank and proved slower than filling cash orders. The order form asked the Mint to accept the purchaser’s order, stated that sales were final, and expressly contemplated verification by the Mint. Its reverse side reserved the right to limit shipments subject to availability and to discontinue orders if bullion prices rose significantly. It also stated that credit-card orders would be billed upon receipt. These qualifications are important parts of the actual solicitation, although the assigned problem quotes only some of the order language. (1578 & n.2.)

Demand substantially exceeded the supply of gold coins. By May 30, 1986, the Mint had received over 756,000 orders for commemorative coins, including 186,000 credit-card orders. Those numbers concern orders for the program’s coins; they are not a finding that all 756,000 were individual gold-coin orders. Gold sold out while the other coin types remained available. The Mint estimated that the last filled gold order had been accepted between December 31 and January 6, but judicial note 3 identified an apparently accepted and filled order for 640 gold coins on January 8. The court thus did not endorse a perfectly administered chronological process. (1578–1580 & n.3.)

## Plaintiffs’ orders and asserted loss
The plaintiffs alleged that Mary submitted the disputed order on November 26, 1985, with authorization to charge $1,675 to Anthony’s credit account. Anthony separately placed orders for eighteen more gold coins on December 30, using nine checks and family members’ names. The eighteen coins paid for by check arrived around May 1986; the earlier credit-card order did not. On February 18 the Mint sent a form letter saying it had tried but could not process the credit-card order, directing the couple to their bank and offering a new form for the coin types still available. The plaintiffs’ bank later confirmed that it had authorized the transaction on December 27. (1579.)

The plaintiffs contended that earlier credit-card buyers were unfairly bypassed while later cash buyers received coins, which increased sharply in market value in early 1986. The record showed that many credit-card orders had deficiencies, but did not establish that the Mesaroses’ order had those listed defects. The court nevertheless observed possible confusion: Mary’s order used Anthony’s signed credit authorization, omitted his address, and did not disclose their marital relationship. It described the verification bottleneck and the depletion of gold before all orders could be verified. That explanation is not a finding that every rejected buyer submitted an invalid order or that the plaintiffs’ bank withheld credit. (1578–1580.)

## Contract claim: solicitation rather than offer
The district court had Little Tucker Act jurisdiction over the pleaded contract claim because the plaintiffs alleged a government contract, breach, and damages not exceeding $10,000. Jurisdiction to decide that claim did not establish that a contract actually existed. Judicial note 4 identifies the statute’s concurrent district-court and Claims Court jurisdiction within the monetary limit. (1580 & n.4.)

The plaintiffs argued that the Mint’s advertisement was an offer and their submitted order was its acceptance. The court instead applied an objective-reasonableness inquiry. Advertisements and general sales solicitations ordinarily invite offers; construing broadly distributed materials as commitments to every respondent could obligate a seller beyond its available supply. Here Congress had limited gold production, and the complete materials contemplated the Mint’s later verification and acceptance. The request that the Mint accept the customer’s order identified the order as the customer’s offer, rather than a completed acceptance of a government promise. (1580–1581.)

The court distinguished Lefkowitz, which involved a specifically identified fur stole offered to the first customer at a designated opening time. The Mint’s materials did not promise a first-come, first-served allocation or specify any equivalent basis binding it to every timely response. Different payment methods required different processing, and the court considered a chronological sales process impracticable under those arrangements. Its holding concerned these actual materials and circumstances; it did not abolish the possibility that a sufficiently definite advertisement could constitute an offer. Because the disputed offer had not been accepted and plaintiffs identified no genuine material factual issue, summary judgment on breach of contract was proper. (1581–1582.)

## Mandamus: no statutory entitlement to the requested contract
Alternatively, the plaintiffs invoked 28 U.S.C. §1361 and the Act’s direction to accept prepaid orders. They sought either delivery or an order requiring the Mint to enter a contract, whose subsequent breach would produce damages even if delivery were impossible. The court rejected both routes. All authorized gold coins had been sold; compelling further production would exceed the 500,000 statutory limit and the authority Congress delegated to the Mint. More fundamentally, the statute did not require contracts with these plaintiffs on a first-come, first-served basis or in another particular order. The Secretary possessed broad administrative discretion, and the court found no abuse in its exercise on this record. Without a statutory duty owed to the plaintiffs, the district court lacked jurisdiction over the mandamus claim apart from the contract claim. It properly dismissed that claim and the appellate court affirmed the judgment. (1582–1583.)
