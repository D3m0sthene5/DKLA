# In re National Football League Players Concussion Injury Litigation — settlement appeal

**821 F.3d 410 (3d Cir. 2016) · April 18, 2016 (amended May 2, 2016) · Civil Procedure**

Judge Ambro wrote for a unanimous panel with Judges Hardiman and Nygaard. The court affirmed final certification of a Rule 23(b)(3) settlement class and approval of the settlement. This is the 2016 appellate decision, distinct from the January 2014 rejection of preliminary approval, the later preliminary-certification appeal, and the April 2015 district-court final approval. The shared assigned editorial account occupies CivPro physical PDF 446–448, beginning below the printed 866 marker and ending on printed 869.

## The claims and the settlement’s development

Retired players alleged that the NFL knew about the long-term risks of repeated head impacts but failed to protect them, warn them, or accurately disclose research. They asserted negligence, fraud, concealment, medical-monitoring, and related claims. The opinion describes these as allegations; settlement approval did not adjudicate the NFL’s tort liability. Claims against helmet manufacturer Riddell were outside this appeal. (421–422.)

After removal, hundreds of actions were consolidated before Judge Brody for pretrial proceedings. The NFL sought dismissal on federal labor-law preemption grounds: resolving the claims allegedly required interpretation of collective bargaining agreements and arbitration. Players disputed dependence on those agreements and noted that not all playing periods were covered. That potentially dispositive defense remained unresolved when the parties settled. (421–422.)

Court-ordered mediation began in July 2013. An August term sheet proposed a capped settlement. In January 2014, the district court declined preliminary approval because the monetary fund might not pay all eligible claims. A special master assisted with financial projections; the parties subsequently uncapped that fund. Preliminary approval followed in July 2014, a fairness hearing in November, and further improvements in February 2015. The April 22, 2015 final approval generated twelve consolidated appeals. The earlier unsuccessful Rule 23(f) petition was a different procedural stage, described here as history rather than decided again. (422–423.)

## What class members received and released

The agreement created an uncapped Monetary Award Fund lasting sixty-five years, a $75 million Baseline Assessment Program, and a $10 million education fund. Six qualifying diagnoses could produce monetary awards: two levels of neurocognitive impairment, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, ALS, and death with CTE for players who died before final approval. A claimant did not have to prove that NFL participation caused the qualifying diagnosis. Maximum awards varied from $1.5 million to $5 million, subject to specified reductions for age at diagnosis, short playing careers, failure to obtain a baseline examination, and unrelated severe brain injury or stroke. These were maxima, not guaranteed payments to every retired player. (423–424.)

Registration and claim-submission deadlines included specified exceptions for good cause or substantial hardship. Award decisions could be appealed; a player’s appeal fee was refundable upon success and waivable for financial hardship. A more serious later diagnosis could support a supplemental award. The baseline program supplied examinations and benefits for certain lesser impairments; its examination guarantee applied to all eligible applicants notwithstanding the stated funding cap. (424–425.)

The class included players retired before July 7, 2014, representative claimants for deceased or incapacitated players, and derivative claimants. One subclass consisted of players without a qualifying diagnosis before that date; the other contained those with an existing qualifying diagnosis. They released concussion-related claims against the NFL, while collective-bargaining, retirement, disability, and health-insurance benefits were preserved. Approximately one percent objected and another one percent opted out; the opinion reported 202 remaining opt-outs, while noting counsel’s position that only 169 had been timely. (425.)

## Commonality, typicality, and the settlement-class requirements

Certification and approval received abuse-of-discretion review, including correction of erroneous legal premises or clearly erroneous factual findings. Rule 23(a)’s prerequisites and Rule 23(b)(3)’s predominance and superiority requirements still applied to a settlement class. Numerosity was uncontested for a class exceeding 20,000. (425–426.)

Objectors emphasized different playing periods, positions, exposure, and injuries. The panel distinguished Wal-Mart: the NFL allegedly injured players through the same course of conduct, rather than unrelated discretionary choices by thousands of supervisors. Questions about what the NFL knew, concealed, or did to protect players could be resolved commonly even though individual injuries differed. The representatives’ claims were typical for the same reason: they used the same legal theories to challenge the same alleged practices. (426–428.)

Predominance demanded more than a single common question, but the alleged league-wide conduct and shared scientific causation questions supplied the required cohesion. Amchem did not categorically prohibit mass-tort settlement classes; the panel distinguished the much larger and more varied asbestos class. Superiority was uncontested: settlement avoided years of duplicative proceedings and delayed recovery. Trial-management difficulties did not have the same force where the proposal contemplated settlement rather than a class trial. (434–435, 440.)

## Representation and the present–future conflict

Amchem’s central concern was that presently injured people would maximize immediate payments while future claimants needed a durable fund, inflation protection, and accommodation of changing science. The NFL settlement used separate representatives and separate counsel for those two interests. The fund was uncapped and inflation-adjusted, paid present claims immediately, and required good-faith meetings every ten years concerning possible scientific modifications to existing qualifying-diagnosis definitions and protocols. Those protections supported adequate representation; the meetings did not guarantee that every newly recognized disease would become compensable. (431–434.)

Subclass counsel came from the existing steering committee and were designated after negotiations began. The panel found no requirement that independent counsel always be recruited from outside that team. They were experienced, had participated early and actively, and negotiated at arm’s length. The district court evaluated their adequacy rather than relying only on their designation. (429.)

Objectors also argued that Arnold Levin, counsel for the future-injury subclass, represented presently injured players. The panel declined mechanical waiver because it had an independent obligation to protect absent class members. On the merits, however, the record did not establish that Levin’s other clients had qualifying diagnoses. Alleged headaches, memory problems, and similar symptoms did not by themselves place a player in the diagnosed subclass. Thus, the panel did **not** find that counsel actually represented both subclasses and simply excuse that established conflict. This distinction corrects the assigned editorial account’s broader description. (429–430.)

Wooden adequately represented players without qualifying diagnoses, including their interest in compensation for potential CTE-related harm. Turner represented diagnosed players. The opinion’s sixth note records Turner’s death and grants substitution of his father as an appellate party, without requiring a new subclass representative for deciding the appeal. Requests for still more subclasses and intervention were rejected in notes 9–10; additional subclasses were unnecessary on this record, not forbidden in all cases. (428 n.6, 430–432, 432 n.9, 434 n.10.)

## Notice and fairness

Notice explained benefits, releases, objections, and opt-out rights. Its warning that legally enforceable liens could reduce awards adequately alerted players to possible Medicare or Medicaid deductions and directed them to the agreement. Rule 23 and due process required practicable, informative notice; they did not require every detail to be reproduced in the notice itself. (435–436.)

The district court applied the Girsh factors and relevant Prudential considerations, assessing litigation expense, class reaction, case development, liability and damages risks, class-maintenance risks, ability to pay, and recovery relative to risk. Extensive informal investigation could support both a fairness presumption and informed bargaining. Formal discovery was not invariably required: counsel had assembled player information, consulted experts, and assessed preemption and causation barriers. Avoiding its cost could itself be part of the bargain. Low objection rates mattered here because many players already had lawyers and had actively pursued claims. (436–439.)

The NFL’s greater financial resources did not automatically make the agreement inadequate. The fund was uncapped, and its amount was not justified by claimed inability to pay. Players still faced substantial preemption, general-causation, and individual-causation risks. The panel compared certain benefits with risk-adjusted litigation prospects rather than the largest imaginable verdict. Those evaluations were settlement judgments, not findings that the NFL’s defenses necessarily would prevail. (439–441.)

## Why CTE’s treatment did not defeat approval

Objectors argued that future post-mortem CTE diagnoses were released for nothing. The panel upheld the district court’s historical scientific findings under clear-error review. In the then-existing record, CTE could only be diagnosed after death, the examined brains were a small selected population, and the association of particular living symptoms with the diagnosis remained uncertain. These are the record and findings reviewed in 2016, not assertions about medical knowledge today. (441–443.)

Many neurocognitive or neuromuscular impairments associated with CTE qualified for compensation independently of a CTE label. Mood and behavioral symptoms were not covered, but had substantial alternative risk factors and presented more difficult causation problems. The pre-approval death-with-CTE benefit served as a proxy for diagnoses deceased players could not obtain while living; after approval, players had notice to seek qualifying diagnoses. The temporal cutoff therefore did not prove that future claims had been sacrificed to enrich current claimants. (433–434, 441–443.)

The Daubert objection had not been raised below and was waived. The panel also distinguished a fairness hearing from a trial resolving contested expert facts. An NFL official’s March 2016 acknowledgment of a football–CTE connection did not cure the unresolved scientific questions or render the settlement unreasonable. The court’s task was to review the negotiated compromise, not design its preferred agreement. (442–444.)

## Fees and disposition

Fees had not yet been awarded. The NFL agreed not to oppose a fee-and-cost award up to $112.5 million, paid separately from settlement benefits. Class counsel could also seek a five-percent assessment on monetary awards for administration. Future petitions remained subject to court scrutiny and objections. Approval of the settlement was not an award of either requested amount. (444–445.)

Deferring the fee petition did not violate Rule 23(h) or due process here. Players knew the funding source, clear-sailing ceiling, and later opportunity to object. The panel distinguished cases where an objection deadline expired before a fee petition became available. Simultaneous fee notice was preferable, but the Rule did not make it categorically mandatory. A clear-sailing provision warranted careful scrutiny rather than automatic rejection; fees were negotiated after principal terms, did not diminish the monetary fund, and the record showed no exchange of player claims for counsel’s compensation. (445–447.)

The court **affirmed certification and settlement approval in full**. There is no separate opinion or judicial appendix.
