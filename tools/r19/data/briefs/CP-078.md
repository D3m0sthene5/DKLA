# United States v. Heyward-Robinson Co.

**430 F.2d 1077 (2d Cir. 1970) · Civil Procedure · CP-078**

The Second Circuit affirmed a subcontractor's recovery arising from two connected construction projects, holding that counterclaims concerning the private project were compulsory and within ancillary federal jurisdiction over the federal project's Miller Act action. Judge Friendly agreed with the judgment but rejected classification of the private-project counterclaim as compulsory. The distinction mattered beyond convenience: a compulsory classification could preclude a later action if the counterclaim had not been pleaded. His preferred jurisdictional theory would have allowed the claim without imposing that compulsory consequence.

D'Agostino Excavators performed excavation work for prime contractor Heyward-Robinson under two Connecticut subcontracts. One involved Navy barracks at the submarine base in Groton; the other involved a private Stelma plant in Stamford. D'Agostino sued Heyward and its surety, Maryland Casualty, under the Miller Act for payments due on the Navy work. Heyward denied liability and counterclaimed for overpayments and completion costs on both projects. D'Agostino replied with its own demand for amounts due on the Stelma project. Thus the private dispute entered through opposing counterclaims, rather than being an independently jurisdictionally sufficient federal complaint. (Opinion ¶¶6–7.)

The parties disputed which contractor first breached. D'Agostino said Heyward failed to make required progress payments. Heyward said D'Agostino allowed compensation and employee-liability insurance to lapse and that Heyward consequently terminated both subcontracts on October 19, 1965. The jury answered a special question finding that Heyward had breached before that date. After the complaint was amended to seek quantum meruit recovery for work on both projects, the jury determined a combined net amount owed of $63,988.36. Judgment was entered against Heyward for that sum. An allocation formula agreed by the parties assigned $40,771.46 to the Navy project, producing judgment against the surety in that amount. The court denied post-trial motions, and both defendants appealed. (¶¶8–11.)

On appeal, defendants argued for the first time that the Stelma counterclaims were permissive and lacked federal jurisdiction. They had urged the opposite classification below, but that position did not waive a subject-matter objection. The court addressed it because a party, or the court itself, could raise absence of federal jurisdiction even at this stage. No independent basis existed: both contractors were New York corporations with New York offices, defeating diversity, and the private project did not qualify as federal public work under the Miller Act. The appellants argued that dismissal of the private claims would undermine the entire intertwined verdict. (¶¶12–16.)

Under the majority's then-governing circuit rule, compulsory counterclaims fell within ancillary jurisdiction without an independent jurisdictional ground, while permissive counterclaims generally required one. Judicial footnote 1 recognized a limited exception for a defensive setoff used only to defeat or reduce recovery, without seeking affirmative relief. Rule 13(a)'s compulsory category covered a counterclaim arising from the transaction or occurrence that was the subject of the opposing claim. The majority applied a broad logical-relationship test, rather than requiring identical facts. Its justification was resolution of connected disputes in one action and avoidance of fragmented litigation. (¶¶16–21 & n.1.)

Several concrete links supported that classification. The same parties made the subcontracts for the same type of work during substantially the same period. Cross-contract provisions permitted termination or withholding across the two jobs. Progress payments were made in lump sums without allocation. One insurance policy covered both projects, and the October letters threatening and effecting termination treated them together. The contested defaults and proof of payments likewise concerned both. Even appellants described the claims as inseparable at trial. Looking at the whole record, the majority concluded that the private-project claims logically and immediately related to the Navy claim and were compulsory. Ancillary jurisdiction therefore supported their adjudication. (¶¶22–26.)

Friendly's concurrence insisted that the compulsory decision should be made at pleading, with attention to what would happen if Heyward had omitted the Stelma claim and later sued on it. Shared parties, type of work, timing, insurance, and a combined termination letter did not, to him, give one separate project legal origin in the other. The boilerplate cross-default provisions might have mattered had Heyward exercised those rights, but he said it had not. Pleadings specified amounts attributable to each project; unallocated payments could be allocated under ordinary payment rules. Practical entanglement that emerged during trial did not justify imposing an unexpectedly harsh compulsory-counterclaim bar. He also read Southern Construction v. Pickard, where a counterclaim was held not compulsory, as cutting against the majority's use of it. (¶¶66–69.)

Nevertheless, Friendly would allow jurisdiction by rejecting the conventional requirement of independent grounds for permissive counterclaims. The setoff exception weakened that supposed general rule, and the approach to related nonfederal claims after Gibbs supported convenient joint adjudication. He would have sought en banc reconsideration of contrary circuit precedent had the decision rested on his theory. Because his colleagues could affirm through compulsory classification, he joined the result and left that jurisdictional question open for another case. He concurred in all other parts of Judge Bryan's opinion. His proposed general rule was therefore not the panel's holding. (¶¶70–72.)

The complete opinion also resolved trial issues omitted from the assigned principal discussion. The trial court should have heard proposed foundational testimony before excluding a memorandum as a settlement discussion, but the error was harmless. The appellate court regarded the memorandum as peripheral to the breach issue and of little relevance to quantum meruit, against substantial supporting evidence. Five of seven instruction challenges were not preserved after the court invited objections; the instructions were also considered adequate. The two preserved objections failed: a supplemental reasonable-time instruction adequately addressed payment timing, and Heyward could not retrospectively excuse nonpayment through supplier debts it had not known of or withheld money to address. (¶¶27–52; nn.2–3.)

Nor was Heyward automatically entitled to credit for the full $36,250 face amount of promissory notes it had dishonored and later reacquired for $18,500. Whether the parties accepted the notes as payment was a jury question; the underlying obligation was not necessarily discharged at face value. The amendment to quantum meruit caused no demonstrated prejudice, and Heyward never sought the additional preparation time the court offered. A request to poll the jury on its first special verdict came too late after its second verdict, and complaints about trial recesses supplied no basis for retrial. (¶¶53–63.)

The judgment was affirmed. Judge Frederick van Pelt Bryan, sitting by designation, wrote the majority joined by Judge Kaufman; Friendly concurred in the result on jurisdiction and otherwise joined the opinion. The complete majority, Friendly concurrence, three numbered judicial notes, and designation note were read, together with the whole assigned principal passage and relocated designation note. This brief describes the 1970 ancillary-jurisdiction analysis; it does not substitute a later supplemental-jurisdiction statute for the law actually applied. The comparison to retain is the majority's flexible transaction approach and Friendly's concern that permission to litigate together should not automatically become an obligation carrying future preclusion.
