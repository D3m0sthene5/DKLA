# Merck Sharp & Dohme Corp. v. Albrecht

**139 S. Ct. 1668 (2019) · Supreme Court of the United States · May 20, 2019 · No. 17-290**  
**Civil Procedure · Judge–jury allocation and federal preemption**

## Facts and the regulatory sequence

Merck manufactured Fosamax to treat and prevent osteoporosis in postmenopausal women. The drug slowed the breakdown of old bone cells, helping preserve bone mass. That same mechanism raised concern that ordinary microscopic stress cracks might not heal and could progress into serious, low-energy breaks, including atypical femoral fractures. The opinion describes developing evidence and uncertainty about that risk; it does not adjudicate each patient's causation or declare every use unsafe. Breyer slip op. 4–5.

When the FDA approved Fosamax in 1995, its label lacked an atypical-femoral-fracture warning. Merck had disclosed theoretical concerns during the approval process. Later adverse-event reports, statistical analysis, and published studies strengthened concern about a connection. In 2008, Merck sought permission to add language to the Adverse Reactions section and a longer discussion of “stress fractures” in the Precautions section. The FDA accepted the proposed adverse-reaction addition but rejected the precautions proposal as inadequately justified, explaining that stress-fracture terminology might not clearly address the atypical fractures in the literature. It invited resubmission addressing the deficiencies. Merck instead withdrew the application and used the changes-being-effected process for the adverse-reaction addition, without making the disputed precautions change. Id. at 5–6.

In 2010, the FDA initiated the process leading to a stronger atypical-fracture warning, and the parties agreed on the label language in January 2011. The FDA again rejected Merck's stress-fracture wording because physicians could understand it to describe a minor injury, understating the seriousness of atypical femoral fractures. The history of ongoing FDA–Merck communications, emphasized by Alito, is important: the final warning date does not imply that the agency and manufacturer were inactive in the intervening period. Id. at 6–7; Alito slip op. 4–6.

More than 500 individuals who took Fosamax and suffered atypical femoral fractures between 1999 and 2010 filed separate diversity actions. They alleged that state law required Merck to warn them and their physicians. One alleged she would not have taken the drug so long had she understood the risk, after a fracture occurred while she turned to unlock her door and required surgical repair. These were plaintiffs' allegations, not a Supreme Court liability finding. Breyer slip op. 7, 13.

## Procedure and parties' positions

Merck argued that federal law made compliance with any state warning obligation impossible. It conceded that the FDA's changes-being-effected regulation would have permitted it to attempt a warning before 2010, but argued that the FDA would have rejected the attempt. Merck relied particularly on rejection of its 2008 proposal as evidence that a warning about atypical femoral fractures would also have been disallowed. The district court accepted preemption and granted Merck summary judgment. Id. at 7–8.

The Third Circuit vacated and remanded. Applying *Wyeth v. Levine*, it treated “clear evidence” as an elevated evidentiary standard and reasoned that a factfinder must find it highly probable that the FDA would reject a proposed change. It also classified that rejection question as a factual issue for the jury. Merck sought Supreme Court review of the jury allocation and the uncertain meaning of the standard. Id. at 8–9.

The Supreme Court held that the preemption question is for a **judge, including subsidiary factual disputes**, and clarified the required showing. It did not restore summary judgment for Merck or decide all plaintiffs' claims. Instead, it vacated the Third Circuit's judgment and remanded for application of the corrected framework. Id. at 9, 13–17.

## What “clear evidence” means

Impossibility preemption requires an actual conflict: could the manufacturer lawfully provide a warning that satisfies its state obligation? The Court explained that, in a case like *Wyeth*, the manufacturer must show that it fully informed the FDA of the justification for the state-required warning and that the FDA informed it that such a warning would not be approved. The relevant inquiry concerns prohibition of any warning sufficient under state law, not merely rejection of one particular wording. The Court assumed, without deciding, that sufficient evidence existed to find a state-law warning violation in this case. Id. at 13.

FDA approval of an original label does not automatically preempt later failure-to-warn claims. The majority's reading of *Wyeth* emphasized continuing manufacturer responsibility and Congress's retention of state tort remedies. The changes-being-effected regulation allowed qualifying warning additions based on newly acquired information without awaiting advance approval, although the FDA retained authority to reject them. Permission to make an adequate change undermines a claim that simultaneous compliance was impossible. Id. at 10–14.

“Clear evidence” was not further defined as preponderance, clear-and-convincing evidence, or another factual probability threshold. The judge must determine whether the legal requirements irreconcilably conflict. A predicted or hypothetical possibility of rejection is insufficient. Relevant agency action must also fall within congressionally delegated authority; examples include rulemaking, a formal warning-label rejection, or another action carrying the force of law. The Court expressly said the precise method of agency disapproval was not before it. It did not approve every informal communication as independently preemptive. Id. at 14–15.

## Why the judge also decides subsidiary facts

Interpreting an agency determination requires legal skills: identifying its scope, reading the instruments embodying it, and understanding the governing statutory and regulatory scheme. Judges' familiarity with administrative law and their role in interpreting written instruments favored judicial allocation. Uniform interpretation of federal agency action also supported that choice. The Court drew on *Markman* without declaring that any complicated question must leave the jury. Id. at 15–16.

Some underlying facts may genuinely be disputed. For example, the parties may disagree about what information Merck supplied to the FDA, affecting the meaning and scope of a rejection. The Court nevertheless treated such disputes as subsidiary to a tightly circumscribed legal analysis. Like subsidiary facts in patent interpretation or confession-voluntariness inquiries, they could be resolved by the judge as part of the broader legal question. Sending the “brute facts” to a jury separately was not the prescribed solution. This allocation does not mean all facts bearing on the patient's separate tort claim become matters for the judge. Id. at 16–17.

## Separate writings and voting

Justice Breyer wrote for Justices Thomas, Ginsburg, Sotomayor, Kagan, and Gorsuch. Thomas also concurred separately. Justice Alito concurred in the judgment, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justice Kavanaugh. All nine agreed on the judgment and judicial allocation; the Breyer opinion had six votes. There is no dissent. Syllabus 3; Thomas slip op. 1; Alito slip op. 1.

Thomas explained his preference for a logical-contradiction understanding of the Supremacy Clause over a test confined to physical impossibility. Even under the Court's existing test, he concluded that Merck's defense failed. In his view, the 2009 complete-response letter invited further agency process and did not finally determine rights or obligations; predicted rejection of a future application was not binding federal law. Merck could have pursued another application, requested a hearing, or made a qualifying changes-being-effected change. **That conclusion on the defense was Thomas's application, not the Court's collective disposition.** His starred note recognized that a proper binding agency action could preempt without the manufacturer itself supplying all information, while reporting no such action here. Thomas slip op. 1–6 & n.* at 6.

Alito agreed that judges decide the question but objected to aspects of the majority's legal and factual account. He emphasized the FDA's statutory duty, enacted in 2007, to initiate appropriate label changes when it learns of relevant information. In his view, the agency's considered decision not to require a change could affect the defense even without a manufacturer-supplied justification or express communication rejecting a warning. He also stressed the prior-approval-supplement route and the FDA's continuing investigation, communications, announcements, and task-force work before the 2011 label. These points were concerns for remand, not a holding that Merck had already proved preemption. His sole starred note identified an immaterial 2018 change in the statutory wording. Alito slip op. 1–6 & n.* at 2.

## Exact judgment and assignment

The Supreme Court **vacated the Third Circuit and remanded to it** because it had treated preemption as fact rather than law and had not had the opportunity to apply the clarified standards. The Court neither reinstated the district-court judgment nor decided whether Merck was liable under state law. Breyer slip op. 17.

The assigned account focuses on institutional allocation and reproduces the explanation concerning legal skills and subsidiary facts. Its compressed procedural description should be understood against the full district summary-judgment and appellate vacatur sequence. The neighboring *Markman* and *Google v. Oracle* discussions are separate assignments, not additional holdings in Merck. The principal lesson is a specific allocation of a federal preemption inquiry, including its subsidiary facts, rather than an unrestricted power to withdraw mixed questions from juries.
