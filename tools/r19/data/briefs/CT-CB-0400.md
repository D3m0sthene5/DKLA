# Langman v. Alumni Association of the University of Virginia
247 Va. 491, 442 S.E.2d 669 (1994) | Contracts

## A charitable transfer that included mortgage liability
Dr. Margaretha Langman and real-estate broker Caleb Stowe bought Ferdinand's Arcade, a commercial property in Maryland, in June 1986. The purchase price was $780,000, and they gave Dominion Federal a $600,000 note secured by the property. The seller guaranteed specified rental income until actual income reached the promised level. Later that year, Stowe wanted to fulfill a university pledge, and Langman wanted to help fund an endowed medical-school chair. University officials suggested transferring the property to the Alumni Association, whose assets could more readily be liquidated. Correspondence valued Langman's equity contribution at approximately $86,000. 247 Va. at 494–495.

A deed dated December 30, 1986 conveyed the property to the Association in fee simple, reciting the grantors' love and respect for the university. It expressly identified the existing mortgage and provided that the grantee assumed payment and would hold the grantors harmless from further liability. No Association representative signed it, and the form provided no place for a grantee signature. Langman testified that she carefully read and understood the clause, knew the difference between taking property subject to a mortgage and assuming payment, and would not have transferred her equity without the assumption. Id. at 494–495.

The Association's director acknowledged the gift by letter. In early 1987 its comptroller received the deed and sent it to an attorney for recording in Maryland; the original was returned to the Association. Its accounting records and audited statements treated the mortgage as its liability, and financial-statement footnotes said it had assumed the mortgage on donated property. The director testified that he had internally objected to the liability entry, but the accountants denied receiving such complaints. The Association also signed documents as owner and lessor. Id. at 495–496.

Stowe agreed to manage the property, collect rents, and arrange a sale. Revenues fell short of operating and mortgage expenses, and he paid the difference. Initially those amounts were recorded as debts to him, but he later forgave them and the parties treated them as charitable contributions. In 1989, after his business entered bankruptcy, he could no longer make up the deficits. The successor lender notified Langman of default. The Association disclaimed responsibility, so she cured the default under a reservation of rights and sued for a declaration of the obligations under the deed. Id. at 496–497.

## The decree below and the full range of disputed issues
After a three-day hearing, the trial court found that the Association had not knowingly accepted contractual conditions, that an unknown draftsman mistakenly inserted the assumption clause, and that the Association had sufficiently repudiated the gift. It declared the conveyance ineffective and the clause unenforceable, relieving the Association of liability for amounts paid and remaining debt. Id. at 497.

Langman argued that the court improperly used parol evidence to contradict an unambiguous deed and allowed a grantee to retain ownership for more than two years before repudiating its burden. Through cross-error, the Association asserted the absence of its signature, the suretyship statute of frauds, fraud, mutual mistake, inequitable conduct inducing unilateral mistake, and failure of consideration. Justice Barbara Milano Keenan wrote the Supreme Court of Virginia's opinion. The listed participants were Chief Justice Carrico, Justices Compton, Stephenson, Whiting, Lacy, and Keenan, and Senior Justice Poff; no separate writing appears.

Ordinarily the trial court's factual findings would bind the appellate court unless plainly wrong or unsupported. But whether the deed was ambiguous was a question of law, and findings resting on improperly admitted parol evidence could not stand merely because they were denominated factual findings. Id. at 498.

## The deed was unambiguous and the gift was not inconsistent with consideration
The parol-evidence rule barred prior or contemporaneous negotiations from varying a complete, unambiguous written instrument. The deed plainly required the grantee to assume the mortgage and protect the grantors from liability. The trial court had treated the gift language and payment obligation as an internal inconsistency permitting extrinsic explanation. Keenan rejected that interpretation. A transfer can be partly a gift and partly an exchange. The encumbered portion yielded consideration to the grantor through the assumption; the unencumbered equity was donated without consideration. Giving effect to both parts did not produce a contradiction. Id. at 498–499.

The court therefore could not use prior discussions or the Association's claimed ignorance to rewrite the assumption clause. This did not exclude evidence for every purpose. The full opinion's footnote explains that testimony elicited to prove fraud or mistake could fall within an exception to the parol-evidence rule. The error was using extrinsic intent to contradict the otherwise clear deed, not forbidding genuine challenges to its validity. Id. at 498–499; 504 n.3.

## Acceptance bound the grantee despite the missing signature
A grantee accepting a deed becomes contractually bound by its provisions, including a mortgage-assumption undertaking. Acceptance is shown by conduct rather than by the grantee's signature alone. A beneficial conveyance carries a presumption of acceptance unless the grantee acts to renounce it. Id. at 499–500.

Here, delivery was established by the intended conveyance and receipt by the Association's agents. During approximately thirty months before the debt controversy, the Association recorded its ownership, accounted for the property as an asset and the mortgage as a liability, acted as owner and lessor, and accepted Stowe's payments as charitable contributions. It took no action resisting passage of title. The director's asserted internal complaints concerned only the debt, not rejection of the property. A grantee could not claim ownership through those affirmative acts while treating private dissatisfaction with the burden as a renunciation of the entire deed. The evidence did not support the trial court's finding of a timely disavowal. Having accepted the conveyance, the Association was bound by its terms. Id. at 500–501.

## An original undertaking rather than suretyship
Virginia Code § 11-2(4) required a signed writing for a promise to answer for another's debt, default, or misdoings. The Association argued that the assumption was collateral because Langman remained secondarily liable to the lender. Without the Association's signature, it said, the clause could not be enforced. Id. at 501–502.

The court identified a different obligation. A grantee assuming a mortgage promises the grantor to pay the lender the debt the grantee owes the grantor as part of acquiring the property. It is an original undertaking supported by the transferred interest, not merely a promise to guarantee another's payment upon default. The Association received a direct benefit and did not act solely as a surety. The fact that the lender could still look to Langman did not alter the character of the Association's promise to her. The suretyship provision therefore did not bar enforcement. Id. at 502.

This is why the missing grantee signature and the creditor's continuing remedies must be kept separate. Conduct supplied acceptance of the deed; the particular debt-assumption promise lay outside the statutory category asserted by the Association.

## The remaining defenses did not invalidate the clause
The Association also alleged that the equity value was overstated. Evidence of a substantially lower tax valuation and the seller's rental guarantee suggested possible overvaluation, but a recent independent appraisal supported the represented purchase value. Fraud required clear and convincing evidence of all its elements. The conflicting proof did not establish a material misrepresentation with the required clarity, so the trial court correctly rejected actual and constructive fraud. Alleged inequitable conduct inducing unilateral mistake rested on essentially the same accusations and did not supply a separate basis for relief. Id. at 502–503.

Mutual mistake required a mistake common to both sides. The trial court had credited Langman's testimony that she knowingly signed the assumption clause and understood its legal effect. That credibility finding was supported and necessarily defeated a claim of mutual mistake. An Association misunderstanding alone did not invalidate the undertaking. Id. at 503–504.

Failure of consideration also failed. The Association's contention that the property was worth less than the mortgage depended on a valuation claim the trial court had not accepted. More fundamentally, it had received all of Langman's interest in the property in return for its assumption. That transfer supplied a benefit and consideration even though the transaction later became financially burdensome. Id. at 504.

## Judgment and practical result
The Supreme Court reversed the decree declaring the conveyance a nullity, the assumption clause unenforceable, and the Association free of liability to Langman or the lender's successor. It remanded for further proceedings on her bill of complaint consistent with its holdings. It did not itself calculate reimbursement or enter a final dollar award. 247 Va. at 504–505.

The assigned excerpt emphasizes the suretyship classification. The full opinion shows how that issue depended on the deed's interpretation, the grantee's acceptance, and unsuccessful validity defenses. Together, those rulings prevent an accepted transfer from being treated as only its favorable features: the Association's conduct accepted the instrument whose clear terms included the debt assumption.
