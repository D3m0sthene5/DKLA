# Holt v. First National Bank of Mobile
418 So. 2d 77 (Ala. 1982) | Contracts

## Holding and judgment
On July 2, 1982, the Alabama Supreme Court affirmed dismissal of two daughters' complaints against the executor of their deceased stepmother's estate. The complaints did not establish an actionable interference with expected inheritances under the circumstances alleged. A separate count claiming third-party-beneficiary rights under an oral premarital agreement was facially barred by the Statute of Frauds. Almon wrote; Torbert, Faulkner, and Embry concurred, and Adams concurred in the result without a separate explanation. Rehearing was denied July 30. (77–81.)

## Alleged promises, interference, and claimed losses
The father and stepmother married in September 1956; he died of cancer in April 1959. Holt alleged that he had formed a fixed intention to leave his home to her. She claimed the stepmother used fraudulent representations, duress, and undue influence to prevent a codicil and instead obtain control through the residuary estate and a marital trust. The stepmother allegedly promised to convey the home to Holt, possessed appointment power permitting the conveyance if the home entered the trust, and sold the home within twelve months after the father's death without disclosing that promise. Holt claimed she learned of the conduct only after the stepmother's death in May 1975. Her principal count demanded $125,000, plus interest and costs; additional counts sought recovery for money had and received, third-party-beneficiary rights under an oral premarital agreement, misrepresentation, and deceit. These were allegations, not findings that either parent acted as described. (78–79.)

A second complaint by Holt and Trimble concerned the old Waring property, which their father allegedly wanted to keep within the family and leave to both daughters. They alleged that the stepmother induced him to leave his will unchanged by promising to devise the property to them herself, but her probated will did not do so. That complaint demanded $100,000 under interference and money-had-and-received theories. The stated amounts were requested damages, not awards. (79.)

These consolidated cases had reached the Supreme Court twice before. An earlier appeal from dismissal of the original complaints was dismissed as untimely. A later mandamus decision required the trial court to rule on dismissal motions addressing amended complaints. The present appeal followed those adverse rulings and properly presented the sufficiency of the pleaded causes of action. It did not reopen or decide the earlier appeals anew. (78.)

## Proposed interference tort and the significance of proof
Alabama's appellate courts had not previously addressed the proposed tort of interference with an expectancy of inheritance. The court considered decisions from other jurisdictions, explaining that the proposed action required independently tortious interference with the testator's intention. Physical prevention of execution, written bequests, or written evidence of fraudulent representations supplied stronger circumstances in successful cases. A decision allowing an unwritten claim involved a living defendant with special access to whether a will existed. The court also noted authorities unfavorable to such claims and the Restatement's treatment. It did not hold that courts elsewhere uniformly rejected the tort. (79–80 & n. 1.)

Here no writing was alleged, the accused stepmother was dead, and the supposed promises preceded suit by more than fifteen years. The court acknowledged that difficulty of proof ordinarily does not determine whether a cause of action exists, but found additional conflicts with testamentary formalities, writing requirements for agreements to make wills or convey land, limitation policies, and the Dead Man's Statute. Fraud concerning a future promise required proving the promisor lacked an intention to perform when she made it. The court was unwilling to permit what it regarded as competing oral accounts of a deceased person's state of mind decades earlier. Its rejection concerned these pleaded circumstances rather than an adjudicated finding that the alleged promise was false or that no interference tort could ever exist. (80.)

The limitation discussion was qualified. The daughters alleged discovery after the stepmother's death, potentially bringing their claims within the then-applicable one-year fraud-discovery provision. But reasonable diligence and good faith mattered, and they had not explained inaction after their father's will was read or the home sold. The court invoked the general policy against stale claims; it did not calculate an independently dispositive limitations deadline while ignoring their discovery allegation. (80 n. 2.)

## Why the novel pleading and alternate counts failed
The daughters invoked a general preference against dismissal of novel or extreme claims before facts could be developed. The court recognized that preference and its evidentiary purpose but declined to apply it where the pleaded case implicated so many countervailing policies and would not sustain a favorable verdict. The affirmance therefore was a ruling on complaint sufficiency, not a judgment following a trial of the alleged wrongdoing. (80–81.)

Money had and received required an actual right or title to the property that the daughters lacked. Even an arguable interference tort would concern an expectancy, not an existing property entitlement to the sale proceeds. Holt's third-party-beneficiary count under the oral prenuptial arrangement was defeated on its face by the Statute of Frauds. Beneficiary status could not remove an infirmity in the underlying agreement. Her additional fraud and deceit counts restated the principal alleged interference and failed for the same reasons. The court affirmed the judgment without awarding either demanded amount. (81.)

## Assigned account and reading scope
The assigned example at printed 1196 accurately illustrates that a third-party beneficiary remains subject to a writing defense against the underlying agreement. Its narrow parenthetical does not convey the whole opinion: the two property disputes, proposed inheritance tort, stale-proof policies, alternate counts, and procedural history are separately explained here. Nor does that example itself establish that any alleged beneficiary agreement was valid before the writing defense arose.
