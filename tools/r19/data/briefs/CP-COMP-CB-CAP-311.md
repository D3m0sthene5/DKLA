# Moore v. New York Cotton Exchange

**270 U.S. 593 (1926). Decided April 12, 1926. No. 200.** Justice Sutherland delivered the Court’s opinion. No separate writing, judicial footnote, or numerical vote tally appears in the complete report. The caption identifies Moore as president of the Odd-Lot Cotton Exchange. Assigned account: Civil Procedure casebook, physical PDF page 382, printed page 731, Note 2 and its quotation about related counterclaims; the preceding Friendly concurrence on printed page 730 also identifies a question Moore left open.

## Facts and the two claims

Members of the Odd-Lot Cotton Exchange contracted for future delivery of cotton in lots of 10 to 100 bales. Members of the New York Cotton Exchange also contracted for future delivery, through open bidding during specified hours at its New York premises. The New York exchange collected the resulting price quotations and contracted with Western Union to distribute them only to recipients it approved. Western Union paid $27,500 annually for this business. Applicants agreed not to use the quotations in connection with a bucket shop or pass them to others. Western Union’s controlled subsidiary, Gold & Stock Telegraph Company, supplied ticker service. **270 U.S. at 601–602.**

Odd-Lot applied for service but was refused because the New York exchange withheld consent. The reported explanation was that an investigation linked Odd-Lot to a predecessor convicted of operating a bucket shop, with many of the same members, and that Odd-Lot had been organized as a cover for continuing that activity. That was the basis given for the refusal; the opinion’s disposition should not be recast as a new criminal conviction of Odd-Lot. **Id. at 602.**

Odd-Lot’s federal bill attacked the arrangement under the antitrust laws. It alleged interstate dealings and shipments, control over nationwide cotton price information, and restraint and monopolization. It sought cancellation of the Western Union contract, a declaration that the New York exchange was a monopoly, and mandatory relief compelling ticker installation and delivery of quotations. The exchange’s answer defended the refusal and counterclaimed that Odd-Lot nevertheless obtained the quotations unlawfully, directly or through another person, and distributed them to members who supplied bucket shops. The counterclaim sought an injunction against that practice. **Id. at 602–603.**

## Procedural history and competing positions

Both sides requested interlocutory injunctions. The district court denied Odd-Lot’s request and granted the defendants’ request. The Second Circuit affirmed both orders. By stipulation, it then directed entry of a final decree dismissing the bill and making the counterclaim injunction permanent. Because nothing remained below except ministerial compliance with that mandate, the Supreme Court treated the decree as final for appeal. **Id. at 603.**

The printed argument for Odd-Lot emphasized that distributing quotations across state lines could itself be interstate commerce, even if the underlying exchange transactions were local. It argued that Western Union had purchased the information and, as a public-service corporation, could not selectively refuse service. The exchange’s printed argument characterized Western Union as its distribution agent, stressed ownership of the quotations, and defended restrictions against bucket-shop use. These are separately reported advocates’ positions, not interchangeable statements of the Court’s conclusions. **Id. at 594–601.**

Odd-Lot also challenged the counterclaim on three grounds: dismissal of the original bill allegedly eliminated federal jurisdiction; the counterclaim did not arise from the transaction underlying the bill; and its allegations and proof did not support a permanent injunction. **Id. at 607–608.**

## Antitrust and unfair-competition rulings

The Court rejected the bill on the merits. It put aside the asserted unfair-competition claim because relief under the Federal Trade Commission Act had to begin before the Commission. It then concluded that the allegations did not establish violations of Sherman Act §§ 1 or 2 supporting relief under Clayton Act § 16. **Id. at 603–604.**

The Court described the exchange’s trading arrangements as local: contracts made on the premises required cotton represented by receipts from licensed New York-port warehouses and delivery there. Possible later interstate shipments did not convert those agreements into interstate transactions; the contracts did not obligate such shipments. The distribution contract also failed to establish the asserted antitrust violation. The exchange remained the real distributor of its collected information, while Western Union transmitted it to approved recipients as an agency. The annual payment bought the distribution business rather than transforming the company into an owner required to disseminate the information to everyone. **Id. at 604–605.**

A carrier’s obligation to carry messages did not authorize it to send a particular sender’s messages to recipients the sender had not designated. The exchange’s selective sale of its own news or property was treated as an ordinary private-business choice, and any effect on interstate commerce was indirect and incidental rather than a direct or unreasonable restraint or monopoly. The Court relied on its earlier protection of exchange quotations in Board of Trade v. Christie Grain & Stock Co. These are the historical antitrust and commerce conclusions in this case; the brief does not certify them as a complete statement of later antitrust law. **Id. at 605–607.**

## Jurisdiction survived the bill’s merits failure

The Court rejected Odd-Lot’s premise that the bill had been dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. A substantial, nonfrivolous federal statutory claim invokes the power to decide its legal sufficiency, even if it ultimately fails. Only a purported federal claim plainly lacking any color of merit would fail to invoke that power on the basis discussed here. Odd-Lot had made a serious, although insufficient, attempt to allege an antitrust violation. The lower mandate therefore dismissed its bill on the merits, not for want of federal jurisdiction. **Id. at 608–609.**

## Counterclaim: a flexible transaction

Historical Equity Rule 30 distinguished a counterclaim arising from the transaction that was the suit’s subject matter, which had to be stated, from an optional counterclaim that could be an independent equity suit. This counterclaim fell in the first category. The Court expressly left unresolved the independent-federal-jurisdiction question associated with the second category. Moore therefore should not be cited as resolving every jurisdictional issue involving permissive counterclaims. **Id. at 609.**

The connection here was logical, even though the allegations were not identical. Odd-Lot sought judicial compulsion to obtain quotations; the exchange defended its refusal and sought to stop Odd-Lot from obtaining them unlawfully despite that refusal. The refusal was an essential link in both controversies. Extra allegations about unlawful acquisition did not make the counterclaim unrelated. Requiring complete factual identity would drain the transaction-based rule of practical meaning because opposing claims rarely depend on precisely the same facts. **Id. at 609–610.**

The Court also linked the relief. Rejecting Odd-Lot’s claimed entitlement to compulsory access did not fully resolve the controversy while unlawful appropriation could continue. The injunction completed the relief against that related conduct. **Id. at 610.**

## Proof, judgment, and assigned discrepancy

Odd-Lot had stipulated that the affidavits supporting preliminary relief could be treated as testimony on the counterclaim and that the appellate court could direct a final decree. The Supreme Court agreed with the lower courts that the pleadings and affidavits supported making the injunction permanent. It **affirmed the decree**. **Id. at 610–611.**

The assigned note accurately highlights the flexible, logical-relationship understanding of transaction and the application of former Equity Rule 30. It says, however, that the defendant counterclaimed for damages. The actual judicial opinion describes a request for an injunction and affirms permanent injunctive relief, not a counterclaim damages award. The surrounding comparison to Gibbs and later counterclaim doctrine is editorial; those later authorities did not supply the governing rule in 1926. The preceding assigned Friendly concurrence’s statement that Moore left the permissive-counterclaim jurisdiction question open matches the reservation at official page 609.
