# Enbridge Energy, LP v. Nessel

**Citation and court:** 608 U.S. 67 (2026), Supreme Court of the United States, No. 24–783, decided April 22, 2026. Justice Sotomayor wrote for a unanimous Court; there are no separate opinions. The official preliminary report contains five substantive judicial footnotes, all considered here.

**Assigned reading and sources:** Civil Procedure Supplement, printed pages 519–520, physical PDF page 348: the complete captioned editorial account. The complete official report, pages 67–84, supplies the facts, reasoning, notes, and disposition omitted from that account. The supplement leaves the U.S. Reports page blank; the official preliminary print supplies page 67. Justia corroborates the date, docket, author, and affirmance. Its HTML note numbering is offset by an empty entry; the locators below use the official report.

**Procedurally material facts.** Enbridge operated Line 5, a 645-mile petroleum pipeline crossing Michigan and extending into Canada. Four miles crossed the Straits of Mackinac under a 1953 state easement. Michigan's Attorney General sued in state court on June 27, 2019, seeking to halt operations on the ground that the easement was void and continued operation violated state law. Enbridge received the complaint July 12, 2019. It did not remove within thirty days, instead briefing cross-motions for summary disposition and participating in oral argument. These are procedural facts; the Supreme Court did not decide whether the pipeline or easement was lawful. 608 U.S. at 72–73.

In November 2020, the Governor revoked the easement and filed a separate enforcement action. Enbridge timely removed that case, asserting substantial federal questions. The Attorney General's earlier case was held in abeyance by agreement. Canada submitted amicus filings about a bilateral treaty and foreign relations. After the federal district court denied remand in the Governor's case on November 16, 2021, the Governor voluntarily dismissed it. On December 15, Enbridge removed the Attorney General's case—887 days after service. The later jurisdictional ruling in the Governor's case did not itself excuse that delay. Id. at 73–74.

**Procedural history.** The Attorney General moved to remand as untimely. The district court denied remand on equitable grounds and certified its order under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b). The Sixth Circuit accepted interlocutory review and reversed: the thirty-day deadline was nonjurisdictional, but the statutory scheme precluded equitable tolling. The Supreme Court reviewed a circuit disagreement over tolling, not the merits of the underlying state action. Id. at 74–75. Judicial note 2 records another, distinct federal action in which Enbridge obtained a December 2025 preemption judgment against the Governor; that judgment is background, not this case's disposition. Id. at 74 n.2.

**Issue and holding.** May a court equitably toll § 1446(b)(1)'s thirty-day deadline for removal? No. Even assuming that the deadline is a statute of limitations presumptively subject to equitable tolling, its text, structure, and surrounding context rebut that presumption. Enbridge's removal was untimely. Id. at 75–84. The Court expressly leaves undecided whether this removal deadline qualifies as a statute of limitations to which the presumption initially attaches. Id. at 76, 83–84.

**Reasoning.** The deadline is nonjurisdictional: it does not speak in jurisdictional terms or demarcate judicial power. But nonjurisdictional does not mean freely extendable. A rule can be mandatory and unavailable for equitable tolling even though it lacks jurisdictional force. Id. at 75–76.

The requirement that notice “shall” be filed within thirty days, using the shorter of the specified periods, is consistent with a strict deadline. That wording alone is insufficient to rebut a tolling presumption. The decisive evidence is the detailed structure of statutory extensions and exceptions. Section 1446(b)(3) permits removal within thirty days after receipt of a later paper first revealing removability. Diversity removal under that provision generally has a one-year cap, with a specific bad-faith exception in § 1446(c)(1). The discovery-like extension and express equitable exception indicate that Congress accounted for specified equitable concerns without granting an unrestricted power to excuse late removal. Id. at 76–78.

Other provisions reinforce that conclusion. Section 1441(d) expressly permits enlargement for cause in foreign-state cases; § 1454(b)(2) permits enlargement for cause in certain intellectual-property cases; § 1441(e)(1) permits later removal with leave in specified fatal-accident cases. Criminal removal under § 1455(b)(1) has an express general good-cause extension. Those provisions modify the removal deadline expressly. Reading a universal equitable-tolling power into § 1446(b)(1) would undercut their distinctions. Id. at 78–79.

The scheme also favors resolving forum questions promptly. Short removal and remand deadlines, coupled with generally unreviewable remand orders, reduce prolonged threshold litigation. Broad tolling would leave the forum unsettled and risk substantial work in state court followed by belated transfer. Efficiency supplements the structural argument rather than replacing it. Id. at 79–80.

**Arguments rejected and boundaries preserved.** Enbridge demanded Congress's “clearest command” against tolling. The Court instead applied the usual inquiry into whether text, structure, and context show Congress did not want tolling. Holland's heightened phrase concerned displacement of traditional substantive habeas principles, not a universal tolling test. Id. at 80–81 & n.3. A deadline's short duration and focus on litigant conduct do not defeat the structural evidence; note 4 also rejects the proposed distinction from Brockamp's detailed timing scheme. Id. at 81 & n.4.

Calling § 1446(b)(3) an accrual rule does not account for the separate bad-faith exception or the extensions elsewhere. The inquiry considers the whole statutory scheme and the reasons for its exceptions, not merely their number. Section 1447(c)'s distinction between jurisdictional and other remand defects likewise does not imply tolling: a nonjurisdictional defect can have different waiver consequences while remaining subject to a mandatory untollable deadline. Id. at 81–83.

Most importantly, note 5 expressly reserves the applicability of waiver, forfeiture, and estoppel. The holding concerns equitable tolling; it does not announce that every conceivable objection to untimely removal must prevail regardless of those other doctrines. Id. at 83 n.5. Note 1 identifies the three Enbridge petitioners collectively; neither it nor the separate preemption litigation enlarges the tolling holding.

**Exact disposition.** The Supreme Court **affirmed the Sixth Circuit's judgment**. That judgment required remand of the Attorney General's action to Michigan state court because removal was untimely. 608 U.S. at 84. The Court did not itself adjudicate the validity of the easement, the pipeline's legality, or the treaty/preemption questions.

**Course significance.** Separate three inquiries: whether a deadline is jurisdictional, whether it allows equitable tolling, and whether other doctrines affect enforcement. Enbridge resolves the second despite answering the first in the negative and reserving the third. The assigned editorial account correctly emphasizes both the detailed exceptions and the express reservation in note 5.
