# Jorgensen v. Pressnall
274 Or. 285, 545 P.2d 1382 (1976) | Contracts

## Purchase, defects, and unsuccessful repairs
The Jorgensens bought a new mobile home from Pressnall, trading in their old one as the down payment and financing the balance. Pressnall assigned the financing contract to Commercial Credit and warranted that it was enforceable. He represented the new home as soundly constructed, of medium quality, and strong enough for frequent moves; he also promised prompt correction of factory defects. 274 Or. at 287.

The home arrived November 1, 1972. The buyers discovered water and air leaks, gaps in the hinged living-room extension, and defective doors, cabinets, vents, and walls. They promptly submitted a list and received renewed assurances of repairs. Repeated requests produced little beyond visits by unprepared workers. The buyers eventually referred the matter to counsel and declined Pressnall’s further efforts. Negotiations with the manufacturer then produced three repairmen, each working approximately ten hours. Some defects were corrected, but substantial leakage persisted and new problems appeared. The buyers rejected the quality of the repairs and refused a proposed release. Id. at 287–88 & n.1.

On December 27, the buyers directed counsel to notify both defendants of rescission, tender the home back subject to their security interest, and demand the down payment and consequential damages. On advice of counsel they nevertheless remained in the home until November 15, 1973. Commercial Credit repossessed it in January 1974 and resold at a loss. Id. at 288. Continued occupancy became a separate legal issue; it did not establish that the home met the promised quality.

## Claims and appellate arguments
The buyers sought rescission and monetary relief. Commercial Credit counterclaimed for repossession losses and sought recovery from Pressnall on his assignment warranty if rescission was granted. The trial court ruled for the buyers against Pressnall and for Commercial Credit on its cross-complaint against him. Pressnall appealed. Id. at 287.

He argued that there was no material misrepresentation inducing purchase, no material defect making the trailer unfit as a dwelling, no justified remedy after the buyers refused further repair efforts, and no valid revocation while they continued exercising ownership through use. The Supreme Court analyzed the rescission letter as revocation of acceptance under Oregon’s UCC, ORS 72.6080, rather than requiring proof of a separate misrepresentation theory. Id. at 288–89 & n.2.

## Revocation and substantial impairment
The statute permitted revocation when a nonconformity substantially impaired the goods’ value to the particular buyer and the buyer had accepted on a reasonable assumption of cure that was not seasonably fulfilled, or accepted without discovering the problem because of difficulty of discovery or seller assurances. Notice, reasonable timing, and the absence of disqualifying substantial changes in condition were also statutory requirements. The operative dispute here was substantial impairment coupled with failure to cure seasonably. Id. at 288–89.

The court used a two-stage inquiry. First, the value of conforming goods must be understood in relation to the actual buyers’ needs and circumstances, rather than an abstract average purchaser. Second, evidence must establish that the nonconformity substantially impaired that value; the buyers’ unsupported statement of dissatisfaction is insufficient. The inquiry is subjective in identifying the affected buyer’s needs and objective in requiring factual proof that those needs were materially defeated. Id. at 289–90 & nn.3–5.

Here the home was purchased as a residence. Defects and persistent failure to repair deprived the buyers of a comfortable home for a substantial period. A potentially small repair bill did not defeat substantial impairment to them, because cost and disruption are not interchangeable measures. Likewise, substantial impairment did not require complete uselessness: buyers could still reside in a home whose value as their residence was substantially impaired. Id. at 290–91 & nn.6–7. The opinion’s later description of the test as subjective refers to value to these purchasers; it does not eliminate the earlier requirement of objective evidentiary support.

## Opportunity to cure and continued possession
Pressnall had ample opportunity to repair before revocation and did not act seasonably. The seller had no unlimited cure period, so refusing still more attempts did not retrospectively excuse his failure. Id. at 291 & n.8.

The continued occupancy also did not constitute a new acceptance inconsistent with the seller’s ownership. The buyers retained a statutory security interest for payments and qualifying expenses under ORS 72.7110(3). Rules governing collateral entitled them to hold the home and use it to preserve its value while exercising reasonable care. Occupancy was the most feasible protection against water damage; covered storage would have been costly. The trial court credited the seller with the rental value of the buyers’ use, preventing loss from that occupancy. Id. at 291–92 & nn.9–12. The conclusion depends on preservation of collateral and the offset, rather than granting every revoking buyer unrestricted beneficial use indefinitely.

## Judgment and financing consequence
The Supreme Court affirmed the decree. Successful revocation meant the assigned financing contract was not enforceable as warranted, so Pressnall also owed Commercial Credit the damages awarded on its warranty claim. Id. at 292.
