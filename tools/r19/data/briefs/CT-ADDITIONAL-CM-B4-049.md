# Belk v. Le Chaperon Rouge Co.
No. 1:18cv1954, 2020 WL 3642880 (N.D. Ohio July 6, 2020) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Judge Pamela A. Barker issued this memorandum opinion and order on July 6, 2020, as docket document 90 in the Northern District of Ohio. She granted in part and denied in part employees' motion to enforce a wage-and-hour settlement. The March 12 oral agreement bound both the company and Stella Moga-Kennedy individually, and pandemic-related financial hardship did not establish impossibility. The court also certified the Rule 23 class. It nevertheless withheld approval of the collective and class settlements, notice, and a fairness hearing until the parties corrected inconsistent financial provisions. This was neither a trial determination that the underlying wage allegations were true nor final approval of every settlement term. (Official opinion pp. 1, 22–23, 31–33.)

## Wage litigation and the agreement
Brianna Belk and Tiffany Morris sued August 27, 2018, alleging unpaid work before and after scheduled shifts at a childcare center. They alleged that Le Chaperon Rouge and its owner and operational manager Moga-Kennedy were joint employers liable under the FLSA and Ohio law. The court conditionally certified an FLSA collective action in February 2019; thirty-five workers opted in. The January 31, 2020 amended complaint also alleged uncompensated mandatory health-certification training and asserted federal wage claims and Ohio overtime, minimum-wage, prompt-pay, and recordkeeping claims. These descriptions were allegations, not adjudicated violations. (Id. pp. 1–3 & n.1.)

Mediation on February 28, 2020 did not resolve the case. Continued proceedings on March 12 produced a settlement whose material terms were recited on the record before Magistrate Judge Jonathan Greenberg. Defendants would pay $200,000 forty days after court approval. The terms included releases, proposed service awards, a joint approval motion and notice by April 10, individual checks and tax forms following final approval, and retained enforcement jurisdiction. Plaintiffs circulated a written draft March 19 and supporting materials March 23. Defendants did not execute the draft. On March 31 counsel cited shutdown and financial repercussions, and the approval deadline passed. Plaintiffs moved April 11 to enforce the agreement and obtain class certification and settlement approvals. (Id. pp. 3–6.)

The timing of the agreement mattered. Ohio declared an emergency March 9; the governor urged remote college instruction and avoidance of indoor sporting events the next day. The WHO declared a pandemic March 11. On March 12 Ohio ordered kindergarten-through-twelfth-grade schools closed and authorized emergency childcare rules. Later measures imposed a stay-at-home order and required childcare centers to close March 25 unless licensed for temporary pandemic care. The complete opinion includes that licensing exception, rather than describing the order as an unconditional closure of every childcare facility. (Id. pp. 3–4 & n.2, 20–21 & n.6.)

## Oral assent and individual liability
Ohio contract law governed by agreement of the parties. Enforcing a settlement required assent to all material terms, assessed objectively. The absence of a completed written memorial did not diminish an already binding oral agreement when only memorialization remained. Moga-Kennedy acknowledged her potential joint-employer liability under the FLSA but argued that this did not establish her separate assent to guarantee the corporate settlement. Her declaration said she had understood the agreement as binding only the company. (Id. pp. 7–10 & n.3.)

The court did not infer contractual liability solely from possible statutory liability. The recorded terms repeatedly referred to the defendants without separating them. Counsel represented both, and Moga-Kennedy personally confirmed that she heard, understood, and agreed to the material terms. Neither she nor counsel added a limitation despite repeated opportunities. The later draft expressly named both defendants, required their payment, and provided two signature lines. Their initial objection concerned financial consequences; the claim of no individual undertaking first appeared at the April 27 status conference, not in their initial opposition brief. (Id. pp. 10–13 & n.4.)

Against Ohio's presumption concerning multiple promisors, the court regarded joint and several settlement liability as the only reasonable reading of the objective assent. Moga-Kennedy's asserted private understanding could not make the recorded agreement ambiguous. A New York wage-settlement decision provided an analogy, not the governing state law. Because the communications' contents were undisputed and construction was a legal issue, a further evidentiary hearing would not aid resolution. (Id. pp. 13–17.)

## Financial hardship and impossibility
Defendants described a previously healthy enterprise with eleven childcare centers, a private school, 232 full-time employees, and approximately 1,174 enrolled children. Moga-Kennedy declared that shutdown, layoffs, sharply reduced attendance, and reopening limits made payment impossible. She estimated a new maximum enrollment of 534, asserted inadequate corporate cash and heavily mortgaged property, and described unsuccessful borrowing and tax-related debt. Those were defendants' submissions; the court did not find every asserted financial conclusion established. Defendants deliberately omitted detailed evidence of her personal ability to fund the settlement because they denied that she had an individual obligation. Plaintiffs responded that financial ability was normally an assumed contractual risk and pointed to assets and rental income. (Id. pp. 17–19 & n.5.)

Under the Ohio authorities applied, impossibility was an affirmative defense defendants had to prove: an unforeseen postcontract event must make performance impossible without their fault, with difficulties not reasonably foreseeable. Difficulty, danger, or burden alone was insufficient. The court found the defense unavailable here for two independent reasons. First, by March 12 the emergency measures and developments made significant business disruption reasonably foreseeable. Defendants acknowledged knowing that schools had just been closed, directly affecting their private school and signaling possible effects on childcare operations. The court did not require foresight of each precise later restriction. It noted that defendants could have deferred settlement, negotiated force majeure protection, or addressed the emerging financial risk, but instead assented to payment. (Id. pp. 19–21 & nn.6–7.)

Second, even assuming the impact was unforeseeable, defendants did not prove that Moga-Kennedy personally could not pay. Despite an opportunity, they supplied no sworn declaration or documentation establishing that inability. References to six homes, rental property, and other income sources suggested that corporate hardship did not resolve individual ability, but the court did not quantify a finding of her net wealth. It declined another hearing to give her yet another opportunity to provide the omitted evidence. Both defendants remained bound. (Id. pp. 21–22 & n.8.)

## Class certification and why approval was deferred
Certification covered teachers subject to the challenged timekeeping and health-training practices, with reduced-hours and course-attendees subclasses. A roster of 746 staff supported numerosity. The court found common questions about compensable time, typical representative claims, shared interests and experienced counsel, and sufficiently definite class descriptions. Common policy questions predominated over individualized hours, and small individual claims and efficient resolution in one forum supported superiority. Defendants did not contest certification. The court applied Rule 23(a) and (b)(3), rather than treating the settlement itself as dispensing with those prerequisites. (Id. pp. 23–31 & nn.9–12.)

Approval was a different matter. The draft agreement allocated one-third of net proceeds to named plaintiffs and opt-ins and two-thirds to other class members, while the proposed joint motion used fifteen and eighty-five percent. The agreement provided $66,000 in fees and $9,630.10 in costs, whereas the motion used $65,000 and $11,030.10. The proposed order followed the one-third allocation but omitted the fee and cost amounts. The court therefore denied the approval, notice, and hearing requests at that time and ordered revised documents within thirty days. Enforcement of the agreed obligation did not constitute approval of these inconsistent submissions. (Id. pp. 31–33.)
