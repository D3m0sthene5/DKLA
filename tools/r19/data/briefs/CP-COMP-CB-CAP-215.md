# Burlington Northern Railroad Co. v. Woods

**480 U.S. 1 (1987). Supreme Court of the United States, February 24, 1987.** Justice Marshall delivered the unanimous opinion; no separate writing is reported. Assigned account: CivPro PDF 262, printed 486–487, Note 3 following Walker.

## How an unsuccessful appeal produced a second monetary award

Alan and Cara Woods brought an Alabama state-court tort action for injuries in a motorcycle accident. Burlington Northern removed the suit to federal court on diversity grounds. The jury awarded Alan $300,000 and Cara $5,000. Burlington posted bond to stay the judgment during its appeal. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed without modification. The Woodses then requested Alabama’s mandatory affirmance penalty: an additional ten percent of the judgment. The court granted their motion summarily and denied rehearing. The parties stipulated that the original judgment had been paid; Burlington withheld only the $30,500 penalty while seeking Supreme Court review. (2–3.)

Burlington challenged the penalty both constitutionally, under equal protection and due process, and under the Erie line of decisions governing federal diversity practice. The Supreme Court held the state penalty inapplicable because it conflicted with a valid federal appellate rule. It therefore did not reach the Fourteenth Amendment objections. The opinion decided the additional appellate award, not whether the underlying accident verdict was correct. (3, 8.)

## The state statute and federal rule

Alabama’s statute automatically imposed the penalty when three conditions were met: a money judgment, a stay obtained through the required bond, and affirmance without substantial modification. Its stated purposes included deterring frivolous or delaying appeals and compensating the successful appellee for defending the judgment. But its trigger did not depend on a finding that a particular appeal was frivolous. The assignment’s description of a penalty for all unsuccessful money-judgment appeals must be qualified by these statutory conditions, especially the stay bond. (3–4 & nn.1–2.)

Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 38, as quoted in the opinion, allowed a court finding an appeal frivolous to award just damages and single or double costs. It thus left both the decision to penalize and the amount to judicial discretion. Burlington argued that an automatic state surcharge contradicted that choice. The Woodses answered that Alabama itself had an appellate rule resembling Rule 38 operating alongside the statute, so federal courts could likewise impose the state penalty and retain federal discretionary authority. (4, 7–8.)

## The governing Hanna inquiry

The Court first asked whether the fairly construed federal rule was broad enough to collide directly with state law or control the issue, leaving no room for the state rule. If it was, the federal rule governed so long as it represented a valid exercise of constitutional rulemaking authority and complied with the Rules Enabling Act. This approach applied to federal appellate rules as well as civil rules because both were prescribed through that authority. It did not begin by mechanically labeling the Alabama penalty substantive or procedural. (4–5 & n.3.)

The constitutional inquiry permits rules addressing matters reasonably classified as procedural, including matters in an uncertain area between substance and procedure. The statutory inquiry adds that a rule cannot abridge, enlarge, or modify substantive rights. Incidental effects on substantive interests do not necessarily violate that prohibition when reasonably necessary to preserve the integrity of the federal procedural system. The institutional study, approval, and congressional-review process also gives federal rules presumptive validity. That presumption accompanies, rather than replaces, the actual validity inquiry. (5–6.)

## Why the provisions could not operate together

Rule 38’s discretionary operation unmistakably conflicted with the statute’s mandatory operation. Both addressed compensation and deterrence arising from unsuccessful appellate litigation, sufficiently overlapping that the federal rule occupied the relevant field. A federal court might find some justification for damages but conclude that an amount below ten percent was just. Applying Alabama’s automatic minimum would then prevent the court from exercising the discretion the federal rule granted. Allowing a further discretionary award on top of the mandatory penalty would not restore discretion to impose less than the state minimum. (7–8.)

The coexistence of Alabama’s own rule and statute did not define the scope of a federal court’s discretion under the federal rule. The Court found persuasive a Fifth Circuit decision rejecting a similar Mississippi surcharge. Footnote 4 traced earlier inconsistent circuit decisions, including the apparent basis for the Eleventh Circuit’s penalty here, rather than treating the result below as an unexplained exercise of federal Rule 38 discretion. (6 n.4; 7–8.)

Footnote 5 supplied related support: federal postjudgment-interest provisions already compensated the lost use of judgment money, and federal stay provisions did not condition a stay on an automatic additional damage award for an unsuccessful appeal. These observations reinforced the conflict analysis; the Court’s principal holding rested on Rule 38 and its valid discretionary procedure. (7 n.5.)

## Validity and the judgment

Rule 38 regulated matters reasonably classified as procedural and therefore satisfied the constitutional standard. Its choice of case-by-case enforcement affected the process of enforcing rights rather than the rights themselves, satisfying the Rules Enabling Act. Because the applicable federal rule was valid and conflicted with the Alabama provision, the state mandatory affirmance penalty had no application to judgments entered by federal courts sitting in diversity. (8.)

The Supreme Court **reversed**. It did not invalidate Alabama’s penalty as a rule for its own courts, order a new accident trial, decide whether the appeal was frivolous, or resolve the constitutional challenges. The lesson is concrete: a state mandatory minimum can conflict with a federal discretionary rule even if both pursue similar objectives and both could arithmetically be added to the same judgment.
