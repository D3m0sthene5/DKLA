# Trump v. New York
592 U.S. 125, 141 S. Ct. 530 (2020), decided December 18, 2020 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Census process and challenged policy

The decennial census provides the population figures used to apportion House seats and also supplies information for federal funding, state redistricting, and other purposes. Congress assigned successive responsibilities to the Secretary of Commerce and the President. Under 13 U.S.C. § 141, the Secretary conducted the census and reported the population tabulation by states required for apportionment. Under 2 U.S.C. § 2a(a), the President transmitted to Congress a statement showing the whole number of persons in each state, excluding Indians not taxed, and applied the method of equal proportions to calculate each state’s House seats. (129.)

In July 2020, President Trump announced a policy to exclude from the apportionment base aliens not in lawful immigration status. His memorandum directed the Secretary to provide additional information beyond the Census Bureau’s ordinary resident counts so that the President could implement that policy. But it qualified both collection and implementation by feasibility, practicability, and delegated discretion. States, local governments, organizations, and individuals challenged the memorandum. (129–130.)

A three-judge Southern District of New York court found standing because the memorandum discouraged census participation, undermined data used for funding, and required some plaintiffs to divert resources to counter that chilling effect. It held that § 141(b) did not authorize the Secretary to submit an additional apportionment count derived from administrative records outside the census and that excluding residents solely by immigration status violated § 2a(a)’s whole-number requirement. It declared the memorandum unlawful and enjoined the Secretary from including information needed to implement it in the statutory report to the President. The Government appealed directly, and the Supreme Court postponed consideration of jurisdiction. (130.)

## The live controversy on appeal

By the time of Supreme Court review, the census response period had ended. Plaintiffs conceded that the chilling effect supporting the district court’s standing determination had dissipated. They instead relied on threatened reductions in representation and federal funding from an unlawful apportionment. A live Article III controversy had to persist throughout litigation; demonstrating an earlier injury did not establish jurisdiction over a different, prospective injury at the appellate stage. (131.)

The per curiam opinion connected two constitutional justiciability doctrines. Standing required a concrete, particularized, imminent injury rather than a conjectural one. Ripeness prevented adjudication that depended on contingent future events that might not happen as predicted or happen at all. Here, the administration’s announced desire to exclude people did not yet establish how many it could or would exclude, or what particular plaintiffs would consequently lose. (131–132.)

## Why the Court found adjudication premature

The Court emphasized the gap between an announced policy and a sufficiently predictable injury. The Secretary’s report and the President’s apportionment statement were still being prepared. Changes had to comply with the actual-enumeration requirement rather than rest on conjectural estimates. The record did not establish which aliens had suitable administrative records, whether the Bureau could match those records to census responses in time, or which groups the President would ultimately direct it to exclude. Plaintiffs acknowledged that the dispute would become more concrete once the Secretary reported. (132.)

Although a substantial risk can support future-injury standing, the majority found this record too uncertain. Everyone agreed that excluding the entire estimated 10.5 million undocumented population was infeasible. Yet the evidence predicting changed House apportionment assumed complete exclusion. The record did not show the consequences of partial implementation, and the majority rejected the dissent’s inference that excluding ICE detainees would affect interstate apportionment. It distinguished earlier pre-apportionment challenges in which concrete census procedures predictably altered the count. Here the census count was already complete, and the challenged apportionment process remained preliminary. (132–133.)

The funding theory had an additional uncertainty. The Government maintained that federal funds followed census-derived data, not necessarily the modified apportionment numbers contemplated by the memorandum. Whether the Secretary or President would use the latter for downstream funding was unresolved. The Court did not settle the correct reading of every funding statute; it treated that uncertainty as another obstacle to establishing a presently justiciable injury. (133.)

The district court’s remedy reinforced this understanding. It prohibited transmitting information that might enable a future exclusion. In the majority’s view, the injury-producing act would be the eventual exclusion of particular people, rather than the memorandum considered abstractly. The Court also noted an implication for the President’s Opinions Clause authority without adjudicating a separate constitutional validity question. Allowing executive decisionmaking to run its course would define the dispute more manageably. The memorandum did not presently require plaintiffs to act or refrain from acting. (133–134.)

## Judgment and scope

The Court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction. It expressly declined to decide the merits of the constitutional or statutory challenges. This was a timing and Article III ruling, not approval of exclusion by immigration status, a determination that all census challenges must wait until apportionment is final, or a finding that states categorically lacked census-related standing. (134.)

The per curiam judgment was 6–3. Justice Breyer dissented, joined by Justices Sotomayor and Kagan; there were no other separate writings. Neither judicial writing contains numbered footnotes. The report’s asterisk lists advocates and amici and is prefatory material rather than a judicial rule.

## Breyer’s dissent

The dissent saw a substantial, imminent risk rather than conjecture. The Government had not disclaimed the memorandum’s purpose of reducing representation for states with many undocumented residents. It was actively trying to implement that policy and had acknowledged feasible exclusions, including tens of thousands of ICE detainees and potentially much larger identifiable groups. Breyer concluded that inability to execute the policy completely did not erase the substantial risk that partial execution would harm representation or funding. He cited statutes connecting federal allocations to census population totals and rejected speculation that excluded residents might later be added back for funding purposes. (134–141.)

Waiting also carried practical costs. Apportionment and state redistricting operated under deadlines; the Government had sought speed in related census litigation on that basis. The dissent found the legal issue fit for decision and thought postponement risked avoidable delays. It would have reached the merits and affirmed on statutory grounds, avoiding resolution of the constitutional question. (141–144.)

On those merits, Breyer read the governing statutes to count persons according to usual residence, not lawful immigration status. “Persons” included undocumented aliens; the specific statutory exclusion for Indians not taxed showed Congress knew how to exclude a resident category. Historical censuses, the 1929 Act’s debates rejecting proposals to exclude aliens, subsequent practice across all three branches, and the Bureau’s own 2020 residency rules supported that reading. (144–149.)

He rejected the Government’s reliance on founding-era inhabitancy concepts and Vattel. Residence standards could apply to immigrants without using immigration status as a proxy; an international-law treatise did not override the specific domestic apportionment history. More fundamentally, the immediate question was the meaning of the 1929 statute against its established background. Executive discretion to resolve residence questions—such as where prisoners resided—did not authorize departure from the statutory resident-counting command. Any overlap between undocumented aliens and persons ordinarily excluded for some other reason did not validate a policy excluding the class solely for immigration status. Breyer would have held the announced policy unlawful and affirmed. These were dissenting conclusions, not holdings of the Court. (149–153.)
