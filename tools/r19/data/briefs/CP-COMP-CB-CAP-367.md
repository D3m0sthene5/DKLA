# O’Gara v. Equifax Information Services, LLC

**No. 1:16–cv–01237–TWP–MPB, Doc. 81 (S.D. Ind. January 23, 2018)**

**Civil Procedure — relevance, proportionality, particularized discovery objections, and discoverability versus admissibility.** Magistrate Judge Matthew P. Brookman. Pinpoints below use the original order’s numbered pages, not unverified Westlaw star-page conversions.

## Alleged credit-file error and the discovery dispute

Charles Michael O’Gara III alleged that Equifax wrongly attributed his father’s property-tax liability to him. When eighteen and living with his father, he received documents indicating responsibility for unpaid property taxes; he later learned that the taxes belonged to Charles Michael O’Gara II. He alleged credit denials between 2007 and 2015. Equifax removed the lien after his first dispute in 2016. According to its response reproduced in the order, the lien first appeared in June 2007 and was removed on March 4, 2016; father and son shared the relevant first name, last name, and address. These descriptions supported a disputed Fair Credit Reporting Act claim, not a final adjudication that Equifax had violated the Act. (1–2, 11–12.)

O’Gara challenged Equifax’s matching procedures, alleging that public records had been matched through names without adequately using unique identifiers such as Social Security numbers or birth dates. He asserted violations of § 1681e(b), concerning reasonable procedures for maximum possible accuracy, and § 1681g, concerning disclosure of information in a consumer’s file. He alleged willful or negligent violations and sought statutory and punitive damages, costs, and attorney fees. The January 2018 order resolved his motion to compel fuller interrogatory and document-production responses. (1–3.)

The parties had met repeatedly over discovery and Equifax had supplemented some responses. O’Gara still sought information concerning matching procedures, his own mixed file, past mixed-file disputes and litigation, accuracy studies, and a New York Attorney General investigation and settlement. Equifax principally objected that the requested information was irrelevant or disproportionate. The court granted some requests, denied others, and imposed defined limits rather than accepting or rejecting the discovery program wholesale. (3–4, 24.)

## Governing approach and result

The court applied Rule 26(b)(1)’s requirements of nonprivileged relevance to a claim or defense and proportionality to the case. It identified the specified considerations: importance of the issues, amount in controversy, relative access, resources, importance of discovery to resolving the dispute, and burden or expense compared with likely benefit. Information within that scope need not itself be admissible. Rule 37 supplied the procedure for compelling discovery. The movant initially had to establish relevance; the opposing party then had to justify why the particular request was improper. (3–4.)

**The motion was granted in part and denied in part.** Equifax had to supplement the responses ordered in the decision within fourteen days. Each party bore its own costs concerning the discovery dispute. The order did not decide liability or award FCRA damages. Its principal lesson for the assignment is that evidence of similar mixed-file incidents can bear on knowledge and the reasonableness of procedures in a particular consumer’s case, but relevance does not dispense with time limits, proper subject scope, and a supported proportionality inquiry. (10–16, 24.)

## Definitions and procedural information

O’Gara’s interrogatories referred to a mixed file “as defined herein” without actually providing the promised definition. The court treated Equifax’s ambiguity objection as understandable in that context and supplied definitions: a mixed file means credit data not matched to the correct consumer; a confirmed mixed file involves a consumer dispute followed by confirmation that information was mixed with another consumer’s. Equifax had to supplement any affected answer under those definitions. The court did not treat the terminology dispute as permission to avoid otherwise relevant discovery. (5.)

Several interrogatories seeking detailed descriptions of Equifax’s procedures were already sufficiently answered, especially in light of produced manuals. O’Gara did not identify specific shortcomings. Those requests were denied except for supplementation made necessary by the definitions. Broad requests for every accuracy study or report had different defects: one reached beyond matching inaccuracies in public records and lacked time limits; another narrowed the subject to mixed files but still sought materials from all time. The court denied both. Requests concerning § 1681i reinvestigations failed because O’Gara had not asserted such a claim or explained their relevance to his actual claims. (5–8.)

The order also distinguished initial disclosures from discovery about information systems. Rule 26(a)(1) did not require a general account of every ESI storage system merely because it required identifying supporting documents or ESI. But a properly framed production request could seek documents identifying systems as necessary to plan meaningful electronic discovery. Here subparts identifying consumer-information and email systems were granted, with supplementation if necessary; bankruptcy-specific systems were not relevant to the alleged tax-lien problem. This was a tailored discovery ruling, not a blanket initial-disclosure mandate for all technical infrastructure. (8–9.)

## The particular error and knowledge of similar incidents

An interrogatory asking about O’Gara’s own file was obviously relevant. Equifax had provided some dates and the lien description but omitted several requested details: information used to determine mixing, the full identity and file identifier of the other consumer, why the mixing occurred, who corrected it, and the information used in correction. The court ordered supplementation. In contrast, another interrogatory asking how Equifax categorized types of mixed files was denied because O’Gara did not explain its relevance and produced manuals were substantially responsive. (11–13.)

Requests for five years of confirmed mixed-file counts, mixed-file lawsuits, and consumer disputes were relevant to whether Equifax knew of recurring problems and nevertheless failed to use reasonable procedures. Evidence need not concern only O’Gara’s specific event. Similar complaints could lead to evidence of negligence or willfulness even if the complaints themselves would not be admissible. The court therefore rejected Equifax’s contention that other files had nothing to do with this individual lien. (10–11, 13–15.)

That relevance conclusion was followed by a separate proportionality assessment. Both sides cited verdicts in other FCRA litigation, but those cases involved materially different facts and did not establish the amount at stake here. Their comparison gave little assistance to the multifactor inquiry. Equifax mostly recited the factors without specific evidence or argument showing disproportionate burden. The court found that insufficient and ordered responses to the five-year interrogatories. Similarly, generalized objections did not establish that the requested five-year internal mixed-file reports were unduly burdensome. The decision required a concrete showing, not a ritual list of Rule 26 terms. (10–11, 15–16.)

## The New York investigation and subsequent changes

The New York Attorney General’s investigation led to a 2015 agreement concerning credit-reporting practices. A 2017 public statement described enhanced public-record matching standards, to be implemented later that year. O’Gara contended that the investigation concerned the same algorithms and shed light on Equifax’s awareness of defects and its reasonableness defense. Equifax responded that the later changes were irrelevant because his lien had already been removed and only a small number of files were mixed. The court found a relevant connection to the alleged public-record matching problem. The low overall incidence did not make the inquiry irrelevant; the announced standards would also affect a substantial share of tax-lien data. (16–18, 21–22.)

The court did not order every document concerning the investigation. It adopted O’Gara’s agreement to narrow discovery to responsive information concerning mixed files with his particular public-record problem. It also found that two interrogatories had already been fully answered, including whether the lien would have appeared under the enhanced standards. It compelled the remaining interrogatories about the reasons for and intended effects of the standards and related document requests, within the narrower scope. (18–23.)

Equifax’s subsequent-remedial-measure objection presented two distinct grounds for rejection. It first raised the objection in supplemental responses about ninety days after its original answers, rather than timely specifying it in the initial responses. The court held it waived **for discoverability**. Even without waiver, whether Evidence Rule 407 would exclude the material at trial was a separate question; relevant discovery need not be admissible. The court did not hold that the remedial evidence was necessarily admissible at trial or that every untimely objection in every case must be rejected irrespective of good cause. Footnote 3 also required supplementation where an otherwise ordered response had withheld responsive information on the rejected investigation-irrelevance ground. (22–23 and n.3.)

## Fees, judge, and assignment accuracy

Although Rule 37(a)(5) permits expense awards, the mixed outcome allowed the court discretion. No discovery from the earlier disqualification phase had been compelled here; Equifax had participated in conferences and provided supplemental answers, and some requests were inapplicable or poorly drafted. Each party therefore bore its own discovery-dispute costs. Magistrate Judge Matthew P. Brookman signed and dated the order January 23, 2018. There is no separate opinion, numerical vote, or appendix. All three footnotes were read, including the overlap of requested topics, uncertainty about the mortgage-denial date, and additional investigation-related supplementation. (4 n.1; 17 n.2; 23 n.3; 24–25.)
