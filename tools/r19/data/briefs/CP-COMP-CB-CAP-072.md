# Wuchter v. Pizzutti

276 U.S. 13 (1928) | Civil Procedure | Supreme Court of the United States

## Identity and assignment

No. 142, argued January 5 and decided February 20, 1928. Chief Justice Taft delivered the Court's opinion. Brandeis, joined by Holmes, dissented; Stone wrote a separate dissent with a different proposed disposition. The supplied CivPro assignment develops Wuchter in Mullane's Note 2, PDF page 148, printed page 245. The complete report supplies the statutory distinctions and separate writings omitted from that capsule.

## Parties, facts, and relief

Pizzutti drove a horse-drawn wagon on a New Jersey public highway. Wuchter, a Pennsylvania resident driving behind him, collided with the wagon, injuring Pizzutti and his horses and damaging the wagon. Pizzutti sued in the Supreme Court of New Jersey for personal injury and property damage. The case concerned service and the validity of the resulting judgment, rather than an appellate determination of the damages amount. (13, 15–16.)

Process was left with New Jersey's Secretary of State under chapter 232 of the 1924 laws. Wuchter did not defend, and an interlocutory judgment and writ of inquiry of damages followed. Notice of the proposed execution of that writ was actually served on him in Pennsylvania, although the challenged statute did not require it. He still did not appear, and a final judgment was entered. Wuchter appealed on constitutional grounds. The Court of Errors and Appeals affirmed, 103 N.J.L. 130; the U.S. Supreme Court reviewed that judgment by writ of error. (13, 15–16.)

## Statutory framework

Chapter 232 treated an unlicensed nonresident's operation of an automobile on New Jersey highways as appointment of the Secretary of State to accept process in suits by residents arising from an accident involving that vehicle. Other sections provided for obtaining a written appointment from an apprehended operator or owner and for certain service on persons controlling the vehicle. The service relied upon here was the first section's service on the Secretary. (16–17.)

A separate licensing law, chapter 211, required an agreement accepting official service and expressly required the Commissioner of Motor Vehicles to send notification to the registered address. The Court emphasized that this licensing law was different from chapter 232. The existence of its notice requirement did not supply a corresponding requirement for the service used in this case. (17–18.)

## Issue, holding, and judgment

Does a nonresident-motorist statute satisfy due process when it authorizes service on a state official without requiring communication reasonably likely to inform the defendant? Can actual notice, not required by the challenged statute, cure that defect?

No. The statutory scheme must provide a reasonable probability of communication to the nonresident. Under the majority's construction, neither chapter 232 nor another applicable law required that communication. Actual out-of-state notice not directed by the statute could not validate the statute or service under it, where Wuchter had not appeared. The reported disposition is “Judgment reversed.” It does not include Stone's expressly qualified remand as the majority's final command. (18–20, 24–25.)

## Majority reasoning

The Court accepted state authority to regulate use of highways by residents and nonresidents. Kane had approved required appointment of an official under a licensing system, and Hess had approved treating highway use as equivalent to appointment without an express appointment. Wuchter did not reject those forms of substituted service. It required safeguards making communication of the suit reasonably probable. (18–19.)

Service on the official alone could permit a plaintiff to obtain default judgment against an uninformed person who had never entered the state or caused the accident. Those were examples illustrating the statutory risk, not findings of fraud by Pizzutti. Requiring the plaintiff, official, or another person to communicate by mail or otherwise would address that defect. The statute should require the plaintiff to supply an address or residence and impose a duty to communicate. A claimant must investigate the wrongdoer's identity; the inability to identify a person does not justify a judgment against an unnotified person selected without adequate basis. (19–20.)

The Court distinguished corporate arrangements that might involve repeated inquiry to an appointed agent or public office. It would be unreasonable to expect an individual motorist to make constant inquiries to a state's Secretary of State. The majority also examined corporate-service authorities, finding support for schemes likely to transmit notice and against schemes lacking that probability. These authorities supported the same practical requirement, rather than dispensing with notice whenever the recipient was an official. (20–24.)

Finally, the majority treated reasonable statutory provision and notice volunteered in a particular case as different things. Although Wuchter had actual out-of-state notice, it was not required by the statute and he did not appear. For the majority, that did not supply the constitutional safeguard missing from the governing law. (24–25.)

## Brandeis and Holmes

Brandeis agreed that the majority's general rule was sound but would affirm. The objection concerning the statute's missing notification requirement had not been made or considered below and was absent from the assignments of error. The earlier objection concerned lack of personal service within New Jersey without an actual appointment of the Secretary. Hess had since rejected the broader premise, and Brandeis regarded the newly asserted notification objection as an afterthought. A general due process objection did not preserve every later theory. (25–26.)

He also questioned the Supreme Court's initial construction of state law. New Jersey's highest court might interpret the law to require notice, potentially informed by the Secretary's administrative practice and constitutional avoidance. If reversal were ordered, Brandeis preferred a remand specifically allowing that court to address the new objection. His suggestion about possible uniform practice was conditional; it was not a factual finding that the statute already imposed a duty. (26–27.)

Brandeis additionally stressed the admission that Wuchter had actual knowledge and an opportunity to defend. He questioned how allowing this unprejudiced defendant to disregard notice and later invoke a drafting defect reconciled with other authorities, while declining to resolve that doctrinal reconciliation because his procedural reasons sufficed. (27–28.)

## Stone

Stone's short dissent agreed that the judgment should be reversed and remanded, with leave for the state court to determine whether the Secretary's actual notification was required by the statute. His position differed from Brandeis's preferred affirmance and from the majority's conclusive treatment of the statutory defect. (28.)
