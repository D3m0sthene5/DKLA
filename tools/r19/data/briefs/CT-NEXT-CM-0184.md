# Sisney v. State
2008 S.D. 71, 754 N.W.2d 639 (S.D. July 23, 2008) | Contracts

## Facts alleged and contract
Sisney, a Jewish inmate following a kosher diet at the South Dakota State Penitentiary, sued the State, prison-operations director Weber, and food-service contractor CBM. A 2002 contract required CBM to provide services to the State at correctional facilities in a manner meeting residents', inmates', and staff needs. It specified a daily caloric base stated in the opinion as 2,700 to 2,500 calories, and substitutions accommodating religious food avoidance. When CBM changed the kosher food in April 2007, Sisney claimed it supplied 400–500 calories less than the contractual minimum and did not satisfy his religious requirements. Weber rejected the grievance, explaining that Sisney's study understated the actual calories. These were pleadings and disputed grievance positions, not adjudicated findings that the meals were deficient. (¶¶ 2–4.)

## Proceedings and claims
Sisney sought contract damages as a third-party beneficiary and asserted that defendants conspired to permit a profitable breach because of his religious beliefs, invoking 42 U.S.C. §§ 1981 and 1985. Defendants moved under South Dakota's failure-to-state-a-claim rule, SDCL 15-6-12(b)(5), asserting immunity, lack of contractual enforcement rights, and inadequate federal allegations. The circuit court dismissed. Sisney appealed and also challenged the absence of an opportunity to amend. Review of pleading sufficiency was de novo: facts must support more than a speculative entitlement, rather than labels or a formulaic recitation. (¶¶ 5–8 & n. 1.)

## Beneficiary rule and holding
SDCL 53-2-6 allowed enforcement of a contract made expressly for a third person's benefit before the contracting parties rescinded it. That did not cover every person who obtained a practical benefit. The contracting parties' intention to create the qualifying benefit at formation had to be clearly manifested; incidental beneficiaries could not enforce. In the public-contract setting, the court applied a presumption against private beneficiary rights because governmental agreements normally benefit members of the public in some sense. Plain contractual language must indicate the intended private enforcement right, including the contractor's liability to third persons for nonperformance. (¶¶ 9–12.)

The court held that this food-service contract did not express an intention to confer on Sisney a direct enforceable benefit. Its stated service undertaking ran to the State, while inmates' collective benefit was incidental to the State's benefit under the court's analysis. Sisney's description of a benevolent connection did not overcome the contractual deficiency. His status as a person directly affected in daily well-being was insufficient. (¶ 13.)

## Application, grievance clause, and public-contract precedents
The court relied on decisions rejecting inmate enforcement of similar public agreements. Sisney distinguished those decisions because their agreements expressly restricted enforcement to the State. Original note 2 explained why that difference did not control: those courts also found no clear signatory intention to confer more than an incidental benefit on the specific inmates. An express exclusion was therefore not indispensable to the result. (¶ 12 & n. 2.)

Sisney also relied on a term requiring the contractor to describe its complaint-resolution process. The referenced process was the general institutional grievance mechanism, independently available for many confinement complaints. Referencing that existing administrative remedy did not create a private contract action under this agreement. Administrative access and an enforceable promise to a beneficiary were distinct. (¶ 14.)

## Federal theories, amendment, and matters not decided
Because all pleaded theories depended on the asserted right to enforce the contract, the court found no need to decide immunity. It also addressed additional defects in original note 3. Sisney alleged religious discrimination rather than denial of contractual rights because of race, which did not support his § 1981 theory as pleaded. His § 1985(3) allegation merely used the word conspiracy and supplied no factual support for a meeting of minds, its participants, or its timing. Liberal treatment of civil-rights pleadings did not cure those omissions. These are the court's historical rulings on these particular allegations, rather than a determination of every possible religious-accommodation claim. (¶ 15 & n. 3.)

The denial of amendment was reviewed for prejudicial abuse of discretion. Sisney had raised amendment only generally in a resistance brief, filed no motion, and specified no new facts that would cure the deficiencies. The circuit court did not have to initiate and grant such a motion on its own. (¶ 16.)

## Judgment, vote, and source scope
The Supreme Court affirmed dismissal. Zinter wrote, and Gilbertson C.J., Sabers, Konenkamp, and Meierhenry concurred: five-to-zero, no separate writing. (¶¶ 17–18.) The entire opinion and all three judicial notes were read. The assigned principal spans textbook pages 1200–1203, scans 194–197, ending immediately before the distinct Sisney v. Reisch heading. Its retained note 1 is original note 2. The full source restores federal theories and amendment analysis omitted from the excerpt. Reisch involved a separate settlement promising meals to an identified class; it is not a later stage reversing this same contract decision. Current law and subsequent treatment are not certified.
