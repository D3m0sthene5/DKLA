# DeMuth v. Miller
652 A.2d 891 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1995) | Contracts

## Court, opinions, and disposition
The Pennsylvania Superior Court filed this decision on January 11, 1995, after argument on June 21, 1994. The panel consisted of Judges Popovich, Olszewski, and Johnson. Popovich announced the affirmance of the judgment for employer Donald DeMuth. Olszewski concurred in the result and in the disposition of the first two issues, but offered different constitutional reasoning. Johnson likewise agreed on those two issues but dissented from enforcement on equal-protection grounds. The judgment therefore stood by a two-to-one division on the outcome; Popovich’s entire constitutional discussion should not be presented as reasoning adopted by both judges who affirmed. (652 A.2d at 891–892, 900–903, 909.)

## Employment, agreement, and competing practice
The court described the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict winner. DeMuth hired Daniel Miller in December 1985 as an independent contractor performing accounting work. In May 1986 Miller became an employee and professional management consultant, signing a contract for June 1, 1986 through May 31, 1987. Their relationship continued through successive fiscal years, but executed contracts could be produced for only two years. DeMuth remembered witnessing a later contract whose physical copy could not be found; Miller disputed having signed an agreement after the 1988–1989 term. This dispute about renewal was material to enforcement, rather than an agreed fact that no contractual terms remained. (Id. at 892, 895–896.)

Paragraph 8, called a contingent note payable, applied if the employee left or was terminated for cause and established a consulting or accounting firm within fifty miles of a current or former client during the following five years. For each former employer client retaining the employee’s services, he agreed to pay 125 percent of the preceding twelve months’ charges. The definition of cause listed several forms of misconduct and expressly included homosexuality. Popovich’s account says that the clause appeared in the contracts other than the 1986 agreement; Johnson’s later recital describes the yearly written contracts more generally. The operative clause and its disputed continuation, rather than that minor historical difference, controlled the appeal. (Id. at 892–893, 903.)

On October 17, 1990, DeMuth discharged Miller after a television appearance representing a gay and lesbian coalition. Afterward, Miller started a competing consulting practice and solicited DeMuth’s clients; seventeen transferred their business to him. DeMuth demanded the contractual payment. Popovich later characterized the dismissal as concerning the public appearance and possible effects on clients as well as sexual orientation. Johnson, citing trial admissions, insisted that the discharge was because of Miller’s acknowledged homosexuality, not work-related sexual conduct. That disagreement between the opinions is preserved rather than resolved by treating one characterization as undisputed. (Id. at 893, 899–900, 903, 906–908.)

## Claims and trial proceedings
DeMuth sued in contract and for misappropriation of property rights, initially demanding more than $120,000. Miller denied that a written agreement governed the last employment year and asserted that dismissal under the homosexuality provision offended public policy. His counterclaims alleged wrongful discharge, defamation through statements about his sexuality and AIDS risk, and interference with client relationships. Johnson’s recital adds that DeMuth wrote former clients suggesting an HIV test if they contemplated continued interaction with Miller. That description is attributed to the separate opinion rather than adopted as an independent appellate finding here. (Id. at 893, 903.)

At trial, Miller sought to exclude evidence that the agreement continued because paragraph 5 of DeMuth’s complaint said it was not renewed or extended after May 31, 1990. The court permitted evidence of the parties’ subsequent conduct. The parties stipulated that damages under the 125-percent provision did not exceed $110,000. The wrongful-discharge counterclaim was removed through compulsory nonsuit. The jury answered special interrogatories that the parties remained bound by the agreement through their conduct and awarded damages later molded to $110,000. The appeal followed denial of the relevant post-trial requests for judgment notwithstanding the verdict or a new trial. The opinion does not separately adjudicate every counterclaim on its merits. (Id. at 893; id. at 896 n. 1.)

## Renewal and judicial admission
All three judges accepted the rejection of the claimed judicial admission. Under the doctrine applied, a binding admission was an express factual concession made for the admitting party’s advantage. The complaint’s nonrenewal statement had to be read with allegations of continued employment, wages, benefits, termination for contractually specified cause, and compensation under paragraph 8. Treating the isolated sentence as conclusively extinguishing the agreement would defeat, rather than advance, DeMuth’s pleaded position. It therefore did not prevent evidence and jury consideration of continuation. (Id. at 893–895, 900, 903.)

Continued employment after a fixed term, without objection or a different arrangement, could imply another contract on the prior conditions. Although Miller denied a later signature, he continued working and receiving pay and benefits; the salary increased from $32,500 to $50,000. Both parties described a practice of executing a formal renewal four or five months into a fiscal year and making increases retroactive. A disputed bonus issue was resolved in February 1990 and the bonus paid. Those circumstances permitted the jury to decide whether the prior terms continued; the absence of a physical signed copy did not itself compel judgment for Miller. (Id. at 895–896.)

## Waiver and preservation
The argument that the payment was an unlawful penalty or an unreasonable restrictive covenant had not been raised in the pretrial, trial, or post-trial motions, and the trial court had not decided it. The appellate court treated that ordinary contractual challenge as waived. Thus the affirmance is not a substantive holding that a five-year, fifty-mile, 125-percent restriction necessarily satisfies every test of reasonableness or liquidated damages. (Id. at 896.)

The policy and constitutional objections received different preservation treatment. Although not included in the relevant earlier submissions, they were presented in a later supporting brief and oral argument, and the trial court chose to decide them. Popovich therefore addressed them under the then-applied rule allowing review when the court below had actually reached the late-presented issue. Notes 1 and 2 supply the procedural chronology and analogy; those preservation observations do not cure waiver of the distinct penalty and reasonableness argument. (Id. at 896–897 & nn. 1–2.)

## Popovich’s rationale for enforcement
Popovich distinguished the already-completed dismissal, which Miller disclaimed directly challenging on this appeal, from the later suit to recover losses associated with seventeen clients moving to the competing business. He regarded the award as enforcing competition-related obligations, not imposing damages simply because Miller was gay. The general attack on the restrictive terms themselves remained waived. (Id. at 897–898.)

He also rejected the state and federal constitutional and public-policy claims on the presented record. His discussion characterized the employer as concerned about the public television appearance, client reactions, and nondisclosure of sexual orientation; it stated that the particular claim was not actionable under the law invoked. Note 3 considered Shelley and employment-property doctrines but distinguished an appeal directed to enforcement of the payment clause from a direct claim to continued employment. His concluding note 4 directed broader employment-policy change toward the legislature and stressed the record and presentation of the issues. These are historical positions in Popovich’s opinion, not an assertion of current constitutional or employment law. (Id. at 898–900 & nn. 3–4.)

## Olszewski’s concurrence
Olszewski avoided deciding whether sexual-orientation discrimination violated equal protection because he found no constitutional state action in enforcing this particular competition promise. He distinguished a court coercing an employer to discriminate and a court imposing a payment directly because of protected status from the damages demanded here for subsequent competition. His two notes explain the Shelley and Barrows settings. He acknowledged the rule against direct or indirect judicial support of private bias but cautioned against converting every enforced private agreement into state action. (Id. at 900–902 & concurrence nn. 1–2.)

On his view, dismissal was completed privately and was not challenged; paragraph 8 could have been activated by numerous contractually defined causes. Enforcement addressed the separately agreed economic consequence of competition, without deciding anew whether homosexuality constituted cause. He suggested a different question might arise if the contract had not specified it and the court itself had to make that determination. Consequently, he concurred in the result while rejecting the need for Popovich’s merits discussion of constitutional protections. (Id. at 902–903.)

## Johnson’s partial dissent
Johnson rejected separating the damages clause from its discriminatory trigger. The cause-based termination activated the competition obligation, and the employer then invoked judicial coercion to profit from it. Under Shelley and Barrows, judicial enforcement could be state action even when a party had originally consented to the private agreement. He would reach equal protection because the payment obligation on these facts depended directly on the discriminatory termination. (Id. at 903–905.)

He found it unnecessary to decide whether sexual orientation warranted heightened scrutiny, concluding that enforcement failed even rational-basis review. The record showed capable performance and partnership prospects, no allegations of work-related sexual conduct, and a termination based on acknowledged status. Prejudice or fear of clients’ prejudice supplied no legitimate basis for judicial enforcement. He distinguished national-security and conduct-based cases, and explained why the then-existing Bowers decision on a privacy challenge to a sodomy statute did not resolve this equal-protection claim. These descriptions report his reasoning in 1995, without treating the authorities he discussed as present law. (Id. at 905–908.)

Johnson also challenged reliance on the absence of legislative or societal consensus: constitutional equal protection did not depend on such consensus, and executive and municipal employment protections supplied contrary policy evidence. He would reverse and direct entry of judgment notwithstanding the verdict for Miller. That proposed disposition did not command the panel. The actual judgment for $110,000 was affirmed. (Id. at 908–909; affirmance at 900.)
