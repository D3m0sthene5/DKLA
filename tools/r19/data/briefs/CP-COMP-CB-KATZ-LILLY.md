# Katz v. Eli Lilly & Co.

**84 F.R.D. 378 (E.D.N.Y. 1979)** — November 29, 1979. District Judge Neaher, sole memorandum and order. Civil Procedure. Assigned: CivPro PDF 676, printed 1343, own Katz account and questions in Note 6. The decision permits discovery concerning possible offensive issue preclusion; it does not invalidate the earlier verdict or finally decide liability in Katz.

Benna Katz sued Eli Lilly in 1975, alleging that her mother took diethylstilbestrol (DES) before her birth in 1953 and that she later developed vaginal adenocarcinoma. The claims sought $5 million for negligence in testing and distributing DES and breach of warranty. After Benna died in 1977, her mother, Esta Katz, brought the wrongful-death action as administratrix. During discovery, a different DES plaintiff, Joyce Bichler, obtained a $500,000 verdict against Lilly after a seven-week state-court trial ending July 16, 1979. The judgment remained on appeal. Katz intended to invoke that judgment to prevent Lilly from relitigating liability issues. The source describes Bichler as a similar DES action, while identifying the death and replacement administratrix complaint specifically in Katz; the assigned note’s characterization of the prior action as wrongful death is not independently established by this opinion. (379–381.)

At the end of Bichler’s trial, the state judge told jurors that they were free either to discuss deliberations or decline discussion. Lilly’s lawyers contacted juror Margaret Donnelly, who initially agreed to talk. According to counsel, she said her liability vote depended on damages being reduced through averaging each juror’s proposed award. Information obtained from juror Willie Bowman also suggested compromise. Those accounts were allegations supporting discovery, not judicial findings that the jury had actually reached an improper verdict. Donnelly later declined to sign an affidavit. Lilly noticed both depositions and obtained subpoenas; Bowman was served, but Donnelly could not be served. Following Donnelly’s complaint of harassment, the state judge barred further contact with the Bichler jurors pending the federal court’s resolution. Katz moved under Rules 26 and 45 to quash the subpoenas and vacate the deposition notices. (379–380.)

The apparent conflict was between discovering facts relevant to a federal case and protecting state-jury deliberations. Neaher began with a decisive limitation: counsel represented that Lilly sought testimony only to resist the judgment’s possible **collateral-estoppel effect in Katz**, not to impeach or attack the validity of the Bichler verdict between the parties to that action. Had the purpose been to invalidate that verdict, both state and federal law would require granting the motion. Footnote 2 further cautioned that federal discovery could not be taken for use in a pending state action. The court therefore considered preservation of testimony for a distinct federal proceeding, not a device to circumvent the state judge’s control of the original lawsuit. (380 and n.2.)

The then-applicable federal discovery rule allowed relevant, nonprivileged matters beyond the exact issues framed by the pleadings. Katz’s complaint did not identify Bichler, but her motion papers clearly showed the intended estoppel argument. New York law controlled issue preclusion in this diversity action, as footnote 3 explains. Mutuality was not required: a different plaintiff could use a prior judgment offensively against a defendant who had received a full and fair opportunity to litigate. But that prerequisite required examining the realities of the earlier litigation, including indications of a compromise verdict. That made the requested information relevant to a foreseeable defense. (380–381 and n.3.)

Katz argued that the established rule against using juror testimony to impeach a verdict made the information inadmissible and outside proper discovery. Footnote 4 cited Rule 606(b) and state and federal authorities for that rule. The court recognized those policies but distinguished disturbing a verdict’s finality from limiting its use against a party in litigation with a stranger to the original action. It did not find the authorities on either side directly controlling. Footnote 5 nevertheless identified decisions supporting the absence of an absolute ban on receiving juror testimony in every context and tied the result to state competency law made relevant by Rule 601. (381 and nn.4–5.)

The court understood collateral estoppel as an equitable doctrine governed by fairness, not an inflexible entitlement. Although a final judgment could remain conclusive between the original parties despite an erroneous compromise, allowing a stranger to use it offensively raised a different fairness question. Katz had no need to escape repeated litigation of her own dispute; she sought to avoid litigation altogether. Lilly’s earlier opportunity to contest Bichler was important, but not necessarily dispositive of whether the same judgment fairly bound it against Katz. Where there was a factual basis to suspect compromise, the defendant needed a reasonable opportunity to investigate that defense before the court decided preclusion. Minimizing litigation did not automatically outweigh that opportunity. (381–382.)

The holding was carefully conditional: when **permissible investigation conducted without compulsory court process** establishes a factual basis for believing that a judgment offered as collateral estoppel rested on a compromise verdict, depositions of jurors shown to possess relevant information are warranted under the Federal Rules. Counsel’s earlier voluntary interviews supplied the asserted basis here. The court **denied** the motion to quash and vacate. It did not determine that the verdict was a compromise, finally refuse preclusion, authorize depositions merely on suspicion in every case, or decide the merits of the DES claims. (382.)

The assigned questions challenge the distinction between undermining the original verdict and restricting its effect, and ask whether investigating the verdict defeats preclusion’s economy. Those are editorial questions, not additional rulings. All five judicial notes and the complete reported judicial text were read; no separate opinion or appendix appears.
