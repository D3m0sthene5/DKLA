# Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co. of America

**511 U.S. 375 (1994) · Supreme Court of the United States · May 16, 1994**  
**Civil Procedure — ancillary jurisdiction and enforcement of a settlement after dismissal**

A federal court’s jurisdiction over the original lawsuit did not automatically give it jurisdiction to enforce the parties’ later settlement contract. The dismissal order neither incorporated that contract nor retained enforcement jurisdiction. Justice Scalia wrote for a unanimous Court, which reversed and remanded. CivPro assigns the developed account under “Note on Nonstatutory Ancillary Jurisdiction,” PDF page 212, printed pages 380–381.

## The agency dispute became a different contract dispute

Guardian Life terminated Kokkonen’s general agency agreement. Kokkonen sued in California state court on state-law claims, and Guardian Life removed on diversity grounds and asserted state-law counterclaims. Two other corporate defendants named below had already been dismissed before trial; Guardian Life alone was the respondent in the Supreme Court. **376 & n.1.**

After closing arguments but before jury instructions, the parties reached an oral settlement of all claims and counterclaims. They recited its substance on the record before the district judge in chambers. In April 1992, they executed a stipulated dismissal with prejudice under the then-numbered Rule 41(a)(1)(ii). The judge signed it on April 13 with the notation that it was ordered. Crucially, the document did not refer to the settlement, incorporate its terms, or reserve jurisdiction to enforce it. **376–377.**

A later dispute concerned Kokkonen’s obligation to return files under the settlement. Guardian Life moved to enforce the agreement in the same federal court. Kokkonen objected that the court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction. The district court asserted inherent power and ordered enforcement; the Ninth Circuit affirmed on a theory of inherent supervisory authority. Kokkonen’s appeal relied solely on his jurisdictional objection. **377.**

The issue was therefore the federal court’s power to adjudicate the settlement dispute, not whether Kokkonen actually owed the files or whether the settlement was a valid contract.

## Dismissal did not carry jurisdiction over the settlement with it

Federal courts possess limited jurisdiction granted by Constitution and statute. The party asserting jurisdiction must establish it; a judicial decree cannot enlarge that authority. Neither Rule 41 nor another identified provision supplied jurisdiction over every contract producing a stipulated dismissal. **377–378.**

Scalia distinguished enforcement from reopening the original suit. Some appellate decisions permitted reopening under Rule 60(b)(6) after a settlement breach, while others took a narrower position. The Court described that disagreement without deciding which reopening approach was correct. Guardian Life sought enforcement—damages or specific performance of the new agreement—which required its own jurisdictional basis. **378 & n.2.**

The distinction matters because a settlement is not simply another step in resolving the original agency claims. It creates obligations whose breach may present different facts and legal questions. Giving federal courts perpetual power over such agreements merely because dismissing an earlier federal suit was part of the consideration would exceed the principle of limited federal jurisdiction.

## Neither rationale for ancillary jurisdiction applied

Guardian Life relied on broad language in *Julian v. Central Trust Co.* describing ancillary proceedings connected with prior litigation. The Court looked to the actual holding rather than that language in isolation. *Julian* involved a foreclosure decree that expressly reserved jurisdiction over claims against the conveyed property and authorized retaking and reselling it if claims were not paid. That was materially different from a dismissal order silent about settlement enforcement. **379.**

The Court identified two general purposes of ancillary jurisdiction. One allowed a single court to dispose of factually interdependent claims. The other allowed a court to manage its proceedings, vindicate its authority, and effectuate its decrees. **379–380.**

The first did not apply: the facts concerning breach of the agency agreement and those concerning breach of the settlement had nothing to do with one another. Resolving them in one court was neither necessary nor particularly efficient. The second also failed. The only judicial command was that the action be dismissed; an alleged breach of a private settlement did not violate or threaten that dismissal. Powers such as sanctions or contempt preserve judicial proceedings and commands, but they did not justify enforcing this separate contract. **380–381.**

## How an order could have changed the result

The Court explained that incorporating settlement terms into the dismissal order, or expressly retaining jurisdiction over the settlement, would make a later breach a violation of the judicial order and permit ancillary enforcement. The judge’s awareness and approval of the settlement were insufficient: they did not put its terms into the order. **381.**

For a dismissal under Rule 41(a)(2), the court could exercise its discretion to impose compliance with the settlement or retained jurisdiction as a term of dismissal. For the stipulated dismissal under historical Rule 41(a)(1)(ii), the court could embody the settlement or retain jurisdiction if the parties agreed. This was not a rule that the parties’ private consent alone created federal subject-matter jurisdiction. The necessary action was an appropriate judicial order; otherwise, enforcement belonged in state court unless an independent basis of federal jurisdiction existed. **381–382.**

## Judgment and assignment distinction

The Court **reversed the Ninth Circuit and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion**, **382**. The decision did not resolve the file-return dispute or foreclose a separately supported federal action. There were no separate writings or appendix. The complete eight-page report includes two judicial notes and an amicus note, all read for this brief.

The textbook frames the case as a question about residual ancillary jurisdiction after §1367. The actual opinion recognizes the two ancillary purposes and cites §1367 comparatively, but does not supply an exhaustive interpretation of every post-codification jurisdictional category. Its narrower holding concerns enforcement of this settlement after this unqualified dismissal.
