# Norrington v. Wright
115 U.S. 188 (1885) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

The Supreme Court decided this case on October 26, 1885, on a writ of error to the federal Circuit Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania. Arthur Norrington sued the buyers for refusing iron shipments; a jury returned a verdict for the buyers under an instruction making their timely rescission depend on their ignorance of the initial short shipment when they accepted its cargo. Justice Gray delivered the Court’s opinion affirming the judgment. The Chief Justice had not attended argument and took no part in the decision; the report contains no separate opinion or numerical voting breakdown. (115 U.S. at 188, 192–193, 203, 212.)

## Transaction and chronology

The January 19, 1880 contract sold 5,000 tons of old T iron rails, to be shipped from European ports for Philadelphia at approximately 1,000 tons monthly beginning in February, with the whole quantity shipped before August 1. The price was $45 per ton of 2,240 pounds, custom-house weight, payable in cash upon presentation of bills and weight certificates. The sellers had to notify the buyers of shipments and vessel names as soon as known. They need not replace a parcel lost after shipment and, when possible, were to obtain the buyers’ right to designate discharging berths. These shipping and payment provisions governed one purchase of 5,000 tons. (Id. at 189, 203–204.)

The material facts proved at trial differed from the seller’s allegations of monthly compliance. Actual shipments were 400 tons in February, 885 in March, 1,571 in April, 850 in May, 1,000 in June, and 300 in July. The buyers received and paid for the February cargo when it arrived in March without knowing that this was the entire February shipment. They gave unloading instructions for March cargoes during April, but first learned the amounts shipped in the first three months around May 14, when the March cargoes arrived. They then notified broker Edward Etting that they declined the March and April shipments for noncompliance. (Id. at 189–190.)

The correspondence sharpened both the substantive dispute and the question whether the buyers had actually rescinded. On May 17 they said that if the early deficiencies released them from the contract, they would take that advantage. Etting maintained that six months allowed later completion, proposed selecting 1,000 tons from April shipments, and demanded a definite decision about future shipments. The buyers’ May 19 answer insisted that the extra month covered minor deficiencies, objected to revising shipment notices, and declared that they would reject future shipments if legally entitled to do so and would accept them if not. They refused Etting’s June offer of precisely 1,000 April tons and his June attempt to correct vessel notifications. Meanwhile the iron’s market price had been below the contract price and continued falling. The cargoes were eventually resold, with the buyers’ consent, for the benefit of whoever was entitled to the proceeds. (Id. at 190–192.)

## Arguments and the trial instruction

Norrington argued that the August deadline permitted deficiencies to be made up later, so long as the buyers were not required to take more than 1,000 tons in a month. Alternatively, monthly shipment and separate payment made the agreement divisible, leaving the buyers only a damages deduction for delayed installments. His Supreme Court arguments added that the buyers’ legally conditional correspondence did not elect rescission and that accepting the February cargo barred rescission of the remainder. He emphasized scattered ports, variable sailing times, and the absence of a requirement to replace lost parcels as reasons not to insist on exact monthly regularity. Those were arguments for his construction, not findings that the buyers had accepted that construction. (Id. at 192–202.)

The trial court instructed that the verdict should favor the buyers if, when they accepted the paid cargo, they lacked notice that the seller had failed to ship approximately 1,000 February tons and, upon learning the deficiency, immediately notified him of their intention to rescind. The seller excepted to that instruction and sought review after the verdict and judgment against him. The Supreme Court upheld the judgment under that framework. (Id. at 192–193, 205, 212.)

## Holding and reasoning

The Court treated shipment time as a material description of the promised goods in this mercantile contract. It ordinarily served practical needs such as estimating arrival, arranging funds, or satisfying downstream contracts. The opinion used “warranty” in the insurance and maritime sense of a condition precedent: failure of the material description could justify repudiating the whole contract. This is the historical rule applied to the agreement before the Court, not a statement that every promise called a warranty has that consequence under later sales law. (Id. at 203.)

The agreement remained one contract for 5,000 tons despite subsidiary monthly shipping and payment terms. Permission not to replace cargoes lost at sea reduced the quantity to be delivered in that contingency; it did not divide the purchase into independent contracts. The schedule required approximately 1,000 tons in each month from February through June, with only slight and unimportant deficiencies made up during July. It did not mean six equal installments of approximately 833 tons or allow the seller to choose materially smaller early shipments. (Id. at 203–204.)

The Court distinguished a quantity estimate tied to an independently identified source or lot from a promise to furnish goods of a described kind in a stated amount. In the first category, “about” might reflect uncertainty about how much the identified lot contained. Here no independently identified lot qualified the quantity undertaking. The approximation allowed accidental, minor variations in number, measure, or weight. Shipping only 400 tons in February and 885 in March therefore failed the promised early installments and gave the buyers the right to rescind the whole purchase if they asserted it distinctly and seasonably. The seller could neither force acceptance of a deficient quantity nor compel selection of the stipulated amount from an excessive one. (Id. at 204–205.)

The buyers did assert that right. Their position that they would reject if the law entitled them to reject, and otherwise would accept, clearly preserved and exercised the claimed right rather than surrendering it because its legal validity was disputed. Acceptance of the February cargo did not waive rescission because it occurred without notice or means of knowing the full February deficiency. Failure to return that cargo caused no injury to the seller, since its paid price exceeded market value, and the correspondence did not rely on nonreturn as a ground of objection. The Court distinguished knowing acceptance and use of part of a specific lot whose conformity the buyer could previously have ascertained. (Id. at 205.)

## Competing authorities and the limits of the decision

Much of the opinion answers the seller’s reliance on English installment cases. The Court recognized conflicting lower-court decisions but favored authorities permitting rejection after a deficient first installment and the House of Lords’ insistence that specified shipping dates describe the purchased article. Equally useful goods shipped at an unbargained-for time were not necessarily the goods the buyer had agreed to take. The Court rejected the suggestion that strict construction should change merely because a falling market made rejection commercially advantageous. (Id. at 206–210.)

Mersey Steel’s rule about a buyer’s failure to pay for an installment did not control a seller’s failure to deliver the prescribed installment. Where delivery was to precede payment, payment of one installment could not ordinarily be a condition precedent of the entire purchase or of later deliveries without express language. The Court also read the cited Pennsylvania decisions as turning on payment not yet due or waiver through delay or informed acceptance, rather than rejecting the right recognized here. (Id. at 210–212.)

The February and March failures sufficed to defeat Norrington’s action. The Court therefore found it unnecessary to dwell on the additional April objection: buyers could not be forced to take a selected 1,000 tons from an excessive shipment, and the seller could not revise previously required vessel designations to manufacture compliance. That discussion supplies an additional objection, but the judgment rests on the early shipment failures and timely rescission. The Court affirmed the buyers’ judgment; it did not award them a quantified damages recovery. (Id. at 212.)

## Assigned account and reading scope

The casebook’s problem at printed page 897, physical PDF page 241, reproduces the buyers’ legally conditional answer and asks whether they repudiated. The actual report shows why that language cannot be assessed independently of the seller’s antecedent defaults and the buyers’ prompt notice upon discovering them: the Court held their assertion of the right to rescind clear and effective. The problem places the buyers in New York; the actual report identifies Wright and the other buyers as Pennsylvania citizens trading as Peter Wright & Sons and the transaction as a Philadelphia contract. That supplied geographical variation does not change the reported holding.
