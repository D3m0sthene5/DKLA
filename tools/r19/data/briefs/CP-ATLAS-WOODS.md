# Woods v. Interstate Realty Co.

**Course:** Civil Procedure

## Citation

Woods v. Interstate Realty Co., 337 U.S. 535–540 (1949). Supreme Court of the United States. Argued March 30–31, 1949; decided June 20, 1949. Justice Douglas wrote the Court's opinion, announced by Justice Reed. Justice Jackson dissented, joined by Justices Rutledge and Burton. Rutledge also addressed the case in his shared dissent published with Cohen, 337 U.S. 557–561. Assigned account: Civil Procedure casebook p. 461, repository PDF p. 251, note 4.

## Parties and material facts

Interstate Realty, a Tennessee corporation, sought a broker's commission allegedly due for a sale of Woods' real estate in Mississippi. Woods was described as a Mississippi resident. The corporation had been doing business in Mississippi without qualifying under a statute requiring foreign corporations to designate an agent for service. The statute prohibited a noncomplying corporation from bringing or maintaining an action in the state's courts. The dispute concerned access to a federal diversity forum despite that restriction. 337 U.S. at 535–536 & n.1.

## Procedural history

The federal District Court granted Woods summary judgment, treated the contract as void under Mississippi law, and dismissed with prejudice. The Fifth Circuit disagreed with that reading of the state decisions: it understood the contract as valid but unenforceable in Mississippi's own courts. Relying on Lupton's Sons, it held that the state-court restriction did not bar enforcement in federal court. It reversed, granted rehearing, and reaffirmed its reversal. The Supreme Court granted certiorari because of the apparent conflict with Angel v. Bullington. Id. at 535–536.

## Issue

Could an unqualified foreign corporation maintain a diversity action in federal court in Mississippi to enforce a state-law claim when Mississippi had closed its state courts to that corporation, even if the contract itself remained valid under state law? Id. at 536–538.

## Rule and holding

The state-law restriction barred the diversity action. Under the Court's Erie/York analysis, a litigant denied recovery in the state's courts could not obtain a more favorable enforcement opportunity by invoking diversity jurisdiction in a federal court there. The Court rejected the older conception of diversity jurisdiction on which Lupton's Sons had permitted the action. Id. at 537–538.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court reversed the Fifth Circuit. Its ruling sustained the bar to maintaining Interstate Realty's federal suit. The Court's reasoning did not depend on adopting the District Court's conclusion that the brokerage contract was void; it answered the Fifth Circuit's distinction between contractual validity and access to state courts. Id. at 536–538.

## Reasoning

Lupton's Sons had allowed an unqualified corporation to enforce a contract in federal diversity court despite a New York statute restricting suit in the state's courts. That decision reasoned that a state could not set qualifications for federal suitors. Woods treated that foundation as obsolete after Erie. Id. at 536–537.

The majority then examined Angel v. Bullington. That case rested both on preclusion from an earlier state judgment and on Erie's opposition to a diversity suit where the state had denied the relevant enforcement remedy. The Fifth Circuit had treated the latter ground as nonbinding discussion. The Supreme Court answered that a decision resting on multiple grounds did not render one of those grounds dictum simply because another independently supported the result. Id. at 537.

York supplied the substantive reason for applying the state restriction. For purposes of enforcing a locally created right in diversity, the Court linked the right to the remedy the state supplied. A federal remedy where the state denied recovery would advantage persons entitled to invoke diversity over those restricted to state tribunals. Eliminating that disparity was the majority's understanding of Erie. Id. at 538.

## Separate opinions

Jackson argued that the majority was giving Mississippi law a more severe effect than the state itself had given it. On the Fifth Circuit's reading, Mississippi withheld its own courts' assistance without destroying contractual rights or prohibiting other remedies. Treating that restriction as also closing federal courts was, in his view, a distortion of state law rather than fidelity to it. Id. at 538–539.

He stressed that foreign-corporation qualification statutes commonly imposed the narrower restriction. The majority's treatment could give the debtor an unearned benefit unrelated to the state's injury from nonqualification. Jackson would follow a state decision actually giving the statute that effect, but he believed the state had done the opposite here. He would affirm the Fifth Circuit; Rutledge and Burton joined. Id. at 539–540.

Rutledge's additional shared dissent criticized the three same-day decisions as extending Erie too far into matters Congress could regulate in federal litigation. He agreed with Jackson's Woods position and would affirm the Fifth Circuit. He did not contend that federal courts should ignore substantive state law generally. Cohen, 337 U.S. at 557–561 & dissent n.1.

## Notes

The textbook places Woods between Ragan and Cohen as a development of York's reasoning. Keep the three procedural results distinct: Woods reversed the Fifth Circuit's decision allowing suit; Ragan affirmed a limitations ruling; Cohen affirmed application of the expense-security statute. Casebook pp. 461–462.

The full sources include the Court's statutory footnote, Jackson's complete dissent, and Rutledge's cross-referenced writing. The opposing views turn partly on how to honor the state's own interpretation of its restriction, not merely on whether corporations should obey qualification statutes.
