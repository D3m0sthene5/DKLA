# Mata v. Avianca, Inc. — June 22, 2023 Sanctions Decision
678 F. Supp. 3d 443 (S.D.N.Y. 2023), No. 22-cv-1461 (PKC), ECF 54 | Civil Procedure

## Underlying litigation and the challenged filings

Roberto Mata sued Avianca in New York state court in February 2022, alleging injury to his left knee when a metal serving cart struck him during a flight from El Salvador to John F. Kennedy Airport. Avianca removed under federal-question jurisdiction arising from the Montreal Convention and later moved to dismiss the claim as untimely under that treaty. Steven Schwartz of Levidow, Levidow & Oberman had handled the state action but was not admitted in the Southern District of New York. His colleague Peter LoDuca appeared as federal counsel while Schwartz continued the substantive work. Findings of Fact (FOF) ¶¶ 1–3, ECF 54 at 3.

On March 1, 2023, LoDuca signed and filed an affirmation opposing dismissal, with a declaration under penalty of perjury. Schwartz had researched and written it. LoDuca checked its flow and grammar but did not read any cited decision or inquire into the research supporting its legal claims. He relied on familiarity with Schwartz, a colleague of more than twenty-five years, although Schwartz primarily practiced in state court and lacked experience with the federal issues involved. FOF ¶¶ 4–6, at 4–5.

The opposition cited purported authorities supporting tolling of the Montreal Convention’s limitations period because of a bankruptcy stay. Avianca’s March 15 reply identified multiple decisions it could not locate and said the few real cases did not support the stated propositions. No respondent withdrew the submission or explained the discrepancies. LoDuca forwarded the reply to Schwartz without reading it. The court independently searched and likewise failed to locate the purported cases. FOF ¶¶ 7–12, at 5–7.

The court ordered production of the actual opinions on April 11 and 12. After an extension, LoDuca filed an April 25 affidavit prepared by Schwartz with purported case excerpts attached. It said one authority could not be located and attributed the incomplete texts to an unspecified online database. It did not identify ChatGPT. LoDuca had no role in preparing the document and no knowledge whether its assertions were true, but signed it without asking questions or checking the attached cases. He had obtained the extension by falsely representing that he himself was on vacation when Schwartz was the person away. The court found that this lie concealed Schwartz’s substantive role and LoDuca’s failure to verify his statements. FOF ¶¶ 13–23, at 7–10.

## What the court found about the authorities and explanations

The attached Varghese, Miller, Petersen, Shaboon, Martinez, and Durden opinions were generated by ChatGPT and did not exist. Schwartz had treated ChatGPT as a search engine. After encountering limited federal access through the firm’s Fastcase subscription, he prompted it for cases supporting tolling and cited its excerpts without obtaining the full decisions. His initial assertion that it merely supplemented his research was misleading: apart from an unsuccessful initial search and limited authentication, it supplied the substantive arguments. FOF ¶¶ 36–41, at 15–18.

The court did not regard the problem as harmless citation formatting. Varghese’s docket and reporter identifiers belonged to unrelated actual cases; its narrative changed plaintiffs and bankruptcy chapters and ended abruptly. It cited other fabricated authorities and real authorities that did not say what its text claimed. Miller borrowed a docket and reporter location from unrelated cases and attributed an opinion to a panel that did not decide it. Petersen likewise used mismatched docket, reporter, and database identifiers, confused jurisdictions, and cited itself as supporting precedent. Names of real judges and parties made the fabricated material appear superficially credible while exposing them to false attribution. FOF ¶¶ 24–35, at 10–15.

The May 4 order required LoDuca to show cause concerning the opposition and the attached purported opinions. The May 25 response first acknowledged their fictitious origin. A supplemental May 26 order brought Schwartz and the firm into the inquiry and also addressed the affidavit’s notarization. The respondents obtained separate counsel, submitted further declarations, and appeared at the June 8 hearing, where the two lawyers answered questions under oath and expressed remorse. FOF ¶¶ 42–50, at 18–21.

Schwartz’s explanations changed. Screenshots offered with his May 25 affidavit showed ChatGPT assuring him that the cases were real and available in legal databases, creating the impression that those assurances explained his earlier reliance. His June 6 declaration instead said that queries made after the first show-cause order confirmed his emerging belief that the chatbot responded without regard to truth. The court treated these contradictory accounts as undermining credibility. It rejected his claim that he had not suspected fabrication before May 4 because he had already read Avianca’s challenge, recognized the court’s difficulty locating the cases, and unsuccessfully searched for Varghese and Zicherman himself. FOF ¶¶ 43–47, at 18–20. No respondent ever formally asked to withdraw the original affirmation or told the court it should no longer rely on it. FOF ¶ 51, at 21.

## Issue and legal framework

The question was whether the lawyers’ preparation, submission, continued advocacy, and explanations concerning fabricated judicial authorities warranted sanctions after notice and an opportunity to respond. The court began by recognizing that assistance from reliable technology, like assistance from people or research databases, is not inherently improper. Existing rules nevertheless require lawyers to ensure the accuracy of what they present. The sanctionable conduct was evaluated through those established obligations and the respondents’ subsequent behavior. ECF 54 at 1–3.

Rule 11(b)(2) certifies that legal contentions are supported by existing law or a nonfrivolous argument for changing or establishing law, following a reasonable inquiry. Incorrect statements or unlikely theories are not automatically sanctionable. Fabricated opinions, however, are neither existing law nor a legitimate basis for changing it; relying on them abuses the adversary system. The Rule reaches signing, filing, submitting, and later advocating a paper. Refusing to correct a position after learning that its support has failed can constitute continued advocacy. Conclusions of Law (COL) ¶¶ 1–5, 9–10, at 21–25.

Rule 11(c)(3) permits a court to begin its own inquiry through an order specifically describing the questioned conduct. After notice and reasonable opportunity to respond, sanctions may reach the attorney responsible even if that attorney did not appear or personally sign the filing; this permitted a sanction against Schwartz. Unless exceptional circumstances exist, the law firm is jointly responsible for its lawyer’s violation. COL ¶¶ 11–13, at 25–26.

Applying Second Circuit precedent to these court-initiated proceedings, the judge required subjective bad faith, a heightened standard compared with the objective unreasonableness standard generally associated with an adversary’s sanctions motion and its twenty-one-day safe-harbor procedure. Inherent-authority sanctions likewise required bad faith and restraint. Knowing falsehoods can establish that state of mind, and conscious avoidance can be equivalent to knowledge when the lawyer recognizes a high probability of the relevant fact and deliberately avoids confirming it. Mere negligence, foolishness, confusion, or sloppy research does not alone satisfy that test. COL ¶¶ 14–19, at 26–28.

## Separate application to each lawyer and the firm

The court assessed each lawyer separately, rather than simply imputing Schwartz’s knowledge to LoDuca. It distinguished innocent reliance on a colleague or client in *Braun v. Zhiguo Fu*, where the lawyer lacked reason to suspect misinformation. Here, the opponent’s submission and the court’s production orders supplied warnings; LoDuca avoided reading the challenge and made no meaningful inquiry. COL ¶¶ 20–22, at 28–29.

LoDuca acted in subjective bad faith by signing and filing the opposition without reading a single cited case or otherwise checking its legal contentions. The complete absence of inquiry, coupled with known limits on Schwartz’s experience and the firm’s research resources, differed from an imperfect investigation. He also swore to the April affidavit without a basis for its assertions. Even a cursory substantive review would have exposed the missing reported case, the incomplete supposed opinions, and their internal inconsistencies. His knowingly false vacation explanation supplied additional evidence of bad faith. COL ¶ 23, at 29–30.

For Schwartz, unsuccessful efforts to locate Varghese and Zicherman, the opponent’s warning, and the court’s orders established awareness of a high probability that the authorities did not exist. Yet he furnished the purported opinions without disclosing his failed searches and consciously avoided confirming the problem. His misleading description of ChatGPT as a supplement, inconsistent accounts of the authenticity queries, and nondisclosure of its role in the April affidavit further supported bad faith. These findings went beyond condemning poor research. COL ¶ 24, at 30–31.

The Levidow Firm identified corrective measures and accepted responsibility but showed no exceptional circumstances relieving it of Rule 11(c)(1)’s joint responsibility. The firm was therefore jointly and severally liable with both lawyers. COL ¶ 25, at 31.

## Authorities considered but not used independently

The court declined separate sanctions under 28 U.S.C. § 1927. Although reliance on fabricated cases caused substantial harm, dilatory tactics and litigation delay were not among the harms found, and § 1927 focuses on unreasonable and vexatious multiplication of proceedings. The final sanctions rested on Rule 11 and, alternatively, inherent authority. COL ¶¶ 26–27, at 31.

The opinion also discussed 18 U.S.C. § 505, which addresses forged judicial signatures and court seals, to illustrate the interest in protecting judicial proceedings from counterfeit authority. Because these fabricated opinions contained no signature or seal, the court expressly concluded that the respondents had not violated that statute. This sanctions opinion is not a criminal conviction for judicial forgery. COL ¶¶ 7–8 & n.13, at 23–24.

## Sanction selection and exact disposition

Rule 11 sanctions must be limited to what suffices for specific and general deterrence. The court considered the firm’s planned mandatory education on technology and artificial intelligence, as well as notarization training, and found additional court-ordered education redundant. Avianca had not requested its fees or expenses, and the court declined such an award. It credited the respondents’ remorse, public embarrassment, lack of disciplinary history, absence of financial gain or personal animus, and low likelihood of repetition. It did not equate those mitigating circumstances with absence of the already-established violations. COL ¶¶ 28–32, at 32–33.

The court imposed a single $5,000 penalty, jointly and severally payable by LoDuca, Schwartz, and the firm into the court registry within fourteen days. It was a deterrent penalty payable to the court, rather than $5,000 from each respondent or compensation paid to Avianca. The respondents also had fourteen days to send individually addressed first-class letters to Mata enclosing the sanctions opinion, the June 8 hearing transcript, and the April 25 affirmation with exhibits. They had to send corresponding letters to every judge falsely named as the author of the six fabricated opinions, including the purported opinion attributed to that judge, and file copies of those letters with the court. COL ¶¶ 33–34 and Conclusion, at 33–34.

The court expressly declined to compel an apology, reasoning that an involuntary apology would not be sincere. The required letters supplied notice and supporting documents; an apology remained voluntary. Judge Castel alone issued the District Court decision, with no separate opinion or appellate voting alignment. This order resolves sanctions, not Mata’s substantive damages claim or the legal merits of Avianca’s limitations defense.

## Assigned scope and practical significance

The assigned supplement begins the June 22 opinion on PDF page 350 and continues its ordered relief on page 351. It retains the introduction and final sanctions while omitting all fifty-one findings-of-fact paragraphs and thirty-four conclusions-of-law paragraphs, as an editorial bracket explicitly states. The complete court-hosted opinion supplies the omitted chronology, individualized bad-faith analysis, and restrictions on the sanctions authorities. All thirty-four judicial pages and all fourteen footnotes were read before drafting. All nine additional PDF pages were visually inspected: pages 35–40 contain Appendix A’s fabricated Varghese exhibit, and pages 41–43 contain Appendix B’s authenticity-query screenshots. The exhibit changes the injured passenger’s identity, alternates between bankruptcy chapters, and contains unrelated arbitration passages; the screenshots show false assurances that the fabricated case and other authorities existed. These materials are evidence of the research and filing failures, not actual precedent.

The case illustrates both a reasonable-inquiry duty and a continuing obligation to correct legal contentions. Its particularly important distinction is between an initial research mistake and conscious avoidance, false statements, and persistence after concrete warnings. The court’s opening discussion permits reliable technological assistance; its holding places responsibility for verification and candid correction on the lawyers presenting the material.
