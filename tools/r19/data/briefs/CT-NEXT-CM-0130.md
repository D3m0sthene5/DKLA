# New England Structures, Inc. v. Loranger
354 Mass. 62, 234 N.E.2d 888 (1968) | Contracts

## Roof-deck subcontract and the termination telegram
Loranger, the general contractor on a school project, subcontracted on July 11, 1961 with New England Structures to install a gypsum roof deck. Work began November 24. Article 5 required sufficient resources to maintain progress and permitted termination on specified defaults, including persistent failure to supply skilled workers, disobeying instructions, or failing contractual provisions. Termination required at least five days’ prior written notice; the general contractor could complete the work and charge the excess cost to the subcontractor. 354 Mass. at 63–64 & n.1.

On December 18, New England received a telegram citing repeated refusal or inability to supply enough properly skilled workers to maintain satisfactory progress. It terminated the right to proceed effective December 26 and said other forces would complete the work at New England’s expense. New England replied that Loranger’s failure to supply approved drawings and inappropriate changes in instructions caused the delay. Evidence conflicted about the work’s quality, staggering of metal bulb tees, welding on both sides by certified welders, adequate staffing, waiver of specifications, and trade practices. The architect had complained December 14. A replacement company finished at a price above New England’s bid. Id. at 63–64.

Other evidence suggested Loranger’s foreman stopped a New England welder from working during the notice period and that the subcontract could have been completed within five days if work had been allowed. That evidence raised questions about both interference and the possible purpose of the notice period. Id. at 64.

## Cross-actions, verdicts, and the limited charge
Loranger sued for increased completion expense caused by subcontractor breach. New England’s cross-action alleged that Loranger breached by terminating its right to proceed. At a consolidated jury trial, New England prevailed in both cases, obtaining $16,860.25 on its affirmative claim. Loranger brought exceptions to the charge. Id. at 62–63.

The judge ruled that Loranger’s stated staffing reason confined its justification as a matter of law. Unmentioned defects could support termination only if inherent in the stated ground; otherwise they could be used as damage elements only after justification on that ground was established. The court also instructed on possible interference during the notice period. Footnote 3 explains that no exception appeared to have been taken to that separate part of the interference instruction. Id. at 64–65 & nn.2–3.

## A stated reason does not automatically exclude other defenses
The Supreme Judicial Court recognized authorities barring a litigant from changing its ground after giving a reason for its conduct, sometimes described as preventing it from mending its hold. Those authorities relied on estoppel or waiver. Massachusetts decisions more specifically required detrimental reliance or a change of position before the statement of one reason precluded another otherwise available defense. Id. at 65–66.

New England therefore had to establish that it relied to its detriment on Loranger’s naming only one ground. A jury need not believe evidence tending to show reliance, and it should have been allowed to consider other termination grounds unless it found the required reliance. The charge incorrectly made the telegram an automatic limitation. Footnote 4 addresses a prior case’s express reservation of other grounds, explaining that the distinction did not remove the actual-reliance requirement. Id. at 66–67 & n.4.

## Notice period construed separately from a cure opportunity
Because a new trial was required, the court also construed Article 5. Whether its five-day notice requirement afforded a cure period was a legal question of contract interpretation. The court considered authorities suggesting that warning notices may permit correction, as well as the principle that a contract remains in force during a notice period, but it found no express right to cure in this provision. Id. at 67–68 & nn.5–6.

A short period before termination of a substantial construction contract was more plausibly intended to allow layoffs, removal of equipment, cancellation of orders, and other protective arrangements. The parties could naturally have written an express cure right if that was intended. Despite obscurity in the language, the court concluded that Article 5 told the subcontractor when it had to quit and allowed steps to protect itself; it did not allow continuing defaults to be cured so as to prevent termination. Id. at 68.

That interpretation limited the reliance theory as well. New England could not claim detrimental reliance based on trying to cure the one default named in the telegram, because after receipt it had no contractual opportunity to cure defaults. Yet Loranger still needed actual justification for giving notice: unjustified termination could itself subject it to breach liability. The court did not finally find any alleged defect proved or any termination justified. Id. at 68–69.

## Disposition, participation, and source scope
The Supreme Judicial Court sustained Loranger’s exceptions, requiring a new trial under the corrected rules. Cutter wrote; Wilkins, Spalding, Whittemore, Cutter, and Kirk are listed as present. No separate opinion or voting division is reported. Id. at 62, 67, 69.
