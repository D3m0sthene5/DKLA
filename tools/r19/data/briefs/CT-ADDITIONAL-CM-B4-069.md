# Dr. Smood New York LLC v. Orchard Houston, LLC
2020 N.Y. Slip Op. 33707(U), 2020 WL 6526996 (Sup. Ct., N.Y. County Nov. 2, 2020) | Contracts

## Court and interim posture
Justice Laurence L. Love decided motion sequence 002 in the New York Supreme Court, New York County, index 652812/2020, on November 2, 2020. The decision was filed November 5 as NYSCEF document 67. The court denied the tenant's motion for a preliminary injunction and the landlord's cross-motion for monthly use and occupancy and a bond. The order marks a non-final disposition. Its statements that rent and taxes remained due should therefore be distinguished from a final arrears judgment or an order actually granting the landlord the requested monetary interim relief. (Decision 1–2, 4–5.)

## Lease, nonpayment, and changing requests
The parties entered a ten-year lease on or about February 22, 2017 for ground-floor and basement space at 181–185 East Houston Street, also known as 195–201 Orchard Street. Its agreed purpose was operation of a full-service café offering fresh and raw quick-service products, meals, beverages, juices, shakes, smoothies, and related items. The tenant last paid monthly rent in March 2020 and withheld later payments, claiming that the pandemic, executive orders, and lease terms excused them. (1, 4.)

After June negotiations about possible rental revisions, the landlord served a July 1 notice to cure April–June defaults by July 10, warning among other things that it would draw on the security deposit. The tenant sought a Yellowstone injunction, which would preserve an opportunity to cure a lease default. The parties later withdrew that first notice and application while attempting settlement. A second notice covering April–September prompted the present application. The landlord sought monthly use and occupancy during the litigation and a $250,000 undertaking securing claimed arrears of $198,764.31 for those six months. These were the amounts requested, not money judgments entered by this decision. (1–2.)

In its reply, the tenant withdrew the Yellowstone request and sought only a preliminary injunction and temporary restraining order. The court noted that the current order to show cause had not been served timely, but chose to decide both applications on their merits. It did not dismiss on that service defect. (2, 4.)

## Tenant's theories and the standard for preliminary relief
A preliminary injunction required a likelihood of ultimate success, irreparable injury without relief, and favorable balancing of equities. The court stated that the elements must be demonstrated by clear and convincing evidence, while explaining that the movant need make only a prima facie showing of entitlement rather than prove the entire merits case. Granting relief remained discretionary. (2.)

The tenant claimed likely success through frustration of purpose and a casualty provision; threatened depletion of its security deposit was its asserted irreparable injury, and it argued that the landlord should not receive an unbargained reward while public-health rules prevented the contemplated business. Its employee's affidavit relied on lease section 11.3, which proportionately reduced specified rent, tax, and operating payments when physical damage by fire or other casualty, or damage to the building affecting access, left space unusable or inaccessible. The tenant asserted that the virus and March 16 restrictions were the triggering casualty, that the premises were wholly inaccessible through July 6 and partly unusable afterward, and that complete abatement followed. These were the tenant's claims, rather than findings that the café sustained physical property damage. (3–4.)

## Casualty and partial frustration did not establish likely success
The court rejected the casualty characterization because there had been no physical harm to the premises. It said the lease did not provide abatement in these circumstances and that the tenant had been required to obtain insurance to assure payment under them. The opinion does not reproduce that insurance provision in full or adjudicate a separate insurance claim. (4.)

For frustration, the tenant invoked the principle that the disappointed purpose must have been so completely the shared basis of the bargain that the transaction otherwise would make little sense. The court applied the further requirement of complete destruction of the underlying basis: diminished business is insufficient where the tenant can still use the space for an intended purpose. (3–4.)

The landlord demonstrated that the café had operated in the premises since at least July, offering counter service and collection of orders placed online. The executive orders prevented indoor dining, but the premises still served permitted aspects of the café business. The court therefore regarded the disruption as partial frustration and held that the tenant had failed to demonstrate likely success on its asserted rent excuse. It did not calculate an alternative percentage rent reduction by comparing indoor-dining space with the kitchen or impose the approach used in Hitz. (4.)

Because the likelihood-of-success showing failed, the court denied preliminary relief without a separate dispositive analysis of the other two factors. It did not find the alleged security-deposit loss irreparable or undertake a quantified balancing of hardship. (4–5.)

## Exact order and relationship to the assignment
The final page states that the tenant's rent and tax obligations continue unabated. It also expressly denies both the injunction motion and the landlord's cross-motion in their entirety. No $250,000 bond was required under CPLR 6312(b), because no preliminary injunction was granted. The court likewise did not grant the cross-motion's requested use-and-occupancy order or award the stated arrears. Both results must be retained even though the court's merits discussion rejected the tenant's asserted excuse. (5.)

The assigned note at printed page 1162 contrasts Dr. Smood's refusal to recognize partial frustration with Hitz's contractual force-majeure relief, and asks whether the presence of that clause explains the difference. The actual Smood decision analyzes casualty and frustration in deciding interim relief. It does not announce a universal prohibition on every negotiated partial excuse or decide how a Hitz-style clause would operate if contained in this lease.
