# Trump v. Cook

**Supreme Court of the United States • 609 U.S. 528 (2026) • No. 25A312 • June 29, 2026**

These are separate segments: note 2 concerns PHH and is not part of this Cook brief. **Actual source:** the complete official preliminary report, 79 physical PDF pages, reported pp. 528–604, including syllabus, counsel, every separate opinion, all judicial footnotes, and reporter’s note. Pinpoints below use the official report’s pagination. The preliminary report remains subject to formal revision.

## Facts and posture

Federal Reserve Governors hold fourteen-year terms unless sooner removed for cause by the President. 12 U.S.C. § 242. Cook joined the Board in 2022 and received a full term in 2023, scheduled to expire in 2038. In August 2025, FHFA Director William Pulte publicized allegations that she had obtained favorable mortgage terms in 2021 by representing two homes as principal residences. The President demanded her resignation through social media, threatened to fire her, and purported to remove her through an August 25 letter. The letter asserted possible false statements, loss of confidence in her integrity, and at least gross negligence affecting her competence as a financial regulator. Those were allegations and asserted reasons for removal; the Court did not adjudicate their truth. 539–540, 557–559.

Cook sued the President, Board, and Chairman, seeking to continue in office. Her claims distinguished three propositions: the alleged conduct was not statutory cause; the statute implied notice and an opportunity to respond; and constitutional due process independently required pretermination process. The District Court issued a preliminary injunction against implementation of the removal. It held Cook likely to prevail because permissible cause was confined to in-office conduct and because her protected tenure gave her a constitutional property interest. It also found the other injunction factors favored her. 540–541. The D.C. Circuit denied a stay; Judges Garcia and Childs relied on constitutional due process, while Judge Katsas dissented. The Supreme Court deferred the Government’s stay application, heard argument January 21, and ruled June 29. 541.

This proceeding was an application for interim relief pending appeal, not a final merits appeal after factual development. The record did not even contain the disputed mortgage documents. The Court could settle legal standards but could not decide mortgage fraud, negligence, or ultimate sufficient cause on that record. 558–559.

## Questions and result

Could the Government obtain a stay of the injunction on the grounds that cause was unreviewable, easily established, or beyond judicial interim protection, and had Cook received the pretermination process required by statute? The Court denied the stay, 5–4. The Government had not shown likely success because it had failed to provide the statutory notice and opportunity to respond. The Court rejected categorical nonreviewability, a merely nominal cause threshold, and a categorical bar on interim equitable protection. It also expressly upheld the Federal Reserve’s existing structure against the Article II objection raised by Justice Thomas. It did **not** decide Cook’s constitutional due process claim or whether properly established facts would permit her removal. 541–559, particularly 550, 555–557 & n.6, 558–559.

Chief Justice Roberts wrote for himself and Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh, and Jackson. Kavanaugh and Jackson each concurred separately. Thomas dissented alone; Alito dissented with Gorsuch; Barrett dissented separately. Thus there are five separate writings. The formal disposition at 559 is denial of the application for a stay, not affirmance of a final judgment, dismissal of the suit, or a conclusive ruling that Cook can never be removed.

## The Court’s reasoning

**1. Reviewability follows from a legal limitation.** Section 242 assigns the removal decision to the President but does not make every asserted reason sufficient or exempt the decision from judicial review. Even discretionary executive authority has statutory boundaries that courts interpret. The Government itself acknowledged review where the President identified no cause, and discerning whether a reason is cause necessarily requires interpreting that term. Reagan did not establish universal nonreviewability: its statute referred to causes prescribed by law, but Congress had specified none and had not otherwise provided removal for cause. The common-law materials did not establish the Government’s proposed blanket bar either; some authorities expressly recognized judicial examination of the sufficiency and reasonableness of removal grounds. 542–544.

**2. Cause is substantial but not an exhaustive list borrowed from other statutes.** The Court rejected both competing extremes. The Government’s test would accept almost any concern about conduct, ability, fitness, or competence. Cook would confine cause to particular grounds found in other removal statutes at the time of reenactment, thereby excluding private pre-office conduct categorically. Section 242 incorporates the common-law concept of cause, not an external statutory list that its text never identifies. Common-law sources contemplated a substantial, reasonable, and just disqualification, informed by the particular office. 544–546.

The Federal Reserve’s distinctive design matters to that interpretation. Its regional banks are privately owned, monetary policy has both public and regional-bank participants, its budget is independent of congressional appropriations, and both actual and perceived insulation from short-term political pressure serve its assigned monetary mission. Cause must therefore clear a substantial threshold. Relevant considerations include the seriousness of misconduct and its nexus to professional duties; the inquiry asks whether the asserted reason genuinely shows unfitness or merely masks a desire for a more congenial replacement. Review is deferential, but need not ignore pretext. The Court did not finally define every adequate cause or find the mortgage allegations insufficient after proof. 546–547.

**3. Equity can protect possession of office during litigation.** The Government and Thomas invoked the historical rule that equity did not finally determine title to public office. The Court distinguished that final determination, traditionally entrusted to legal remedies such as mandamus or quo warranto, from interim protection of an incumbent while title was litigated. Historical treatises recognized the latter. Sawyer and White did not, in the majority’s reading, foreclose protection of Cook’s ongoing exercise of functions pending a contest over removal. A Governor otherwise satisfying the preliminary-injunction requirements could remain in office during litigation; no categorical equitable bar prevented that remedy. 547–549. Footnote 2 rejects Thomas’s insistence that Cook needed an express congressional cause of action, relying on traditional suits in equity to prevent unlawful official conduct. 549 n.2.

**4. The statute requires modest but real pretermination process.** The dispositive defect was statutory. A term of years protected by removal for cause carried a background understanding requiring notice and an opportunity to defend before removal. Reagan and Shurtleff supplied a Supreme Court interpretation against which Congress legislated in 1913 and 1935. Express procedural language in other statutes did not eliminate that background rule here. The Court treated the earlier interpretation as governing, not disposable dicta, and distinguished presumptions based on Supreme Court precedent from those requiring a broader consensus among lower courts. An at-will officer with a statutory expiration date has only a ceiling on tenure, unlike a protected fixed term. 550–554 & nn.3–5.

Cook did not have to receive a personal audience with the President or a judicial trial. Written material could suffice. She needed some explanation of the evidence, an avenue for responding with argument and, if necessary, informal proof, and a response deadline before a final removal decision. A social-media demand to resign and a link to reporting did not furnish that process. Her right to respond did not depend on proving in advance that she would persuade the President. Facts and sufficiency of cause remained for later determination after that opportunity. 551–552, 557–559. Because the statute resolved the stay application, the Court did not decide whether her office was constitutional property or what the Due Process Clause independently required. 550.

**5. The Court expressly addresses Article II and the Fed’s history.** The Government did not challenge the constitutionality of the for-cause provision. Nevertheless, responding to Thomas, the Court upheld the Federal Reserve’s existing independence by reference to the First and Second Banks’ separation from presidential control and their monetary functions. Historical practice supplies content to the constitutional structure under modern circumstances; the contemporary institution need not duplicate the early banks precisely. 554–557. This conclusion was contested as both substantively inconsistent with Slaughter and procedurally improper because unbriefed. The majority consciously chose to address it rather than leave uncertainty about the central bank’s status. Footnote 6 expressly limits the conclusion: upholding the existing structure and enforcement authorities does not authorize Congress to add regulatory powers attenuated from monetary policy. Footnote 7 rejects constitutional avoidance because the majority found no statutory ambiguity and because Thomas’s narrower process interpretation would not eliminate his broader objection to any removal limit. 555 n.6, 556 n.7.

**6. The injunction does not confer permanent immunity.** The Court read the injunction as preventing implementation of the August 25 removal letter, while allowing the President to try again through adequate process, subject to judicial review. Footnote 8 rejects Barrett’s broader interpretation and observes that even an overbroad injunction would warrant clarification rather than automatically removing Cook immediately through a full stay. 558–559 n.8. The final paragraph reiterates that facts, not resolved here, will matter to the eventual legality of removal. 559.

## Separate opinions

**Kavanaugh, concurring, 559–562.** He joins the entire opinion and emphasizes both its interim limits and its affirmative recognition of the Fed’s protected status. Whether the President can ultimately remove Cook depends substantially on facts still undetermined. The Government itself accepted the Fed’s historical independence while challenging other agencies’ protections in Slaughter. After Slaughter, leaving the Fed’s Article II status open could invite political upheaval and destabilize domestic and world economies. Historical practice supports its continued independence; eliminating its protections would require legislative change. His footnote identifies Thomas as the only Justice affirmatively arguing that Article II requires at-will removal of Governors. These are Kavanaugh’s additional reasons, not a finding that alleged fraud is harmless or insufficient. 561 n.1.

**Jackson, concurring, 562–565.** She joins the merits reasoning but would deny the stay on equities alone. The Government identified only the President’s inability to remove an official he wanted removed, not a concrete interim injury. Her footnote explains that a genuine real-world injury from Cook’s continued service might change the analysis, while the asserted right to remove remained the disputed legal question. Public-interest evidence emphasized long-term economic stability and dangers from actual or perceived political intimidation of monetary officials. This was Jackson’s additional equitable analysis; the Court itself stopped at likely success. 563 & unnumbered footnote, 564–565.

**Thomas, dissenting, 565–596.** He would grant a stay on merits, judicial-authority, and equitable grounds. He distinguishes the privately operated early banks from the Federal Reserve Board, a federal regulatory agency with substantial executive powers. His historical account attributes its creation to a different administrative tradition; footnote 1 catalogs original regulatory authorities, and the text identifies later ones. He denies that a public office is property under the Due Process Clause, regards alleged mortgage fraud as a sufficient cause given the President’s stated financial-integrity concerns and the presumption of regularity, and rejects an implied hearing requirement absent statutory language. 566–580.

Thomas disputes the majority’s common-law and prior-construction arguments: Reagan and Shurtleff ruled against the removed officers; their hearing language was dicta about different statutes; and no requisite judicial consensus supplied the newly asserted rule. Footnotes 2–5 examine fixed-term statutes, the claimed dicta, express hearing provisions, and contrasting cause provisions. He also sees any restraint on removal of a principal officer exercising executive power as unconstitutional under Article II and Slaughter, or at least as demanding constitutional avoidance. 580–590. Finally, he argues Cook lacks an authorized action overcoming sovereign immunity, has not satisfied demanding ultra vires or mandamus standards, and cannot receive equitable reinstatement. Footnote 6 states the further requirements he believes ordinary nonstatutory review imposes. He rejects the distinction between interim and final equitable intervention and invokes presidential authority and harms recognized in other removal stays. 590–596. These arguments are dissenting positions, not the Court’s interpretation of § 242 or its remedial holding.

**Alito, joined by Gorsuch, dissenting, 596–600.** He criticizes resolving novel, underdeveloped questions through a stay application and identifies possible standing, appellate, right-of-action, and equitable difficulties. A prompt brief ruling on the two grounds actually relied on below would have allowed factual and legal development in the lower courts. He would grant the stay because cause is not limited to in-office conduct and Cook has no constitutional property interest in her office; he would leave statutory process and other questions for later proceedings. The other stay factors, in his view, follow prior removal cases. His unnumbered footnote denies that this limited disposition would imply a judgment against the Fed’s constitutional independence. He does not join Thomas’s categorical Article II challenge. 600 & footnote.

**Barrett, dissenting, 601–604.** She objects to deciding numerous novel questions, particularly a constitutional challenge the Government expressly waived and the parties did not brief. The historical analogy requires more analysis, and the Fed exception conflicts with Slaughter’s categorical account of executive power. She highlights the uncertain boundary of footnote 6’s allowance for existing regulatory powers. She also disputes the majority’s reading of the injunction: its text prohibits removal based on the grounds in the August 25 letter, rather than merely through that letter. Thus, she believes denying a stay leaves the President unable to remove Cook for the alleged mortgage fraud even after adequate process and proof of substantial cause. That interference supplies irreparable harm, albeit reduced because the Government conceded it could not remove Governors for policy reasons. Her proposed full stay and construction of the injunction were rejected by the majority. 603–604; compare 558–559 n.8.

## Assigned-note reconciliation and limits

The assignment accurately presents judicial review, substantial cause, the historical exception, and the competing separate opinions, but several details require care. It calls the lower-court relief a temporary restraining order; the decision reviewed here concerns a preliminary injunction. Its statement that Thomas regarded removal as unreviewable is too broad: the Court expressly distinguishes the Government’s categorical argument from Thomas at 542, while Thomas advances more specific limits on review, action, equity, and Article II. Its newspaper account of a mistaken mortgage checkbox and a lender’s understanding remains attributed reporting, not a Supreme Court factual finding. 540–541, 542, 558–559, 578–579, 590–595.

Note 3 at assigned p. 53 introduces a quotation as Kavanaugh’s Slaughter concurrence, but the quotation and citation belong to his Cook concurrence, official pp. 560–561. The note’s critique of the historical analogy is editorial; Thomas’s historical objections and Kavanaugh’s destabilization concerns must remain identified by author. The assigned account of a later White House notice letter is aftermath, not adjudicated compliance with Cook; its narrative and cited letter dates also differ. This brief does not certify a later removal or later outcome.

Justia corroborates the case’s identity, decision date, and stay disposition. Its editorial summary should not replace the opinion: describing the decision as avoiding broader constitutional questions obscures the express Article II discussion and footnote 6, and the cause discussion should not become a categorical requirement excluding all pre-office conduct. The official text, not the annotation, controls those points.
