# National Development Co. v. Triad Holding Corp.

**930 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1991) — April 15, 1991. Civil Procedure.** Circuit Judge McLaughlin wrote for a panel comprising Judges Kearse, Pratt, and McLaughlin. No separate opinion is reported. Assigned account: CivPro, physical page 153, printed page 258. This brief addresses the appeal concerning the original default judgment compelling arbitration, rather than the later judgment confirming the arbitration award.

## Facts and the two judgments

National Development Company, a corporation wholly owned by the Republic of the Philippines, entered a joint venture called Triad Asia with Triad Holding Corporation, a company controlled by Adnan Khashoggi. When the venture dissolved, NDC alleged that Khashoggi converted approximately $3.5 million in assets that should have gone to NDC. An arbitration clause required disputes concerning the venture to be finally settled under International Chamber of Commerce rules. In August 1986, NDC requested arbitration and sent the request and notice to the defendants by registered or certified mail. Return receipts came back, including receipts from Khashoggi. The ICC twice sent additional copies and again received receipts. None of the defendants responded. These were arbitration communications; their receipt did not independently establish valid service of a federal summons. [Preserved full opinion, lines 64–67.]

NDC therefore brought a federal action to compel arbitration. On December 22, 1986, its process server delivered the summons and complaint to Aurora DaSilva, a housekeeper at Khashoggi’s Olympic Tower condominium on Fifth Avenue in New York. Khashoggi did not appear. On September 23, 1987, the district court entered a default judgment ordering him to arbitrate. The subsequent ICC hearing took place without the defendants in February 1989. Shortly afterward, Khashoggi’s criminal lawyer, Robert G. Morvillo, wrote to the tribunal explaining that participation would be inadvisable while unrelated criminal proceedings remained pending. In April 1989, the tribunal found Triad Holding to be Khashoggi’s alter ego and awarded NDC more than $3.4 million, plus costs, interest, and attorney’s fees, against them jointly and severally. Those were the tribunal’s findings and award; this appeal did not decide the underlying conversion claim. [Lines 68–71.]

NDC then obtained permission to file a supplemental complaint asking the district court to confirm the award under the Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards. It mailed that pleading to Khashoggi’s New York apartment and to Morvillo. On August 4, 1989, another default judgment confirmed the award. Later that month, NDC personally served Khashoggi with postjudgment discovery requests. In October, he moved under Rule 60(b)(4) to vacate both judgments as void for lack of personal jurisdiction resulting from defective service. NDC also commenced a second, separate confirmation action as a precaution. [Lines 72–75.]

After an evidentiary hearing, District Judge Milton Pollack denied vacatur of the 1987 judgment compelling arbitration but granted vacatur of the 1989 confirmation judgment. Only the denial concerning the original 1987 judgment was before the Second Circuit. The district court had reasoned that the New York apartment was not Khashoggi’s dwelling or usual abode under the relevant federal or New York provision, but that his actual notice nonetheless made service adequate. The appellate court rejected that legal reasoning while affirming the result on a different ground: the apartment qualified as an abode under the federal rule. [Lines 76–84.]

## Why the New York apartment mattered

Khashoggi was a Saudi Arabian citizen who regarded his six-villa compound in Riyadh as his domicile. But domicile did not describe the full pattern of his life. In 1986, he spent only three months in Riyadh, another two months at a home in Spain, and much of the remaining time traveling internationally. He maintained twelve locations worldwide, including houses in Rome, Paris, and Monte Carlo. DaSilva testified that he stayed at Olympic Tower for thirty-four days during 1986 and, critically, was staying there from December 15 through December 23. The parties stipulated that she accepted the papers on December 22. Thus he was actually living in the apartment on the date of service. [Lines 76–79, 97.]

This was also a permanently maintained home rather than an occasional hotel booking. The apartment occupied more than 23,000 square feet on at least two floors and was valued at approximately $20–$25 million. It included a swimming pool, sauna, office, and separate furnished apartments for guests and Khashoggi’s brother. Two full-time and three part-time staff members maintained it. Khashoggi had purchased it in 1974, personally arranged more than $1 million in remodeling, and later transferred legal ownership into a chain of companies ultimately wholly owned by him. He also identified it as a residence in a 1989 bail application. These facts supplied evidence of permanence; the court did not announce a minimum property value, size, or number of employees required for valid abode service. [Lines 78–80, 106.]

## Issue, arguments, and rule

The disputed question was whether the New York apartment could be a dwelling or usual place of abode despite Khashoggi’s Saudi domicile and numerous other homes. There was no dispute that DaSilva was a person of suitable age and discretion residing in the apartment. Khashoggi argued that, unless served personally, he could be served at his Saudi compound alone because that was his true dwelling or usual abode. NDC defended the service at the New York home. [Lines 62, 87–92.]

The governing historical provision was Federal Rule 4(d)(1), which permitted leaving the summons and complaint at the individual’s dwelling or usual abode with a suitable person residing there. The assigned textbook presents the comparable method under its later numbering, Rule 4(e)(2)(B). The decision should be understood under the rule actually applied in 1991. Its separate, important proposition is that actual notice does not cure otherwise void service. The court therefore could not affirm merely because Khashoggi knew about the proceeding. Its sole footnote observes that Morvillo’s February 1989 letter undermined Khashoggi’s claimed lack of knowledge until August, but that factual skepticism was not a substitute for compliance with the service rule. [Lines 81–84, 87–92, note 1.]

## Holding and reasoning

A person may have more than one dwelling or usual abode for purposes of service, provided each has sufficient signs of permanence. Reading the rule as allowing only one home would fit poorly with an individual who permanently maintains several residences and travels among them. Citizenship or domicile did not require the court to disregard the places where Khashoggi actually lived. The court examined conflicting state and federal authorities without attempting to settle every possible variation in multi-residence service. It decided the particular facts before it. [Lines 94–105.]

Two features together supported the result: Olympic Tower was permanently maintained as Khashoggi’s home, and he was actually living there when the housekeeper received the papers. Ownership, furnishing, remodeling, and his later identification of the apartment as a residence established permanence. His presence during the December stay connected the service to his actual living arrangements. Under Mullane’s reasonable-notice principle, delivery there to a resident housekeeper was reasonably calculated to alert him to the action. That reasoning validated the particular form of service; it did not revive the district court’s proposition that any defective method becomes valid when notice happens to arrive. [Lines 106–108.]

The court expressly reserved whether service at Olympic Tower would have been valid if Khashoggi had not been actually living there at the time. Accordingly, the textbook’s closing question—whether service should matter differently when he is staying at another dwelling—identifies an issue this opinion leaves open. The brief holding cannot be expanded into permission to serve any one of twelve homes on any date. [Line 108; assigned printed 258.]

## Judgment and assigned significance

The Second Circuit **affirmed the denial of Khashoggi’s Rule 60(b)(4) motion concerning the original default judgment compelling arbitration**. It did not reinstate the separately vacated confirmation judgment or decide the second confirmation action. There is no reported separate writing or numerical voting statement to add. [Line 111.]

The assigned account accurately emphasizes multiple abodes, the substantial New York home, and Khashoggi’s actual presence. The complete opinion adds the crucial distinction between the two defaults, the rejection of actual-notice cure, the undisputed qualifications of the housekeeper, and the express reservation concerning service during an absence.
