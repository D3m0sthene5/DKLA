# Mock v. Garland
75 F.4th 563 (5th Cir. 2023) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and procedural posture

On August 1, 2023, a divided Fifth Circuit reversed the denial of a preliminary injunction against ATF's 2023 stabilizing-brace rule and remanded for the district court to consider the remaining injunction factors and appropriate scope. Judge Jerry Smith wrote the majority opinion, joined fully by Judge Willett; Judge Higginson dissented. The majority concluded that plaintiffs were substantially likely to succeed on their APA notice-and-comment challenge: the final rule was legislative, and the proposal did not give adequate notice of its eventual approach. This was an interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1), not a final judgment setting aside the regulation or resolving all statutory and constitutional challenges. The panel continued its existing injunction pending appeal until the district court ruled or sixty days elapsed, whichever occurred first, and directed a ruling within sixty days. (Official slip opinion 3, 20–23, 38–40.)

## Statutory setting and regulatory chronology

The National Firearms Act's specialized definition of a firearm includes short-barreled rifles, but generally excludes ordinary pistols. A rifle must be designed, made, and intended to be fired from the shoulder; a barrel shorter than sixteen inches ordinarily brings a rifle within the heightened NFA regime. The broader Gun Control Act regulates firearms generally and defines a handgun by its short stock and design for firing with one hand. Consequently, otherwise similar AR-style weapons can receive different treatment depending on whether they have a shoulder stock. Classification as an SBR entails registration, authorization for manufacture or transfer, interstate restrictions, ordinarily a $200 transfer tax, and potential felony consequences for noncompliance. The majority reviewed the NFA's historical response to gangster weapons and Congress's omission of pistols from its eventual coverage; that background supported the stakes of classification rather than a separate holding invalidating Congress's scheme. (Slip 3–9 & nn.3–14.)

A stabilizing brace attaches behind a handgun and supports or attaches to the user's forearm, permitting steadier one-handed firing. In 2012, an applicant submitted a brace designed to assist disabled users. ATF concluded that this specimen did not convert a pistol into a shoulder-fired rifle. Its subsequent letters fluctuated. A March 2014 letter rejected classification merely by how an individual used a weapon; an October letter treated subjective use as potentially altering classification. A 2015 open letter required NFA compliance before using a brace as a shoulder stock. A 2017 clarification said incidental shouldering of an unmodified approved brace did not necessarily redesign the weapon. The agency nevertheless rejected particular configurations whose features suggested shoulder firing, including ineffective straps or additions serving no brace function. The majority described an expanding market accompanied by uncertain and inconsistent classifications, not an unconditional approval of every brace. (Slip 10–13 & nn.15–22.)

ATF published its proposal on June 10, 2021, with comments due September 8. Proposed Worksheet 4999 assigned points to design characteristics and treated a score of at least four in either of two sections as indicating a rifle. Although the majority acknowledged subjectivity and double counting within the worksheet, it offered an identifiable scoring method and three worked examples; an AR-type firearm with an SB-Mini accessory was classified as an approved braced handgun. Approximately 217,000 of 237,000 comments opposed the proposal. Those figures included many form letters, so the opinion did not equate every submission with an independent assessment. (Slip 13–15.)

The January 31, 2023 final rule abandoned the worksheet. It first asked whether a rearward accessory supplied surface area permitting shoulder firing and then considered six factors: weight or length relative to rifles; length of pull; sights or optics requiring shoulder use; whether the shoulder surface was necessary to operate the weapon; manufacturer marketing; and evidence of likely community use. ATF maintained that these factors came from the proposal and that the rule regulated classification rather than banning braces. Its estimate nevertheless suggested that approximately 99% of brace-equipped pistols would be classified as rifles. About sixty accompanying classifications supplied results without explanations; the majority could find no affirmative example of an exempt braced handgun and noted that the earlier SB-Mini example now appeared classified as an SBR. (Slip 15–17 & nn.23–26.)

The rule became effective January 31, with a compliance deadline of May 31. Options included replacing the short barrel with one at least sixteen inches long; permanently removing and disposing of or altering the brace so it could not be reattached; surrendering or destroying the firearm; or registering it as an SBR. Timely registration received a tax waiver. Mere detachment while retaining an unaltered brace was not the compliance option described in the rule; note 28 distinguished contrary congressional testimony and ATF's subsequent clarification. Approximately 250,000 registration applications did not establish total compliance because brace removals, destruction, and surrender were uncounted. (Slip 17–19 & nn.27–31.)

## Parties, litigation, and competing positions

Texas residents William Mock and Christopher Lewis owned braced pistols and wanted to purchase additional ones. Maxim Defense manufactured and sold braces and braced pistols, allegedly deriving most revenue from affected products. Firearms Policy Coalition represented gun owners and businesses; the other three plaintiffs were members. They challenged ATF's statutory authority, invoked lenity and nondelegation, advanced APA procedural and constitutional objections, and sought injunctive relief. (Slip 19–22.)

The Northern District of Texas denied a preliminary injunction. It considered ATF likely authorized to issue the rule, rejected lenity and vagueness arguments, treated the rule as interpretive, and alternatively found adequate logical outgrowth. It also rejected the preliminary First and Second Amendment claims. A Fifth Circuit motions panel then granted an injunction pending appeal on May 23. The merits panel's May 26 clarification included represented customers and members of Maxim Defense and the Coalition, together with the individual plaintiffs' family members. The court was reviewing denial of preliminary relief, not reviewing a completed trial record. (Slip 20–21, 40.)

The plaintiffs argued that replacing a points worksheet with an opaque balancing system deprived them of an opportunity to comment on the actual regulatory method. ATF answered that it had clarified preexisting statutory coverage, so the rule was interpretive and exempt from compulsory notice and comment. Alternatively, its announced subject and the factors discussed in the proposal supplied notice, and removal of the unpopular worksheet responded to comments. ATF also asserted harmless error. The majority considered the statutory-authority, lenity, and nondelegation arguments potentially colorable but unnecessary to decide. It likewise left the constitutional claims for appropriate later consideration. Importantly, note 38 identifies a distinct arbitrary-and-capricious challenge as forfeited on appeal: plaintiffs' opening brief did not present that standard. The court's criticism of the rule must not be converted into an adjudicated § 706(2)(A) holding. (Slip 22–24 n.38, 31–38 & n.59.)

## Majority reasoning: legislative character

The panel distinguished rules carrying legal force from interpretations advising the public how an agency understands existing law. Mandatory language alone cannot resolve the distinction, because a genuine interpretation of a mandatory statute can also sound mandatory. Nor did plaintiffs correctly identify the test by citing criteria for policy statements. Acknowledging uncertainty in circuit precedent, the majority adopted the district court's five-factor approach in Cargill: intended legal force, CFR publication, invocation of legislative authority, a claim to Chevron deference, and significant effects on private interests. It did not treat the agency's label as controlling. (Slip 23–26 & nn.35–43.)

ATF's disclaimer of Chevron deference weighed against legislative classification. The other considerations pointed the opposite way. The rule amended two CFR definitions, invoked delegated statutory enforcement authority, used prospective compliance language with a deadline and specified disposal methods, and announced circumstances under which possession would expose owners to penalties. Comparing Guedes, the majority found that this language functioned as a new binding command rather than advice that unchanged conduct had always been unlawful. An agency could not avoid scrutiny of a rule's effects simply by saying that a criminal court would ultimately apply the statute. (Slip 26–31 & nn.44–50.)

The estimated effect also mattered under the majority's test: the low estimate produced annualized combined private and governmental costs exceeding $245 million at a 3% discount rate and ten-year costs approaching $1.9 billion. These were ATF's estimates, not independently established economic losses. The panel emphasized the contrast between longstanding letters that facilitated lawful brace ownership and the new consequences imposed on millions of owners. Taken together, the factors made the rule legislative and subject to APA § 553. (Slip 28–31 & nn.46–49.)

## Majority reasoning: notice, logical outgrowth, and prejudice

An agency need not announce every precise final provision. It must, however, adequately frame the issues so affected persons could reasonably anticipate the eventual course and comment meaningfully. The majority read that requirement as demanding sufficient similarity in regulatory kind, not simply a shared broad subject. The worksheet was the proposal's organizing method; ATF had even described it as necessary for consistent enforcement. Requests for additional or better criteria did not announce possible replacement of that method with an unweighted six-factor balancing test. Criticism of the worksheet's subjectivity and overbreadth did not notify participants that the final rule would become still more discretionary and expansive. (Slip 31–34.)

The court stressed the loss of a method by which an owner could assess a firearm and contest ATF's result. It saw neither explained classifications nor a reliable exemption example in the final rule. Manufacturer marketing and community use introduced evidence concerning third parties beyond the owner's control; in the majority's reading these were new features not fairly disclosed by the proposal. If comments showed the entire method needed replacement, ATF should have published a new notice allowing comment on that replacement. General responsiveness to comments did not excuse inadequate notice of the alternative eventually selected. (Slip 33–37 & nn.51–56.)

Under circuit precedent a procedural challenger needed to show prejudice. The majority found it in plaintiffs' inability to comment on the substantially changed method, supplemented by briefing identifying comments they would have offered. It distinguished City of Arlington's adequate advance notice and rejected an absolute requirement that every logical-outgrowth challenger prove specific additional comments, discussing City of Waukesha's qualifications. Its statement that the unlawful rule must be set aside or remanded for remediation expressed the likely APA consequence; the actual judgment in this preliminary appeal remained reversal and remand of the injunction denial. (Slip 37–38 & nn.57–59.)

## Remaining injunction factors and exact relief

Likelihood of success satisfied only one of four requirements. Plaintiffs still had to establish irreparable injury, favorable equities, and consistency with the public interest; governmental and public interests merge when the government is the opposing party. Although the panel discussed a sliding-scale approach, note 60 explicitly warned that a strong merits showing cannot substitute for attention to the other factors. Because the district court had not made those findings and the record was limited, the panel declined plaintiffs' request that it issue nationwide relief itself. (Slip 21, 38–39 & nn.60–61.)

Scope also required first-instance factual assessment. The majority observed that injunctions ordinarily provide complete relief to plaintiffs, sometimes benefiting nonparties incidentally; broad organizational membership could justify broader relief in an appropriate case. Other brace injunctions already complicated enforcement, but that did not determine the remedy here. The court reversed, remanded for expeditious consideration, and temporarily preserved its existing injunction with the earlier clarification. It expressly imposed no restriction on the district court's other matters or anticipated decisions. Higginson also emphasized that remand permitted a narrow injunction or no injunction if remaining factors so required. (Slip 39–40 & nn.62–63; Higginson 58.)

## Separate writings

Willett joined the majority completely. His two-page concurrence suggested that common modifications improving the stability and accuracy of otherwise lawful arms could be protected by the Second Amendment and that ATF had not identified a historical tradition supporting a lengthy, costly, discretionary approval process for such accessories. Even an attachment meeting the statutory rifle definition might present a constitutional question. He agreed that the APA ground sufficed for this early appeal; his proposed constitutional analysis was not the panel's holding. (Slip 41–42 & concurrence nn.1–2.)

Higginson would affirm. He first questioned announcing a new legislative-rule test on expedited review when plaintiffs devoted only half a page to an incorrect standard and ATF's briefing offered little more. His central doctrinal objection was that substantial impact distinguishes substantive rules from procedural rules, not legislative rules from interpretive rules: even a correct interpretation of existing law can have major economic consequences. He traced the cited authorities through Gulf Restoration, Texas, Kast Metals, Cabais, and Baylor to explain the mismatch. In a particular enforcement case, courts could apply the statute independently without Chevron deference and assess the actual firearm. (Slip 43–49 & dissent nn.1–6; the firearm comparison photographs in n.1 were also inspected.)

Even assuming a legislative rule, Higginson found adequate notice. Replacing a heavily criticized worksheet was a foreseeable response to comments. Plaintiffs' counsel's center had called the worksheet incomprehensible, and the Coalition itself had attacked the proposal as subjective, malleable, and discretionary. Those comments showed awareness of precisely the difficulties later asserted as surprises. Four final factors concerned physical design; marketing and likely use were objective evidence of the statutory requirement of intended shoulder firing. He rejected the majority's additional similarity-in-kind requirement as inconsistent with precedent requiring fair notice of subjects and issues, and found any defect harmless because plaintiffs did not show an omitted argument that could change the outcome. (Slip 50–55 & dissent nn.7–9.)

Higginson also answered Willett. Improved accuracy could increase lethality when combined with concealability, rather than necessarily establish constitutional safety benefits. Plaintiffs did not challenge the NFA itself; its registration treatment of dangerous and unusual weapons differed from an outright ban, and Bruen did not invalidate all licensing regimes. He considered the preliminary constitutional showing insufficient, without making that position a majority constitutional ruling. (Slip 55–58 & dissent n.10.)
