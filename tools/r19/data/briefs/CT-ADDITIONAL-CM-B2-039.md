# Denbow v. Tesch
66 S.D. 21, 278 N.W. 16 (1938) | Contracts

## Court and procedural posture

The South Dakota Supreme Court decided the appeal on February 25, 1938. Justice Smith wrote for a four-member majority, joined by Presiding Justice Roberts and Justices Polley and Rudolph. Justice Warren dissented, but the report supplies only his dissenting vote, not a separate written explanation. Isabel Denbow sued her siblings to cancel a family settlement for duress and menace and to obtain an accounting from her brother Walter for their deceased father’s estate. The trial court avoided the agreement, fixed an amount owed by Walter to the estate, and denied a new trial. On appeal, the parties conceded that the accounting judgment depended on the cancellation judgment. The Supreme Court therefore confined its substantive analysis to the settlement’s validity and the evidence supporting the trial court’s findings. (21–22, 31.)

## Family property and the agreement

John and Mary Bones had accumulated property in their respective names. Mary died in 1929 after preparing conveyances to children Victoria, Fred, and Isabel. Isabel testified that the deeds were delivered before Mary’s death; Victoria said they were found in a cedar chest afterward. The land attributed to Victoria and Fred carried approximately $7,400 in encumbrances apiece, while Isabel’s was clear. John had also advanced substantial sums or property to other children. His transaction with Walter called for $34,000 to be paid in monthly installments or as needed for support. When John died on April 5, 1935, his remaining property consisted of the family home, an adjoining lot, household goods, and a balance due from Walter. A recent statement of that balance had been shown to Isabel and placed in John’s account book. (22–23.)

Isabel was forty, of ordinary intelligence and experience, and familiar with her father’s affairs. Her siblings were older; apart from Walter, they were fairly comparable in intelligence and experience. Walter had broad business experience and a strong personality. The settlement arose from three conferences on May 9 and 10, 1935. It contemplated equalizing the children’s shares by accounting for prior advances and property values, subtracting encumbrances, and dividing future sale proceeds equally. As ultimately revised, only Walter had to repay an excess over the average share. The agreement also arranged disposition of the house and lot, appointment of Victoria as administrator, and division of personal property between Victoria and Isabel. It provided $30 monthly for Isabel’s care of an aunt, a $275 settlement for past care, and a credit for net farm rents her father had collected. These reciprocal arrangements form the context for the court’s rejection of a separate finding of no consideration. The opinion does not calculate an ultimate distributive share for every child. (23–25, 27, 30–31.)

## The three conferences and conflicting testimony

At the first afternoon conference, the children listed prior receipts and valued property. Isabel participated and made objections or suggestions. She testified that four siblings pressed her and that she refused to sign; they described her departure differently. That evening, Walter and Harry visited her home and demanded her father’s papers and the statement of Walter’s debt. Isabel refused to produce the statement. She testified that Walter accused her of stealing it and threatened arrest if she did not produce it. When Harry proposed searching the house, she threatened to get a gun and shoot him. Walter then called her insane and said that, if they did the right thing, they would send her to Yankton. Harry refused to leave when ordered out. Isabel neither signed that night nor surrendered the statement until trial. (25–27.)

The following morning, all the siblings met again, inventoried household goods, and agreed on their division. According to the defendants, Isabel objected to mortgaging her farm to equalize shares, and the repayment clause was changed to apply only to Walter. They also described changes addressing her aunt’s care and her father’s collections of farm rent. Isabel testified that Walter would “law it and law it” if she did not sign and that Harry said he would like to beat her. When she wanted to see her attorney, Harry responded that the family had done business without one. She finally signed around two in the afternoon, saying she had eaten nothing that day, was nervous, and would do almost anything to get them out of her home. The siblings testified that she afterward embraced Walter and expressed relief; she denied knowing what she was doing if that occurred. These conflicting accounts are reported testimony, not uniformly adopted findings. (27–28.)

## The majority’s evidentiary distinction

The trial court had found threats of deprivation of property through litigation, arrest, and commitment to an asylum if Isabel refused to sign. The Supreme Court deliberately recounted testimony tending to support those findings, while omitting a substantial body of defense evidence. Even on that presentation, it concluded that the evidence did not support connecting the arrest and asylum statements to execution of the settlement. (28–30.)

Isabel initially testified that the siblings threatened to send her to Yankton if she refused to sign. On cross-examination, however, she acknowledged uncertainty when asked whether that threat had instead occurred two weeks later when they wanted her signature on a deed. A later threat was undisputed, but it was not evidence of a threat inducing this earlier agreement. The majority characterized her qualification as a retraction and assessed the remaining inference from the evening dispute. The arrest statement concerned withholding the debt statement; the insanity remark followed her threat to shoot Harry. It treated these as a mutual outburst of temper over the papers, rather than a threat conditioned on signing the settlement. That distinction defeated the trial court’s principal factual findings. The majority did not announce that a threat of unlawful arrest or confinement could never constitute duress. (28–30.)

## Litigation pressure and the required legal threat

Once those threats were removed from the cancellation analysis, the court considered the threat to prolong litigation. Even assuming that it actually induced Isabel’s signature, the majority held that this particular threat was not legally sufficient under Revised Code §§ 813 and 814 and the cited duress authority. It was not a threat of confinement, unlawful or violent injury to person or property, or injury to character. Equity followed the law in identifying duress or menace. Thus actual causal pressure alone did not make this threat legally actionable. (30.)

Isabel’s counsel urged a modern, subjective assessment that considered the parties’ individual character and relationships rather than hypothetical external standards of fortitude. The court answered that before measuring a threat’s effect on an individual mind, a legally qualifying threat had to be established. It did not substitute a finding that every person of ordinary firmness would resist. It held that, on the evidence it accepted, the antecedent legal requirement was absent. This is the historical court’s analysis of these facts and its then-governing statutes, not a universal modern rule permitting every threat of civil litigation. (30.)

## Consideration and exact result

The trial court also found the agreement wholly without consideration for Isabel. The Supreme Court found the contract’s provisions sufficient on a cursory examination and noted that her appellate counsel supplied no supporting theory for the contrary finding. It did not require a separate cash payment to Isabel as the sole permissible consideration or adjudicate the precise value of each reciprocal undertaking. It concluded that the clear preponderance of the evidence opposed the crucial coercion findings and the finding of no consideration, leaving the legal conclusions unsupported. (30–31.)

The judgment and order of the trial court were reversed. This displaced cancellation of the settlement and, by the parties’ conceded dependency, the accompanying accounting judgment against Walter. The opinion states no new damages award and provides no separate remand instruction or ultimate estate distribution. Warren’s unexplained dissenting vote must remain distinct from the majority’s reasoning. (22, 31.)
