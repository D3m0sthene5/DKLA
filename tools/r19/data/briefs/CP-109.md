# Beacon Theatres, Inc. v. Westover

**Course:** Civil Procedure
**ID:** CP-109
**Review:** Complete source comparison; historical rule attribution repaired. Independent source review passed.

## Citation

Beacon Theatres, Inc. v. Westover, 359 U.S. 500 (1959). Supreme Court of the United States; argued December 10, 1958; decided May 25, 1959. Justice Black wrote for Chief Justice Warren and Justices Douglas, Clark, and Brennan. Justice Stewart dissented, joined by Justices Harlan and Whittaker. Justice Frankfurter took no part. Casebook pp. 1054–1060; full judicial opinion: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-359/pdf/USREPORTS-359-500.pdf. The discussion of the dissent, temporary equitable relief, and mandamus draws in part on the full opinion. The decision concerns the order of trial and the right to a jury, not the ultimate legality of the theater arrangements.

## Parties

Beacon Theatres operated a new drive-in theater and sought a jury trial of its antitrust counterclaim. Fox West Coast Theatres, operator of a competing first-run theater, had initiated the underlying action for declaratory and injunctive relief. Another theater operator intervened and became the target of Beacon’s cross-claim. District Judge Harry C. Westover was the respondent in the mandamus proceeding because Beacon sought an order controlling his proposed trial procedure; he was not a party to the commercial dispute. 359 U.S. at 502–504; casebook pp. 1054–1055.

## Material Facts

Fox operated a theater in San Bernardino, California, under distribution arrangements giving it first-run rights and periods of clearance. A clearance barred competing exhibitors from showing a film during an agreed period following its first run. Beacon opened a drive-in about eleven miles away. Whether the theaters were in substantial competition mattered because a clearance that restricted a theater outside the relevant competitive relationship could be unreasonable under the antitrust laws. Id. at 502–503. Beacon contended that Fox’s arrangements restrained its access to films and warned that it could seek antitrust treble damages. Fox responded by suing for a declaration that the clearances were reasonable and that its arrangements did not violate the antitrust laws. It also sought to restrain Beacon from bringing antitrust litigation while the declaratory action was pending. The Ninth Circuit read the complaint as seeking broader relief against threats interfering with Fox’s business relationships; the Supreme Court majority considered the case even on that reading. Id. at 502–503, 505–506. Beacon denied the relevant allegations and asserted a counterclaim against Fox and a cross-claim against the intervening exhibitor, alleging an antitrust conspiracy and seeking treble damages. The parties’ opposing positions therefore raised overlapping factual questions. A finding that Fox’s clearances were reasonable, or that the theaters were in substantial competition, could affect the later adjudication of Beacon’s damages claim. Those were disputed questions, not findings the Supreme Court made. Id. at 503–504.

## Procedural History

Beacon demanded a jury on the issues involved in its damages claim. The district judge directed that Fox’s supposedly equitable issues be tried to the court first, with the counterclaim and cross-claim to follow. Rules 42(b) and 57 were invoked in support of the sequencing. Although the order did not formally deny every later jury proceeding, the judge’s earlier findings could bind the common issues and substantially restrict what the jury would be permitted to decide. Id. at 503–504. Beacon petitioned the Ninth Circuit for mandamus to protect its jury right. The court of appeals denied relief, reasoning that the declaratory and injunctive claims could be treated as equitable and that the district judge possessed discretion to determine the order of trial. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed. It held that the proposed equitable-first procedure could not, in the circumstances presented, deprive Beacon of jury determination of factual issues common to its legal damages claim. Id. at 504–511.

## Issue

May a court try a plaintiff’s declaratory and injunctive antitrust claims without a jury before a defendant’s compulsory treble-damages counterclaim, when the first trial could conclusively determine facts common to the legal claim? Does the availability of discretionary sequencing or the plaintiff’s earlier filing justify that effect, and can mandamus protect the jury right? Id. at 504–511.

## Rule and Holding

When legal and equitable claims present common factual issues, trial management must preserve the jury right attached to the legal claim. Discretion over sequencing is narrowly confined by that obligation. The Court stated that only the most imperative circumstances could justify losing jury determination of legal issues through a prior equitable adjudication, and it anticipated no such circumstances in the case before it. Id. at 508–511. Fox could not obtain priority over Beacon’s jury right by filing first for a declaration of antitrust nonliability and adding a request for an injunction. Modern joinder and declaratory procedures made it possible to resolve the entire controversy in one action while preserving jury determination of the common legal issues. Mandamus was available to correct the improper denial of that right. Id. at 504–511.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court reversed the Ninth Circuit’s judgment denying mandamus. The district court’s discretion could not be exercised to defeat Beacon’s right to a jury on the common factual issues. The Court neither awarded antitrust damages nor decided whether the clearances were lawful. Its ruling governed the manner in which the underlying dispute would be tried. Id. at 510–511.

## Reasoning

The legal character of Beacon’s claim was central. An action for antitrust treble damages was a legal claim carrying a jury right. Fox’s request for a declaration that it was not liable anticipated that controversy. The Declaratory Judgment Act supplied a procedure for obtaining earlier resolution; it did not allow a party to eliminate the jury right that would attend the coercive action whose liability it sought to negate. Otherwise the right could depend on which adversary reached the courthouse first. Id. at 504–505. The Court separately examined the request for injunctive relief. Fox sought to prevent Beacon from initiating other proceedings pending resolution of the declaratory suit, and the Ninth Circuit construed the complaint to encompass protection against business interference from threats of suit. The majority assumed that broader construction for purposes of analysis. Even a genuine equitable component did not itself justify resolving the common legal issues first without a jury. The court had to examine whether equitable relief required that sequence and whether the legal procedure provided an adequate remedy. Id. at 505–507. The adequacy of a legal remedy had to be assessed in light of available modern procedure. Older cases sometimes permitted an equity proceeding to go first because separate procedural systems made it difficult to settle the entire controversy at law. The Federal Rules’ single civil action, joinder provisions, and the availability of a declaratory judgment changed those practical conditions. A court could hear the legal and equitable aspects in the same lawsuit rather than forcing the parties into separate, incomplete proceedings. Historical limitations on joinder therefore could not simply be assumed to require equitable priority. Id. at 507–509. Preclusion made the proposed sequence consequential. Beacon was not adequately protected by the prospect of a jury at some later stage if the judge would already have conclusively resolved the facts that mattered to the damages claim. A jury allowed to decide only what remained after the judge’s binding findings would not supply the same right as a jury deciding the common factual issues in the first instance. The inquiry concerned the actual effect of the order, rather than its formal preservation of a later jury date. Id. at 504, 508–510. A practicable alternative preserved both forms of relief. The common issues could be submitted to a jury as part of Beacon’s legal claim, after which the judge could determine any remaining equitable matters consistently with the jury’s findings. Fox had no corresponding constitutional right to insist that a judge decide those common facts before a jury. The Court also noted the availability of temporary equitable relief in an appropriate case while the controversy was being resolved. Preserving the jury right did not require courts to abandon every protective equitable measure. Id. at 508–510. Rules authorizing separate trials or declaratory relief did not confer unlimited discretion. The Court constrained Rule 42(b) discretion by the constitutional jury right and Rule 38(a), which expressly preserved that right. The version of Rule 42(b) quoted in the 1959 opinion did not itself contain an express jury-preservation clause. 359 U.S. at 503 n.3, 510 n.16. Convenience and efficiency remained relevant, but they operated within that constraint. The majority’s formulation left a narrow theoretical possibility for imperative circumstances rather than announcing that no issue could ever be tried separately; it found no basis for sacrificing the jury right here. Id. at 510–511. Finally, the Court treated mandamus as an appropriate means of enforcing a jury right improperly denied. The issue was not simply whether an appellate court preferred another convenient order of proof. A trial order threatened a constitutional allocation of factfinding, and the discretionary label attached to that order did not place it beyond correction. The All Writs Act supplied authority for the protective writ. Id. at 511.

## Separate Opinions

Justice Stewart, joined by Harlan and Whittaker, would have sustained the denial of mandamus. He emphasized that declaratory proceedings may concern either legal or equitable rights and that Fox’s complaint, liberally construed, sought equitable protection of existing contractual and business relationships against continuing threats. In his view, the majority too readily characterized the original suit as a reversed damages action rather than examining that equitable controversy on its own terms. Id. at 511–516 (Stewart, J., dissenting). Stewart relied on the historical principle that properly invoked equity jurisdiction did not disappear merely because a defendant later asserted a legal counterclaim. The compulsory nature of Beacon’s counterclaim protected Beacon from a theory that it had voluntarily waived a jury, but did not, in his view, change the nature of Fox’s original equitable claim. Rules merging procedure could not enlarge the substantive jury right beyond its historical scope. Id. at 514–518. The dissent also defended trial-court discretion over sequence. Historically, equitable findings could have consequences for related legal claims, and Stewart did not regard that possibility as automatically requiring the legal claim to be tried first. He saw no demonstrated abuse justifying an extraordinary writ and would have allowed the district court’s proposed procedure. The disagreement thus involved both the characterization of Fox’s claim and the effect of modern procedure on historical equitable priority. Id. at 516–519. Justice Frankfurter did not participate. There was no separate concurrence. The division was five Justices in the majority and three in dissent; the nonparticipating Justice should not be counted with either side. Id. at 511.

## Notes

Classify the underlying rights and identify common factual issues before choosing a sequence. A declaratory label alone does not establish whether a jury is required, and an injunction request alone does not permit the judge to preempt facts necessary to a legal damages claim. The decisive procedural problem in Beacon was the threatened binding effect of the judge’s earlier findings on issues Beacon was entitled to have a jury determine. Id. at 504–510. The decision preserves the distinction between jury factfinding and judicial equitable relief. A judge may still determine genuinely equitable questions and shape an injunction after the common factual issues have been resolved in a manner respecting the jury right. Likewise, the majority’s discussion of temporary relief should not be confused with permission to enter a final equitable adjudication that disposes of the legal issues first. Id. at 508–511.
