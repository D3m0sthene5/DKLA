# Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau

**Citation and court:** 591 U.S. 197 (2020), Supreme Court of the United States, No. 19–7, decided June 29, 2020. Chief Justice Roberts wrote Parts I–III for Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh. His Part IV severability analysis was joined by Alito and Kavanaugh. Kagan, joined by Ginsburg, Breyer, and Sotomayor, dissented from the constitutional holding but concurred in the judgment concerning severability. Thomas, joined by Gorsuch, concurred in part and dissented in part, favoring rejection of the investigative-demand enforcement petition without reaching severability. The constitutional result was 5–4; seven Justices agreed the agency should survive by severing the removal protection, although only three joined Roberts's Part IV rationale.

The full 102-page report, including both separate writings, was read before drafting: twelve Roberts notes, eight Thomas notes, and fourteen Kagan notes. The original report contains no appendices. Counsel and amici lists are distinguished from judicial reasoning.

**Facts and agency design.** Congress created the CFPB in the 2010 Dodd-Frank Act after the housing and financial collapse. It consolidated administration of eighteen existing consumer-finance statutes and added a prohibition on unfair, deceptive, or abusive practices. The agency could issue regulations, investigate, serve subpoenas and civil investigative demands, prosecute civil actions, conduct adjudications, and order legal or equitable relief. The opinion recounted more than $11 billion in consumer relief, including a $1 billion bank penalty, as historical evidence of its enforcement reach. 591 U.S. at 205–207.

Congress placed these powers under a single Senate-confirmed Director serving five years and removable only for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance. Funding came from the Federal Reserve, subject to a statutory ceiling, rather than annual appropriations. Initial proposals had contemplated a multimember body. Id. at 205–208. These facts describe the statutory arrangement challenged in 2020, not a certification of all subsequent agency arrangements.

Seila Law, a California firm providing debt-related legal services, received a 2017 demand for information concerning potentially unlawful advertising, marketing, or sale of debt-relief services. It objected that the Director's removal protection violated separation of powers and refused compliance. The CFPB petitioned for enforcement; the district court ordered compliance with a modification immaterial here. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, viewing Humphrey's Executor and Morrison as controlling despite differences between the CFPB and the agencies in those cases. Id. at 208–209. The Supreme Court did not decide whether the firm actually committed unlawful consumer practices.

**Threshold questions.** A court-appointed amicus defended the judgment because the Government agreed that the removal restriction was unconstitutional. The parties continued to dispute enforceability of the demand. Three objections did not prevent merits review. Id. at 209–213.

First, Seila Law had not invoked district-court jurisdiction; it was defending the CFPB's suit. It also had appellate standing because compelled disclosure was concrete injury traceable to the judgment and redressable by rejecting enforcement. The Court did not require a counterfactual showing that a President with unrestricted removal authority would have prevented the challenged demand. Arguments about later Directors and alleged ratification did not eliminate jurisdiction. The Court noted that the current Director's agreement with the Government did not establish that she would disregard the statutory protection if removal were attempted. Id. at 210–211 & n.1.

Second, review need not await a contested presidential removal. An affected private party could challenge the exercise of executive power while its holder enjoyed allegedly unconstitutional insulation. Third, constitutional agreement between the litigants did not eliminate their concrete dispute over compliance; appointed adversarial argument addressed prudential concerns. Id. at 212–213. These were threshold holdings, not a final ruling that all prior agency actions were void.

**Constitutional issue and holding.** Could Congress insulate a single principal officer, wielding significant executive power, from presidential removal except on the specified grounds? The five-Justice majority held that this CFPB arrangement violated separation of powers. It declined to extend existing exceptions to this configuration and expressly did not overrule Humphrey's Executor, Morrison, or other removal precedents. Id. at 204–205, 213–232.

**Majority's removal framework.** Article II vests executive power in the President, who must supervise the subordinate officers needed to discharge executive responsibilities. The majority traced a general removal power through the Decision of 1789, Myers, and Free Enterprise Fund. It identified two recognized categories of permissible protection: the expert multimember agency described in Humphrey's Executor and certain inferior officers with limited duties described in Perkins and Morrison. Id. at 213–218.

The majority acknowledged that the 1935 FTC's activities would now be understood as executive to some degree. It nevertheless treated the powers and institutional characteristics on which Humphrey's Executor itself relied as defining that precedent's reach. Wiener similarly concerned an adjudicatory commission. Principal and inferior officer status involved duties and supervision rather than a single universal criterion. Id. at 215–217 & nn.2–3.

The CFPB did not fit either category. Its Director was a principal officer with broad policymaking, enforcement, and adjudication powers over private citizens and businesses. Unlike the described FTC, it lacked partisan balance and staggered membership, and could impose final relief or seek substantial court penalties rather than merely recommend action. Unlike Morrison's counsel, its jurisdiction and administrative responsibilities were broad. Id. at 218–220 & n.4.

**History, concentration, and accountability.** The majority found four isolated examples of protected single heads—the Civil War Comptroller of the Currency, Office of Special Counsel, Social Security Administration, and FHFA—insufficient historical support for this arrangement. They were brief, modern, contested, or materially different in authority. The OSC was distinct from the independent counsel in Morrison and the later investigatory special counsel. The historical financial-regulator tradition did not, in the majority's account, justify the CFPB's distinctive combination of powers. Id. at 220–222 & nn.5–8.

Its structural account distinguished the President, whose singular authority carries electoral accountability, from an unelected subordinate able to make major policy without removable supervision. The Director's five-year term could prevent a President from choosing the agency's leadership; independent funding reduced another avenue of influence. The Financial Stability Oversight Council's limited veto of rules threatening financial stability did not control enforcement or adjudication and did not cure the constitutional defect. Id. at 222–226 & n.9. The funding discussion supported the removal analysis; it was not a separate decision invalidating the CFPB's funding statute.

The majority rejected a theory making modest removal limits generally permissible, with only congressional participation or total removal elimination forbidden. It also rejected constitutional avoidance through an elastic reading of inefficiency, neglect, or malfeasance: no workable text-based construction had been offered, and Humphrey's Executor had not allowed policy disagreement to satisfy the identical standard. Avoidance could not rewrite the statute. Id. at 226–232 & nn.10–11.

**Severability and unresolved ratification.** Seila Law sought rejection of enforcement. The Government contended that an Acting Director, removable at will, had ratified the demand. Whether ratification occurred and whether it legally cured the problem had not been resolved below or fully briefed here. The lower courts would consider those matters first. Roberts's plurality regarded severability as necessary because, if the agency's powers fell with the protection, remand on ratification would be futile. Id. at 232–233.

The plurality severed only the Director's removal protection. The remaining CFPB provisions could function independently, and Dodd-Frank expressly directed that invalidity of any provision should not affect the remainder. Placement of the clause early in a long statute and another, redundant subtitle clause did not deprive the language of effect. Congress's preference for an independent agency did not establish a preference for no agency over a dependent one. Abolishing it would disrupt transferred statutory responsibilities, including powers no predecessor possessed. Id. at 233–237.

Congress remained free to consider other designs, including a multimember agency. The judicial disposition preserved the CFPB's powers with a Director removable at will. The plurality did not decide Thomas's separate argument that a tenure-protected successor's litigation activity itself required dismissal regardless of ratification. Id. at 233 n.12, 237–238. The Court vacated and remanded; it did not affirm enforcement, dismiss the suit outright, or find ratification valid.

**Thomas's partial concurrence and dissent.** Joined by Gorsuch, Thomas supported the constitutional holding and urged eventual reconsideration of Humphrey's Executor in its entirety. He rejected the idea that agencies could exercise quasi-legislative or quasi-judicial power outside the Constitution's three branches and regarded Morrison, Free Enterprise Fund, and this decision as undermining that precedent's rationale. His historical discussion of Humphrey's removal and the later salary suit explained his criticism rather than adding a new disposition in that earlier case. Id. at 238–251 & nn.1–4. The assigned text says the Justices argued for overruling; the full writing specifies reconsideration in a future case, while joining the narrower present holding.

Thomas would deny the enforcement petition without deciding severability. Courts render judgments in concrete disputes and disregard unconstitutional laws; they do not erase statutes. He argued that any Acting Director ratification could not cure continuing enforcement by a successor still protected under the statute. The Government had disclaimed current-Director ratification merely from her view that the law was unconstitutional. Id. at 251–256 & nn.5–6.

He also questioned modern severability's hypothetical congressional-intent inquiry. Multiple provisions together caused the alleged defect: the removal rule and enforcement authority. The severability clause did not necessarily identify which provision to disregard and could be read as addressing an unconstitutional application. He criticized the plurality's choice of removal protection over enforcement powers, comparing Booker and Free Enterprise Fund. Those were his objections, not the controlling severability rationale. Id. at 256–261 & nn.7–8.

**Kagan's separate writing: text and history.** Joined by Ginsburg, Breyer, and Sotomayor, Kagan would sustain the removal protection. She read the Constitution's silence on removal, Necessary and Proper Clause, Appointments Clause, and interdependent branch structure as permitting broad legislative choices so long as the President retained capacity to perform constitutional duties. Faithful execution required compliance with law, not necessarily agreement with every presidential policy preference. The Opinions Clause supplied further reason to doubt an already exhaustive supervisory power. Id. at 261–269 & nn.1–3.

She disputed the claim that 1789 settled unrestricted removal, describing competing positions and a decision rejecting required Senate participation without resolving Congress's authority to impose other limits. Treasury arrangements, the Second Bank, Comptroller legislation, the Interstate Commerce Commission, and later financial regulators supported a tradition of independence. Her note 6 recognized limits concerning the President's own military or diplomatic responsibilities and treated the broad Tenure of Office Act differently. These were disputed historical interpretations, not additional unanimous findings. Id. at 269–276 & nn.4–6.

**Kagan on precedent and institutional design.** Kagan viewed Humphrey's Executor, Wiener, and Morrison as limiting Myers and establishing a functional inquiry into interference with presidential duties. Executive character did not alone invalidate protection. Her notes explain that agency actions may take legislative or judicial forms while remaining executive, and distinguish execution of statutes from the President's independent war or foreign-affairs responsibilities. Id. at 276–282 & nn.7–9.

The CFPB exercised powers comparable to other independent financial regulators and used the same familiar removal language. She disputed that the 1935 FTC lacked meaningful enforcement authority, noted that funding and long terms were common, and challenged the majority's treatment of Morrison's duties and office status. Novelty did not itself defeat a congressional choice. Id. at 284–291 & nn.10–12.

In her view, a single head could be easier to monitor and influence than several commissioners. Oversight, rule review, appointments, budgets, organizational rules, and informal relationships mattered along with removal. The FSOC's review could influence rules through threatened vetoes even without formal exercise; scholarship and institutional reports supported treating multimember design as a buffer against presidential control. Id. at 292–296 & nn.13–14. Her preferred functional approach differed from the majority's rule.

Kagan's conclusion expressly accepted severing the protection if it was unconstitutional, helping form the different seven-Justice majority preserving the agency. She did not join Roberts's Part IV analysis. Id. at 296–298.

**Exact disposition.** The Court **vacated the Ninth Circuit's judgment and remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion**, including initial consideration of valid ratification. Id. at 238. The CFPB survived, with the Director removable at will; the decision did not resolve the firm's alleged underlying violations or categorically undo every prior agency action.

**Course significance.** Distinguish the five-Justice constitutional rationale from the three-Justice severability opinion and seven-Justice severability result. The case contrasts categorical removal rules with functional congressional latitude, while its actual remedy preserves the agency and leaves a case-specific enforcement question to lower courts.
