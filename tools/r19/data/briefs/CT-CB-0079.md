CT-CB-0079 | Harrington v. Taylor
Harrington v. Taylor
225 N.C. 690, 36 S.E.2d 227 (1945)
Court and source scope
The Supreme Court of North Carolina issued a per curiam opinion concerning a promise to
compensate an injured rescuer. The assigned opinion appears at printed pages 79–80; the
complete judicial text is unusually short and was read in full. The additional explanation
below separates the court's concise rationale from study analysis. It does not invent a longer
judicial discussion merely to make the brief appear substantial.
Parties and material facts
Lena Harrington brought the action against Lee Walter Taylor on his promise to pay for her
injuries. According to her complaint, Taylor assaulted his wife, who took refuge in
Harrington's home. The following day he gained access to the house and resumed the
assault. His wife knocked him down with an axe and was about to strike again while he lay on
the floor. Harrington intervened and caught the descending axe. The blow badly injured her
hand but saved Taylor's life. 225 N.C. at 690–91.
Taylor afterward orally promised to pay Harrington her damages. He paid a small amount
and then stopped. The report supplies no fixed total promise amount, no schedule of
installments, and no finding valuing her injury. Those details should remain unspecified. The
sequence is clear: the voluntary rescue and injury occurred before the promise, and the
promised compensation concerned an injury already suffered. Id. at 691.
The recipient of the benefit and the alleged promisor were the same person. Harrington's
expense was not merely care for Taylor's relative, as in the arrangement described in Mills.
That makes this case particularly difficult to reconcile with Webb solely by pointing to a
direct benefit to the promisor. The comparison is developed below as analysis; the North
Carolina opinion itself does not discuss either case. Id.; casebook 79–80.
Procedural history
Taylor demurred to the complaint, asserting that it failed to state a cause of action. The trial
court sustained the demurrer, and Harrington appealed. The Supreme Court affirmed. Thus
the reported decision concerns the legal sufficiency of the pleaded contract claim. There was
no jury verdict finding the alleged assault or rescue facts and no judicial determination of the
amount of compensable injury in this action. 225 N.C. at 691.
Issue
Did Harrington's voluntary humanitarian act of saving Taylor's life, performed before he
promised compensation, constitute consideration recognized by North Carolina law for that
later promise? The dispute was not whether the rescue conferred a serious benefit or
whether gratitude was warranted. It was whether those circumstances gave the
compensation promise the legal support necessary for this action. Id.

Rule and holding
The court held that a voluntarily performed humanitarian act of this kind was not sufficient
consideration for the subsequent promise. It acknowledged the reason for gratitude and the
desire to alleviate Harrington's misfortune, but concluded that those reasons did not entitle
her to recover at law on the promise pleaded. The opinion does not formulate or apply a
broad material-benefit exception comparable to the reasoning in Webb. Id.
Actual judgment
The judgment sustaining the demurrer was affirmed. Harrington did not obtain a contract
recovery in this decision. The court did not rescind a settlement, assess negligence, order
Taylor to pay the remainder of a specified sum, or decide a restitution claim. Those would be
different questions, and the brevity of the opinion does not authorize filling them in as
decided issues. Id.
Judicial reasoning
The act was voluntary and preceded the promise
The court's operative characterization is that Harrington performed a humanitarian act
voluntarily. Her intervention was not described as performance requested by Taylor in
exchange for compensation. The later promise accordingly did not make the rescue part of a
previously formed bargain. The chronology is important because detriment alone does not
answer whether that detriment supports the particular promise sued upon. Id.
Gratitude did not provide the missing legal basis
The court expressly recognizes that ordinary gratitude should move Taylor to help. It
nevertheless distinguishes that motive from legally sufficient consideration. Its conclusion
therefore cannot be explained as a factual finding that the injury was trivial, the rescue
unnecessary, or Taylor's life not saved. Taking the complaint's account as given, the legal
classification still defeated the claim. Id.
Partial payment did not alter the reported result
The complaint disclosed a small payment after the promise. The court still affirmed the
demurrer. The opinion gives no separate extended account of ratification, acknowledgment,
or completed gifts, so a brief should not invent one. What can be stated is that the described
partial payment did not persuade this court that Harrington had stated an enforceable claim
to further compensation. Id.
Separate opinions
The decision is per curiam. No concurrence, dissent, named author, or division of votes is
reported in the judicial text reviewed. The absence of a separate opinion should not be turned
into a claim about individual judges' unexpressed views.

Comparison and study analysis
Comparison with Webb
Both cases involve an injured rescuer, a direct lifesaving benefit, a subsequent promise, and
some payment. Webb stresses the material benefit and treats the later undertaking as
ratification; Harrington calls the rescue voluntary and finds no consideration. Webb's regular
payments over years and specified lifetime amount differ from Harrington's unspecified
promise and small payment, but the Harrington court does not say that a longer payment
history or more definite amount would have changed its legal rule. Those differences are
possible analytical distinctions, not holdings. Compare Webb, 27 Ala. App. at 85–86, with
Harrington, 225 N.C. at 691.
Identifying a different cause of action
The contractual promise, the underlying physical injury, and any enrichment from the rescue
present distinct legal inquiries. The 1945 opinion resolves only the asserted promise-based
action. Whether tort or restitution principles might provide another route would require
separate elements and authority. It would be inaccurate to say that this decision adjudicated
all possible responsibility for the assault or all forms of relief for a rescuer.
How to use the case in a problem
Start with the timing of the request, act, and promise. Then determine whether the relevant
jurisdiction recognizes a basis for enforcing a promise made after a material benefit has been
conferred. The emotional force of a rescue cannot substitute for specifying that doctrinal
basis. Conversely, the decision should not be generalized into a rule that every promise
associated with a rescue is unenforceable: a prior requested exchange would present
different facts.
The casebook supplies historical discussion after the opinion, including attention to the social
circumstances of the litigation. Those materials may support classroom criticism of the legal
result, but they are editorial supplementation. They are not facts found or reasons articulated
in the short per curiam opinion. Casebook 80–81, notes 2–3.
