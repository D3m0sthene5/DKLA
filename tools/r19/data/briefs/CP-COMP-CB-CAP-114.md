# Howlett v. Rose

**496 U.S. 356 (1990) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 11, 1990.** Justice Stevens wrote for a unanimous Court; there are no separate opinions. Assigned account: *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 175, printed page 303, Note 5, before the distinct *Haywood* account.

## Alleged school misconduct and the state-court dismissal

Howlett, a former high-school student suing through his mother, filed in Florida’s Pinellas County Circuit Court against the school board and three school officials. He alleged that an assistant principal illegally searched his car on school premises and that officials wrongfully suspended him from regular classes for five days. He asserted Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment violations through 42 U.S.C. § 1983, alongside state constitutional claims, and sought damages and removal of the suspension from his school records. These were allegations; the Supreme Court did not find the search unconstitutional or the suspension procedurally deficient. **358–359.**

The defendants sought dismissal on several grounds, including administrative exhaustion. The board additionally claimed that Florida’s sovereign-immunity waiver allowed state-law claims but did not open state courts to § 1983 claims against it. The circuit court dismissed with prejudice. The Florida appellate court affirmed dismissal of the federal claim against the board, relying on *Hill v. Department of Corrections*. It treated the State’s willingness to waive immunity in its own courts as exclusively a state-law matter and declined to determine whether federal law would immunize a school board. The state constitutional claims and individual defendants’ liability were not briefed or addressed on that appeal. **359–360 & n.3.**

## Question, holding, and exact result

Could a Florida court otherwise competent to hear the dispute invoke a state-law sovereign-immunity defense unavailable under the governing federal cause of action? **No.** Florida could not give a federal-law defendant extra immunity, or selectively close an otherwise competent court to this category of federal claims while hearing comparable state claims. Either characterization violated the Supremacy Clause. The Supreme Court **reversed and remanded** for further proceedings. **375–383.**

The ruling did not impose liability on the board. Footnote 2 noted that liability would require the relevant final decisionmaking authority to have been delegated to the school officials under state law. More importantly, the Court declined to decide the board’s late-raised argument that it was an arm of the State and therefore not a § 1983 “person.” That contention had not appeared in its opposition to certiorari. **359 n.2, 381 n.24.**

## Why Hill did not justify the school board’s extra immunity

Florida’s statutory waiver allowed tort claims against the State and subdivisions, including school boards, largely as against private defendants. Florida courts had construed it to permit numerous nondiscretionary-duty claims arising from governmental activities: policing, maintaining public facilities, child-protection work, and protecting inmates. The waiver was not confined to governmental commercial activity. **361–364 & nn.4–11.**

In *Hill*, however, Florida had exempted governmental entities from § 1983 suits. *Hill*’s actual defendant was the Department of Corrections, an arm of the State. Federal law itself excluded States and their arms from the statutory category “person,” as explained in *Will*. The actual result in *Hill* could therefore comport with federal law, even though its language and reasoning swept further. Extending that reasoning to municipalities, counties, or school districts otherwise subject to § 1983 created the conflict here. **364–366 & nn.12–13.**

Calling the obstacle state law did not shield it from review. Whether a nonfederal ground adequately supports a state judgment preventing enforcement of a federal right is itself a federal question. The Court reviewed that adequacy independently rather than accepting the State’s characterization as conclusive. **366–367 & n.14.**

## State courts’ obligation and valid excuses

Federal law is law within each State, not foreign law that its judges may disregard because they dislike federal policy. The Supremacy Clause gives state courts a coordinate duty to enforce it according to their regular procedures. The Court derived three related principles: an otherwise competent court cannot deny a federal right without a valid excuse; disagreement with federal law is no valid excuse; neutral rules of judicial administration may justify declining a case, subject to federal preemption. **367–372 & nn.15–17.**

The opinion illustrated these boundaries through earlier cases. A state court of general jurisdiction could not refuse FELA or federal price-control actions because it disapproved of their policy or characterized them as penal. Conversely, the Court had accepted neutral rules concerning nonresident parties, territorial jurisdiction, and impartial forum non conveniens practice. States retain substantial control over their court structures and ordinary procedures. *Howlett* did not decide whether Congress could require a State to create a new competent forum or extend process to persons beyond an existing court’s authority. **372–375, 378 n.20.**

## Florida’s rule failed under either characterization

If the board’s defense was substantive immunity, federal law controlled. Congress’s statutory category of liable persons and the federally recognized defenses could not be expanded or contracted by a State’s preferred treatment of its subdivisions. The same principle prevented state immunity from overriding § 1983 in *Martinez*, and prevented a state notice-of-claim procedure from frustrating the federal right in *Felder*. **375–378.**

If the defense instead meant that Florida courts lacked jurisdiction over this category of claims, it remained discriminatory. The circuit court was a general-jurisdiction court that heard comparable tort claims against school boards and other governmental entities, heard § 1983 claims against individual officials, and could furnish the required remedies. Howlett had followed the procedures for invoking that court’s jurisdiction. Its refusal turned on the federal source and substantive content of the claim, not genuine incompetence over the parties or subject matter. **378–380 & nn.20–23.**

Amici suggested that § 1983 suits were more likely to be frivolous or that the precise conduct alleged was not independently unlawful under state law. Neither justified rejecting a whole federal category. A State could use valid, neutral procedures against frivolous litigation, but could not predeclare federal claims undeserving of its resources. **379–380.**

Nor did the label “jurisdictional” cure the discrimination. A rule must reflect the actual power over persons or competence over the subject matter that jurisdictional rules protect. Otherwise, States could defeat federal law simply by recasting forbidden substantive barriers as withdrawn jurisdiction. Similarly, although Congress considered common-law immunities in shaping § 1983, a State could not use its own immunity heritage to exempt additional persons Congress subjected to liability. **381–383 & nn.24–26.**
