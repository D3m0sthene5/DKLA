# Linderman Machine Co. v. Hillenbrand Co.

**75 Ind. App. 111, 127 N.E. 813 — Indiana Appellate Court; McMahan, C.J.** The supplied casebook identifies the decision as 1920. The original Indiana report's caption instead prints “Filed June 18, 1921,” followed by rehearing denied November 16, 1920, and transfer denied March 10, 1921. Those printed dates are internally inconsistent; this brief preserves the discrepancy rather than certifying an exact filing date. Assigned account: CivPro PDF page 648, printed pages 1283–1284; accompanying Notes 1–2 appear on the same PDF page.

## What happened and how the dispute reached the court

Linderman sold Hillenbrand a machine described as a jointer and matcher, intended for Hillenbrand's factory in Batesville, Indiana. The agreement made acceptance conditional on a trial and Hillenbrand's approval. In July 1914, Linderman brought an action in the federal District Court for the District of Indiana to recover the purchase price. Hillenbrand answered with a general denial, a defense that fraud had induced the purchase, and a defense based on the conditional nature of the transaction. The latter alleged that Linderman installed and operated the machine for two weeks in an attempt to demonstrate that it could perform its intended work, that it failed to do so, and that Hillenbrand refused acceptance and notified Linderman to remove it. Hillenbrand maintained that it had performed its own obligations. The federal judgment favored Hillenbrand and ordered that Linderman take nothing. **113.**

After that judgment, Hillenbrand sued Linderman in the Ripley Circuit Court for damages caused by the alleged fraud. It sought expenses incurred in transporting, installing, testing, and removing the machine, including losses of material and time spent attempting to operate it. This was an affirmative claim for losses surrounding the failed transaction; it was not another request to avoid paying the purchase price. **113–114.**

Linderman challenged the sufficiency of the complaint by demurrer, which the trial court overruled. It then answered with a general denial and two paragraphs asserting the former federal judgment as a bar. Both argued that Hillenbrand could have claimed its damages through a cross-complaint in the federal action but had failed to do so; the third paragraph reproduced the prior pleadings and judgment. The trial court sustained Hillenbrand's demurrer to those two paragraphs. It made special findings and conclusions of law favoring Hillenbrand and entered judgment accordingly. Linderman appealed after an unsuccessful new-trial motion, retaining objections that the decision lacked sufficient evidence and was contrary to law. **114.**

## The arguments and the questions presented

Linderman made two principal arguments. First, because Hillenbrand could test and reject the machine, rejection was its only remedy. Linderman also maintained that suing for damages necessarily affirmed the sale and that the asserted damages could not coexist with rescission. Second, Hillenbrand's successful use of fraud as a defense in the first action, without requesting affirmative relief there, supposedly barred a subsequent damages action based on the same fraudulent conduct. **114–115, 117–118.**

The appellate court rejected both arguments. Its analysis separately addressed the substantive availability of special damages after rejecting a fraudulent transaction and the procedural effect of an omitted cross-claim under the historical Indiana rules it applied.

## Why rejecting the machine did not eliminate the damages claim

The court distinguished damages for fraud from a claim for breach of warranty. Hillenbrand sought compensation for expenses caused by entering the transaction, rather than contractual recovery premised on retaining the sale. Decisions Linderman cited involving warranty claims did not establish that rescission necessarily eliminated every claim for fraud damages. **115.**

The court did recognize a limitation: rescinding a sale ordinarily precludes a later claim for general fraud damages founded on the continued existence of that sale. Special damages stood differently. A defrauded purchaser could recover necessary expenses that were the natural and proximate consequences of the fraud, even after rescission. The court drew support from a tractor-testing case involving labor, gasoline, and lubricating oil, from a case permitting repair expenses after rescission, and from authorities concerning foreseeable installation costs and lost time. The amounts mentioned in those authorities are not damages awards in Linderman itself. **115–117.**

The reasoning connects the remedy to the injury: rejecting the machine ends the purchase obligation but does not itself repay resources expended because the buyer was induced to undertake the transaction. Reimbursement of those special losses seeks to restore the buyer toward its position before the fraud. The court therefore refused to treat this damages action as an affirmation of the sale. **115–117.**

## Why the earlier judgment did not bar the later claim

The court stated that a sued party must interpose its defenses and that the resulting judgment is conclusive as to those defenses, whether pleaded or not. It then drew a different rule for an affirmative claim against the plaintiff. Under the cross-complaint statute and authorities it cited, a defendant was not compelled to present that affirmative cause of action in the first suit. Failure to present it did not itself adjudicate it. **118.**

That distinction controlled even though the same fraudulent conduct supported Hillenbrand's earlier defense and later claim. In the purchase-price suit, the pleadings did not put Hillenbrand's entitlement to reimbursement for installation and related expenses in issue. To determine what a prior judgment adjudicated, the court examined what the pleadings actually submitted. Fraud's defensive use defeated Linderman's price claim; it did not convert an unrequested affirmative recovery into an adjudicated damages claim. **118–119.**

This was not a permission to relitigate an adverse determination. The earlier judgment had favored Hillenbrand. The later claim sought additional relief that had not been submitted, rather than undoing a right Linderman had established through the first judgment. The court's optional-cross-complaint rule is historical and should not be equated with a certification that modern compulsory-counterclaim rules allow every defendant to save a related claim for later litigation.

## The remaining objections and exact result

The complaint sufficiently alleged fraud, and the special findings followed that valid complaint and supported the conclusions of law. The court therefore found no error in overruling Linderman's demurrer to the complaint or sustaining Hillenbrand's demurrer to the preclusion defenses. **119.**

Linderman also argued that Hillenbrand's testing expenditures reflected its own initiative and responsibility, rather than reliance on false representations. The appellate court found the evidence sufficient to sustain the trial court's finding. Its earlier discussion disposed of the further objection based on Hillenbrand's approval-or-disapproval right. The judgment for Hillenbrand was **affirmed**. The published opinion gives no damages amount, numerical vote, dissent, or concurrence; none is inferred here. **119–120.**

## How the assigned account frames the decision

The casebook summarizes the transaction and quotes the preclusion discussion at original page 118. Its first note compares Linderman with Mitchell and Lucky Brand, explaining that omission of a noncompulsory counterclaim generally permits a later action while warning about a later claim that would impair or nullify rights established in the first judgment. Its second note discusses common-law compulsory-counterclaim concerns through Jacobson's successive rent litigation and a hypothetical about reliance on the first action. Those are editorial comparisons, not additional rulings by the Linderman court. **Assigned printed 1283–1284, PDF 648.**
