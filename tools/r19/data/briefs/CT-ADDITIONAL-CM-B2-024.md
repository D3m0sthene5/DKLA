# Rivera v. Reading Housing Authority
819 F. Supp. 1323 (E.D. Pa. 1993) | Contracts

## Decision and judicial stage
District Judge Van Antwerpen entered summary judgment for the Reading Housing Authority and its executive director on January 25, 1993. The court upheld the authority’s requirement that applicants under eighteen obtain a judicial emancipation decree before admission to public housing. It rejected Rivera’s challenges under the United States Housing Act, HUD regulations, and the Fourteenth Amendment. This brief addresses the district court’s opinion and final order, rather than a later appellate judgment. It describes the historical statutes, regulations, and guidance considered in that decision. (1323, 1325, 1336.)

## Facts and the challenged policy
The authority operated eight low-income housing projects and assisted applicants through the Section 8 program. Its annual-contributions contract required compliance with HUD regulations and the Public Housing Occupancy Handbook. In March 1991 it revised its admissions policy; HUD approved the revision on July 2, and the authority made it effective July 23. The revised policy expressly required an applicant under eighteen to obtain a judicial decree declaring emancipation and entitlement to contract for necessities. An earlier written policy, interpreted by the authority to require such a decree, also referred to other minors able to establish capacity to make an enforceable contract. The authority had not developed its own procedure for evaluating a minor’s contractual capacity. (1326–1327 & nn.3–4.)

The parties stipulated that the authority sought to ensure enforceable leases, establish need for housing, and establish capacity to comply with a lease and live independently. Carmen Rivera, born January 17, 1975, had been raised by paternal grandparents. Her parents were separated and largely absent; her father had experienced incarceration and institutionalization, and she rarely saw her mother. After moving from Puerto Rico to Pennsylvania at fifteen, Rivera lived with her grandparents and later with David Gonzalez. In April 1991, while pregnant, she and Gonzalez left her grandparents’ Reading apartment for a place of their own. They paid $260 monthly plus utilities and received public assistance; Gonzalez subsequently obtained work at McDonald’s. (1327–1328.)

They applied for public housing on May 21, 1991, before the revised policy’s effective date but after the authority had reaffirmed its earlier policy. The authority put Rivera on a waiting list pending an emancipation decree and notified her of the requirement on August 9. She did not seek a decree. Her attorney instead argued that Pennsylvania minors could make enforceable contracts for necessaries. The authority denied admission on September 9 for failure to comply with its minor-applicant policy. Rivera gave birth in November; in February 1992 the family moved to a larger apartment costing $400 monthly including utilities, where they encountered rats. They supported themselves through earnings and public benefits, with occasional purchases by her grandfather that they repaid. These facts were undisputed and stipulated for resolution of the claims. (1326–1328.)

## Claims and procedure
The December 23, 1991 complaint initially named three plaintiffs and proposed a class. The court denied class certification in April 1992; the other two named plaintiffs dismissed their claims without prejudice when their whereabouts became unknown. Only Rivera’s claims remained. She sought a change in the policy, public housing, and monetary compensation under section 1983. She elected not to pursue an Age Discrimination Act claim. After discovery the parties agreed that the material facts were undisputed and submitted cross-motions for summary judgment. The authority alternatively sought dismissal. (1325–1326 & nn.1–2.)

## Housing policy and HUD regulations
Rivera argued that requiring a decree frustrated the Housing Act by excluding needy minors. The court read the Act as serving both the provision of safe, decent housing and substantial local responsibility for efficient administration. Statutory concerns included rent collection and sound management. Local discretion remained bounded by the Act’s objectives and HUD regulations, but the Act did not guarantee admission to every needy applicant. Eligibility and tenant-selection requirements could therefore exclude some applicants without defeating the statute’s purpose. (1328–1330 & nn.5–7.)

She also invoked regulations forbidding automatic exclusion of a category of otherwise eligible applicants and requiring criteria reasonably related to individual attributes or behavior. The court distinguished eligibility from admission and concluded that the policy did not exclude every minor: an otherwise eligible minor could qualify through a decree. Its premise concerned the legal ability to disaffirm a lease, rather than an assumption that all minors were irresponsible or incapable of managing a household. The court also relied on HUD Handbook language permitting minimum ages to avoid leases unenforceable under state law. Footnote 10 disclosed that a July 1991 handbook revision had inadvertently omitted the minor provisions; the record showed HUD intended to continue applying them and had circulated a draft restoring them. The opinion did not conceal that qualification in the guidance on which it relied. (1330–1331 & nn.8–10.)

## Minority, necessaries, and emancipation
Pennsylvania generally treated a minor’s contracts, other than contracts for necessaries, as voidable rather than void from inception. The protective rule was not a license for unconscionable conduct. Whether something was a necessary depended on the minor’s situation and the ability and willingness of a parent or guardian to supply it. The court rejected the proposition that shelter was invariably a necessary. Housing supplied by another could fall outside that category when a parent or guardian was able and willing to provide it. Authorities recognizing shelter as potentially necessary did not establish an unconditional rule. (1331–1333 & nn.11–12.)

The distinction was essential to the court’s policy analysis, but it did not adjudicate whether housing was necessary for Rivera herself. Footnote 13 expressly reserved that individual issue. Footnote 14 further explained, by reference to other jurisdictions, that even necessary lodging could create liability for reasonable rent during actual occupancy rather than the entire agreed lease term. Thus the category did not necessarily give a housing authority the same security as an ordinary enforceable lease. The textbook quotes the policy discussion; it should not be read as a factual finding that Rivera had adequate parental housing available. (1332–1333 & nn.13–14; assigned printed page 456.)

Rivera correctly identified the general rule that emancipation from parental control alone does not eliminate a minor’s right to disaffirm. The court distinguished emancipation’s different legal contexts—parental support, public assistance, and an adjudication directed specifically to contractual capacity. The authority relied on an affidavit from Judge Arthur Grim describing a nonstatutory Berks County procedure: a petition docketed as a miscellaneous action, followed by a hearing concerning the minor’s status, could seek emancipation for determining ability to contract for housing. The court accepted that uncontroverted evidence and noted the Court of Common Pleas’ broad equitable and declaratory jurisdiction. It declined to speculate about the particular standards or dictate state-court procedures. For this policy challenge, referral to a court gave greater contractual certainty than the authority’s own nonbinding assessment. (1333–1334 & nn.15–17.)

## Due process and exact disposition
Rivera characterized the requirement as an unconstitutional irrebuttable presumption that minors lacking decrees could not make enforceable contracts for necessaries. She relied on decisions requiring an opportunity to rebut a presumption that was not universally true. The court held that subsequent Supreme Court and Third Circuit social-welfare decisions required rational-basis scrutiny here. It acknowledged a hypothetical minor denied emancipation who might nonetheless need housing, but explained that overinclusion or underinclusion did not itself make a welfare rule unconstitutional. Protecting scarce housing resources and rent collection through enforceable leases supplied a rational justification. (1334–1336 & nn.18–19.)

The final order granted defendants’ summary-judgment motion, denied their alternative motion to dismiss as moot, denied Rivera’s summary-judgment motion, entered judgment for defendants, and closed the case. It ordered neither admission nor compensation.
