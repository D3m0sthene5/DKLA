# Black Industries, Inc. v. Bush
110 F. Supp. 801 (D.N.J. 1953) | Contracts

## Court and posture

Chief Judge Forman of the federal District of New Jersey decided Bush’s summary-judgment motion on March 13, 1953. Black Industries sued for breach of two component-parts supply agreements. Bush argued that the agreements were void as against public policy because Black’s large resale profits would ultimately increase the cost of military procurement. The court denied that motion even assuming Bush’s asserted facts. It did not enter a breach judgment or award Black either amount claimed. Other disputed contract defenses remained outside this ruling. 110 F. Supp. at 801–802, 806.

## The two transactions and contested performance

Black, an Ohio manufacturer and purchaser of subcontract work, obtained an invitation to bid on components for Hoover. It alleged that around March 22, 1951, New Jersey supplier Bush agreed to make 1.3 million anvils at $4.40 per thousand, 750,000 holder primers at $11.50 per thousand, and 700,000 plunger supports at $12 per thousand, using government specifications and drawings. Black would service the agreement, handle Hoover dealings, and retain the difference between Bush’s price and the customer’s price. Hoover agreed to pay $8.10, $16, and $21.20 per thousand respectively. Black alleged that Bush began but failed to complete performance, causing $14,625 in loss, plus interest. Id. at 801–802.

A second alleged agreement concerned 525,000 plunger supports at $13.20 per thousand and 969,500 anvils at $4.40 per thousand. Relying on those terms, Black accepted a Standby Products order at $23.20 and $8.10 per thousand respectively. It claimed Bush’s failure caused another $4,460.95, plus interest. Bush disputed that this second agreement existed, its alleged quantities and prices, and the asserted reason for nonproduction, maintaining that Standby canceled the order. Id. at 802.

As to Hoover, Bush asserted that his quotations were tentative, nonperformance resulted from strikes and Black’s own failure, and a later agreement divided the spread equally. He said he did not know the customer arrangement’s details when undertaking performance. His answer also invoked the statute of frauds, vagueness, an alleged limitation of compensation to money actually collected, and impossibility. Those contentions were pleaded defenses, not facts resolved against Black on this motion. The motion addressed the public-policy defense to both agreements. Id.

## The written arrangement and asserted profits

An April 13 letter from Black’s Franklin Gepfert, signed as agreed by Bush on April 19, memorialized the Hoover arrangement. It described Black’s work developing the customer order and made its compensation the spread between Bush’s fixed price and Hoover’s purchase-order price. Although the order would formally run to Bush and Bush would manufacture and ship directly, Black would service it, receive shipping and title documents, exclusively bill on Bush’s forms, cash checks payable to Bush, remit Bush’s contractual amount, and retain the balance through an agency account. That detailed arrangement, rather than a bare promise to pay an unexplained referral fee, supplied the factual context for Bush’s concealment and profit arguments. Id. at 802–803.

Bush characterized Black’s role as comparatively minor and calculated spreads of about 84%, 39%, and 68% relative to the Hoover supply prices, with comparable figures under the Standby arrangement. He asserted that the billing structure concealed the spread from the buyers and himself. The court did not determine that concealment or fraud actually occurred. It framed its governing rule around freely made agreements without evidence of fraud and later assumed the defendant’s public-policy facts without accepting them as trial findings. Nor did it label the quoted percentages adjudicated net profits after all business expenses. Id. at 803, 805–806.

## Public policy had to rest on identifiable law

The customers would use the components in products fulfilling government defense contracts. Bush invoked the Renegotiation Act’s policy of eliminating excessive profits in defense contracts and related subcontracts, as well as statutory prohibitions on subcontractor fees or kickbacks in certain reimbursable arrangements. The court nevertheless required a legally recognized basis for invalidity. Drawing on Muschany, it explained that public policy comes from law and precedent, supported by definite indications of a dominant policy, rather than a judge’s general assessment of supposed public interests. Preserving freely made good-faith agreements is itself significant. Id. at 803–804.

The cited precedents largely fell into three categories. One concerned compensation for inducing public officials to exercise discretion, exemplified by Tool Company v. Norris and the risk of corrupting government operations even without proof of improper means. Another involved agreements to commit an illegal act or resting on illegal consideration, such as a lease for unlawful dog racing. A third involved collusive restraints on government bidding: Gulick concerned payment for withholding a competing mail-contract offer, undermining the statutory lowest-bid system. The court noted attorney–client and employment cases among the cited exceptions, but did not treat those relationships as the arm’s-length business dealings before it. Id. at 804–805.

Neither supply agreement fell within the principal categories. Black was not paid to influence an official, perform an illegal act, or suppress competitive public bidding. Neither party dealt with the United States on account of these agreements; their connection to government was that their components would eventually enter products sold to it. Black’s prospective profit therefore did not result from procuring discretionary official action or interfering with government competition. That indirect procurement connection did not make the agreements void under the precedents. Id. at 805.

## Unequal returns and the court’s institutional role

The spread might reflect an excessively generous customer price, a low supplier quotation, or other circumstances requiring further proof. Even if Black stood to earn much more for a smaller contribution, relative consideration values alone would not invalidate a bargain between businesspeople dealing at arm’s length without fraud. The court applied that ordinary principle rather than deciding the appropriate price of Black’s services. Id. at 805.

A contrary approach would require either invalidating all compensated intermediaries in the chain of government supply or deciding the reasonableness of every such intermediary’s return. The first lacked precedential support and ignored commercial realities; the second would impose price-regulation functions on a court deciding a contract suit. Competitive purchasing procedures, the prime contractor’s commercial incentives, and any applicable statutory renegotiation process were more suitable protections against excessive government cost. The opinion did not rule that fraud, kickbacks, collusion, or an applicable statute could never invalidate a private agreement. It ruled that the specific theory of high intermediary compensation and eventual government purchase did not supply that invalidity here. Id. at 805–806.

## Exact result and source limits

Bush’s summary-judgment motion was denied, and the court called for an order implementing its opinion. The breach action and unresolved factual and contractual defenses continued; Black’s claimed $14,625 and $4,460.95 were not awarded. Id. at 806.
