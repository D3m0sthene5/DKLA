# Campbell Soup Co. v. Wentz
172 F.2d 80 (3d Cir. 1948) | Contracts

## Crop bargain, market rise, and two defendants
On June 21, 1947, Campbell contracted with Pennsylvania farmers George and Harry Wentz for all Chantenay red-cored carrots grown on fifteen acres that season, delivered to its Camden plant. The delivery-period prices ranged from $23 to $30 per ton; the January 1948 price was $30. The farmers harvested about one hundred tons. With the January market at least $90 and this variety virtually unobtainable, they refused delivery at the contract price and sold about sixty-two tons to neighboring farmer Lojeski. He resold about fifty-eight tons, roughly half to Campbell. Suspecting that it was buying its own contract carrots at inflated prices, Campbell stopped purchasing January 9 and sued both the Wentzes and Lojeski to prevent further outside sales and compel performance. 172 F.2d at 81.

The district court denied equitable relief. An appellate arrangement preserved the dispute while allowing Campbell to receive the remaining carrots at a stipulated $90 price: $30 went to defendants and the remaining $60 to the court registry pending the appeals. Thus the litigation did not become moot merely because the physical carrots had been delivered. Id. at 81 n.1.

## Damages were inadequate on the supply evidence
The appellate court rejected the trial court’s reason for denying relief. Specific performance of chattel contracts was available if the legal remedy was inadequate under the particular facts. Contract formation location was not established, creating a choice-of-law gap; the court treated form of relief as a federal question but observed that federal law and the New Jersey and Pennsylvania Uniform Sales Act rules agreed on this point. This is the opinion’s historical analysis, not a present-day assertion about federal equitable choice of law. Id. at 81–82 & nn.2–5.

Chantenay carrots were virtually unavailable in the open market. Their blunt shape facilitated processing, and their bright color and texture supplied uniform appearance in Campbell’s branded soups. The absence of proven nutritional superiority or a precise soup-stock/visible-ingredient breakdown did not defeat those commercial interests. Campbell used carrots in fifteen of twenty-one soups and used this variety visibly diced in some. Id. at 82.

The trial court’s objective-uniqueness conclusion was legal or mixed, requiring independent appellate judgment. Personal or commercially distinctive value could matter even without an objectively incomparable object. Campbell had contracted well ahead for a special type and built a reputation partly dependent on product uniformity. An order here would not require continuing difficult judicial supervision. Supply and use evidence therefore made the dispute appropriate for specific performance if nothing else intervened. Id. at 82–83 & nn.6–8.

## The form contract nevertheless barred equitable assistance
The court affirmed because the agreement, considered as a whole, was too harsh and one-sided for equity to enforce. Campbell drafted the printed form. Its unilateral conformance determination was not independently unconscionable: analogizing satisfaction clauses, the court reasoned that it could be liable for refusing carrots that actually met specifications. Other clauses, however, permitted refusal of yield beyond twelve tons an acre, restricted outside sales and use of the land, and imposed $50-per-acre liquidated damages for grower breach without a corresponding buyer provision. Id. at 83 & nn.9–10.

Most objectionable was paragraph 9. It excused Campbell’s receipt and payment when specified circumstances prevented its operations, including labor disturbances, while generally leaving the grower unable to sell elsewhere without Campbell’s written consent. Carrots the grower could not deliver could not be otherwise disposed of, and storage was confined to the farm. The court found that allocation drove the buyer’s protections too far. The complete paragraph, not merely a label of unfairness, appears in judicial note 11. Id. at 83 n.11.

The court did not declare the agreement illegal, excuse the growers’ deliberate breach, or enter a damages award. It denied the additional assistance of a court of conscience to a party that secured this cumulative bargain. Although individual provisions were separable, severing illegal terms was beside the point: illegality was not the holding, and the sum of the provisions defeated equitable relief. Id. at 83–84 & n.12.

## Judgment, unresolved purchaser liability, and source scope
The Third Circuit affirmed both judgments denying equitable relief. That disposition made further discussion of Lojeski’s separate liability unnecessary; the opinion does not adjudicate a complete independent damages theory against him. Goodrich wrote for a panel with Biggs and O’Connell, with no separate writing reported. Id. at 81, 84.
