# Eastern Air Lines, Inc. v. Gulf Oil Corp.
415 F. Supp. 429 (S.D. Fla. Oct. 20, 1975) | Contracts

## Relationship, price formula, and crisis
Eastern and Gulf had traded aviation fuel for decades. Following arm’s-length negotiations initiated by Gulf more than a year before existing agreements expired, they signed Gulf’s standard-form contract on June 27, 1972. It consolidated and extended supply arrangements through January 31, 1977 at specified cities. Gulf obtained a long-term outlet for new refinery capacity; Eastern obtained immediate price savings. Eastern had to purchase its fuel requirements at the covered locations from Gulf, which had to supply reasonable good-faith demands there. (415 F. Supp. 431–435.)

The parties anticipated higher crude prices and chose an escalator intended to transmit crude-cost changes proportionately to jet-fuel prices, replacing a predecessor that passed through only half of increases. The specified indicator was the average posted price for West Texas sour crude of a defined gravity quoted by Gulf, Shell, and Pan American in Platts Oilgram’s crude supplement. Prices were already regulated during negotiation. Eastern’s jet-fuel payments rose from approximately 11 to 15 cents per gallon as the indicator increased. (Id. 432–433.)

The 1973 energy crisis combined war, an Arab oil embargo, sharply higher foreign crude prices, and domestic two-tier controls. Under the court’s description, production at a well’s May 1972 level was “old” controlled oil; increased output was “new” oil, with a matching quantity of old oil released from controls. A well increasing from 100 to 150 barrels thus had 50 controlled barrels and 100 uncontrolled barrels. Platts continued publishing the specified controlled-old-oil postings, while it did not publish the additional premiums for new and released oil. The contractual index therefore did not mirror every increase in Gulf’s other crude purchases. Eastern continued paying according to that index. (Id. 433–434.)

## Litigation and defenses
By March 8, 1974, Gulf demanded a price increase and threatened to stop supplying Eastern within fifteen days. Eastern sued for mandatory preliminary and permanent relief. An agreed March 20 preliminary injunction required supply and payment according to the contract. Gulf disputed formation for lack of mutuality and definiteness, alleged Eastern manipulated its requirements, and invoked commercial impracticability under Florida’s UCC§§ 2-614 and 2-615. The trial produced evidence on industry practices, price controls, costs, and Gulf’s corporate accounting. (Id. 431–432.)

The complete notes explain that other Eastern counts alleging tort, antitrust, and federal energy violations were struck on Gulf’s motion. Gulf also counterclaimed for judicial determination of a jet-fuel price; counsel agreed to defer that claim and treat it as moot if Eastern prevailed on the contract claim. The ultimate findings identify Gulf’s December 31, 1973 declaration that the contract was no longer effective as its breach; that finding should not be conflated with the later March ultimatum. (Id. 431–432 nn. 1–2,443.)

## Requirements contract and good faith
The agreement was a valid requirements contract under § 2-306. Quantity was measured by actual good-faith business requirements, constrained by unreasonable disproportionality to stated estimates or comparable prior requirements. Those obligations answered Gulf’s mutuality and indefiniteness objections; Eastern could not simply demand any quantity at whim. The parties’ long practice confirmed exclusivity at covered stations and the obligation to meet reasonable requirements. Estimates existed for some locations and were exchanged more frequently under petroleum allocations. (Id. 434–436 & nn. 4–5.)

Eastern’s “fuel freighting” or “tankering” did not breach this bargain. Airlines may load additional fuel at a cheaper station and less at a later expensive one, within operational limits. Fuel needs also fluctuate with schedules, weather, aircraft, passenger loads, turnaround time, taxes, and a captain’s judgment. Gulf knew those practices and had accepted substantial swings over decades, including particular large increases without objection. The court found the practice commercially established and embedded in these parties’ course of performance and dealing. Good faith between merchants included honesty in fact and reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing. (Id. 436–437 & n. 6.)

The court did not hold that every increase, disappearance, or manipulation of requirements is permissible. It recognized § 2-306’s protection against unreasonable demands and reserved hypothetical extreme cases. On this record, however, Gulf was challenging previously accepted ingredients of the aviation-fuel trade after the dispute arose. Eastern had continued performing its contractual duties.

## Impracticability: index, proof, and risk
Gulf offered two theories: two-tier controls made the escalator cease functioning as intended, and higher crude prices made supply commercially impracticable. The court considered § 2-615 notwithstanding Eastern’s notice objection: Gulf had identified the defense in its summer 1974 opposition memorandum and its later answer, entitling it to a substantive ruling. The court did not deny relief solely for lack of notice. (Id. 437–438.)

The specified index remained available and calculable. Clear language bound the parties to the published postings, not an unspecified measure of all Gulf crude costs. The postings were controlled when the contract was made and throughout its operation. Gulf still used old-oil prices in intercompany contracts; substantial domestic production remained old oil. Footnote 10 separately rejects the contract’s publication-suspension proviso: Platts had not stopped publishing the specified quotations, and Gulf had not used the proviso’s notice and substitution procedure. Failure of the index to track every input was therefore different from failure of the contractual means of calculation. (Id. 439–440 & nn. 7–10.)

Gulf also failed to prove actual hardship from performing the Eastern contract. Its witnesses could not say what producing a gallon of jet fuel cost, whether the particular sales lost money, or how that changed over time. Broadly increased “average crude costs” included transfer prices and profits assigned among Gulf’s subsidiaries. Foreign crude profit margins had substantially increased, and domestic oil’s recorded production cost was much lower than the transfer-price amount used to calculate average refining cost. The evidence did not reveal the magnitude of all internal margins. (Id. 440–441 & n. 11.)

The court declined to guarantee preservation of those intracorporate profits as if they were external expenses. Gulf could legitimately maximize profits and arrange its affairs, but those arrangements did not establish the legal hardship claimed. Its record overall profits reinforced the finding without substituting for the missing contract-cost proof. The holding should not be reduced to a rule that a profitable corporation can never establish impracticability. The decisive proof problem included Gulf’s failure to demonstrate what this performance actually cost it. (Id. 440–441.)

The court also gave an independent risk-allocation reason. Even if serious hardship had been proved, Middle Eastern volatility, interruptions in supply, host-government control, expected crude-price increases, and changing domestic controls were sufficiently foreseeable to be business risks Gulf could address. Gulf’s executives were deeply engaged in federal petroleum-policy discussions; Eastern’s own negotiator anticipated both shortages and regulatory inhibition of price rises. Gulf drafted the agreement and selected the domestic postings. The court held it to that selection. Its stated foreseeability analysis is more categorical than Transatlantic’s warning that foresight alone does not always prove allocation; each formulation should be attributed to its own opinion. (Id. 441–442.)

Section 2-614(2) did not supply another escape. It concerns failure of the agreed means or manner of payment due to regulation—for example, prohibition of a specified monetary medium—not disappointment with the contractual price. No such payment-mechanism failure was present. (Id. 442.)

## Remedy and exact disposition
Judge James Lawrence King concluded that specific performance was appropriate under § 2-716. Gulf stipulated its capability to perform, and the preliminary arrangement had operated for more than a year. Gulf supplied approximately 100 million gallons annually, about 10% of Eastern’s requirements; abrupt cessation threatened chaos and irreparable injury. The court regarded continued supply at the contract prices as the natural relief under the UCC’s more liberal specific-performance standard. It made the preliminary mandatory injunction permanent. (Id. 442–443 & n. 12.)

The final findings established a valid contract, Eastern’s continued performance, Gulf’s December 31 breach, and failure of Gulf’s formation and impracticability defenses. This was a single district judge’s findings and conclusions, with no appellate vote or separate writing. The complete report contains 12 judicial footnotes. Gulf’s deferred price counterclaim became moot under counsel’s agreement when Eastern prevailed; this is distinct from an award of a judicially adjusted price.
