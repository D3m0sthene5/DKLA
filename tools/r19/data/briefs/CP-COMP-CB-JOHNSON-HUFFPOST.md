# Johnson v. TheHuffingtonPost.com, Inc.

**21 F.4th 314 (5th Cir. 2021), decided December 23, 2021.** Civil Procedure — specific jurisdiction over an online publisher, claim-related market contacts, and jurisdictional discovery. Judge Jerry E. Smith wrote for himself and Judge King; Judge Haynes dissented. ## The article and the asserted Texas connections

HuffPost published a story about Charles Johnson meeting two members of Congress in Washington, D.C. It described him as a Holocaust denier and white nationalist. Johnson claimed the portrayal was libelous and sued in the Southern District of Texas. The story said nothing about Texas, recounted no conduct there, and relied on no Texas sources. The court did not decide whether the descriptions were true, defamatory, or actionable on the merits. Court opinion pp. 1–3.

Johnson initially relied on his Texas citizenship and asserted that the libel occurred there. HuffPost, however, was a citizen of Delaware and New York, with no Texas office, employees, or property. Johnson amended his complaint to emphasize its virtual connections. Texans could read the story on the site; HuffPost marketed merchandise and an ad-free experience to readers including Texans; Texas advertisers contracted for space; and the site collected visitors' locations so advertisers could display relevant ads. Footnote 1 clarified that reading HuffPost was free—the optional payment removed advertisements, rather than purchasing access to otherwise unavailable articles. Id. at 2 & n.1.

The district court dismissed for lack of personal jurisdiction because the story was not directed at Texas or based on Texas events or sources. Johnson appealed, arguing that website features rather than the story's forum focus should control. Alternatively, he requested jurisdictional discovery. HuffPost responded that advertising and merchandising did not supply a connection between Texas and this libel claim; visibility alone would expose it to suit everywhere. Id. at 2–3.

## The majority's jurisdictional framework

The court reviewed dismissal de novo, accepting uncontroverted, nonconclusory factual allegations while placing the jurisdictional burden on Johnson. In this diversity suit, Texas's authority remained constrained by federal due process. Specific jurisdiction required the defendant's purposeful forum contacts, a claim arising out of or relating to those contacts, and fairness and reasonableness. Johnson conceded that he could not establish general jurisdiction, which would require HuffPost to be essentially at home in Texas. Id. at 3–4, 13.

The majority regarded the circuit's Revell Internet-libel precedent as controlling. Website interactivity was a prerequisite to that analysis, not a sufficient jurisdictional showing. An interactive site's capacity to obtain information, purchases, or advertising clicks did not prove that it had purposefully directed the alleged wrong to a particular state. Revell then applied Calder's focus on the story and the forum-related harm. HuffPost's story lacked the Texas connections necessary under that precedent. Id. at 4–7.

Johnson attempted to distinguish advertising and purchasing opportunities appearing on the same webpage as the libel from features on separately navigable pages. The majority first observed that the amended complaint had not pleaded that distinction. It then rejected the distinction substantively: the relevant inquiry was the relationship between the forum contact and the claim, not whether unrelated features were adjacent on a screen. Different degrees of interactivity did not eliminate that requirement. Id. at 6–7 & nn.4–5.

## Advertisements and the majority's understanding of targeting

The majority independently concluded that ordinary jurisdictional principles required dismissal. Merchandise purchases did not relate to the reputational injury from the article. Nor did an advertiser's Texas citizenship connect its advertisement to the alleged libel. Location-based advertising still concerned the unrelated goods or services advertised. In the majority's view, none of these activities produced the necessary relationship between Texas and the claim. Id. at 8–10.

The majority also distinguished soliciting Texas readers from showing tailored ads to people who had already visited. Johnson did not allege that HuffPost reached into Texas to attract readers to the site or the story—for example, through geographically targeted advertisements elsewhere directing them to the alleged libel. Instead, users came to a universally accessible website, which then used their locations to display third-party advertising. Their decisions to visit did not demonstrate the defendant's purposeful, suit-related Texas conduct. Id. at 10–11 & nn.7–10.

A physical-store analogy illustrated the majority's distinction. A Texan visiting a New York store and receiving a compensated referral to a nearby shop would not, solely through that interaction, permit another Texan to sue the New York store in Texas over an unrelated tort. Deliberately directing the tort toward Texas or soliciting a claim-related visit could present a different case. But the majority did not establish a comprehensive rule about what additional online targeting would suffice; it held these pleaded connections inadequate. Id. at 11–12 & nn.11–13, 18.

Avoiding jurisdiction did not require HuffPost to block Texas visitors or refuse all commerce with them. It could avoid jurisdiction over this claim by not purposefully directing the allegedly wrongful conduct toward Texas. The majority also invoked interstate federalism: treating unrelated advertising or merchandising as sufficient would collapse specific jurisdiction into general jurisdiction and undermine the distinctive authority of the states where the defendant was at home. Id. at 12–14.

## Why the majority distinguished Ford and Keeton

The majority accepted that Ford rejected a strict causal requirement. It nevertheless read Ford to require a meaningful connection between the forum's market and the product underlying the injury. Ford's local marketing, sales, and servicing involved the same vehicle models alleged to be defective, even though the particular vehicles originally were sold elsewhere. HuffPost's unrelated merchandise and ads lacked the corresponding relationship to Johnson's libel claim. Thus, rejecting strict causation did not mean every commercial activity could support every claim. Id. at 15–16 & nn.19–21.

Keeton's regular magazine shipments likewise demonstrated an affirmative decision to distribute the publication in the particular state. The majority agreed that basic jurisdictional principles should not change with publication technology, but found that national website accessibility alone did not show the equivalent purposeful forum entry. As pleaded, HuffPost had not aimed either the libel or its website at Texas. The majority refused to infer the missing targeting merely from local visits and unrelated revenue-producing features. Id. at 16–18 & nn.22–24.

## Haynes's dissent: two different routes to jurisdiction

Judge Haynes disagreed with both the treatment of precedent and the description of HuffPost's forum activity. She distinguished Keeton's publication-circulation route from Calder's effects analysis for individual authors or editors. The National Enquirer itself had not contested jurisdiction in Calder; the Court assessed the individual employees' own connections. Fielding, a circuit precedent, recognized jurisdiction over a publication with adequate deliberate circulation or over an author or publisher aiming a story at the forum with known effects there. The majority's exclusive emphasis on the story's Texas focus, she argued, erased the independently relevant publication route. Id. at 20–25.

For Haynes, HuffPost's substantial Texas circulation and Texas-specific advertising constituted deliberate commercial exploitation. It was a publication seeking revenue from Texas readers, not a passive personal blog accidentally viewed by a Texan. Keeton did not require the disputed article itself to address New Hampshire; the analogous absence of a Texas-centered story therefore did not defeat jurisdiction over HuffPost. She distinguished Revell because it did not establish deliberately cultivated substantial Texas circulation. Id. at 25–26.

Haynes also saw Ford's relationship principle as supporting Johnson: an online newspaper's core product was its articles, and the allegedly harmful article reached a consumer in the same market the publisher deliberately cultivated. She argued that requiring the advertising itself to cause the libel claim improperly restored a strict causal requirement. Her theory retained relatedness through the publication and its forum market; it did not rely only on unrelated merchandise or every universally accessible website. Id. at 27–28.

She rejected a technology-based restriction of Keeton, citing other circuits' Internet applications, and stressed the circuit's obligation to follow Supreme Court and circuit precedent. She would **vacate the dismissal and remand**, rather than decide the defamation merits. Her view did not command the panel majority. Id. at 26–29 & n.3.

## Discovery, judgment, and the assignment

The majority reviewed denial of jurisdictional discovery for abuse of discretion. Johnson needed to identify specific facts likely to establish jurisdiction. His request sought only the extent of activities the majority already regarded as legally insufficient. Discovery confirming more of the same contacts would not cure the missing jurisdictional relationship, so denial stood. The court **affirmed the dismissal**. Id. at 19.
