# Miller v. Sam Houston State University

**986 F.3d 880 (5th Cir. 2021)** — January 29, 2021; consolidated appellate Nos. 19-20752 and 19-20753. Cory T. Wilson, joined by Jolly and Southwick. Civil Procedure: fair sua sponte dismissal, discovery before summary judgment, and reassignment. Assigned: CivPro.pdf physical pages 501–502, printed pages 980–981, Note 9. Pinpoints below use the filed opinion's printed pages, not an inferred mapping to reporter pagination.

## Facts and procedural posture

Audrey Miller became a tenure-track psychology professor at Sam Houston State University (SHSU) in 2007. She disputed her workload and treatment, while the university described problems with collegiality. SHSU denied tenure in March 2013; she pursued administrative discrimination charges and a grievance. Her employment ended in May 2014. These were competing accounts, not appellate findings that discrimination or retaliation occurred. Filed opinion 3–4.

Miller also applied to the University of Houston Downtown (UHD). Its search committee rated her highly and recommended an offer, but UHD changed course after department chair Jackson called SHSU department chair Christopher Wilson about her tenure denial. The record did not establish what was said. UHD hired three candidates with lower hiring-metric scores; its dean attributed the decision to teaching and service concerns, and denied knowledge of administrative complaints. That unexplored call became a concrete example of discovery material to both actions. 4–5, 18.

In September and October 2015, Miller filed separate suits against SHSU and the Texas State University System (TSUS), and against UHD and the University of Houston System (UHS). The first alleged sex discrimination, retaliation, hostile environment, and Equal Pay Act violations; the second alleged retaliatory refusal to hire. The district court handled the suits jointly without formally consolidating them. Initial orders required court permission for discovery and specified an exchange of personnel, performance, pay, and organizational information. 2, 6.

At the initial January 2016 conference, the judge required Miller to choose two of the four defendants and dismissed both systems. He did not permit meaningful discussion, briefing, or amendment before entering dismissal with prejudice. Reconsideration was denied the day after it was sought. The judge also made statements suggesting prejudgment and threatened counsel with an adverse result when both parties opposed consolidation. 6–8.

The court repeatedly refused Miller permission for written discovery or depositions, while allowing defendants to depose her in the judge's jury room. The judge attended parts of that deposition and participated. After summary-judgment briefing and argument, he allowed one deposition of Dean Fulton, limited to two hours, but refused further examinations of the relevant chairs and search participants. In September 2019 the court granted summary judgment. Miller appealed dismissal, reconsideration, discovery restrictions, and requested reassignment. 8–11.

## Holdings and standards

**The court reversed the systems' dismissal and the universities' summary judgments, remanded for further proceedings, and directed reassignment to another district judge.** It did not determine that Miller had proved discrimination, retaliation, unequal pay, or damages. 20.

The sua sponte pleading dismissal received de novo review. A court may dismiss on its own initiative when the procedure is fair; notice and a meaningful opportunity to respond generally are required, although notice is not invariably necessary once a plaintiff has fairly presented her best case. Here, even assuming the abrupt conference exchange constituted notice, Miller lacked an adequate opportunity to respond or amend. Note 3 rejects the argument that she had already pleaded her best case. 11–14 & n.3.

Forcing Miller to rely on reconsideration after an immediate dismissal did not cure that unfair procedure. Reconsideration was also prematurely denied under the fact-specific inquiry into whether the systems were employers. The relevant considerations included control over hiring, firing, supervision, schedules, and the economic realities of employment. A judge's general view that university systems did nothing could not replace development of the allegations and evidence. The opinion allowed that a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal might be proper in an appropriate case; it did not adjudicate either system's ultimate employer status. Note 4 applies the same employer inquiry to UHS as a prospective employer. 14–15 & n.4.

## Discovery and substantial prejudice

Discovery rulings received abuse-of-discretion review, with reversal requiring an effect on substantial rights through arbitrary or clearly unreasonable restrictions. District courts retain broad management discretion, but a party must have a full and fair opportunity to discover information essential to opposing summary judgment. The court emphasized the significance of discovery in Title VII litigation. 16–18.

The error was the cumulative, nearly blanket refusal of necessary discovery in these cases. The opening management orders alone were not condemned: the opinion initially described them as potentially unremarkable. The judge subsequently allowed only Miller's own deposition before summary-judgment briefing and argument, then one tightly limited defense-witness deposition afterward. These refusals denied her a fair opportunity to present her claims. The voluminous records obtained through an earlier Texas public-information request did not answer the need for the refused discovery. 6, 16–18.

The Jackson–Wilson call illustrated actual prejudice rather than an abstract demand for unrestricted discovery. UHD reversed its proposed hiring decision after the call, yet neither participant could be deposed. The potentially central contents remained absent from the record. Miller's requests and Rule 56(d) application preserved her effort to obtain the evidence. The appellate court reversed summary judgment to allow relevant discovery, rather than deciding the disputed employment merits itself. 10, 18.

The filed opinion twice uses a disjunctive formulation when describing Rule 26(b), joining relevance and proportionality with “or.” This brief records its discovery holding without presenting that wording as an independently corrected or current rule quotation. Filed pages 16, 18.

## Reassignment and exact mandate

The court treated reassignment as an extraordinary remedy, available here under either of its two approaches. The stricter approach considers difficulty setting aside erroneous prior views, preservation of the appearance of justice, and whether duplication or waste outweighs the fairness benefit. The more lenient approach asks whether an objective observer reasonably would question impartiality. 19.

The cumulative prejudicial remarks and peremptory rulings satisfied both. Reassignment also would not create disproportionate waste because full discovery had not occurred. The court directed the **Chief Judge of the Southern District of Texas** to reassign both cases on remand. This was an express appellate direction; the court did not merely suggest a new judge or resolve an independently presented damages claim against the original judge. 19–20.
