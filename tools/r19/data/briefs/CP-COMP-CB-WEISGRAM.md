# Weisgram v. Marley Co.

**Civil Procedure — Appellate judgment as a matter of law after exclusion of expert evidence.** Supreme Court of the United States, February 22, 2000. **528 U.S. 440.** Justice Ginsburg wrote for a unanimous Court. The assigned account is Note 1 in the Civil Procedure casebook, physical PDF page 567, printed page 1117.

## Facts and trial

On December 30, 1993, firefighters found flames around the front entrance of Bonnie Weisgram’s home. Inside, they found her dead in an upstairs bathroom from carbon monoxide poisoning. Her son Chad sued Marley Company, the manufacturer of an electric baseboard heater located inside the entrance, individually and on behalf of her heirs. He alleged that a defect in the heater caused the fire and her death. The suit proceeded in federal district court in North Dakota under diversity jurisdiction. A consolidated insurer’s action sought recovery of payments for damage to this townhouse and an adjoining one; the insurer was dismissed from the Supreme Court appeal after certiorari. 528 U.S. at 444 & n.1.

To establish defect and causation, Weisgram introduced three expert witnesses. Marley objected before and during trial that their testimony did not satisfy Federal Rule of Evidence 702 and the reliability analysis required by *Daubert*. The district court admitted the evidence. Marley also moved for judgment as a matter of law at the close of plaintiffs’ evidence and again at the close of all evidence, asserting that plaintiffs had not met their burden on defect and causation. The court denied those motions, and the jury returned a verdict for Weisgram. Marley renewed its request for judgment and alternatively sought a new trial under Rules 50 and 59, again challenging the experts’ reliability. The district court denied relief and entered judgment on the verdict. Id. at 445.

## The appeal and the limited Supreme Court question

The Eighth Circuit majority concluded that the expert testimony was speculative and had not been shown scientifically sound. Because it was the sole support for the product-defect charge, excluding it was decisive. The panel then examined the remaining evidence in the light most favorable to Weisgram and found it insufficient to support the verdict. It directed judgment for Marley. Although it recognized discretion to remand for a new trial, it considered this an insufficient case that plaintiffs had already had a fair opportunity to prove. The dissenting circuit judge disagreed both with exclusion and with terminating the action instead of allowing a new trial. Id. at 445–446.

The Supreme Court granted review to resolve a conflict over **appellate remedial authority**. It accepted as final the Eighth Circuit’s determinations that the expert testimony was inadmissible and that the remaining evidence could not establish a submissible case under state law. It did not independently decide whether these particular experts were reliable or whether the heater actually caused the fire. The question was whether an appellate court could direct judgment after removing inadmissible evidence, or whether it had to send the case back for the district court to consider a new trial. Id. at 446–447 & nn.2–3.

## Rule 50 and the available remedies

The operative Rule 50(a) required a legally sufficient evidentiary basis for a reasonable jury to find for the party opposing judgment. Under the then-existing Rule 50(d), a verdict winner could defend the verdict on appeal and alternatively assert grounds for a new trial if the appellate court found that judgment as a matter of law should have been granted. The appellate court could itself determine that a new trial was warranted or direct the trial court to make that determination. The rule did not expressly say whether judgment was available when insufficiency resulted from appellate exclusion of evidence. Id. at 447–449 & nn.4–5.

The Court looked to *Neely v. Martin K. Eby Construction Co.* That decision rejected an absolute requirement of remand after reversal of a denied judgment motion. Appellate entry of judgment did not intrude on the jury’s constitutional role more than a trial court’s equivalent judgment; 28 U.S.C. § 2106 also provided broad remedial authority. But recognizing that power did not erase the trial judge’s superior familiarity with witnesses and trial circumstances. Courts of appeals had to remain alert to valid grounds for a new trial that the trial judge should decide. Id. at 449–451 & nn.6–7.

Accordingly, an appellate court could order a new trial, remand for the district court to choose the appropriate remedy, or direct judgment for the former verdict loser. The appropriate option depended on the case. The Court’s discussion of *Iacurci* illustrated why remand might be necessary where unanswered interrogatories left issues unresolved and the trial judge was best situated to assess a new trial. Appellate power and informed use of that power were separate questions. Id. at 451–452 & n.7.

## Why exclusion on appeal did not require an automatic remand

Weisgram proposed a distinction between a plaintiff who never produced enough evidence and one whose evidence became insufficient only because the appellate court rejected testimony the trial court had admitted. He argued that reliance on the trial judge’s evidentiary ruling made a second opportunity necessary. Some circuits had adopted that approach. Id. at 452–453 & nn.8–9.

The Court rejected the categorical distinction. Inadmissible testimony could not count toward Rule 50’s requirement of legally sufficient evidence merely because the jury had heard it. The majority also rejected the corresponding Seventh Amendment objection: judgment based on a legally inadequate record did not become constitutionally different when appellate correction removed testimony that should never have reached the jury. Id. at 453–454 & n.10.

Fairness still mattered, but it mattered in both kinds of insufficiency case. A proper pre-verdict Rule 50 motion identified the alleged deficiency before the evidence closed. The verdict winner then had an opportunity on appeal to defend the verdict and explain why a new trial was necessary if that defense failed. A rehearing petition supplied a further opportunity, though the Court expressly did not guarantee consideration of arguments that could plainly have been presented earlier. Thus it rejected automatic remand without endorsing automatic termination. Id. at 454–455 & n.11.

The existing reliability precedents gave parties notice that expert testimony required a sound foundation. A plaintiff could not reasonably assume that he could present an incomplete expert case first and obtain a second attempt if appellate review invalidated it. The Court noted that the later *Kumho Tire* decision was consistent with Eighth Circuit precedent already existing when this case was tried. Id. at 455–456 & n.12.

That reasoning was especially strong here. Marley contested the expert evidence throughout the litigation, yet Weisgram did not add or substitute other proof. More importantly, he identified no specific basis for a new trial in the Eighth Circuit. His rehearing petition argued that the panel misapplied state law, lacked authority to direct judgment, and insufficiently deferred to the trial court’s evidentiary rulings. Those arguments did not establish a distinct reason why a retrial was warranted. The Court contrasted circumstances where trial-court error excluded helpful evidence or imposed an excessive burden of proof, potentially making a new trial appropriate. Id. at 456 & n.13.

## Judgment and practical significance

The Supreme Court **affirmed the Eighth Circuit’s judgment**, leaving its direction to enter judgment for Marley in place. In these circumstances, choosing final judgment rather than further district-court proceedings was not an abuse of discretion. There were no separate Supreme Court writings. Id. at 456–457.

The case combines two principles: evidence legally inadmissible on appeal cannot support a verdict, and appellate correction does not itself entitle the verdict winner to another trial. A party seeking that alternative must identify concrete grounds for it; the appellate court must assess them with appropriate regard for the trial judge’s knowledge. The assigned Note 1 accurately states the remedial holding. Its reference to “Neeley” is a spelling variation from the actual *Neely* caption. The printed rule reproduced in the opinion contains the deadlines and subdivision labels operative in 2000; this brief does not present them as verified current procedural instructions.
