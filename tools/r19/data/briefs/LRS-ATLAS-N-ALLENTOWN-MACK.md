# Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. National Labor Relations Board
522 U.S. 359 (1998) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Result: a valid rule, but an unsupported application

The Supreme Court upheld the NLRB’s good-faith reasonable-doubt standard for employer polling, but held that the Board’s finding that Allentown lacked the required doubt was unsupported by substantial evidence on the whole record. It reversed the D.C. Circuit and remanded with instructions to deny enforcement of the bargaining order. The decision did not replace the Board’s standard with unrestricted permission to poll, or treat the eventual poll result as a substitute for the required pre-poll grounds. 522 U.S. at 363–380.

Justice Scalia’s opinion had different coalitions for its two central conclusions. Part I was unanimous. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer joined Scalia in Part II upholding the rule. Rehnquist, O’Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas joined Scalia in Parts III and IV rejecting its evidentiary application. Rehnquist’s four-Justice separate opinion would also have rejected the rule itself. Breyer’s four-Justice separate opinion would have sustained the Board’s application and enforcement. Thus the facial validity ruling and the ultimate judgment each had five votes, with Scalia participating in both majorities. Id. at 360, 380, 388.

## Sale, employee statements, and the Board’s order

Mack Trucks announced in May 1990 that it would sell its Allentown, Pennsylvania factory branch. Several managers formed Allentown Mack Sales & Service, bought the business’s assets on December 20, and operated an independent dealership. They hired 32 of the original 45 employees between December 21 and January 1. Local Lodge 724 of the machinists’ union had represented the branch’s service and parts employees. The ALJ found Allentown a successor employer, carrying a bargaining duty and a rebuttable presumption of continuing union majority support; that successor status was not challenged in the court of appeals. Id. at 361–363, 390–391.

Before and immediately after the sale, employees made statements to the new owners about union support. Eight interview statements arguably indicated personal opposition. Shop steward and bargaining-committee member Ron Mohr said that employees did not want the union and that it would lose a vote. Mechanic Kermit Bloch reported that the entire night shift, five or six employees, did not want it. The context and reliability of these statements became the central dispute; they were evidence of the employer’s grounds for doubt, rather than a Board-supervised determination of actual majority sentiment. Id. at 362, 368–371.

The union requested recognition and negotiations on January 2, 1991. Allentown refused by letter on January 25, asserting good-faith doubt and announcing an independent secret-ballot poll. A Roman Catholic priest supervised the February 8 poll, in which the union lost 19–13. The union filed an unfair-labor-practice charge. The ALJ found that the poll satisfied the Board’s procedural safeguards, but that Allentown lacked the necessary objective reasonable doubt. The Board adopted that conclusion and ordered recognition and bargaining. The D.C. Circuit enforced the order over a dissent. Id. at 362–363.

The historical Board framework offered an employer three routes: a Board-supervised election, withdrawal of recognition, or an internal poll. The same good-faith reasonable-doubt threshold governed the challenged alternatives. Allentown attacked the polling rule’s rationality, the adequacy of evidence supporting its application, and what it characterized as covert replacement of reasonable doubt with a strict employee head count. Id. at 361, 363–364.

## Why the polling threshold survived facial review

Allentown argued that requiring enough evidence to withdraw recognition before permitting a poll made polling pointless. The Court acknowledged the policy’s awkwardness but found it rational and consistent with the Act. The Board considered polls disruptive to established bargaining relationships and unsettling to employees, and could limit them to circumstances where withdrawal might otherwise occur. Id. at 364–365.

Polling could still serve useful ends: reassuring employees before withdrawal, obtaining greater certainty about actual support, and avoiding withdrawal that would be followed by a quick union victory in a later election. An employer might want stronger confirmation than the minimum legal defense to an unfair-labor-practice charge. The Court also noted that a poll might provoke a charge less often than outright withdrawal, especially if the union won. Id. at 365.

The Board’s preference for formal elections suggested a higher polling threshold, but an election had more serious consequences: a losing union could not seek another for a year, while a union losing an internal poll could still seek a Board election. Those competing considerations made an equal threshold permissible. The Court did not demand that the Board select the best policy or adopt either theoretically higher or lower threshold. Id. at 365–366.

## Why substantial evidence did not support the finding

The Court asked whether a reasonable factfinder could conclude on the whole record that Allentown lacked genuine, reasonable uncertainty about continuing majority support. The Board could not redefine doubt as a firm belief in the opposite proposition. Nor did the requirement of objective considerations demand conclusive proof: it required evidence external to the employer’s subjective impressions supporting its reasonable uncertainty. Id. at 366–368 & nn.1–2.

The Board credited six employees’ disaffection and apparently accepted a seventh statement. Seven out of 32, standing alone, did not compel reasonable doubt. But the Board discounted other probative evidence. Dennis Marsh’s complaint that he was not receiving representation worth his $35 dues could express a desire for improved representation or a desire to dispense with the union; either possibility made it relevant to uncertainty. Bloch’s report about his night-shift colleagues might not prove their actual opposition with ordinary trial reliability, but could reasonably affect the employer’s uncertainty absent a basis to believe he lacked knowledge or was lying. Id. at 368–370.

Mohr’s statement was particularly important because he was a union official, well positioned to observe sentiment, and had not expressed personal hostility. The Board rejected reliance partly because he referred to the predecessor’s employees rather than precisely those later hired. Yet its successor bargaining presumption itself relied on continuity between the workforces. The Board could not accept that continuity to impose the obligation and then disregard it entirely when assessing evidence tending to rebut support. The majority distinguished the ALJ’s assessment from the Board’s own language, which gave Mohr’s statement no weight. Id. at 370–371 & n.3.

With seven known opponents, the other 25 would need to favor the union at least 17–8 to produce an overall majority. The statements concerning broader disaffection supplied grounds to question that ratio, and the Board identified no significant contrary evidence the employer should have weighed. The majority held that fair consideration of the circumstantial record compelled a finding of reasonable doubt, without requiring proof that the union actually lacked a majority. Id. at 371.

## An agency must apply the rule it announces

The Court did not finally determine whether the Board had systematically imposed a stricter unannounced test in prior cases. Even if it had, repeated departures would not redefine the announced rule or justify the present finding. Reasoned decisionmaking governed adjudication as well as rulemaking. Secretly changing either the substantive standard or the burden of proof confused ALJs, obstructed judicial review, and disguised policy choices as findings of fact. Id. at 372–377 & n.4.

The majority distinguished permissible, explicit rules from unexplained undervaluation. Subject to review for statutory consistency and reasonableness, the Board could openly adopt policy-based evidentiary exclusions or substantive presumptions. But when it professed ordinary factfinding under reasonable doubt and preponderance standards, it had to draw the inferences the evidence fairly demanded. Substantial-evidence review already supplied deference by asking what could satisfy a reasonable factfinder; further deference to an eccentric evidentiary understanding would erase that review. Consistent skepticism could be appropriate where a reporter lacked knowledge or had incentives to mislead, but prior decisions could not supply an arbitrary rule of complete disregard. Id. at 377–380.

## The separate opinions and the disagreement about expertise

Rehnquist, joined by O’Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas, agreed with the evidentiary reversal but rejected the polling threshold. He considered noncoercive polls protected by extensive safeguards—stated purpose, notice, assurances against reprisal, secrecy, and absence of a coercive atmosphere. The Act’s goal of bargaining stability did not justify denying employees and employers a neutral means of learning whether the union retained support. Polls, recognition withdrawals, and formal elections had different consequences, making identical thresholds irrational in his view. He also invoked serious First Amendment concerns about soliciting employee views and constitutional avoidance, expressly declining to resolve a direct constitutional violation. Id. at 380–388, including all three separate-opinion footnotes.

Breyer, joined by Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg, agreed that the standard was valid but disputed the evidentiary reversal. He objected that the application issue was outside the certiorari question and that Supreme Court intervention in substantial-evidence review should be rare. He considered objective reasonable doubt a specialized workplace standard developed through agency precedents, rather than ordinary dictionary uncertainty. On that view, the majority had improperly rewritten the standard and discounted administrative expertise. Id. at 388–391.

Breyer examined details the majority treated differently. Interviewees seeking reemployment could tell managers what they thought would preserve their jobs. Dissatisfaction with representation need not mean opposition to union representation. Bloch’s general report was unsupported by named colleagues or their testimony. Mohr spoke about a different service-employee complement, could not speak for all parts employees, did not identify the size of the dissatisfied majority, and made what the ALJ considered an offhand assertion. Breyer read the ALJ as weighing Mohr’s statement together with other evidence, rather than finding it wholly irrelevant. He regarded these cautions as permissible agency-developed evidentiary guidance and would have deferred to the Board and D.C. Circuit. Id. at 391–397.
