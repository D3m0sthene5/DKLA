# Peña-Rodriguez v. Colorado

**580 U.S. 206; 137 S. Ct. 855 (2017) — Supreme Court of the United States, March 6, 2017.** Civil Procedure. Assigned: CivPro PDF page 589, printed page 1164, the Peña-Rodriguez account within Note 1. Justice Kennedy wrote for five Justices; Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan joined. Justice Thomas dissented separately and joined Justice Alito’s dissent with Chief Justice Roberts. The judgment was **5–3: reverse the Colorado Supreme Court and remand for further proceedings**, not an immediate award of a new trial. Pinpoints below refer to the original U.S. Reports pages.

## Facts and procedural path

In 2007, two teenage sisters reported that a man had sexually assaulted them in a bathroom at a horse-racing facility. They identified Miguel Angel Peña-Rodriguez. The prosecution charged harassment, unlawful sexual contact, and attempted sexual assault on a child. Before trial, prospective jurors answered a questionnaire about their ability to be fair and impartial; the judge and counsel also explored impartiality. No selected juror expressed a reservation. After a three-day trial, the jury convicted Peña-Rodriguez of the harassment and unlawful-contact offenses but could not reach a verdict on the attempted-sexual-assault charge. 580 U.S. at 211–213.

After discharge, defense counsel entered the jury room to discuss the trial. Two jurors stayed behind and reported anti-Hispanic comments by another juror, H.C. Counsel obtained affidavits with the trial court’s supervision. According to the affidavits, H.C. invoked his former law-enforcement experience to assert that Mexican men were inclined toward sexual aggression and that nine out of ten were guilty in such cases. He linked his belief in the defendant’s guilt to the defendant’s being Mexican and distrusted an alibi witness as supposedly unlawfully present in the country. The witness had actually testified that he was a legal resident. These were reported statements about the juror’s reasoning, not an independently proved generalization about the defendant or witness. Id. at 212–214.

The trial judge acknowledged apparent bias but held that Colorado Rule of Evidence 606(b) barred using statements from deliberations to challenge the verdict. The rule generally prohibited juror testimony or affidavits concerning deliberative statements and mental processes, with exceptions for extraneous prejudicial information, outside influence, and a mistaken entry on the verdict form. The judge denied a new trial and imposed two years’ probation and sex-offender registration. A divided appellate court affirmed; the Colorado Supreme Court affirmed 4–3, reasoning that Tanner and Warger supported applying the evidentiary bar despite the asserted constitutional injury. Id. at 213–215.

## Issue, rule, and precise holding

The issue was whether the Sixth Amendment permits a court to close its inquiry under a no-impeachment rule when jurors provide clear evidence that another juror used racial animus to convict a criminal defendant. The Court held that, in this exceptional situation, the rule must yield so the trial court can consider the evidence and any resulting denial of an impartial jury. The opinion treats the anti-Hispanic allegations under its racial-bias terminology, consistently with the parties’ treatment and the Court’s discrimination precedents. Id. at 214–215, 225–226.

The threshold matters. An isolated offensive or offhand remark does not automatically authorize an inquiry. There must be an overt statement showing serious racial bias that casts serious doubt on the verdict’s fairness; the evidence must tend to establish that racial animus was a **significant motivating factor** in the juror’s vote to convict. The rule does not demand that race be the sole cause of the vote, but neither does it make any discussion of race enough. Trial judges retain substantial discretion to assess the statement’s content, timing, and reliability. Id. at 225–226.

The Court decided the evidentiary barrier, not the ultimate new-trial motion. It expressly left unresolved the procedures for investigating and hearing such a motion and the standard for determining when the evidence requires setting aside a verdict. The Colorado judgment was reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings. Id. at 228–229.

## Why the majority recognized a narrow exception

The Court began with reasons to preserve the ordinary no-impeachment rule. Protecting deliberations encourages candid discussion, shields jurors from posttrial harassment, promotes finality, and prevents routine disappointment with a verdict from becoming litigation over individual jurors’ mental processes. Historically, however, the rule was neither a single immutable procedure nor an entirely exceptionless principle. The opinion traces the Mansfield rule, the more flexible Iowa approach, and the federal distinction between internal deliberations and extraneous interference. Earlier decisions had preserved the possibility of exceptional cases in which enforcement of the bar would itself violate basic principles of justice. Id. at 215–221.

Tanner had rejected an effort to impeach a verdict with juror testimony about intoxication. Its safeguards included voir dire, observation during trial, jurors’ ability to report misconduct before a verdict, and posttrial proof from nonjurors. Warger applied the federal rule to deliberative statements suggesting that a civil juror had concealed partiality during voir dire. Those cases established a powerful general rule; they did not hold that every imaginable constitutional problem could be answered by refusing the evidence. Id. at 219–221.

Racial animus presented a distinctive problem because of its history in the administration of justice and its capacity to undermine both a defendant’s trial and public confidence in the jury system. The majority connected the exception to the constitutional effort to eliminate race-based exclusion and discrimination in juries. The ordinary safeguards also had special weaknesses here: general impartiality questions might fail to expose racial prejudice, more pointed questions might aggravate it, and jurors might hesitate to accuse a colleague of being a bigot. Racially biased reasoning during deliberations might leave no nonjuror witness who could later prove it. The argument was that these safeguards were inadequate for this particular category, not that voir dire and observation never work. Id. at 222–225.

H.C.’s reported statements crossed the threshold because they linked stereotypes directly to guilt and urged other jurors to adopt that reasoning. The record also did not present counsel harassing reluctant jurors to manufacture a challenge: the two jurors approached counsel, and the court authorized affidavits limited to the reported bias. The narrow holding responded to that concrete record while leaving the ordinary rule in place. Id. at 226–227.

The Court emphasized continuing safeguards against abusive posttrial contact. Professional rules and local court restrictions remain relevant; this exception is not permission for unrestricted questioning or a general search through deliberations. Careful voir dire, instructions, and collective deliberation can still reduce the risk that prejudice will influence a verdict. Id. at 226–229. The Court’s appendix identifies jurisdictions with codified exceptions and jurisdictions whose courts had recognized racial-bias exceptions; it illustrates existing experience rather than deciding the scope of every listed precedent. Id. at 229–230.

## Separate writings and their disagreement

Justice Thomas argued that the original understanding of the Sixth Amendment did not include a right to impeach a verdict with juror testimony. In his view, neither the common-law background at the founding nor the understanding associated with the Fourteenth Amendment supplied the new constitutional exception. Changes to evidentiary rules should be made through ordinary rulemaking and legislative processes rather than a constitutional holding. His dissent and both footnotes discuss the historical record and the limits of deriving such a right from early jury practice. Id. at 230–235.

Justice Alito, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justice Thomas, agreed that racial prejudice was reprehensible but disputed the inference that confidentiality must yield. Legal privileges often exclude relevant evidence to protect institutional relationships; deliberative secrecy likewise protects candid discussion and the durability of jury verdicts. He stressed the deliberate adoption of Rule 606(b), the Court’s reliance on alternative safeguards in Tanner and Warger, and the uncertainty of assuming those safeguards uniquely fail for racial bias. Id. at 235–249.

Alito also questioned a constitutional hierarchy among forms of juror partiality. The Sixth Amendment promises impartiality, so limiting the exception to race might prove unstable when comparable allegations involve religion, sex, or national origin. Ambiguous statements, selective affidavits, and posttrial questioning could invite harassment or coercion and inhibit deliberation. His final footnote raises questions about how contact restrictions and other privileges might interact with the exception; those objections are not limitations the majority actually resolved. Id. at 250–255, especially 253–254 n.15.

## Assigned context and source limits

The textbook accurately presents the 5–3 result as a Sixth Amendment exception for racial bias in a criminal case and asks whether the reasoning extends to civil litigation. Its following Harden citation attributes a later civil-case application to that court. Peña-Rodriguez itself does **not** decide that later equal-protection question or create a general civil exception. The surrounding rule text and exercises provide editorial context, not additional holdings of this case.
