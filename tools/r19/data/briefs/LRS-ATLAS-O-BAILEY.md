# Bailey v. Richardson
182 F.2d 46 (D.C. Cir. 1950), affirmed by an equally divided Court, 341 U.S. 918 (1951) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Two decisions and their distinct effects

On March 22, 1950, a divided D.C. Circuit sustained Dorothy Bailey’s removal from federal employment but invalidated a separate three-year bar to future federal employment. Judge Prettyman wrote for himself and Judge Proctor; Judge Edgerton dissented. The court reversed in part, affirmed in part, and remanded with instructions to enter a decree preserving the removal while invalidating the bar. This was not an unqualified rejection of every claim Bailey brought. (182 F.2d at 46, 48–49, 54–55, 66.)

On April 30, 1951, the Supreme Court affirmed that judgment by an equally divided Court. Justice Clark took no part. The official order contains no reasoning or separate writings identifying the respective positions of the eight participating Justices. It therefore preserves the appellate judgment without supplying a Supreme Court majority rationale for the appellate opinion’s right/privilege analysis. The Supreme Court order and the reasoned appellate decision must be kept separate. (341 U.S. at 918.)

## Employment and loyalty investigation

Bailey worked in the classified civil service from August 19, 1939, until a reduction in force separated her on June 28, 1947. She received a temporary appointment on March 25, 1948, and reinstatement on May 28. Civil Service Commission regulations made reinstatement conditional: an investigation within eighteen months could reveal disqualification and lead the Commission to require removal. One stated disqualification was reasonable grounds, on all the evidence, to believe a person disloyal to the United States. Her position after reinstatement was training officer, general fields, CAF-13, in the Federal Security Agency. (182 F.2d at 49–50 & nn.2–3.)

On July 31, 1948, the regional loyalty board sent questions based on undisclosed information suggesting Communist Party or Communist Political Association membership, attendance at Communist meetings, associations with Communists, and membership in organizations listed under the federal loyalty program. It also asked about Nazi or Fascist affiliations and organizations seeking unconstitutional overthrow. Bailey specifically denied the reported accusations except brief past membership in the American League for Peace and Democracy, and asserted her loyalty. (49–50.)

She requested and received an administrative hearing, appeared with her own witnesses, and supplied affidavits. No adverse informant testified. The board did not reveal the informants’ identities or the dates and places of the alleged activities. Thus, the dispute concerned proceedings without an effective opportunity to confront adverse sources, rather than a complete absence of any opportunity to submit a defense. (50, 58.)

On November 1, the regional board found reasonable grounds to believe her disloyal, instructed the employing agency to separate her, canceled examination eligibilities, and imposed a three-year examination bar effective October 29, 1948. Bailey appealed to a panel of the Loyalty Review Board. She testified and submitted affidavits, but again no adverse witness testified on the record. On February 9, 1949, the panel sustained the finding and requested removal from the rolls; the full board declined further review. The appellate opinion lists her actual employment in the position as ending November 3, 1948. (50.)

## Civil action and statutory claims

Bailey sought a declaration and an order directing reinstatement. Defendants included the Federal Security Agency administrator, Commission members, and loyalty-board members. Both sides moved for summary judgment, supported by affidavits; the District Court entered judgment for defendants. The Secretary of Labor was added as an appellee on Bailey’s motion during the appeal. The proceeding was a civil challenge to removal and debarment, not a prosecution for treason or an appellate factual finding that Bailey committed espionage. (48–49 & n.1.)

Bailey argued that Executive Order 9835 required an actual evidentiary hearing and sufficiently specific accusations. Prettyman instead read “evidence” in the order as all information available to the boards, including confidential investigative reports. The order and a presidential confidentiality memorandum protected informants and reports. Reading the order as a whole, the majority considered secrecy deliberate and deferred to the administrative interpretation of this administrative direction. The order’s specification requirement also left security-sensitive detail to the responsible agency’s discretion. (51–52 & nn.4–7.)

The majority separately rejected Bailey’s claim to the Lloyd-LaFollette Act’s protections for persons in the classified civil service. Although she retained a reinstatement preference or civil-service status after the reduction in force, that was not actual service in a position. The governing statutes authorized presidential and Commission employment rules, including conditional reinstatement. Her return remained subject to the eighteen-month condition, so the majority treated her as outside the Act’s protected category for this removal. It also observed that the Act itself did not require an oral hearing or confrontation of adverse witnesses. Edgerton disputed this characterization of her reinstatement and the Commission’s authority to treat her as an applicant. (52–54 & nn.8–26; dissent 66–67 n.2.)

## Why the future employment bar failed

The majority distinguished removing an employee from individually proscribing her future employment. Under its reading of United States v. Lovett, permanent federal-employment proscription was punishment requiring the constitutional safeguards of criminal adjudication. A limited three-year proscription differed in degree, not principle. Nothing in this record made the particular bar the application of a general congressional classification or general administrative regulation; it appeared to be a punishment imposed on this individual through adjudication. The court therefore invalidated it. Its suggestion that a general rule governing future eligibility might be valid did not sustain this particular order. (54–55.)

## The historical constitutional reasoning sustaining removal

For its constitutional discussion, the majority assumed Bailey was a permanent classified employee being dismissed, even though its statutory analysis treated her as conditionally reinstated. It addressed the issue because the judges differed about whether her challenge extended beyond the future-employment bar. The Sixth Amendment, in its view, did not apply: ordinary removal was not the punishment involved in individualized proscription. Congress and the President historically controlled executive personnel, subject to statutory restrictions, without a judicial criminal trial for each dismissal. (55–57 & nn.27–35.)

The majority then treated government office as outside the Fifth Amendment categories of life, liberty, and property. On that historical premise, it held that the Due Process Clause did not govern holding the office, even when removal rested on confidential loyalty information. Because it found no protected interest, it expressly did not reach whether the actual procedures would satisfy due process if the Clause applied. Its additional comments doubting that every criminal-trial safeguard would be necessary were not an alternative holding that this record satisfied due process for a recognized protected interest. (57–59 & nn.36–47.)

On the First Amendment, the majority likewise distinguished a citizen’s freedom to express political views from a claimed constitutional entitlement to continued federal employment. It invoked historical political appointment and removal, the Hatch Act, and United Public Workers to reject Bailey’s contention that political beliefs or associations could never affect eligibility. The opinion did not claim Congress could enact every discriminatory restriction; it recognized precedent limiting blanket exclusions while treating presidential judgments about personnel loyalty differently. (59–63 & nn.48–64.)

Its final discussion placed loyalty-risk estimation with the political branches, emphasizing protection of confidential investigative methods and the international conditions it described. Reputational hardship did not, in the majority’s view, convert the dismissal into a deprivation of a constitutionally protected employment right. It concluded that the particular removal violated neither the Executive Order nor a statutory restriction and sustained it, while maintaining the separate invalidation of the future bar. These are the majority’s historical positions, not a certification of present public-employment law. (63–66 & nn.65–78.)

## Edgerton’s dissent

Edgerton emphasized the actual stakes: removal from a wholly nonsensitive job despite a high efficiency rating, nationwide publicity, stigma approaching an accusation of treason, and impaired future employability. He described four supporting witnesses, numerous affidavits, Bailey’s sworn denials, and a public record containing no evidence against her. The boards nevertheless relied on confidential FBI reports about unnamed informants, without exposing the underlying statements to her or permitting examination of those who supplied them. His description of reliability and the absence of adverse public-record evidence was dissenting analysis of the administrative process, not a majority factual finding that every confidential accusation was false. (66–67.)

First, Edgerton read the Executive Order’s evidentiary and protective language to demand a genuine opportunity to meet adverse evidence. Investigators could use confidential tips to pursue leads, but the government could not then establish disloyalty through secret, untested assertions. If it could not substantiate accusations openly, it should withdraw them. (67–69.)

Second, he considered a stigmatizing loyalty dismissal punishment under Lovett, not merely the loss of a discretionary privilege. He therefore would require the safeguards of a judicial trial before inflicting that injury. His claim addressed this nonsensitive position and did not resolve what procedures might be permitted for genuinely sensitive work. (69–71.)

Third, he rejected the majority’s right/privilege approach to First Amendment protections. Government could not condition its employment benefits on surrender of protected thought, association, or expression merely by labeling employment a privilege. He objected to vague disloyalty criteria and guilt by association and thought the program threatened civil-service morale and efficiency as well as individual freedom. The dissent’s separate statutory, evidentiary, punishment, and unconstitutional-conditions objections should not be collapsed into a single generic demand for “fairness.” (71–74 & dissent nn.1–39.)

## Final judgment and assigned focus

The appellate mandate required the District Court to invalidate the portions of the loyalty-board and agency orders imposing the three-year bar while sustaining the portions removing Bailey from the rolls and office. The subsequent Supreme Court equal division affirmed that mixed judgment; the Court supplied no reasoned resolution of the majority–dissent debate. (182 F.2d at 66; 341 U.S. at 918.)
