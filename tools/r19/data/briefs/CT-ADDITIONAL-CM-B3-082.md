# United States v. Peck; Peck v. United States
102 U.S. 64 (1880) | Contracts

## Decision and posture
The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Claims judgment in cross appeals over a military supply contract. Justice Bradley's published opinion held that surrounding circumstances could establish the contract's subject matter and the government's conduct preventing performance. A party could not invoke nonperformance that its own interference caused. The report contains no separate writing or numerical vote statement. It expressly notes that other minor points were omitted at Bradley's suggestion; the account below covers the entire published report, not those unpublished points. (102 U.S. 64–66.)

## The wood and hay contract and the proposed deduction
Peck contracted with a military officer to furnish and deliver specified quantities of wood and hay to the Tongue River station in the Yellowstone region by a designated time. He delivered the wood but did not deliver the hay. Others supplied the hay at increased expense, and government accounting officers sought to deduct that additional cost from the amount owed on his wood account. The main question was whether the government could charge Peck for that failure in light of its own conduct affecting his supply. (Id. at 64.)

The Court of Claims considered the written agreement together with the surrounding circumstances. It concluded that both sides contemplated hay gathered in the Yellowstone valley, particularly Big Meadows near the mouth of Tongue River. That was the only source Peck could obtain within hundreds of miles, and his reliance on it was known. Government officers, concerned that he might not perform and regarding the hay as indispensable, permitted other parties to cut the Big Meadows hay and use it to supply the station. Peck objected that the government both deprived him of the supply on which he relied and charged him the extra cost of obtaining it through others. (Id.)

## Parol evidence and the government's interference
The written contract did not name the source from which Peck had to obtain the hay. The dispute therefore required deciding whether surrounding circumstances could establish why losing the Big Meadows supply excused him. Bradley identified two proper uses of parol evidence. First, it clarified the subject matter: hay to be cut and gathered in the region of delivery. Second, it established the government's agents' subsequent conduct. They had encouraged reliance on a particular means of performance until alternatives were no longer feasible, then themselves removed that means. The Court treated the resulting inability as government prevention and hindrance rather than an unexplained failure by the supplier. (Id. at 64–65.)

The distinction matters because the Court did not simply add a source term to the written bargain or excuse a contractor who independently chose an unreliable source. Its reasoning tied the parties' circumstances and known reliance to the government's own actions. Evidence of that conduct was relevant to whether the government could rely on the very nonperformance it caused. (Id. at 65.)

## Authorities and the prevention principle
The opinion cited authority permitting proof of contractual subject matter through surrounding circumstances, then explained the rule that one party's prevention excuses the other's nonperformance. Its principal analogy involved a bond requiring procurement and cancellation of a mortgage. After the obligor obtained it, the obligee directed placement with a third person; parol proof of that instruction excused incomplete literal compliance. Without that direction, the obligor might have taken the remaining steps to discharge the mortgage. The preventing party could not benefit from the resulting omission. (Id. at 65–66.)

A second analogy involved a bond to convey land, later orally rescinded and followed by sale to another purchaser. Although the bond and papers had not been formally surrendered or changed, the parties' agreement and acts discharged it. Bradley found the principles illustrated by those cases applicable to Peck. These were supporting precedents, not additional facts of the military transaction or separate judgments decided in these appeals. (Id. at 66.)

## Judgment and assigned significance
The Court affirmed the judgment. The published discussion rejects the government's proposed use of the hay default to justify the increased-cost deduction from the wood account; it states neither a quantified recovery nor the results of the other omitted issues. The case supplies a prevention excuse grounded in the government's taking away the expected performance means, rather than a holding about modern good-faith tort claims or an award of damages for every form of interference. (Id. at 64–66.)

The assigned account at C&M physical page 233, printed page 889, accurately summarizes the Big Meadows interference and quotes the prevention rule. It attributes its factual description to the next case, whereas the original report independently supports the essential reliance and supply-removal facts. The surrounding discussion of good faith is editorial framing, not additional language of Bradley's decision.
