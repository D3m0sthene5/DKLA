# Plante v. Jacobs
10 Wis. 2d 567, 103 N.W.2d 296 (1960) | Contracts

## Facts and procedural posture
The assigned casebook introduction explains that Eugene Plante agreed to provide materials and build a house for Frank and Carol Jacobs on their Brookfield lot for $26,765. They paid $20,000 before disputes about workmanship and completion led them to stop further payment. Plante did not finish and sought a lien to recover the unpaid balance and claimed extras. The owners asserted deficient and incomplete construction. Those introductory particulars come from the assigned editorial account; the actual judicial opinion begins with the parties’ appellate arguments about substantial performance. C&M printed page 969; 10 Wis. 2d at 570.

Plante conceded omissions of kitchen cabinets, gutters and downspouts, a sidewalk, closet clothes poles, and an entrance seat, totaling $1,601.95. The trial court allowed that amount to the owners. They asserted about twenty additional defects and argued that full correction would cost 25 to 30 percent of the contract price. Their principal objection was a misplaced interior wall that narrowed the living room by more than a foot. Moving it would cost approximately $4,000. The house was finished and decorated around the wall, and the owners occupied it. Experts for both sides agreed that the narrower room did not reduce the house’s market price. Id. at 570, 573–574.

The trial court found substantial performance and deducted compensation for established defects from the builder’s recovery. The owners appealed. Plante also sought review of the denial of claimed extras and the allowance for rebuilding a patio wall. Both sides challenged the damage methodology, the owners arguing for $10,000 in damages and Plante for only $650. The Supreme Court affirmed the judgment rather than accepting either proposed total. Id. at 572–574.

## Issues and governing rules
The first issue was whether defects and omissions prevented recovery on the contract, leaving the builder, at most, a claim in quantum meruit. Contract recovery required substantial performance. The court defined that inquiry by the essential purpose of the promised performance, rather than a mathematical percentage of completion or repair cost. A building need not comply perfectly with every specification unless the parties make particular details essential. A feature of special or great personal importance could nevertheless defeat substantial performance if left unperformed. Id. at 570–572.

If performance is substantial, the builder recovers the contract price less damages attributable to incomplete or defective work. Diminution in value compares the building as delivered with the building as properly promised. Repair cost can assist that valuation, but it need not equal the relevant loss. Small, separable defects that can reasonably be corrected without destroying substantial completed work may be measured by correction cost. Where separating defects would cause confusion, diminution in value may apply collectively. The nature and magnitude of each defect determine the appropriate application, with unreasonable economic waste limiting destructive reconstruction. Id. at 572–574.

## Reasoning and application
The owners’ percentage-based objection did not establish a failure of substantial performance. Prior decisions involving a grossly inadequate boiler, a practically useless boathouse, defective painting, or a heating system that failed its intended function illustrated failures to accomplish a contract’s essential purpose. This house differed. Its plan was a stock floor plan, not detailed blueprints; the specifications were standard forms with modifications and additions, and practical construction experience resolved many details. The owners received a usable house, although one with numerous dissatisfactions. The record did not show that every detail was essential to their agreement. Id. at 570–572.

The court did not replace all repair allowances with an undifferentiated market-value calculation. The defects could be separated without confusion. The trial court had rejected unproved items, and those findings were not against the great weight and clear preponderance of the evidence. Correcting ceiling plaster cracks, mud jacking and repairing the patio floor, and reconstructing a nonstructural patio wall did not involve unreasonable waste when the house and its cost were considered as a whole. Those repair allowances therefore stood. Id. at 573.

Moving the living-room wall presented a different problem. It would substantially destroy completed work, damage other portions of the house, and require replastering and redecorating walls and ceilings in at least two rooms. Nothing showed that the owners demanded its relocation while construction was underway. The court treated the applicable diminution measure as contemplating that the wall would remain. Because both sides’ experts agreed it did not affect market price, the owners established no recoverable loss for that defect, even though their preference for the specified room dimensions was unsatisfied. This conclusion depended on these facts; the court’s earlier recognition of specially important contractual details prevents treating it as a categorical refusal to consider personal purposes. Id. at 573–574.

Plante’s claimed extras also failed. The contract required written agreement for extras, and none existed. The evidence conflicted about whether the disputed work constituted extras or whether the owners waived the contractual requirement. Plante carried the burden of establishing those claims and did not demonstrate error in their denial. His challenge to the patio-wall allowance failed under the same repair-cost analysis already applied to the owners’ appeal. Id. at 574.

## Holding, disposition, and source scope
Hallows wrote for the court; the complete report contains no separate judicial writing or numeric vote specification. The court affirmed substantial performance and the trial court’s differentiated treatment of proven defects, including no award for relocating the living-room wall and denial of the builder’s disputed extras. The report records denial of rehearing on October 4, 1960, with $25 costs; its opening asterisk carries that procedural note. Id. at 569–570, 574.
