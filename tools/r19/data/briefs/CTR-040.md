# Vokes v. Arthur Murray, Inc.

212 So. 2d 906 (Fla. 2d DCA 1968) | Contracts

## Citation and Parties

Vokes v. Arthur Murray, Inc., 212 So. 2d 906 (Fla. 2d DCA 1968). Florida Second District Court of Appeal; Judge Pierce. Assigned excerpt: CB 523-526. Audrey Vokes was the student seeking equitable relief. The defendants were Arthur Murray, Inc. and J.P. Davenport, who operated the franchised dance studio.

## Alleged Material Facts

Vokes, a 51-year-old widow, wanted to become an accomplished dancer and find a new interest in life. After attending a studio event, she purchased an introductory set of eight half-hour lessons for $14.50. Over less than sixteen months, she was sold fourteen courses totaling 2,302 hours for $31,090.45. Those figures describe hours purchased, not a finding that she completed 2,302 hours of instruction. CB 523-525; 212 So. 2d at 907-908.

The complaint described sustained praise of her grace, improvement, and dancing potential, accompanied by supposed aptitude tests and sales tied to medals, memberships, and trips. The studio repeatedly sold additional lessons while she retained hundreds or more than a thousand unused hours. Allegedly, the instructors knew that she had little dance aptitude, had trouble hearing the beat, and was not making the represented progress. They withheld that assessment while continuing to sell. CB 524-525.

The allegation was not simply that she paid a great deal for recreation and later regretted it. It was that the instructors knowingly misrepresented their professional evaluation of her present progress and ability to induce further purchases. The surrounding sales practices supported the claimed pattern of overreaching and impaired judgment. CB 524-525.

## Procedural History and Relief Requested

The trial court dismissed Vokes's fourth amended complaint with prejudice for failure to state a cause of action. She appealed. The appellate court accepted the material allegations as true for purposes of testing legal sufficiency and reversed. It held that she was entitled to proceed with her claim, not that fraud had been proved after trial. CB 523-526.

Vokes sought cancellation of the dance contracts, an accounting, and recovery of the portion of the payments not chargeable to instruction already given. The opinion did not award the entire $31,090.45 on appeal. Separating the requested equitable relief from the procedural holding prevents a common overstatement. 212 So. 2d at 908-909.

## Issue, Rule, and Holding

Can purported opinions about a customer's dancing ability and progress support relief for fraud or misrepresentation when delivered by instructors with superior knowledge as part of an alleged course of deceptive sales? Yes, at the pleading stage presented. Statements framed as opinions may be actionable in circumstances involving superior knowledge, trust, unequal ability to evaluate the subject, or devices that make the opinion misleading. CB 525-526; 212 So. 2d at 908-909.

## Reasoning

The ordinary distinction between fact and opinion did not dispose of the case. The studio's instructors possessed expertise and repeatedly evaluated the student's actual performance. The alleged statements about rapid improvement and favorable aptitude could communicate a knowledgeable factual assessment, not merely enthusiasm. On the pleaded facts, the instructors knew their expressed assessment was false. CB 525.

The court also invoked the obligation to tell the whole truth once one undertakes to speak. Even without an initial duty to disclose every observation, an instructor cannot necessarily offer selectively reassuring evaluations while concealing the contrary knowledge that gives those evaluations meaning. That reasoning links Vokes to Kannavos, despite the different subject matter. CB 525-526.

The continuing pattern mattered: staged achievements, escalating purchases, large balances of unused lessons, and pressure tied to special opportunities. These allegations made the transaction different from isolated flattering sales language. The court used broader equitable language about undue influence and unconscionability, but the assigned analysis should still identify the concrete allegedly false representations and why reliance on expertise could be justified. CB 524-526.

## Reading the Opinion Critically

The opinion uses patronizing and theatrical language about Vokes's age, aspirations, and dancing ability. Those rhetorical choices are not independent legal elements. The notes invite the reader to question what is truly objectionable: deception, price, lack of instructional value, or judicial discomfort with what the customer wanted. A careful analysis grounds potential relief in provable conduct and misrepresentation rather than a judgment that her interests were foolish. CB 526.

A customer may knowingly pay for companionship, pleasure, encouragement, or instruction with modest prospects of technical improvement. The case does not say courts can rescind whenever those benefits are expensive. The allegation that professional instructors knowingly fabricated progress to induce more purchases does the relevant work.

## Comparison with Speakers of Sport

Both cases involve favorable statements made to sell services. Speakers treats the particular forecast as aspirational competition for a professional baseball player and separately applies Illinois's scheme restriction. Vokes alleges false expert evaluations of a student's present ability within an ongoing pattern of manipulation. Future-oriented wording and numerical specificity do not automatically determine which case controls.

Application. “You may enjoy the class” is ordinarily different from an instructor's knowingly false report that testing shows substantial technical improvement. A bona fide, clearly qualified assessment differs from fabricated progress. The analysis must remain sensitive to context, proof, reliance, and the governing jurisdiction.

## Participation and source scope

Judge Pierce wrote the opinion, with Chief Judge Liles and Judge Mann concurring; no separate writing or judicial footnote appears in the complete report. The appeal was No. 67-476, decided July 31, 1968. The court reversed the order dismissing the fourth amended complaint with prejudice, permitting the pleaded claim to proceed. It did not find fraud after a trial or enter a refund judgment. 212 So. 2d at 906, 908–909.
