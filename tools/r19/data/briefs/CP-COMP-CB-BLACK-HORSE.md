# Black Horse Lane Associates, L.P. v. Dow Chemical Corporation

**228 F.3d 275 (3d Cir. 2000)** — September 20, 2000; argued August 8; No. 00-5031. Greenberg, joined by Barry and Weis. Civil Procedure: an unprepared Rule 30(b)(6) designee and Rule 37(d). Assigned: CivPro.pdf physical pages 500–501, printed pages 978–979, Note 6.

## Facts and procedural posture

Essex sold environmentally contaminated New Jersey property to United States Land Resources for $3.6 million in 1985. The buyer assigned its rights to Black Horse Lane. Related entities and their principal, lawyer Lawrence Berger, joined the eventual suit. Dow acquired Essex's stock in 1988. Paragraph 16 of the sale agreement required Essex, at its own expense, to implement the state-approved cleanup plan and obtain Department of Environmental Protection approval. The buyers knew the contamination existed. Soil remediation finished within two years; groundwater work continued. 278–280.

The purchasers sued in 1997, alleging breach through unreasonable delay, breach of good faith, CERCLA response costs, New Jersey Spill Act relief, and related damages. Essex counterclaimed for permission to employ institutional and engineering controls. These claims explain why the noticed corporate deposition covered the sale agreement, cleanup work, and expenditures. 280–282.

The plaintiffs designated Berger as their Rule 30(b)(6) representative. After his deposition began on October 2, 1998, scheduling disputes required an order that he resume on October 13. He did not appear. An October 15 order imposed costs for that absence and the application, and set October 19 for continuation. At later sessions he appeared physically but repeatedly offered evasive answers, disclaimed knowledge of the deposition notice and his representative role, and refused to prepare. 280–281, 299–305.

The magistrate judge's June 30, 1999 order imposed a bar against positions or evidence inconsistent with Berger's testimony, and fees and costs associated with the deposition and sanctions motion. The district court separately granted defendants summary judgment on August 10, then affirmed the sanctions and dismissed the counterclaim without prejudice for lack of a ripe controversy on December 16. Plaintiffs appealed both orders; defendants did not cross-appeal the counterclaim dismissal. 281–283.

## Issue and discovery holding

**Does physical attendance preclude Rule 37(d) sanctions when the corporation's designated representative is unprepared and unable or unwilling to supply the noticed information?** No. For a corporate Rule 30(b)(6) deposition, inadequate preparation can amount to nonappearance. The court adopted the Fifth Circuit's functional approach in Resolution Trust Corp. v. Southern Union Co. and upheld the monetary sanctions on this record. 302–305.

The corporation bears responsibility for selecting and preparing a witness who can testify about the designated subjects. The procedure is intended to obtain the organization's information without successively deposing officers who each deny personal knowledge. Physical presence alone does not fulfill that responsibility. This holding concerned an organizational designee; Note 18 distinguishes cases involving natural-person deponents. The court did not decide that every incomplete answer automatically warrants sanctions. 303–304 & n.18.

Rule 37(d), unlike Rule 37(b), did not require a prior order compelling discovery as a prerequisite to the monetary sanction. Under the rule text then in force, the good-faith-conference certification applied to failures under clauses (2) and (3), involving interrogatory answers and inspection responses, whereas this motion arose under clause (1), nonappearance. That historical distinction should not be restated as an independently verified description of today's certification requirements. 301–302 & n.17.

The facts supported the discretionary ruling. Berger claimed no recollection of an agreement he signed and likely negotiated, and could not explain cleanup expenditures or consultant invoices relevant to plaintiffs' own CERCLA claim. He professed ignorance of Rule 30(b)(6), repeatedly denied his designated role, and ultimately admitted that he had not read the notice. His education, professional status, and repeated opportunities reinforced the finding that the failures represented deliberate noncooperation. Seventeen hours of testimony and a lengthy transcript did not resolve these deficiencies. 301, 304–305.

**Important limit:** Note 16 expressly declined to reach the challenge to the Rule 37(b) evidence/position-preclusion sanction because the entire complaint was properly dismissed on summary judgment. The appellate court identified Rule 37(d), rather than Rule 37(b), as the basis of the monetary award. General language affirming both orders should not be read as a separate merits endorsement of every preclusion objection. 300–301 n.16.

## Other holdings in the complete opinion

The contract judgment survived, but the Third Circuit rejected one of the district court's rationales. New Jersey law implied a **reasonable time for cleanup performance** despite the requirement for state approval. Otherwise Essex could delay indefinitely. Nevertheless, plaintiffs failed to produce evidence that a reasonable time had expired. A missed advance estimate, interruptions in pumping, and an expert's general description of slow, ineffective progress did not adequately establish unreasonable delay in this particular remediation. Note 8 emphasizes the continuing obligation to proceed diligently; the decision did not give Essex unlimited time. 283–287 & n.8.

The good-faith claim also failed. New Jersey recognizes an independent implied covenant, but questioning soil standards in a letter to regulators did not establish bad faith, particularly when the soil cleanup was timely completed and a later memorandum reaffirmed compliance. No sufficient evidence supported the claimed purposeful delay. Note 10 distinguishes the governing New Jersey doctrine from Pennsylvania law. 287–290 & n.10.

The consultant expenses submitted for reimbursement were litigation-related rather than necessary CERCLA response costs. The consultant reviewed Essex's reports, but did not investigate the property or perform cleanup. Even treating the work as oversight rather than litigation preparation did not make it recoverable under the circuit's then-governing interpretation in Rohm & Haas. Prior undefined consulting work did not change the character of the specific November 1996–May 1998 invoices claimed. This brief records the 2000 holding without certifying that its treatment of oversight costs remains current. 290–299 & nn.11–14.

Note 11 rejects declaratory relief because no sufficiently immediate prospect of plaintiffs' future recoverable costs was shown, rejects the undeveloped injunction contention, and sustains dismissal of the Spill Act claim. Note 14 disposes of the residual damages count because no independent substantive basis remained. Note 5 sustains dismissal of Dow, including absence of evidence justifying veil piercing. The earlier denial of Rule 11 sanctions was outside this appeal. 281 n.5, 282 n.6, 291–292 n.11, 299 n.14.

The counterclaim was properly dismissed **without prejudice for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction**. Essex had not applied for, and could not yet obtain, a Classification Exception Area; plaintiffs' threats to oppose it did not create a ripe controversy. The court did not adjudicate a permanent entitlement to refuse all controls. 299 n.15.

## Exact disposition, textbook comparison, and verification

**Both August 10 and December 16 orders affirmed:** dismissal of the complaint with prejudice, denial of plaintiffs' counterclaim summary-judgment motion, counterclaim dismissal without prejudice, and monetary discovery sanctions. The monetary sanctions had been quantified and apparently paid, but the opinion does not state the amount. Merits and counterclaim review was plenary; sanctions review was for abuse of discretion. 282–283 & n.7, 305.

The textbook accurately locates the corporate-deponent holding at 304 and the district judge's quoted criticism at 300. It compresses the preparation and noncooperation history, and omits the distinct contract, statutory, counterclaim, and Note 16 rulings. Baker and Southern Union in the same note are separate authorities. Assigned 500–501.
