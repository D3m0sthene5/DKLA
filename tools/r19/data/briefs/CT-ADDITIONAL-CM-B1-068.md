# Marx v. Department of Commerce

220 Mich. App. 66, 558 N.W.2d 460 (Mich. Ct. App. Nov. 12, 1996) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B1-068

The Michigan Court of Appeals affirmed summary disposition for the landowners and the order vacating Birch Lane's public dedication. The public authorities had not established a timely manifest acceptance of the 1911 dedication. The township's 1979 resolution was sufficient in form, assuming its authority, but came after the offer had both lapsed and been withdrawn by inconsistent private use. These were separate grounds: lapse arose from unreasonable delay; withdrawal arose from affirmative conduct. Judge O'Connell wrote for Fitzgerald, O'Connell, and assigned circuit judge T. L. Ludington; no separate writing appears. (220 Mich. App. at 68–70, 78–82.)

## A platted road that the authorities had not developed

George and Laura Franklin platted Zenith Heights in Bay Township, Charlevoix County, in August 1911, designating its streets and alleys for public use. Birch Lane lay between Lots six and seven, running from Valley Brook Avenue to Walloon Lake. The township approved the plat on August 14, 1911. Raymond and Jean Marx later acquired Lot six in 1970, Lot seven in 1976, and Lot five in 1977. The factual narrative describes their residence on Lot seven and a driveway encroaching on Birch Lane. In 1993 they sought vacation of Birch Lane and a declaratory judgment quieting title, alleging that neither the township nor the county road commission had ever accepted its dedication. (Id. at 69.)

The government defendants moved for summary disposition under MCR 2.116(C)(10), relying on plat approval, asserted maintenance expenditures, and an alleged assumption of road jurisdiction under the McNitt Act. The landowners responded under MCR 2.116(I)(2), asking for judgment in their favor because no acceptance had occurred and their own use was inconsistent with public dedication. The trial court granted that relief and ordered vacation. Although the complaint named other defendants, including the Department of Commerce, only the township and county road commission pursued the consolidated appeals. The appellate court reviewed summary disposition de novo, examining whether documentary evidence created a genuine material factual issue and which party was entitled to judgment as law. (Id. at 68–70 & n.1.)

## The McNitt Act evidence did not establish acceptance of this road

The road commission argued that the McNitt Act had brought Birch Lane into its road system. The statute, repealed in 1951, directed staged incorporation of township highway mileage and then dedicated streets and alleys in specified recorded plats. But the defendants could locate no resolution assuming jurisdiction. They supplied only a purported McNitt map and a 1938 survey of certified subdivision-road mileage. (Id. at 70–71.)

Under Kraus, even a general resolution claiming all township roads was insufficient to manifest acceptance without identifying the disputed road or its recorded plat. Treating generic compliance as acceptance risked assigning public duties and expenses for roads the authority had not knowingly accepted. Here there was not even a general resolution. The map did not refer to Birch Lane or disclose its purpose or significance; the mileage survey omitted Birch Lane's mileage and did not show a connection to McNitt implementation. These materials could not support an inference that the commission accepted this particular dedication. (Id. at 71–73.)

## Plat approval, public expenditures, and a formal resolution were distinct theories

A valid public dedication required a recorded plat manifesting the proprietor's intent and timely acceptance by the proper authority. Acceptance had to appear through a manifest public act. It could be formal, through confirmation or acceptance, or informal, through opening, improving, regulating, or using the road in the qualifying public manner. The requirement protects authorities from responsibility for unwanted or unneeded property and avoids waste land. A timely completed dedication would also constrain judicial vacation without the authority's consent. The burden of proving acceptance lay with the public authority; withdrawal had to be proved by the owner. (Id. at 73–75.)

Although § 253(1) of the Subdivision Control Act described the recorded plat as a conveyance vesting fee simple for designated uses, the court followed decisions requiring acceptance rather than automatic vesting upon recording. It specifically identified formal resolution, actual expenditure of public money for repair, improvement and control, and public use as potential ways of accepting. The opinion therefore did not require a formal governmental resolution as the exclusive means of acceptance. (Id. at 74–75.)

The township first relied on its 1911 plat approval. Earlier appellate panels had disagreed whether approval alone sufficed: Bangle equated it with acceptance, while Salzer rejected that equation. The court adopted Salzer, reasoning that approval without reference to accepting the dedicated property lacked the necessary manifestation. That approach accorded with Kraus's protection against unwanted public obligations. Note 2 distinguished Richey, a dispute involving another Zenith Heights road that had actually been frequently used for traffic; it did not decide that the township had accepted every road in the subdivision regardless of use. (Id. at 75–78 & n.2.)

The asserted expenditures also failed. The evidence showed a 1952 discussion of underwriting improvements to roads including Birch Lane, but no actual work or expenditure on that lane. Discussion of a possible project did not constitute a manifest acceptance. (Id. at 78.)

The 1979 resolution formally accepting previously unaccepted dedicated lands did specifically refer to Zenith Heights. The court assumed that the township had authority and acknowledged that this resolution ordinarily would satisfy the form of acceptance. Note 3 reserved the institutional question: the road commission asserted at argument that only it could accept, but had not briefed the question, which was treated as abandoned. The resolution failed here because of timing, without a holding that the township necessarily possessed or lacked the contested power. (Id. at 78 & n.3.)

## Lapse from delay and withdrawal by private use

An offer to dedicate can be treated as continuing when neither the proprietor nor successors withdraw it, but continuation ultimately depends on the circumstances. The court compared decisions finding eighty-six and eighty-seven years unreasonable with a decision it described as allowing twenty-six years. It found the present sixty-eight-year interval closer to the former cases. The township offered no evidence that the dedication was meant to remain indefinitely available, and three generations was unreasonable on this record. The 1979 resolution therefore followed lapse of the offer. This was a contextual determination, not a new fixed sixty-eight-year statutory cutoff for every dedication. (Id. at 78–79.)

The court independently found affirmative withdrawal. Using dedicated land inconsistently with public ownership can manifest withdrawal, with the type of use and government acquiescence evaluated in context. Other decisions involved structures, fences, trees, or underbrush. Here the owners supplied unrefuted evidence of a driveway built on Birch Lane in 1970 and of the road commission telling them the township did not intend to develop the road. Their regular parking and treatment of the strip as private property were inconsistent with its offered public use. Complete blockage of passage was unnecessary, even though an earlier case had involved such blockage. (Id. at 80.)

Those affirmative acts supplied the distinction the assignment asks students to explain. Merely waiting left the question of unreasonable lapse; constructing and using the driveway, accompanied by public acquiescence, demonstrated withdrawal. On the unrefuted record there was no material factual issue requiring trial on timely acceptance, and the vacation order was proper. The court's later statutory analysis expressly placed the withdrawal in 1970. (Id. at 80–82.)

## Remaining issues and exact disposition

The court declined to decide road-end access arguments because they were unnecessary to the trial and appellate resolutions. It treated a claimed public policy favoring lake access as abandoned because it was not in the statement of questions and was unsupported by pertinent authority. Those decisions are not merits holdings against public lake access generally. (Id. at 81.)

It also rejected reliance on a 1978 statutory presumption of acceptance. Under § 255b(2)(a), competent evidence of withdrawal before the statute's effective date and before acceptance rebutted the presumption. The owners' 1970 withdrawal preceded both 1978 and the township's attempted 1979 acceptance. The judgment was affirmed, preserving summary disposition for the owners and the order of vacation. The appellate court did not remand for more evidence, award contract damages, or declare a general private right to close every road end. (Id. at 81–82.)
