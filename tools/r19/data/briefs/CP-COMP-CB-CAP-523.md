# New York Telephone Co. v. Maltbie

**291 U.S. 645 (1934) — Supreme Court of the United States, February 19, 1934, No. 586.** Per curiam; no separate opinion or numerical vote is reported. Civil Procedure: appeal by a prevailing party and the preclusive effect of findings that party cannot appeal. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 629, Note 1, printed 1244.

## Facts and procedural posture

New York Telephone challenged public utility rate orders. A specially constituted federal district court in the Southern District of New York, operating under the then-applicable three-judge-court statute, permanently enjoined enforcement of those orders as confiscatory. The injunction was unqualified: the company obtained the judicial relief it sought. (645.)

The decree also fixed the value of the company’s property for 1924, 1926, and 1928 and the rate of return to be allowed. Despite winning the injunction, the company appealed to obtain review of those portions of the decree. A motion to dismiss its appeal was submitted February 3, 1934. The short report supplies no dollar valuations, particular rate percentages, or additional evidentiary account; those details should not be inferred from other utility cases. (645.)

## Issue and holding

Could the company appeal its favorable decree simply to challenge the embedded valuation and return determinations? The Court held that it could not. Having received an unqualified injunction against the challenged orders, the company was not entitled to prosecute an appeal from the decree in its favor for that purpose. The Court **granted the motion to dismiss the company’s appeal**. This was an appellate dismissal, not a reversal of the injunction or a fresh adjudication that the challenged rates were valid. (645.)

## Preclusion and the reach of the ruling

The Court expressly addressed the company’s possible concern about future consequences. The valuation and return portions of this decree were not to operate as res judicata concerning later legislative action by the Public Service Commission setting future rates, or judicial proceedings concerning those future rates. The company therefore could not use this appeal to revise findings accompanying complete present relief, and those findings did not bind it in the specified future rate-setting disputes. (645.)

The ruling should be kept within that setting. It does not erase the decree, reopen the original rate controversy, or declare that all favorable judgments lack preclusive effect. Nor does its brief explanation resolve every circumstance in which a winner may challenge an adverse determination. It links the unqualified relief obtained here to the absence of an appeal and expressly limits the future effect of the determinations the company sought to contest. (645.)

## Textbook connection

The assigned excerpt accurately reproduces the operative reasoning and the future-rate preclusion limitation. It omits the opening statutory description, the final sentence granting dismissal, and supporting citations; those portions appear in the full report. The subsequent textbook questions concern *Electrical Fittings*, patent validity, declaratory judgments, and Rule 54(c), followed by the separate *Partmar* account. They supply comparison problems and are not facts, holdings, or reservations stated in this utility-rate order. (Assigned physical 629; actual 645.)

A useful comparison is the difference between winning all requested operative relief while disliking accompanying findings and losing an operative portion of a judgment. Here, the company’s injunction was unqualified, and the Court dismissed its appeal while expressly protecting the identified future rate proceedings from preclusion. The competing majority and dissent accounts in *Partmar* must be analyzed separately rather than attributed to this per curiam report, which gives no numerical vote.
