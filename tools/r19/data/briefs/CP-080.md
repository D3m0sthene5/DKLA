# Ryder v. Jefferson Hotel Co.

**Civil Procedure · CP-080**  
121 S.C. 72, 113 S.E. 474 (1922) · Supreme Court of South Carolina · September 7, 1922

## Facts and procedural setting

Charles and Edith Ryder sued together over their expulsion from a hotel. Charles became a guest on October 2, 1921, and Edith joined him on October 4. Their complaint alleged that, late that night, a hotel agent roused them by rapping on their door, behaved rudely, insulted Edith, and forced both spouses to leave and find accommodation elsewhere at midnight. They asserted injuries to reputation, credit, and business, including lost customers and profits from Charles's business, and demanded $10,000. These were allegations tested on demurrer, rather than facts established after trial or damages actually awarded. The dissent explained that the alleged insult included treating the spouses as an unmarried couple. 121 S.C. at 73–74, 77.

The defendants separately demurred because the complaint improperly combined the spouses' causes of action. The circuit court overruled the demurrers, and the defendants appealed. The appeal concerned whether the two plaintiffs could maintain this combined action under South Carolina's pleading statutes; it did not determine whether the hotel had wrongfully expelled either guest.

## Question and governing rule

Could spouses jointly sue for their personal injuries from the same hotel incident when each possessed a distinct right to compensation? Justice Marion's majority answered no under the applicable state code. Section 218 required joined causes to belong to the specified statutory classes, affect all parties to the action, satisfy the venue requirements, and be separately stated, subject to a foreclosure exception. A shared event could satisfy the same-transaction component without satisfying the requirement that the claims affect all parties. 121 S.C. at 74–76.

A preexisting legal relationship could support a joint claim when the wrong injured a common legal interest and produced common damage. Partnership interests supplied one example. But marriage did not automatically merge a husband's and wife's personal rights to recover for separate insults or injuries. The majority distinguished a husband's formal participation in his wife's action, and a husband's independent claim for expenses or loss of services, from the attempted recovery here for both spouses' own personal injuries.

## Majority's reasoning

The majority accepted that both claims arose from one transaction and the same alleged wrongful conduct. Its objection was to the legal interests and damages being combined. Each spouse had a separate personal claim and a separate potential recovery; neither possessed a legal interest in the damages due the other. Even spouses injured while sharing a railroad berth would ordinarily have separate personal-injury causes of action. The existence of the marital relationship therefore did not supply the missing common entitlement. 121 S.C. at 74–76.

The court also relied on its earlier refusal to combine a survival claim belonging to an estate with a death claim benefiting statutory beneficiaries. Although those claims could arise from the same wrong and be prosecuted by the same representative, the beneficiaries and recoveries differed. The reasons for keeping the Ryder claims separate were at least as strong: neither spouse had a legal right to benefit from the other's personal recovery. Separate verdicts and judgments would be needed to determine the respective injuries.

The full opinion adds a procedural point omitted from the assigned excerpt. A motion requiring more definite allegations or separately stated counts would not cure the actual joinder defect. Nor was one spouse's claim merely redundant matter that should be struck: each plaintiff was entitled to allege that person's own injury. The defendants' demurrers were therefore an appropriate way to raise the misjoinder. 121 S.C. at 77.

## Dissent and judgment

Justice Fraser dissented. He treated the alleged denial and disparagement of the spouses' marital relationship as an injury to their common legal relationship, comparable to an injury affecting a partnership. In his view, that feature distinguished the case from two separate physical injuries in a railroad accident. Charles's individual business losses might properly be removed on a motion to strike, but that did not require rejection of the combined claim for injury to the marital relationship. He would have upheld the circuit court's ruling. 121 S.C. at 77–78.

The court **reversed the order overruling the demurrers**. Chief Justice Gary and Justice Cothran joined Marion; Fraser dissented, yielding three concurring votes and one dissent among the votes reported. The decision did not absolve the hotel of the alleged misconduct or adjudicate either plaintiff's personal claim on its merits.

## Significance and source scope

Ryder illustrates why the same incident and a close relationship between plaintiffs did not necessarily create a joint entitlement under this historical state pleading regime. Its holding turns on South Carolina's then-existing statutory requirement that joined causes affect all parties. It should not be mechanically restated as a rule governing modern federal permissive joinder.
