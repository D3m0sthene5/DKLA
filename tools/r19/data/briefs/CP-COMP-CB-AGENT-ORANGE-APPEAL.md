# In re “Agent Orange” Product Liability Litigation — 1980 appeal

**635 F.2d 987 (2d Cir. 1980) · November 24, 1980 · Civil Procedure**

Judge Amalya L. Kearse wrote for the majority, joined by Judge Van Graafeiland. Chief Judge Feinberg dissented. The court reversed, 2–1, the district court’s refusal to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The assigned account is Boyle Note 3, CivPro physical PDF 287, printed 538–539; this is the early federal-common-law appeal, distinct from the later 2008 contractor-defense litigation.

## Allegations and the interlocutory appeal

Vietnam veterans and their families sued five chemical manufacturers—Diamond Shamrock, Monsanto, Thompson Hayward, Hercules, and Dow—for injuries allegedly caused by exposure to Agent Orange. They alleged contamination with dioxin and sought relief for both actual injuries and risks of future injury under theories including negligence, strict product liability, and warranty. Defendants disputed causation. Because this was a pleading-stage jurisdictional appeal, the court accepted the factual allegations as true for that limited purpose. It did not adjudicate whether Agent Orange caused particular injuries. (988–990.)

The litigation began with suits filed in late 1978 and early 1979. Thirteen actions with 30 named plaintiffs were initially transferred to Judge Pratt for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings under §1407; further filings brought the named-plaintiff count to more than 800 by the time of the appeal. The complaint purported to represent 2.4 million veterans and many family members or survivors in fifteen subclasses. It sought damages, a trust fund drawn from defendants’ earnings to protect later claimants, and an injunction against further manufacture. These descriptions concern proposed representation and requested relief, not a certified class or granted remedies. (988–989 & n.5.)

The third amended complaint replaced the second by consent and was the operative pleading. Pratt had rejected statutory causes of action but retained claims under federal common law, concluding that those claims supplied §1331 jurisdiction. Defendants obtained certification under §1292(b), and the court of appeals granted leave to appeal on January 16, 1980. Thus, this was an authorized interlocutory appeal of the jurisdictional ruling, not an appeal from a damages judgment. (988–992 & nn.1, 3–4.)

## The issue and governing distinction

A federal-common-law cause of action can arise under federal law for §1331 purposes. The decisive question was whether such a cause of action existed here. Both sides accepted Pratt’s three-part analytical framework: substantial federal interests, the effect of applying state law on those interests, and the effect of displacing state law on state interests. The majority accepted that framework for discussion, but resolved the case on the first consideration and expressly did not reach the second or third. (989 n.4, 993 & n.11.)

Drawing on Wallis and Miree, the majority required a specifically demonstrated significant conflict between state law and an identifiable federal policy or interest. Federal concern about the parties or events was insufficient unless it revealed a federal policy concerning the outcome of their private dispute. That distinction explains why an extraordinarily important national controversy could still fail to support judicially created federal tort law. (993–995.)

## Why the majority rejected federal common law

Plaintiffs emphasized the unique national relationship between soldiers and the government, relying on Standard Oil and Clearfield Trust. The majority distinguished those cases because the United States was asserting its own rights there. Those settings implicated uniform rules for governmental rights and duties. Here, veterans’ claims ran against private manufacturers, and no substantial governmental rights or duties depended on their resolution. Note 6 acknowledges that defendants had impleaded the United States, but explains that third-party complaints could not enlarge jurisdiction over plaintiffs’ claims. The majority did not overlook the government’s presence in that separate procedural capacity. (990 n.6, 993.)

Uniformity alone did not justify federal common law between private parties. Different state results are a normal feature of the federal system, and federal common law, even when authorized, sometimes borrows state rules. The majority used the adoption of state limitations periods in federal labor litigation to show that the existence of federal law does not invariably demand a single national substantive rule. Consequently, potential differences in compensation among veterans did not themselves identify a federal policy requiring judicial displacement of state law. (993–994.)

More fundamentally, the federal concerns pulled in different directions. Protecting veterans could support broad contractor liability; protecting military procurement could support restraints on liability, avoiding higher prices, supply conditions, or indemnification demands. Choosing which interest should prevail required a substantive policy judgment. Congress, not the court in the first instance, had to identify the balance. Congress had ordered an epidemiological study, as note 13 records, but had not determined the policy governing this contest between veterans and suppliers. The number of potential claimants and possible financial effects did not reveal that policy’s content. (994–995 & nn.13, 15.)

The majority distinguished judicial supplementation of an existing federal regulatory scheme from creating one in the face of congressional inaction. Owens involved a statutory scheme protecting federal prisoners; it did not authorize the court to devise a corresponding private product-liability regime for veterans. Note 14 further explains that FIFRA’s regulation of pesticides did not supply a basis for displacing the entire body of state product-liability law. Without an identifiable federal policy threatened by state law, the asserted federal-common-law cause of action failed. (995 & n.14.)

## Statutory claims and reserved procedural issues

The majority separately endorsed rejection of a private FIFRA cause of action in note 9. Unlike Pratt’s broader description of persons protected by the statute, it found no indication that the original enactment specially benefited military personnel. It examined legislative intent, the administrative enforcement scheme, the continuing state-law role, and the burdens of an additional remedy. For the 1972 amendment, Congress’s express rejection of citizen-suit proposals made the negative legislative-intent inference stronger. The result covered both enactments, rather than treating the later amendment as an independent private damages remedy. (991–992 n.9.)

Note 10 declines to decide whether the requested trust fund or claims by merely “at risk” persons should be struck. Although interlocutory review could extend beyond the certified issue, the court thought immediate review inappropriate: jurisdiction might end the federal litigation, the district judge had not assessed the merits of those motions, and reargument remained reserved. Whether plaintiffs would invoke diversity jurisdiction was unclear. The reversal therefore was not an adjudication that all plaintiffs had no state-law claim or that all requested remedies were categorically unavailable. (992–993 n.10.)

## Feinberg’s dissent

Feinberg would have retained federal jurisdiction. He considered the federal government’s protective relationship with soldiers both inherently national and supported by medical-care, compensation, and military-welfare statutes and regulations. His two footnotes identify the prison statute used in Owens and examples of military regulations. In his view, it was anomalous to recognize a sufficiently strong federal interest in protecting prisoners while denying comparable protection to soldiers. (995–998 & dissent nn.1–2.)

He also rejected the majority’s balancing objection. Fair treatment of contractors need not conflict with protecting soldiers, and possible future financial distress of suppliers was speculative compared with the alleged present injuries. He treated veterans’ welfare and fair, uniform treatment as paramount. Applying numerous state product-liability standards would frustrate uniformity for soldiers who served together without regard to state citizenship. Because these unprecedented claims did not implicate a settled state-law framework, he agreed with Pratt that the third factor also favored federal common law. Feinberg expressed no view on the plaintiffs’ unusual substantive or procedural remedies; his disagreement concerned jurisdiction. (995, 998–999.)

## Disposition and source scope

The majority reversed the order denying dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The mandate is “Reversed,” not a final liability determination or a damages award. The holding is that these circumstances did not support a federal-common-law right of action because no sufficiently identifiable federal policy justified its creation. (995.)
