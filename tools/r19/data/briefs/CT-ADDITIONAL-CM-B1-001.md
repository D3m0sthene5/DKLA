# Guilmet v. Campbell
385 Mich. 57, 188 N.W.2d 601 (1971) | Contracts

## Decision and significance

The Michigan Supreme Court affirmed a $50,000 judgment against two surgeons on a contract count, although the jury returned a defense verdict on the malpractice count. The question on appeal was whether the trial court should have entered judgment notwithstanding the verdict. Five justices joined Justice T. G. Kavanagh's opinion allowing the jury to determine whether the surgeons' statements formed a clear promise of a particular result. Justice T. E. Brennan concurred in affirmance on a different understanding of the general verdict; Justices Adams and Williams joined that concurrence as well as the majority. Justice Black dissented. The result was therefore six votes to affirm and one to reverse. (385 Mich. at 61, 65–71, 75, 102.)

The decision concerns the historical common law of medical promises. The assigned note uses it to explain legislative override: it reports that Michigan adopted a signed-writing requirement for medical promises of cure three years later. That later statutory development is not part of the 1971 holding.

## Facts and procedural course

Richard Guilmet experienced nearly fatal bleeding from a peptic ulcer in fall 1963. His treating physician, Dr. Klewicki, recommended the defendant surgeons, Drs. Campbell and Arena. At a January 1964 consultation, Guilmet asked whether he should undergo an operation. According to the majority, nobody told him that he had to undergo it; Campbell testified that Guilmet was in excellent physical condition before surgery and that the planned operation was not an emergency. (Id. at 61–62.)

The alleged promises came from Guilmet's testimony, rather than an undisputed written guarantee. He said Campbell represented that the operation would remove his troubles, permit him to eat and drink freely, and allow him to resume normal activities. Campbell allegedly described himself and Arena as experienced specialists, the operation as simple and without danger, the hospitalization as four or five days, and the total absence from work as at most three or four weeks. He allegedly said Guilmet could discard his medication after surgery and invited him to compare the operation's cost with decades of medication and medical visits. Guilmet then underwent the operation. The trial judge doubted that Campbell had made every statement attributed to him but recognized that the jury could credit Guilmet's account. (Id. at 62–66.)

Guilmet's postoperative experience differed markedly from the promised result. The majority described a diagnosis of ruptured esophagus associated with surgical trauma during the vagotomy, further operations to insert drainage tubes, hepatitis that Campbell thought probably resulted from transfusions, severe coughing and vomiting, and a decline in weight from 170 to 88 pounds. Guilmet could no longer maintain his previous work and recreational activities. The majority also reported testimony that one infection could recur for as long as twenty years. These descriptions establish the alleged failure of the promised result; they do not establish negligent surgery, which the jury rejected on the tort count. Black's dissent disputed the causal characterization of the esophageal injury and reproduced testimony describing a possible postoperative spontaneous perforation. (Id. at 64–65, 84–87.)

Richard and Dorothy Guilmet sued under separate contract and negligence counts. Their contract pleading included both an undertaking to cure the stomach disorder and allegations of unskillful performance, deficient postoperative care, and failure to achieve satisfactory results. The trial court denied a directed-verdict motion. The jury found for the surgeons on malpractice and awarded the plaintiffs $50,000 for breach of contract. The trial court denied judgment notwithstanding that verdict, and the Court of Appeals affirmed. The Supreme Court granted leave and affirmed in turn. The $365,000 mentioned in the dissent was the amount claimed, not the award. (Id. at 65–66, 71–75, 78–80.)

## Parties' positions and issue

The surgeons argued that a contract to effect a cure required a clear enforceable promise contemplated by the parties, separate consideration, and reliance. Their position also denied that the disputed assurances amounted to a guarantee: they maintained that their undertaking was to exercise the care, knowledge, and attention ordinarily possessed by physicians under comparable circumstances. The plaintiffs treated the assurances as terms offered before the operation contract was accepted, relied upon in accepting the operation, and breached because the described postoperative condition did not occur. (Id. at 66–68, 70.)

The controlling issue was whether those statements and circumstances could support the contract verdict, rather than whether the surgeons had committed malpractice. The court did not announce that any unsuccessful treatment, or any encouraging prediction by a doctor, establishes a contract claim.

## Majority rule and reasoning

The majority rejected the premise that plaintiffs had to prove a separate special contract supported by separate consideration. Their theory was that the promises were terms of the operation agreement itself. Footnote 1 explains that warranties allegedly made before acceptance did not present the past-consideration problem associated with a later separate undertaking. A physician could make an express contractual commitment to a result; the decisive question here was whether these physicians had done so. (Id. at 66–67 & majority n.1.)

The court treated contested oral terms, the parties' intentions, and their surrounding circumstances as questions for the fact finder. The jury could determine whether the statements were an offer, whether Guilmet accepted in reliance upon them, what result the agreement required, and whether its failure justified damages. The court resisted making the label “cure” decisive, because the parties had not used that word in negotiating and replacing one disorder with another did not necessarily accomplish the particular result described. (Id. at 67–69 & majority n.2.)

This reasoning preserved the importance of context. Therapeutic reassurance must not automatically become a binding promise merely because the patient is disappointed. The physician–patient relationship differs from an ordinary retail transaction, and the fact finder must account for that difference when evaluating words of undertaking. Nevertheless, the majority did not impose a separate contract standard for physicians. It said that recovery required a specific, clear, express promise to cure or achieve a specified result, reasonably contemplated by both parties and relied upon by the patient. The evidence allowed the jury to find such a promise here, so judgment notwithstanding the verdict was inappropriate. (Id. at 69–70.)

## Separate opinions

Brennan agreed with affirmance but rejected the premise that the verdict necessarily rested on a promise of cure despite an absence of negligent conduct. The contract count included allegations that the surgeons had promised care and skill. The jury returned a general verdict on that count. In his view, the judge's oral clarification that the jury found “no negligence” did not create a special verdict under the governing court rule or restrict the contract verdict to a guarantee of cure. The judge had removed several allegations from the tort count for lack of evidence of a departure from community medical standards, but had not similarly restricted the contract count. Brennan therefore thought the verdict could rest on a failure to perform the promised care and skill, without encouraging future litigation that transformed ordinary reassurance into guarantees. His reasoning was a concurrence, not the majority's account of what the verdict required. (Id. at 71–75.)

Black would have reversed and directed entry of judgment for the surgeons. He read the contract claim as alleging negligent performance of the same acts alleged in tort, so the jury's finding of no negligence defeated that pleaded basis. He also challenged the evidentiary account: testimony concerning a spontaneous esophageal perforation did not, in his reading, prove that the surgeons negligently injured the esophagus, and the alleged forgotten clamp was actually an intentionally retained surgical clip. He criticized reliance on cases involving different pleadings or specially supported arrangements, and feared that medical encouragement would become contractual exposure extending beyond ordinary malpractice limitations periods. Those evidentiary and policy conclusions remained dissenting views. (Id. at 78–93.)

The published dissent includes a June 21, 1971 addendum responding to revisions of the majority. Black expressly recognized that an earlier majority passage saying that failure to achieve a promised result constituted breach regardless of skill had been deleted. That withdrawn passage should not be cited as language of the final majority opinion. His addendum continued to dispute the sufficiency of the contract theory and the breadth of assigning oral-contract questions to the jury, and concluded with his vote to reverse and remand for judgment for defendants. (Id. at 94–102 & dissent nn.4, 12–13.)

## Exact disposition and assigned-source context

The court affirmed the judgment and allowed plaintiffs to tax costs. It did not order a new trial, increase damages, or make a new finding of malpractice. (Id. at 70–71.)
