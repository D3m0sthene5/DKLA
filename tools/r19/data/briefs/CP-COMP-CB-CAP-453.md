# Marsh v. Illinois Central Railroad Co.

**Civil Procedure — Legal sufficiency, weight of evidence, and independent new-trial discretion.** United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, July 8, 1949. **175 F.2d 498.** Judge Sibley wrote for a panel comprising Judges Hutcheson, Sibley, and Waller. No separate writing is reported. The assigned passage is Note 2 in the Civil Procedure casebook, physical PDF page 592, printed page 1169.

## Injury claim and verdict

Marsh worked as a fireman on a switch engine. He claimed that negligent maintenance of the switching track and the apron covering the coupling between the engine and tender caused a personal injury. He stood on that apron while firing the engine. The complete opinion characterizes his action as a common-law negligence suit modified by federal statute; it does not give the accident date, verdict amount, or a detailed account of exactly how the injury occurred. The appellate court’s treatment of his proof should therefore not become an invented factual narrative. 175 F.2d at 499.

At the close of the evidence, the railroad moved for an instructed verdict. The district court denied the motion, and the jury found for Marsh. The railroad then sought judgment notwithstanding the verdict and, alternatively, a new trial on several grounds, including that the verdict was contrary to the overwhelming weight of the evidence. These alternative requests asked different questions even though both challenged the verdict. Id.

## How the trial court confused the two motions

The district judge considered the evidence overwhelmingly against Marsh and reasoned that this made it his legal duty to remove the case from the jury and enter judgment for the railroad. He granted judgment notwithstanding the verdict and denied a new trial. The railroad then asked for a conditional new-trial ruling that would operate if the judgment were reversed. The judge refused: if he was wrong that the evidence could not go to the jury, he thought no new trial should occur because there had been no other legal errors. Id.

Marsh appealed the judgment that displaced his verdict. The railroad cross-appealed the denial of a new trial. Thus the appellate court had to assess both the final judgment and the judge’s refusal to exercise the distinct discretion associated with retrial. A reversal of judgment would not automatically answer whether the original jury verdict should stand. Id. at 499–500.

## Legal sufficiency does not turn on the judge’s personal belief

The court explained that a directed-verdict or judgment-notwithstanding-verdict motion raised a question of law: was there evidence which, if believed, would authorize a verdict against the movant? The judge did not exercise discretionary control over the outcome by choosing whether he personally believed that evidence. Legal error in answering this sufficiency question could be corrected on appeal. Id. at 500.

The Fifth Circuit agreed with the trial judge’s expressed view that the weight of the proof strongly opposed Marsh. Yet that did not establish legal insufficiency. Marsh had supplied testimony which, although neither very explicit nor positive, could authorize a jury to find that he was injured as claimed if it believed him. His contradictions, conduct, and the contrary evidence might explain why the judge disbelieved him. They did not permit the judge to use disbelief alone to extinguish a legally supportable claim through judgment notwithstanding the verdict. Id.

This distinction preserves the jury’s role without requiring courts to accept every verdict as sound. The panel’s acknowledgment that the evidence favored the railroad did not become appellate factfinding that Marsh’s injury account was false. The question on the judgment motion was what a believing jury could lawfully find, not which account the appellate judges preferred. Id.

## A new trial supplies a different remedy and standard

A new-trial motion invoked the trial judge’s discretion. The judge could order retrial because of error committed during trial, but legal error was not indispensable. He could also set aside a verdict he considered wrong on the evidence even when enough evidence existed to permit the jury’s finding. A retrial did not replace the jury with the judge’s final determination of liability; it submitted the dispute to another jury, potentially on a different record. Id. at 499–500.

The court situated that distinction within the Seventh Amendment’s protection against improper reexamination of jury findings. Appellate courts did not possess the trial judge’s common-law discretion simply to revise a verdict on its weight. Ordinarily the trial judge’s exercise of new-trial discretion was not reviewable, but a failure to exercise it or an abuse could be corrected. Rule 50’s procedural changes did not merge the two motions or their standards. The court relied on *Montgomery Ward & Co. v. Duncan* for their independent treatment. Id. at 499–500.

Here, the trial judge had emphatically disapproved the verdict but believed that the absence of other legal error prevented a new trial if judgment notwithstanding the verdict was unavailable. That was a legal misconception limiting his discretion, not a fully informed decision that a supported verdict should remain undisturbed. The appellate court therefore could correct it without itself assuming the trial judge’s fact-weighing function. Id. at 500.

## Judgment and the condition on remand

The Fifth Circuit **reversed on both appeals and remanded**. It directed the district judge to grant a new trial instead of judgment notwithstanding the verdict **if he continued to regard the verdict as against the overwhelming weight of the evidence**. The condition matters: the mandate did not itself restore Marsh’s verdict as an unalterable judgment, adjudicate liability for the railroad, or unconditionally require a new trial irrespective of the judge’s considered assessment. Id.

The assigned Note 2 reproduces the key distinction and asks whether the judge could change his view on remand. The actual conditional instruction answers the procedural point: the judge was to exercise the discretion he had mistakenly thought unavailable. The court did not prescribe a new evidentiary finding that he had to adopt.
