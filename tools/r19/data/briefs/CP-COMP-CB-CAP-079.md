# Roller v. Holly

**176 U.S. 398 (1900) · Supreme Court of the United States · February 26, 1900 · Civil Procedure**

Justice Brown delivered the Court’s opinion. The Chief Justice and Justice Brewer dissented without a reported separate writing. The Court reversed the Texas Court of Civil Appeals and instructed that the case be remanded to the trial court. The assigned account is *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 150, printed page 251. Its description of a Virginia defendant concerns the earlier foreclosure proceeding; Roller was the plaintiff in the later action before the Supreme Court.

## Facts and successive proceedings

Roller bought 114 acres in Limestone County, Texas, in January 1887. Part of the price was a $216.17 note payable to John W. and Cora E. Jordan, due November 1, 1890, secured by a vendor’s lien retained in the note and deed. The note passed to McClintic & Proctor. Meanwhile, on January 1, 1890, Roller sold the land to Stephen and William Holly, put them in possession, and took five $228 notes secured by his own vendor’s lien. The two obligations and successive liens explain the competing claims; they were not one loan or one foreclosure. (399–402.)

McClintic & Proctor sued Roller in Texas on December 24, 1890, for the debt and foreclosure of their lien. Roller lived in Harrisonburg, Virginia. He received notice there on December 30 directing him to appear in Groesbeck, Texas, on January 5, 1891. The court entered a default judgment on January 9 for $276.65, interest, costs, and foreclosure. The sheriff sold the land for $300 on March 3 to Williams, with Jackson interested in the purchase. They later conveyed it to Peoples. The Hollys, already in possession, were not joined in that proceeding; Brown suggested their deed may not have been recorded, without establishing that explanation as a fact. Their rights were not involved in the Supreme Court’s decision. (399–402.)

In July 1894, Roller sued the Hollys on their notes and sought to foreclose his retained lien. He joined Peoples, Williams, and Jackson because they asserted interests in the land. They relied on the earlier foreclosure sale as divesting Roller’s title and sought repayment of their $300 if he recovered the property against them. Roller attacked the earlier judgment, arguing lack of jurisdiction, absence of a preliminary seizure, lack of statutory authorization, and insufficient time to respond. (399–401.)

On agreed facts, the trial court awarded Roller $1,722.66 against the defaulting Hollys but refused to enforce his lien against the land and awarded the other defendants costs. The Court of Civil Appeals affirmed; the Texas Supreme Court refused a writ of error. Roller then obtained review in the United States Supreme Court. The federal question was whether the earlier foreclosure notice afforded due process and could therefore bar his later action. (401–402.)

## Issue and holding

Could the earlier Texas judgment bind Roller’s interest in the land when out-of-state notice demanded an appearance after only five intervening days, most of which would be consumed by travel?

The Court held that the notice was constitutionally unreasonable under these circumstances. The judgment obtained on it did not bind Roller and could not bar the later action. The Court also rejected his broader contention that Texas necessarily lacked authority to enforce a preexisting lien against local property without first seizing it. Jurisdiction over the property and adequate opportunity to defend were separate questions. (402–407, 413.)

## Authority over the land

The earlier judgment’s personal-liability aspect was conceded invalid under *Pennoyer*. But that did not resolve the foreclosure against Texas land. Brown explained that state law could authorize proceedings affecting local property with notice outside the state or by publication, even where the defendant was a nonresident. His treatment of *Hart v. Sansom* and *Arndt v. Griggs* distinguished an attempted personal cancellation decree from proceedings vindicating possession or an existing lien. (402–405.)

A creditor seeking to create a lien through attachment ordinarily needed that attachment to supply the property basis. A creditor already holding a mortgage, statutory lien, or contractual lien could seek to enforce the existing interest without a preliminary seizure. The seizure would not create a necessary interest that the creditor lacked. That historical distinction defeated Roller’s argument that omission of attachment automatically voided the foreclosure. (405–406.)

Texas’s provision for notice to absent defendants was also broad enough to support lawful property proceedings. Brown declined to treat it as useless merely because it did not specifically name equitable purchase-money liens. A statute identifying only certain permitted cases might restrict authority to those classes; this general provision did not. The state courts’ construction embracing lien foreclosure was, in any event, binding as their construction of state law. These conclusions addressed historical property-based authority, not a claim that out-of-state service alone could impose personal liability. (406–407.)

## Why the time allowed was inadequate

Texas generally allowed five days excluding service and return days, without distinguishing nearby defendants from people in other states or countries. Roller had to travel continuously for about four days to reach the Texas court, leaving only one day—a Sunday—to prepare under the notice’s stated deadline. The estimate allowed nothing for travel disruptions. (407–408.)

The default actually occurred four days after the January 5 date because Texas practice delayed calling the case until the term’s appearance day. That did not cure the notice. A Virginia resident was not obliged to know that local practice and could read the notice according to its stated terms. Nor could the possibility of discretionary relief from default replace a constitutional right. Due process could not depend on a court’s favor or willingness to reopen its judgment. (408–409.)

Reasonable notice had to allow preparation as well as arrival. Unlike a subpoena commanding immediate attendance, an ordinary private civil summons had to accommodate retaining counsel, collecting evidence, arranging witnesses, and handling other responsibilities. Brown discussed historical practice and numerous state statutes providing longer periods or allowing the judge to select a suitable date. Those provisions were evidence of general legislative judgment, not binding constitutional rules imported from other jurisdictions. (409–413.)

## Judgment and limits

The Court expressly declined to fix a universal minimum period. It held only that **five days was unreasonable here**, considering the distance and circumstances. The textbook’s questions whether seven or twenty days would suffice therefore remain teaching questions, not answers supplied by this holding. This brief likewise makes no present-day Rule 12 deadline assertion from the historical opinion. (413; assigned physical 150.)

The Court reversed the Court of Civil Appeals and directed remand to the Limestone County District Court for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. It did not itself foreclose Roller’s lien, award him the land, or adjudicate the unjoined Hollys’ rights in the earlier action. The Chief Justice and Brewer’s dissent is recorded without reasons, which are not reconstructed here. (402, 413.)
