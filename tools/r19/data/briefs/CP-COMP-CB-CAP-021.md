**284 U.S. 421 (1932)** · February 15, 1932 · Nos. 200 and 201  
**Hughes, C.J., delivering the Court's opinion; Roberts did not participate. No separate writing or numerical vote tally is reported.**

## The witness, the two subpoenas, and the penalties

Harry M. Blackmer was a United States citizen living in Paris. He moved his residence to France in 1924 but retained American citizenship. The Supreme Court of the District of Columbia issued two subpoenas requiring him to return and testify for the United States at a criminal trial, with appearances at different times. They were personally served on him in France under the Act of July 3, 1926. He failed to respond, and separate contempt proceedings followed. Those proceedings were heard together and resulted in two fines of $30,000, with costs, to be satisfied from his property seized within the United States. The District's Court of Appeals affirmed, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari. 284 U.S. at 433, 436.

The court bearing the name “Supreme Court of the District of Columbia” was the court issuing the subpoenas and contempt decrees; it was not the United States Supreme Court reviewing them. The textbook identifies the underlying criminal matter with the Teapot Dome scandal. The reviewing opinion's own opening account focuses on the witness's status, statutory process, and contempt proceedings rather than recounting that prosecution's substantive facts. The precise judgments reviewed were the two contempt penalties. Id. at 433.

## The statute's sequence and the parties' arguments

The 1926 statute allowed a judge, on a proper governmental showing, to order a subpoena for a witness abroad who was an American citizen or domiciled in the United States. A consul was to serve it personally, first tendering the necessary expenses of travel and attendance. Upon proof of service and default, the court could issue an order to show cause, direct seizure of domestic property as security, and require personal service of that order and publication. Only after a hearing and proof sustaining the charge could the court impose a fine, up to $100,000, and authorize satisfaction from the seized property. The complete statute is reproduced in judicial note 1. Id. at 433–436 & n. 1.

Blackmer challenged Congress's authority to reach a citizen abroad and argued that consular service required treaty authorization. He characterized the procedure as an invalid effort to obtain personal jurisdiction through property seizure. He also asserted inadequate notice, unconstitutional adjudication in his absence, discrimination based on ownership of domestic property, and an unreasonable search and seizure. Additional objections concerned the sufficiency and form of the subpoenas, notice of the contempt charge, the second levy on the same property, and the date of the second required appearance. The government relied on continuing allegiance, a citizen's duty to give evidence, and the statute's notice and hearing protections. Id. at 424–433, 436, 441–443.

## Continuing citizenship supplied national authority

The Court held that moving abroad did not end Blackmer's obligations to the United States. He remained subject to American laws made applicable to him in that situation. The issue of whether a law applied abroad to citizens was a question of statutory construction rather than an absence of legislative power. The Court acknowledged the usual presumption of territorial application unless contrary intent appeared, but distinguished that interpretive presumption from Congress's power over its citizens abroad. Id. at 436–437 & nn. 2–4.

One continuing duty was to assist the administration of justice by attending courts and testifying when properly summoned. The national government could require an absent citizen's return when the public interest required it and could penalize refusal. Under the American constitutional system, Congress possessed authority to prescribe that duty; it was not merely an executive power incapable of legislative implementation through courts. Id. at 437–438.

The Court carefully separated legislative obligation from judicial process. Citizenship made Blackmer subject to applicable law, but judicial power to enter a personal judgment still required appropriate notice of the proceeding and an opportunity to be heard. The statute's subpoena and subsequent show-cause procedure supplied those protections. Judicial note 5 distinguishes cases in which obligations inherent in allegiance are absent, so the opinion should not be treated as authorizing equivalent process against every foreign resident regardless of citizenship or legal relationship. Id. at 438–439 & n. 5.

## Consular delivery and adjudication in the witness's absence

The consul's act was communication of the government's requirement to its own citizen. The Court rejected the claim that this ordinary delivery invaded France's rights or necessarily required a particular treaty grant. Blackmer also lacked standing to invoke the supposed foreign-government injury. Although consular privileges could depend on treaties, service here did not exercise a special consular privilege; a designated person could convey the same actual notice. Id. at 439–440 & n. 6.

The show-cause order followed proof of the subpoena's service and disobedience. It notified the witness that the issuing court would consider contempt and afforded him an opportunity to explain his failure. That was adequate notice of the nature of the proceeding. The Court also rejected the contention that a hearing and judgment necessarily violated due process because Blackmer did not attend. It characterized contempt proceedings as distinct from criminal prosecutions within the Sixth Amendment's ordinary meaning and considered suitable notice and an adequate opportunity to appear sufficient in this case. This states the Court's 1932 analysis rather than a verification of later contempt law. Id. at 440.

## Domestic property was security, not the source of the duty

Seizure of Blackmer's domestic property was a provisional remedy securing a fine that might be imposed after hearing. Its validity depended on the valid contempt proceeding, not on treating the property itself as the offender or as the source of his duty to testify. The order informed him of the seizure, and the property would be applied if a fine was imposed and remained unpaid. The underlying liability arose from disobedience, not from the fact that he happened to own reachable property. Accordingly, the Court rejected the asserted unconstitutional classification between witnesses with and without domestic assets. Id. at 441.

The authorized levy, made in the legally prescribed manner to secure or satisfy the owner's liability, also did not violate the Fourth Amendment. That ruling concerned the particular statutory seizure arrangement; it did not remove all constitutional limits from governmental property seizures. Id. at 441–442.

## Remaining objections and exact judgment

Blackmer argued that giving the government access to this process while supposedly denying it to criminal defendants violated their compulsory-process rights. The Court did not decide whether the statute had that construction or whether a defendant could establish such a violation. A recalcitrant witness could not raise that distinct objection on their behalf. Id. at 442.

The government's showing about the importance and materiality of his expected testimony was sufficient to empower the court to issue the subpoenas. Unless set aside on proper application, they required obedience. They did not need an additional statement identifying their statutory source, since Blackmer was chargeable with knowledge of applicable law. The show-cause orders adequately identified the subsequent contempt proceedings. The second levy on the same property did not abandon the first. Finally, continuance of the second trial from April 2 to April 9, 1928 did not excuse never responding to a subpoena that required attendance and continued presence until excused. Id. at 442–443.

The Supreme Court **affirmed both decrees**, preserving the two $30,000 fines and associated costs. Roberts did not participate; the report contains no concurrence, dissent, or individual vote tally. Id. at 443.

## Assignment and reading scope

The assigned paragraph summarizes foreign personal service and citizenship as a continuing jurisdictional relationship. The complete opinion adds the distinction between allegiance, notice, and provisional property security, as well as the narrow standing disposition of the compulsory-process argument. It should not be reduced to either “notice alone creates jurisdiction” or “property alone permits a personal contempt judgment.”
