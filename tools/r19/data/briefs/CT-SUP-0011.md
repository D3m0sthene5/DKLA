# Balfour v. Balfour
[1919] 2 K.B. 571 (Court of Appeal) | Contracts | Domestic arrangements and intention to create legal obligations

## The promise arose during an amicable, temporary absence

The Balfours married in August 1900. The husband was a civil engineer and Director of Irrigation for the government of Ceylon, where the couple ordinarily lived together. They returned to England in November 1915 while he was on leave. When his leave expired in August 1916, he had to return to Ceylon. His wife remained in England on medical advice because she suffered from rheumatic arthritis. The original arrangement concerned this medically necessary absence, rather than a negotiated marital separation. (K.B. 571–572; assigned PDF pp. 11–12.)

According to the wife's evidence, on August 8, 1916, the day he sailed, the husband gave her a £24 cheque covering the rest of August and promised £30 a month until she returned. They had discussed her expenses and considered figures together. She expected him to return to England within a few months. She acknowledged that they had not then agreed to live apart; differences developed afterward. The husband later wrote suggesting that they should remain apart. Those later difficulties did not establish that the earlier promise had been made as a binding separation agreement. (K.B. 572.)

In March 1918, the wife began proceedings for restitution of conjugal rights. She obtained a decree nisi on July 30 and an alimony order on December 16, 1918. These events are part of the chronology, but the reported contract action concerned enforcement of the August 1916 allowance promise. The Court of Appeal did not decide that the wife had no rights of maintenance through other legal proceedings. (K.B. 572, 575.)

## The trial court treated assent to the allowance as consideration

Sargant J., sitting as an additional King's Bench judge, entered judgment for the wife. He reasoned that the husband already owed a duty to support her and that the parties contracted to define the amount of that obligation. Her consent to the arrangement supplied sufficient consideration to make the monthly promise actionable. The husband appealed. (K.B. 571–572.)

For the husband, counsel argued that the arrangement was domestic, temporary, and intended to last until the wife rejoined him. It was neither a separation agreement nor a transaction intended to have contractual effect. Counsel also disputed consideration. The wife's counsel answered that spouses could contract with one another and that, in exchange for the allowance, she had relinquished the ability to pledge his credit for necessaries. Counsel relied on authorities concerning spouses living apart and contended that the parties intended a binding agreement. The judges questioned whether those authorities applied to a couple still living in amity and whether the supposed surrendered right existed independently of the husband's will. These were arguments in the reported hearing, not findings that the wife actually exchanged such a right. (K.B. 572–574.)

## Issue and common result

Did the discussion and promise concerning a wife's allowance during this temporary absence establish an enforceable contract, either expressly or by necessary implication? All three Lords Justices answered no. They each gave reasons, and their reasoning should be distinguished rather than compressed into a single opinion about consideration. The appeal was allowed; Sargant J.'s judgment could not stand. Warrington expressly directed that judgment should be entered for the defendant husband. (K.B. 574–580.)

## Warrington: no bargain was proved or properly implied

Warrington L.J. expressly allowed that a husband and wife could make a contract of the kind alleged. The question was whether these spouses had done so. That required either express contractual terms or circumstances necessarily implying a contract. Here, the wife had made no express bargain. They had amicably estimated what would maintain her while illness kept her in England, but there was no promise by her to accept £30 as satisfaction of his maintenance obligation in every circumstance. (K.B. 574.)

Implying the asserted contract would bind the wife to accept £30 however her needs changed, while binding the husband to pay indefinitely however his circumstances changed. Neither implication followed from the ordinary domestic discussion. Treating such promises as legal bargains would also imply contractual enforceability throughout the ordinary concerns of married life. For Warrington, these parties had not intended an enforceable bargain. The husband had expressed an intention and undertaken an obligation of honour; the wife had supplied no corresponding bargain. (K.B. 575.)

Because the wife had not proved the contract on which she sued, Warrington found it unnecessary to decide whether nonpayment could authorize her to pledge the husband's credit or pursue another arrangement. The absence of a contract did not resolve every possible legal consequence of the husband's failure to maintain her. (K.B. 575.)

## Duke: the asserted exchange lacked consideration in this relationship

Duke L.J. agreed with the result but focused on consideration. Spouses might make an agreement creating a contractual cause of action, including an undertaking associated with a wife's separate estate under the Married Women's Property Act 1882. An agreement for separation, when established, could involve mutual consideration. Neither possibility described the agreement proved here. At its inception, the communications were grounded in the relationship of spouses living in amity, and no separation agreement existed. (K.B. 575–577.)

Duke rejected the formulation that the wife surrendered her right to pledge her husband's credit in exchange for the allowance. The authorities described the wife's power to charge her husband as an agency; they did not establish the independent, irrevocable right counsel assumed on these facts. Giving up something that was not a right did not furnish consideration. The assent to an arrangement within the husband's discretion could not transform his domestic promise into the contractual obligation alleged. Duke also feared unlimited litigation from treating ordinary reciprocal domestic promises as necessarily contractual. (K.B. 577–578 and cited authorities in the report's footnotes.)

## Atkin: consideration in form does not establish legal intention

Atkin L.J. supplied the clearest distinction between agreement and contract. People agree to walks or hospitality without creating legal obligations. Domestic allowances likewise may involve reciprocal promises and something that would qualify as consideration between other parties, yet still lack contractual force because the spouses never intended legal consequences. His reasoning did not depend entirely on the absence of consideration identified by Duke. (K.B. 578–579.)

Atkin considered how domestic arrangements actually operate: spouses vary, renew, or abandon them as performance and personal circumstances change. Imposing ordinary contractual enforcement would allow reciprocal lawsuits over weekly allowances and their application, multiplying litigation in matters the participants had not intended courts to regulate. His description of the home as outside the King's writ concerned these promises and their contractual treatment, not a general holding that courts lacked authority over marriage. (K.B. 579.)

The wife bore the burden of proving a contract. Atkin found neither her undisputed oral evidence nor the letters sufficient. The spouses had contemplated her return. They had not contemplated binding her to meet every potential medical expense from £30, however her illness developed, or binding him to that payment under every circumstance. The promise belonged to the noncontractual class of domestic arrangements. (K.B. 580.)

## Judgment and limits

The Court of Appeal allowed the husband's appeal unanimously, with separate reasons by Warrington, Duke, and Atkin L.JJ.; there was no dissent. The wife's contract judgment was reversed, with judgment for the husband expressly called for by Warrington. This was a failure to establish the particular contract pleaded, rather than a damages calculation or a ruling on the separate alimony proceedings.

The case supports examination of legal intention in ordinary domestic promises, while preserving the possibility of actual contracts between spouses. It should not be rendered as a categorical rule that spouses can never contract, that mutual affection makes every promise legally irrelevant, or that every later separation converts an earlier allowance into a contract. The material setting was the couple's amicable relationship and anticipated reunion when this promise was made.
