# Crawford Fitting Co. v. J. T. Gibbons, Inc.
482 U.S. 437 (1987) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Two cases and their procedural paths

The Supreme Court considered two consolidated disputes over reimbursement of a prevailing party’s expert witness fees. In the antitrust case, J. T. Gibbons sued Crawford Fitting and other defendants. The district court directed a verdict for the defendants, and that judgment was affirmed. The prevailing defendants then sought more than $220,000 in litigation expenses. Reading Rule 54(d) as granting discretion beyond the statutory witness allowance, the district court awarded $86,480.70 for experts. The en banc Fifth Circuit reversed that award because 28 U.S.C. § 1821(b) limited attendance compensation to $30 per day. (438–439.)

In the companion case, International Woodworkers sued Champion International for race discrimination under Title VII and § 1981. After trial, the district court dismissed the claims. Champion sought expert witness costs, including $11,807 described in the majority’s account. The district court declined to award compensation exceeding the statutory allowance, and the en banc Fifth Circuit affirmed. Thus, the same court of appeals had rejected a larger award in one case and sustained refusal of a larger award in the other. (439.)

Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote the June 15, 1987 opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, Powell, Stevens, O’Connor, and Scalia. Blackmun also concurred separately. Marshall dissented, joined by Brennan. The seven-to-two judgment affirmed both Fifth Circuit decisions and remanded the antitrust case, No. 86-322, for proceedings consistent with the opinion. The different underlying claims matter because the Court was resolving the ordinary costs rule, rather than deciding the later Casey question about expert fees under § 1988. (437–438, 445–446 n. 1.)

## Statutory and rule framework

Section 1920 enumerates taxable litigation expenses, including witness fees and compensation of court-appointed experts. Section 1821 specifies the attendance fee for ordinary witnesses, including party-retained experts. Rule 54(d), in its then-applicable form, generally allowed costs to a prevailing party unless the court directed otherwise, subject to express statutory or rule provisions. The question was whether the rule supplied independent authority to exceed the statutory witness-fee limit. (440–441.)

The majority reconstructed the federal costs statutes’ history. Congress’s 1853 Fee Act had replaced varied practices with detailed regulation of taxable compensation. Sections 1920 and 1821 carried forward that comprehensive statutory control. Reading the provisions together, § 1821 fixed the witness payment, § 1920 made that payment taxable, and Rule 54(d) established the ordinary allocation to the losing party. The rule’s discretion permitted refusal to tax authorized costs; it did not authorize inventing additional categories or increasing amounts Congress had specifically limited. (439–442.)

The prevailing parties argued that § 1920’s permissive language merely identified costs a court could award, leaving Rule 54(d) free to authorize more. The Court rejected that construction because it would make the statutory enumeration ineffectual. Section 1920 defined costs for purposes of the rule. Its permissive phrasing reflected discretion to deny otherwise allowable costs. The specific distinction between court-appointed and party-retained experts reinforced that conclusion: Congress expressly allowed compensation of the former without the ordinary witness cap, while controlling the latter through § 1821. Contract or explicit statutory authority could change the result, but Rule 54(d) alone could not. (441–442.)

## Precedent and implied repeal

The parties relied on Farmer v. Arabian American Oil Co., which had stated that discretion concerning nonstatutory expenses should be exercised sparingly. The majority classified that sentence as unnecessary dictum in a case sustaining refusal of certain witness travel costs, disapproved it, and declined to convert it into authority for enhanced expert fees. (442–443.)

Henkel v. Chicago, St. Paul, Minneapolis & Omaha Railway Co. had directly rejected expert compensation beyond statutory limits. The argument that Henkel predated the merger of law and equity did not persuade the Court: merger did not repeal the fee statutes or displace their specific commands. Alyeska Pipeline Service Co. v. Wilderness Society likewise supported comprehensive congressional control rather than a roving judicial authority to redistribute expenses. Repeals by implication were disfavored, and the Court found no conflict requiring displacement of §§ 1920 and 1821 by Rule 54(d). Specific cost provisions controlled unless Congress or a contract explicitly authorized a different allocation. (443–445.)

## Concurrence and dissent

Blackmun joined on the express understanding that the Court had not decided whether § 1988 independently authorized expert witness fees. Marshall’s first dissenting note explained why that issue was outside these appeals. In the civil-rights case, the district court had denied a separate § 1988 attorney-fee-and-expense motion because the unsuccessful suit was brought in good faith and was not frivolous, unreasonable, or unfounded. Champion had not appealed that denial; the appealed order concerned expert fees under Rule 54(d). A decision concerning that rule therefore could not resolve the scope of § 1988. (445; 446 n. 1.)

Marshall and Brennan would have preserved broader discretionary authority. They read Rule 54(d) as adopting the equitable practice of awarding appropriate expenses, not merely withholding statutory costs. Farmer’s reasoning, in their view, had affirmatively recognized that discretion; its instruction to use discretion sparingly was not disposable dictum. Henkel concerned an action at law before the rules merged law and equity, so it did not settle the rule’s later operation. (445–448.)

The dissent also stressed that the 1948 revision omitted the earlier statute’s language expressly allowing only the listed compensation. Section 1920’s permissive wording did not purport to establish an exclusive universe of expenses. If courts already had discretion under that provision to disallow listed costs, reducing Rule 54(d) to the same function made the rule redundant instead. The enumeration could sensibly govern routine items automatically taxed by clerks while leaving unusual expenses to judicial discretion. Marshall also pointed to the heavy and sometimes unavoidable cost of expert testimony, arguing that a prevailing litigant should at least be able to request an appropriate compensatory award. (448–450 & nn. 3–5.)

## Holding, disposition, and assigned scope

The Court held that absent explicit statutory or contractual authorization, courts taxing a party’s witness expenses must observe §§ 1920 and 1821. It affirmed both judgments and remanded No. 86-322. Necessity or usefulness of the expert testimony did not create authority to exceed those limits. The opinion describes the $30 daily allowance then applicable; the assigned Katzmann account’s $40 figure reflects the later statutory rate, rather than the numerical limit this 1987 judgment applied. (439, 445; assigned printed 83.)
