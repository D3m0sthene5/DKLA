# In re Sealed Case (Three Cases)
838 F.2d 476 (D.C. Cir. 1988), decided January 22, amended February 5, 1988 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision stage and judgment

This is the divided D.C. Circuit decision that preceded the Supreme Court’s *Morrison v. Olson*, rather than another name for the Supreme Court’s judgment. Judge Silberman, joined by Judge Williams, held the independent-counsel provisions unconstitutional and **reversed contempt orders against Theodore Olson, Carol Dinkins, and Edward Schmults**. Judge Ruth Bader Ginsburg dissented and would affirm. The Supreme Court later reversed this panel judgment in *Morrison*, 487 U.S. 654, 697 (1988). The panel’s propositions below are its historical reasoning, not the later Supreme Court’s controlling resolution. (476–478, 518, 536.)

## Document dispute, investigation, and contempt appeals

In 1982, House subcommittees sought EPA records concerning hazardous-waste cleanup and investigated whether expenditures favored Republican Senate candidates. EPA provided some material but withheld enforcement-sensitive documents on Justice Department advice. The Administrator asserted executive privilege at the President’s direction, received a congressional contempt citation, and joined litigation against the House. An agreement on production followed in March 1983. A separate Judiciary Committee investigation examined the Justice Department’s role; Olson testified March 10, 1983. The committee’s later report alleged serious wrongdoing, over Republican members’ dissent. These were allegations prompting investigation, not this court’s findings of criminal guilt. (478–479.)

The committee requested an independent-counsel preliminary investigation in December 1985. The statute covered designated senior officials and required the Attorney General to investigate sufficiently supported information within a limited period, without ordinary tools such as grand juries, subpoenas, immunity, or plea bargaining. On April 10, 1986, the Attorney General sought independent counsel to investigate whether Olson’s testimony and revisions about the OLC response and EPA’s willingness to produce records violated federal criminal law, including obstruction and false-statement provisions, and to investigate related matters. He found no reasonable grounds for further investigation of Schmults or Dinkins. The Special Division first appointed James McKay and then, after his resignation, Alexia Morrison. (479.)

Morrison requested expanded authority to investigate whether Olson’s testimony formed part of a concerted obstruction plan involving the other officials. The Attorney General refused. The Special Division held that it could not refer allegations the Attorney General had declined to pursue, but construed its original jurisdiction to include investigation of an Olson conspiracy involving others. Morrison then subpoenaed all three officials. The district court rejected their constitutional attack on July 20, 1987. They refused to appear before the grand jury, were held in contempt under § 1826(a), and appealed those orders. (479–480 & nn. 3–5.)

The majority treated constitutional review through contempt appeals as available under circuit precedent. It also held that Morrison had forfeited her objection to this mode of review by not raising it below, and regarded that objection as nonjurisdictional. This was not an appeal from a conviction after trial. (480 n. 6.)

## Majority: independent counsel was a principal officer

The parties agreed that Morrison was an officer rather than an employee. The majority rejected her reading that, apart from the specifically named Supreme Court Justices and diplomats, all federal officers could be treated as inferior. The exception for inferior-officer appointment had to preserve a broader category requiring presidential nomination and Senate confirmation. Department heads and lower federal judges fell on that principal-officer side, and the clause had to be read in harmony with the Constitution’s executive and separation-of-powers structure. (481–484.)

For Silberman, an inferior officer was functionally subordinate to a principal officer. The Attorney General did not appoint this statutory independent counsel, define and supervise her duties, terminate her office, or freely remove her. Morrison had declined a parallel Justice Department appointment. A previously sustained regulatory independent counsel was different: the Attorney General could amend or revoke the regulation, retaining ultimate authority. The panel did not decide whether the Special Division was Morrison’s constitutional superior, a characterization no participant advanced; accepting that characterization would create its own separation-of-powers problem. (484–485 & nn. 15–18.)

The majority emphasized the breadth and independence of her prosecutorial authority. A limited mission and eventual expiration did not suffice for inferiority: diplomats could receive discrete assignments, and temporarily performing a superior’s functions, as in *Eaton*, differed from transferring the whole relevant prosecutorial responsibility out of the Attorney General’s control. The majority regarded depth and breadth, rather than merely expected duration, as decisive. It reserved whether independent regulatory commissioners were principal officers and whether this counsel was herself a department head, finding her at least a principal officer whose judicial appointment was invalid. That conclusion alone sufficed, but the panel addressed the additional constitutional grounds to permit informed Supreme Court review. (485–487 & nn. 19–20.)

## Majority: appointment, removal, and supervision impaired executive authority

The panel described prosecution of general federal criminal law as a core executive function, subject to political accountability through a President entrusted with faithful execution. Congress enacted criminal law; the Executive chose investigations and prosecutions; a neutral judiciary tried cases. That allocation protected individual liberty even where it sacrificed convenience. (487–490.)

Even assuming inferior-officer status, the panel construed the appointment exception generally to prohibit courts from appointing officers performing another branch’s central duties. It examined text, Convention debates, early practice, and *Hennen*. It distinguished *Siebold*’s election supervisors, whose functions and constitutional setting did not involve transferring ordinary federal prosecutorial authority away from the President. It also distinguished *Young v. Vuitton*: court-appointed counsel prosecuting contempt vindicated a court’s own orders, rather than exercising general law-enforcement jurisdiction. The opinion’s hypotheticals about congressional transfer of executive staffing authority illustrated its concern; they were not findings about appointments actually made in this case. (490–496 & nn. 23–34.)

The statute allowed removal personally by the Attorney General for good cause or specified impairment, rather than at will. The majority read *Myers* and *Humphrey’s Executor* to forbid restricting removal of an officer performing this purely executive, discretionary function. Civil-service protection and *Perkins* involved different functions and control arrangements; the panel did not claim every government employee must be removable at pleasure. It also distinguished regulatory bodies whose role helped develop the statutory policy they administered from a counsel enforcing preexisting criminal law. (496–501 & nn. 35–40.)

The majority found the original judicial-review arrangement an additional restraint. Before the 1987 amendment, the same Special Division that appointed counsel could review removal for error of law or fact, provide reinstatement, and appoint interim counsel. Silberman saw an effective requirement of judicial concurrence, not meaningful unilateral executive removal. The panel recognized Congress’s intervening transfer of review to the district court and narrowing of its standard, but held that the amendment did not erase the original tenure arrangement’s asserted effect when these contempt challenges arose. It separately reserved whether the breadth of judicial review itself was unconstitutional; that breadth mattered here for its effect on executive control. (501–503 & nn. 41–43.)

The panel also objected to lack of executive supervision over scope, duration, charging standards, resources, and policy conflicts. Counsel followed Justice Department policies only when possible, with that judgment left to her. The majority viewed the appointment, removal, and supervisory provisions both individually and together as a serious invasion of executive responsibility, even acknowledging that separation was not absolute or hermetic. (503–504.)

## Majority: necessity and judicial participation did not cure the defects

The Act’s defenders emphasized distrust arising from Watergate and conflicts in investigating senior officials. The majority responded that the President could appoint a special prosecutor under executive regulation, and that impeachment and political accountability already checked abuse. Even a powerful necessity argument could not justify revising constitutional allocations. Nor was actual congressional acquisition of prosecutorial authority indispensable to finding unconstitutional displacement of the Executive. The panel distinguished *Schor*’s limited, voluntary administrative adjudication and continuing judicial control. It thought removing executive control over even a single target’s prosecution could jeopardize individual safeguards. (504–509.)

Silberman further criticized incentives confronting an office devoted to a particular target: pressure to justify the investigation through indictment, isolation from ordinary prosecution priorities, and possible divergence in charging thresholds or foreign-policy judgments. The opinion expressly did not impugn this counsel’s motives. These were structural concerns, not a finding that Morrison had committed misconduct or that the three witnesses were innocent. (509–511 & nn. 50–55.)

The Article III analysis supplied a reciprocal defect. The Special Division defined and interpreted jurisdiction, received reports, and could terminate the office when work was complete or substantially complete. The majority viewed these as substantive prosecutorial supervision beyond adjudication of cases and controversies. It discussed actual jurisdictional and conflict-of-interest orders, and explained why appointment authority did not automatically authorize continuing administration. Unlike grand-jury assistance, search-warrant review, or control of courtroom conduct, deciding whom and what to investigate involved executive policy. Entrusting it to judges also threatened judicial neutrality when prosecutions later came before their colleagues. (511–518 & nn. 56–63.)

## Ginsburg’s dissent: checks, bounded roles, and functional analysis

Ginsburg accepted that prosecution was executive, but disputed both the claim that another branch controlled it and the assumption that every prosecutorial decision must remain without exception under presidential supervision. Congress could request appointment and obtain explanations without compelling prosecutorial decisions. The Attorney General alone initiated the appointment process and supplied the jurisdictional foundation. Reading the Act as a whole and avoiding constitutional conflict, she would require the Special Division’s definition to track that foundation substantially. The Division did not supervise investigative methods or day-to-day charging choices; reports and termination provisions were bounded administrative tasks. (518–523 & nn. 1–17.)

She considered the 1987 removal-review amendment a clarification that ordinary good-cause review was intended. Independent review was now in the district court, with Special Division judges excluded. She also thought speculative effects of a hypothetical unusually intrusive review standard insufficiently ripe. Those arguments did not reject review of every removal limitation; they distinguished that particular theory. (522–523 & nn. 7, 15.)

Under her reading, *Bowsher* governed transfers of executive control to another branch, while *Schor* required functional evaluation where power was limited without such transfer. The inquiry considered the extent of displacement, whether a core function was implicated, and Congress’s purpose. The Attorney General retained significant initiating and removal authority; counsel had a circumscribed assignment, although substantial tools within it. Ginsburg questioned whether historical decentralized prosecution made this an executive function constitutionally requiring unbroken presidential control in every instance. The Act’s anti-abuse purpose implemented checks and balances rather than merely administrative convenience. She expressly disclaimed an argument that necessity alone justified constitutional departure. (523–528 & nn. 18–28.)

For removal, she distinguished congressional participation from congressional limits on a power remaining in the Executive. *Humphrey’s Executor* and *Wiener* supported protecting an otherwise constitutional office whose tasks required independence. She rejected unstable functional labels as a complete test and required judicial examination of the office’s constitutional role; Congress’s declaration of independence alone would not suffice. On her prior conclusion that this office’s independence was permissible, good-cause protection was permissible too. (528–530 & nn. 29–34.)

On appointment, Ginsburg found the historical meaning of inferior status insufficiently settled to make personal subordination the exclusive test. Existing circuit precedent allowing executive appointment of counsel with the same duties, *Siebold*, limited jurisdiction, and Congress’s considered choice supported inferior status. Congress’s view was not final, but warranted respect where the classification was fairly debatable. Judicial appointment met both constitutional and statutory congruity on her understanding of the limited intrusion and the purpose of avoiding favoritism. (530–534 & nn. 35–43.)

She regarded definition of an ad hoc office’s jurisdiction as incident to authorized appointment. The actual contested request for clarification involved opposing submissions from the Justice Department and counsel; it could be resolved consistently with Article III. Olson’s separate statutory objection to inclusion of conspiracy allegations was premature: he had not alleged or shown that the subpoena or contempt order would not have issued without that interpretation. She therefore would not decide that claim’s merits, and would affirm the district judgments. (534–536 & nn. 44–48.)
