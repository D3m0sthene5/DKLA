# Arlington Central School District Board of Education v. Murphy
402 F.3d 332 (2d Cir. 2005), reversed, 548 U.S. 291 (2006) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## The dispute and the two decisions

Pearl and Theodore Murphy represented themselves in litigation seeking reimbursement for their son Joseph’s private-school tuition under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). They prevailed on that educational-placement dispute in the district court in March 2000, and the Second Circuit affirmed the merits judgment in 2002. This brief concerns the later dispute about their consultant’s fees, rather than a new adjudication of the appropriate placement. In January and February 2003, the parents requested $29,350 for services provided by educational consultant Marilyn Arons, who had a master’s degree. The district court ultimately awarded $8,650. The Second Circuit upheld that award on March 29, 2005; the Supreme Court reversed the court of appeals on June 26, 2006. (Second Circuit opinion ¶¶ 3–8; 548 U.S. at 294.)

The assigned reading is Judge Katzmann’s retrospective account of these decisions. Its heading dates the Second Circuit case 2004, but the actual opinion was decided in 2005. Both stages are developed here because the Supreme Court rejected the interpretation on which the earlier judgment rested.

## Fee proceedings and the parties’ positions

The district’s initial objections addressed both entitlement and the amount requested. It argued that Arons could not recover attorney’s fees because she was a layperson; that she did not qualify as an expert because she had not testified and lacked training in legal procedure; that the records and proof of prevailing market rates were inadequate; and that services in nonjudicial state proceedings were outside the provision. At that stage, its expert objection accepted the availability of expert fees while disputing Arons’s qualification. The categorical argument that IDEA did not authorize expert fees became the central appellate issue. The parents’ status as prevailing parties was conceded. (Second Circuit opinion ¶¶ 4–9.)

Judge Haight’s July 22, 2003 order distinguished expert consultation from unauthorized legal representation. Arons could recover for consultation during the period from the September 3, 1998 request for an impartial hearing through the March 1, 2000 merits decision, but not for appellate work or legal services rendered as a nonlawyer. The court used $200 an hour as the relevant market rate and rejected claimed mileage because Arons did not possess a driver’s license. It also denied interest because the parents had not paid the fees. The school district appealed on August 20, 2003. The Second Circuit reviewed the legal interpretation de novo and the amount for abuse of discretion. (Second Circuit opinion ¶¶ 5–10.)

The statutory provision, 20 U.S.C. § 1415(i)(3)(B), authorized a court, in its discretion, to award reasonable attorney’s fees as part of the costs to parents who prevailed in an IDEA action or proceeding. The question was whether that formulation permitted reimbursement of expert and consultant fees. The parents relied especially on a conference report explaining that the formulation included reasonable expert expenses, fees, and necessary tests or evaluations. The district relied on the absence of expert-fee language in the enacted provision and on the ordinary, restricted meaning of taxable costs.

## The Second Circuit: context and an apparent departure from a term of art

Judge Katzmann wrote for Judges Newman and Pooler in a unanimous opinion affirming the award. The panel recognized that costs ordinarily excluded unrestricted compensation for privately retained experts. In Crawford Fitting, the Supreme Court had required compliance with the witness-fee limits of 28 U.S.C. § 1821 absent explicit statutory authority or a contract. In West Virginia University Hospitals v. Casey, the Court had similarly held that the then-existing language of § 1988 did not authorize expert fees. Thus, the panel’s reasoning depended on identifying a basis for treating IDEA differently; it did not assume that every reference to costs authorized every litigation expense. (Second Circuit opinion ¶¶ 11–18.)

Casey itself supplied the panel’s principal link. Its footnote 5 had discussed IDEA’s conference report and characterized the report as an apparent attempt to depart from ordinary meaning and define a term of art. The Second Circuit understood this discussion to signal that Congress had sufficiently expressed a different intended meaning for IDEA. The conference report was particularly authoritative legislative history, and its language specifically addressed expert expenses and necessary evaluations. The panel therefore read the statutory wording in light of that explanation. (Second Circuit opinion ¶¶ 16–20.)

Subsequent congressional action reinforced that reading for the panel. Congress responded to Casey by expressly adding expert-fee authority to § 1988, but repeatedly amended IDEA without similarly changing its fee provision, including in December 2004. The panel inferred that Congress saw no need to correct IDEA because the conference report, as recognized in Casey, already supported expert-fee recovery. This was an inference from enactments and their context, rather than a claim that the mere failure of a proposed amendment controlled interpretation. (Second Circuit opinion ¶¶ 18–19 & n.6.)

IDEA’s objectives and procedures furnished additional support. Effective educational-placement litigation often required specialized knowledge of a child’s disabilities and the adequacy of proposed services. The statute protected parents’ participation and allowed them to be accompanied and advised by persons with special knowledge or training. The panel thought that preventing reimbursement for such assistance would frustrate those procedural protections and make access particularly difficult, given the focus on educational relief rather than damages. It also distinguished compensation for an expert’s consultation from compensation for a nonlawyer’s legal representation, treating its approach as consistent with the Third Circuit’s Arons decision. (Second Circuit opinion ¶¶ 20–24 & n.5.)

The panel accepted the district court’s limited calculation of recoverable services. It announced that expert-fee applicants generally should maintain contemporaneous time records, but declined to impose that newly announced expectation retroactively on Arons. It found no abuse of discretion and **affirmed** the $8,650 award. There were no separate opinions. (Second Circuit opinion ¶¶ 25–26.)

## The Supreme Court: holding, judgment, and votes

The Supreme Court held that IDEA’s fee provision did not authorize recovery of expert fees. It **reversed the Second Circuit and remanded** for proceedings consistent with its opinion. The vote on the judgment was 6–3. Justice Alito wrote the Court’s opinion, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. Justice Ginsburg concurred in part and in the judgment, joining the textual discussion at pages 296–298 and the treatment of precedent at pages 300–303, while rejecting reliance on the Spending Clause framework. Justice Breyer dissented, joined by Justices Stevens and Souter; Souter also wrote a short separate dissent. The six votes to reverse therefore should not be described as six votes for the Court’s Spending Clause reasoning. (548 U.S. at 293, 304–308.)

## The Court’s reasoning: clear conditions, costs, and precedent

The five-Justice Court treated IDEA as Spending Clause legislation. States accepting federal money must be able knowingly and voluntarily to accept the attached conditions. The relevant question was whether a state deciding whether to participate had clear notice that prevailing parents could obtain expert fees. Neither a legislator’s subjective expectation nor an inference that a desirable remedy would advance the program answered that question. (Id. at 295–296.)

The text did not provide the requisite notice. Costs was an established legal term encompassing a restricted category of items, rather than all expenses incurred in litigation. The authorization to add reasonable attorney’s fees to those costs did not also add expert fees. The surrounding provisions specified how attorney’s fees should be calculated and limited but provided no comparable expert-fee regime. IDEA’s required parental notice likewise addressed attorney’s fees without identifying expert-fee liability. The Court emphasized the ordinary taxable-cost framework under §§ 1920 and 1821. Its holding did not abolish ordinary statutory witness allowances, including the daily attendance allowance; it rejected shifting the expert’s professional fees under IDEA’s attorney-fee provision. (Id. at 296–298.)

The parents pointed to the 1986 Act’s separate direction that the General Accounting Office study attorney’s fees, costs, expenses, and the hours of personnel including consultants. The Court found that this study provision did not authorize a court to make an expert-fee award. A direction to collect information on expenses and consultant hours did not establish which expenses federal law made recoverable. The study could inform future legislation, and some expenses might be recoverable under state law; moreover, the presumption against surplusage was not absolute. The Court’s first note addressed that expense language, and its second distinguished the statutory versions relevant to fee awards against parents. (Id. at 298–300 & nn.1–2.)

Crawford Fitting and Casey reinforced the text’s restricted meaning. Crawford Fitting required express authority to depart from the statutory witness-fee framework; Casey had interpreted virtually identical attorney-fee language as excluding expert fees. The Casey footnote discussed by the Second Circuit did not hold that the conference report correctly interpreted IDEA or that it supplied the necessary notice under the Spending Clause. Its observation illustrated that attorney’s fees ordinarily did not include expert fees and that a legislature might attempt to alter an ordinary meaning. It did not resolve this case in the parents’ favor. (Id. at 300–303.)

The Court considered IDEA’s general objectives too abstract to decide the fee question. Congress also faced fiscal concerns, and a broad educational purpose did not specify the exact expenses recoverable. Although the conference report unmistakably supported the parents’ position, legislative history could not overcome the text, structure, and precedent here. For the Court, the dispositive concern was what participating states had clearly been told, rather than what members of Congress intended. The GAO’s statement about reimbursable expert expenses also did not provide notice: the GAO was not the agency charged with implementing IDEA. (Id. at 303–304 & n.3.)

## Ginsburg: the same result without the Spending Clause premise

Justice Ginsburg thought ordinary interpretation sufficed. She accepted the Court’s treatment of the wording and precedents: Congress had not enacted expert-fee language, even though its conference explanation made good policy sense. Legislative history could not supply the missing authorization. Her examination of the conference and subsequent floor discussions also found no further attention to expert fees comparable to the focus on reversing the Smith v. Robinson attorney-fee restriction. (Id. at 304–307 & nn.1–2.)

She rejected use of a categorical Spending Clause clear-notice rule for the remedy at issue. IDEA also implemented Congress’s Fourteenth Amendment authority, and Pennhurst’s concern with an unexpected substantive obligation differed from identifying the remedies for breach of an already accepted duty, a distinction reflected in Bell v. New Jersey. She further noted that parents could obtain an independent educational evaluation at public expense under the applicable regulation. That distinct entitlement was not eliminated by denying expert litigation fees under this statutory provision. (Id. at 305–308 & n.3.)

## Breyer and Souter: history, the study provision, and workable access

Justice Breyer regarded the Court’s result as the opposite of Congress’s clearly communicated objective. He traced the 1985–1986 bills, changes in formulations concerning costs, and bipartisan explanations of expert expenses. The conference’s shift to attorney’s fees as part of costs incorporated Marek v. Chesny; in his view, it did not abandon the repeatedly expressed inclusion of experts. The conference report expressly identified expert fees and necessary evaluations, and both Houses adopted it without voiced disagreement on that point. His Appendices A–C reproduced the conference explanation and the relevant adoption proceedings. (Id. at 308–314, 325–330.)

He connected that history to IDEA’s promise of a free appropriate education and parents’ central role in enforcing it. Meaningful participation could depend on costly professional assessments and advice. Drawing on the Court’s reimbursement decisions and data on family resources, he argued that refusing reimbursement could make statutory protections practically unavailable to parents who prevailed. This was his explanation of the legislative purpose, not a separate holding granting compensation for all services. (Id. at 314–315.)

Breyer also disputed the Court’s Spending Clause analysis. He understood Pennhurst to address a new, unexpected substantive condition, not every detail of relief for violation of an existing condition. Bell and Jackson showed that Spending Clause programs could carry remedies not exhaustively listed in advance. A reasonable expert-fee award was unlike unforeseeable punitive damages and was modest relative to IDEA’s potential educational obligations. Although he agreed that the fee clause alone did not clearly grant expert fees, he rejected treating that isolated observation as decisive. (Id. at 315–320.)

His structural argument centered on the enacted GAO study requirement, which expressly sought the amounts of attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses awarded and included consultants’ hours. The provision directed study of the impact of the newly adopted fee amendment. Its statutory placement and language, read with the conference report, supported a broader meaning of costs. He also questioned importing § 1920 as an exclusive definition when IDEA proceedings began administratively and could proceed in either state or federal court. Treating costs that way risked varying definitions by forum. Accepting that the ordinary term of art was restricted did not foreclose Congress’s redefinition of it through the statute understood in context. (Id. at 319–323.)

Souter joined Breyer and separately emphasized that the GAO provision had the same statutory dignity as the fee clause. The study supplied a textual reason to consult the history, rather than an appeal to unenacted history detached from the law. Breyer concluded that faithful interpretation required retaining text, structure, history, and purpose as available tools instead of excluding a tool in advance. Both dissents would have sustained the expert-fee interpretation. (Id. at 308, 323–324.)

## Assigned context and limits

Katzmann’s account presents the dispute as a disagreement over the authority of conference reports, the significance of later congressional amendments, and the role of statutory purpose. It additionally reports that the school district introduced its Spending Clause theory in the Supreme Court and that the parents objected to that timing. That procedural characterization belongs to the assigned retrospective account; the Supreme Court opinion supplies no separate general holding about preservation. The actual judgments concern consultant-fee recovery, not the already resolved placement merits. This brief records the original decisions and their internal disagreement; it does not certify current statutory amendments or subsequent treatment.
