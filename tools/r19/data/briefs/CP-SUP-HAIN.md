# Hain Celestial Group, Inc. v. Palmquist
607 U.S. 421 (2026) | Civil Procedure

## Facts and procedural posture

Sarah and Grant Palmquist fed their son E. P. baby food manufactured by Hain Celestial and purchased at Whole Foods during the first two years of his life. At approximately two and a half, he developed serious disorders and was diagnosed with physical and mental conditions. Tests showed high levels of arsenic, lead, and mercury, and some doctors attributed his conditions to heavy-metal poisoning. After a 2021 congressional staff report identified elevated heavy-metal levels in certain baby foods, including Hain’s, the Palmquists sued both companies in Texas state court. They alleged product liability and negligence against Hain and breach of warranty and negligence against Whole Foods, including representations that Hain’s products were safe. These allegations and diagnostic background did not establish at the Supreme Court that the baby food caused E. P.’s conditions. Majority slip opinion at 2.

Hain removed the suit on diversity grounds. It was a Delaware corporation with its principal place of business in New York; the Palmquists and Whole Foods were Texas citizens. Complete diversity was therefore absent as the action stood on removal. Hain argued that Whole Foods was improperly joined because the complaint did not plausibly state a claim against it. The Palmquists disagreed and promptly sought remand. Applying the Fifth Circuit’s improper-joinder test, the District Court dismissed Whole Foods and refused remand, leaving only Hain opposite the Palmquists. Majority slip opinion at 2–3 & n.1.

The suit proceeded to a federal jury trial. After the Palmquists rested, the court granted Hain judgment as a matter of law on all claims because their evidence did not adequately establish causation, including whether ingestion of heavy metals could cause the identified symptoms and at what levels. The Palmquists appealed both that judgment and Whole Foods’s dismissal. The Fifth Circuit held that they had plausibly pleaded state-law claims against Whole Foods, reversed the improper-joinder ruling, and vacated Hain’s merits judgment for lack of diversity jurisdiction. After rehearing was denied, both defendants petitioned for Supreme Court review of the required vacatur. Majority slip opinion at 3–4.

Two points were accepted for that review: Whole Foods had been improperly dismissed, and correct joinder analysis at removal would have required remand. The Supreme Court did not reconsider the state-law plausibility determination or the removing court’s improper-joinder standard. It addressed what the conceded error meant for the later final judgment. Majority slip opinion at 4–5.

## Issue, holding, judgment, and vote

Does an erroneous interlocutory dismissal of a nondiverse defendant cure a jurisdictional defect existing at removal and preserve the eventual federal merits judgment? No. When the erroneous dismissal is reversed on appeal, the properly joined defendant returns to the action, and the jurisdictional defect remains uncured. The merits judgment must be vacated. Majority slip opinion at 7–9.

Justice Sotomayor wrote for a unanimous Court affirming the Fifth Circuit and remanding for further proceedings. Justice Thomas joined the opinion in full and separately questioned the modern improper-joinder doctrine. No Justice dissented. The judgment leaves Hain without the federal merits victory; it does not determine that Hain or Whole Foods is liable or that the Palmquists’ causation evidence necessarily suffices in later proceedings. Majority slip opinion at 11.

## General rule and the Caterpillar exception

Section 1332(a) ordinarily requires complete diversity: no plaintiff may share citizenship with any defendant, and the amount in controversy must exceed $75,000. A federal appellate court must examine the lower court’s jurisdiction as well as its own. Jurisdiction is generally evaluated by the facts existing when the action was filed or removed. If jurisdiction was absent, a merits judgment ordinarily must be vacated. Majority slip opinion at 3, 5.

A relevant exception permits preservation when the jurisdictional defect is properly cured before final judgment. In *Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis*, a Kentucky plaintiff sued an out-of-state manufacturer and a Kentucky servicing company, and an insurer intervened with claims against both defendants. The plaintiff settled with the servicing company, but the insurer’s claim kept that nondiverse company in the case when the manufacturer prematurely removed it. Although the District Court erroneously denied remand, the servicing company later settled with the insurer and was voluntarily dismissed by a partial final judgment under Rule 54(b), with the parties’ consent. Complete diversity existed before trial. After the manufacturer won, the Supreme Court allowed that judgment to stand despite the earlier removal error. Majority slip opinion at 5–7.

*Caterpillar* distinguished a statutory removal misstep from a jurisdictional defect continuing through final judgment. Finality, efficiency, and economy justified preserving an adjudication after jurisdiction had genuinely been established. But if the defect lingered through judgment, *Caterpillar* expressly required vacatur. Its exception therefore does not treat time spent litigating in federal court as a substitute for subject-matter jurisdiction. Majority slip opinion at 6–7.

## Why Whole Foods’s dismissal was no cure

The dismissal here was interlocutory. Under ordinary final-judgment and merger principles, it became reviewable after entry of the final judgment for Hain. The Fifth Circuit’s reversal established that Whole Foods should have remained a party from the outset. Its temporary absence resulted from reversible judicial error, rather than the correct and final dismissal of the nondiverse defendant in *Caterpillar*. Restoring Whole Foods also restored the Texas citizenship overlap that defeated diversity. Majority slip opinion at 7–8.

Hain urged that the correctness of the dismissal was irrelevant: at the actual moment of final judgment, only diverse parties remained. The Court rejected that snapshot approach. A court cannot create jurisdiction through its own mistake and then insulate the mistake by entering a merits judgment. Treating an erroneous interlocutory dismissal as sufficient would let courts enlarge jurisdiction beyond Congress’s limits. The challenged order must withstand appellate review before it can serve as a valid cure. Majority slip opinion at 8.

Hain also emphasized the expense of a new state-court trial. The Court reiterated that *Caterpillar*’s efficiency considerations arose only after a proper and final cure; they excused the earlier removal violation, not an enduring failure of § 1332 jurisdiction. A potentially wasteful second trial therefore did not preserve the judgment. The Court noted that Rule 54(b) partial final judgments and § 1292(b) certification might sometimes permit earlier review of uncertainty about a nondiverse party, reducing the risk of litigating a full trial before the issue is settled. That observation does not make every joinder ruling automatically appealable. Majority slip opinion at 8–9 & n.3.

## Why Rule 21 did not save the judgment

As a fallback, Hain requested that Whole Foods now be dropped under Rule 21, which permits adding or dropping a party on just terms. The Court rejected the Palmquists’ forfeiture objection: the request was fairly included in the question presented, raised in the certiorari petition, and pressed below. It then rejected the request on its merits. Majority slip opinion at 9 n.4.

*Newman-Green v. Alfonzo-Larrain* permits dismissal of a dispensable nondiverse party in appropriate circumstances but requires careful attention to prejudice and tactical effects. There, the plaintiff sought dismissal to preserve federal jurisdiction. Here, the defendants sought to remove a properly joined defendant over the plaintiffs’ sustained objections. The distinction mattered because plaintiffs ordinarily control the claims and parties in their complaint and may choose a state forum by properly including a nondiverse defendant. Majority slip opinion at 9–10.

The Palmquists had exercised that choice from the original state complaint and diligently defended it through their remand motion. Dropping Whole Foods under Rule 21 would force them into a federal forum for an action that never should have been removed, prejudicing the legitimate litigation arrangement they selected. Rule 21 did not authorize overriding that choice in these circumstances. The Court reserved its use where a nondiverse party is added after proper removal and in actions originally filed in federal court. The ruling is consequently narrower than a ban on all Rule 21 jurisdictional cures. Majority slip opinion at 10–11 & n.5.

## Thomas’s concurrence

Thomas agreed fully with the judgment and majority reasoning but questioned the premise behind modern improper-joinder practice. Federal courts must establish jurisdiction before reaching the merits. Yet a court lacking diversity because of a nondiverse defendant may assess the strength of state-law claims against that defendant, dismiss them through a Rule 12(b)(6)-type inquiry, and use that merits determination to establish diversity. Thomas regarded this sequence as potentially inconsistent with both statutory jurisdictional limits and the obligation to determine jurisdiction first. Concurrence slip opinion at 1–3.

He distinguished the Supreme Court’s early fraudulent-joinder precedents, which he read as addressing actual fraud or bad faith, such as false assertions about citizenship or conduct, rather than merely weak claims. Lower courts’ contemporary merits-based doctrine appeared to exceed those decisions and transfer state-law questions to federal courts that lack jurisdiction to decide them. His footnote noted that several circuits retain the term “fraudulent joinder” even when applying this merits analysis; the majority used “improper joinder” because no fraud by the Palmquists was alleged. Thomas invited reconsideration in a case squarely presenting and briefing the issue. The Court did not invalidate improper joinder in this case. Concurrence slip opinion at 3–4 & *; majority slip opinion at 3 n.1.

## Connections, limits, and assigned material

The useful comparison with *Caterpillar* is a correct final elimination of the diversity defect versus an erroneous interlocutory dismissal reversed on appeal. *Dupree* supplies ordinary merger and final-judgment principles, while *Royal Canin* reinforces the importance of the operative action and the plaintiff’s legitimate choice of claims and parties. Neither efficiency nor a court-created appearance of complete diversity replaces a valid jurisdictional cure.
