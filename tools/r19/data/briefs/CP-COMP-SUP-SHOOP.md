# Shoop v. Twyford

**596 U.S. 811 (2022) • Supreme Court of the United States • June 21, 2022**

**Assigned source:** Civil Procedure Supplement, printed p. 506 (PDF 341). The assigned account emphasizes collateral-order jurisdiction; the complete opinion also decides the All Writs Act question. Chief Justice Roberts wrote for five Justices: Thomas, Alito, Kavanaugh, and Barrett joined. Justice Breyer dissented, joined by Sotomayor and Kagan. Justice Gorsuch separately dissented. The result was 5–4.

## Facts and procedural history

An Ohio jury convicted Raymond Twyford of aggravated murder, kidnapping, robbery, and other offenses and sentenced him to death. The murder followed a planned ruse to lure the victim to a remote location; Twyford and a coconspirator then concealed the body. The state appellate courts affirmed. In state postconviction proceedings, Twyford argued that trial counsel should have presented evidence of a head injury sustained during a teenage suicide attempt. The state courts rejected that ineffective-assistance claim on the merits: counsel had instead presented a competing psychological explanation consistent with Twyford's deliberate conduct and confession. 814–815.

Twyford petitioned for federal habeas relief in 2003. After substantial delay, the district court dismissed most claims as procedurally defaulted but allowed some ineffective-assistance claims to continue. He sought an order requiring Ohio to transport him to Ohio State University's medical center for neurological testing. A neurologist recommended further evaluation, partly because prior scans showed metal fragments in his skull. Twyford asserted that testing could plausibly support claims concerning counsel, an expert witness, competency, and his confession, or counter procedural-default and exhaustion objections. He urged the court to postpone the question whether the resulting evidence would be admissible. 815–816, 822–823.

The district court granted transportation under the All Writs Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1651(a), without deciding whether it could consider the new evidence. It stayed the order pending appeal. The Sixth Circuit affirmed, reasoning that the evidence plausibly related to the habeas claims and that admissibility need not yet be considered. Judge Batchelder dissented. 816–817.

## Questions presented

Could the court of appeals immediately review this transportation order despite the absence of a final habeas judgment? Could the district court use the All Writs Act to facilitate new evidence without a showing that the evidence would be usable in the habeas case?

## Holdings and governing rules

**Appellate jurisdiction:** The majority held that this All Writs Act transportation order satisfied the collateral-order doctrine. It conclusively required transportation, resolved an important state-sovereignty question conceptually distinct from the habeas claims, and would be unreviewable after final judgment. Taking a convicted prisoner outside prison created safety risks and burdens that later review could not remedy. 817–818 n.1.

**Evidence and transportation:** An order enabling a prisoner to search for new evidence is not necessary or appropriate in aid of federal habeas jurisdiction when the prisoner has not shown that the evidence would be admissible in connection with a particular claim for relief. 824. The Court did **not** decide Ohio's broader contention that the All Writs Act never authorizes transportation for medical testing. 817.

## Reasoning and application

The All Writs Act operates within statutory limits; it cannot circumvent AEDPA. For state-court merits decisions, § 2254(d) demands more than a federal court's belief that the state court was wrong, and review ordinarily rests on the state-court record. Where a prisoner failed to develop a claim's factual basis in state proceedings, § 2254(e)(2) sharply restricts new evidence. Its exceptions require a qualifying new constitutional rule or previously undiscoverable factual predicate, together with the statutory clear-and-convincing innocence showing. These requirements are conditional: the opinion does not say § 2254(e)(2) governs every request for new evidence regardless of the prisoner's conduct. 818–820.

Before facilitating evidentiary development, a habeas court must determine whether the desired material could lawfully be considered. Otherwise the order needlessly prolongs litigation and undermines finality and comity. A public transportation order adds safety and administrative burdens; the majority's second footnote gives an example of escape risks. 820–822 & n.2.

Twyford did not explain how the contemplated testing overcame the state-record restriction, how he satisfied § 2254(e)(2), or why that provision would not apply. His passing reference to procedural default identified neither the specific defaulted claims nor how testing would establish cause and prejudice. Under the cited Shinn decision, development of otherwise unusable merits evidence merely to assess cause and prejudice served no purpose when § 2254(e)(2) applied and its conditions remained unsatisfied. The lower courts therefore erred by treating a plausible connection to a claim as sufficient. 822–823. This was a failure to justify the transportation order, not a Supreme Court adjudication that every underlying habeas claim lacked merit.

## Separate opinions

**Breyer, joined by Sotomayor and Kagan (824–831):** The Sixth Circuit lacked interlocutory appellate jurisdiction, so the Court should not reach the All Writs Act merits. Transportation was analogous to discovery, whose burdens and irreversibility generally do not make it immediately appealable. Mere cost to a State did not establish a sufficiently important sovereign interest. The designated medical center routinely accommodated prisoners, and Twyford reported numerous prior trips without incident. Moreover, the majority's admissibility requirement intertwined the transportation question with the habeas merits and risked duplicative appellate review. Certification, mandamus, and certain sanction appeals supplied alternatives. Earlier cases supplied few examples rather than a compelling consensus; Pennsylvania Bureau of Correction had not actually decided appellate jurisdiction.

**Gorsuch (831):** He would dismiss certiorari as improvidently granted once the jurisdictional complication emerged. The Court had taken the case to resolve transportation authority, and should exercise its repeatedly stated caution about expanding Cohen through adjudication rather than rulemaking. He did not join Breyer's opinion or decide the transportation merits.

## Exact disposition and course significance

The Court **reversed the Sixth Circuit's judgment and remanded** for proceedings consistent with its opinion. 824. The reversal concerned the order affirmed on interlocutory appeal; it did not vacate Twyford's conviction or grant habeas relief.

The supplement uses this case to expose a dispute within collateral-order doctrine: the majority treated compelled prisoner transportation as an important, irreparable sovereign burden; the dissents treated it as discovery management requiring narrow appellate review. The separate All Writs Act holding requires a legally usable evidence showing before transportation.
