# Reeves v. Crownshield

**CP-127 · Civil Procedure · 274 N.Y. 74, 8 N.E.2d 283 (1937) · April 27, 1937 · Finch, with all six other judges concurring**

The Court of Appeals upheld an installment-payment order and contempt sanction against a debtor who could comply after reasonable living needs were considered. Its reasoning distinguished inability to pay a debt from willful refusal to obey an affordable court order, and distinguished collecting paid salary from interfering with the federal government’s payment of its employee.

## Collection proceedings and statutory safeguards

Reeves sought to collect a judgment of approximately $400 from Charles Crownshield. Supplementary examination showed that Crownshield worked for the federal government as a steamship inspector, earning $230 per month less a small pension deduction. He had no children, his wife’s whereabouts were unknown, and apart from $48 rent and living expenses he had no financial obligations. The court ordered $20 monthly installments until the judgment was satisfied. After he failed to pay, it adjudged contempt, imposed a $20 fine, and provided for commitment if the fine was not paid. 274 N.Y. at 77.

A 1935 New York enactment authorized installment orders from income in addition to the existing garnishment procedures. Under Civil Practice Act § 793, the debtor had to receive notice and an opportunity to show inability to pay. The order had to account for reasonable requirements of the debtor and family and obligations to other creditors. Section 801 authorized punishment for contempt upon refusal to comply. Finch situated the enactment in efforts to improve collection from debtors able to pay who evaded judgments. Id. at 76–77.

The debtor appealed directly from the City Court on constitutional grounds. The parties stipulated to consolidate review of the installment and contempt orders. The court regarded the challenged order as a final order in a special proceeding, permitting review. Id. at 77–78.

## Due-process objection

Crownshield argued that the enactment effectively authorized imprisonment for debt and violated state and federal due process. The court observed that neither constitution expressly prohibited imprisonment for debt, while the state statutory prohibition excepted cases specially authorized by law. It left open possible due-process concerns with imprisoning a debtor without regard to ability to pay. The present order instead concerned payments from income after allowance for reasonable needs and an opportunity to establish inability. Id. at 78.

Crownshield did not claim that $20 monthly payments were unjust, harsh, or inequitable on his circumstances. He asserted that a court lacked power to require any such use of his income. The court characterized his position as arbitrary refusal by someone able to comply. Contempt enforced obedience to the judicial order; it was not imprisonment simply for the existence of an unpaid debt. Without enforcement authority, the direct collection remedy would be ineffective. The opinion’s quoted execution-law discussion similarly distinguished protection of the honest poor debtor from a shield for a solvent debtor concealing assets or evading process. Id. at 78–80.

## Federal-employment objection

Crownshield also claimed the collection and possible imprisonment interfered with a federal instrumentality. He invoked decisions invalidating state taxes on federal officers’ salaries. Finch distinguished their historical rationale: taxing salary reduced federal compensation and could force the federal government to increase its payments. The installment order did not demand payment by the government or reduce the compensation it provided. Id. at 80.

Once salary was paid, it became the employee’s general income. Property purchased with it could be subject to execution, and the court saw no reason to exempt the same value merely because it remained money. The opinion acknowledged the then-existing bar on garnishing federal wages, but distinguished an order requiring the recipient to apply income already received to personal debts. Federal employment also conferred no privilege to disobey a lawful state-court order, just as it did not immunize criminal conduct. These are the court’s 1937 explanations of federal immunity and collection law; this brief does not present them as an updated account of current garnishment law. Id. at 80–81.

## Exact result and scope

Both orders were affirmed with costs. Finch wrote, and Crane, Lehman, O’Brien, Hubbs, Loughran, and Rippey concurred. The complete judicial report contains no separate opinion or footnotes. The assignment’s title uses “Crownshield”; the archived reporter caption is transcribed “Crownshield” as well.

The holding depends on the procedure and ability-to-pay circumstances actually described. It does not establish that every unpaid judgment permits confinement or that an indigent debtor may be punished without inquiry into means. The assigned later discussion of body-execution statutes and another jurisdiction’s invalidation of such a statute is editorial context, rather than an additional holding of Reeves. Its central enforcement distinction is between punishing debt alone and compelling compliance with a properly tailored order that the debtor can obey.
