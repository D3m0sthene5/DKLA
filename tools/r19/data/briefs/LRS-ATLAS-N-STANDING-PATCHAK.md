# Match-E-Be-Nash-She-Wish Band of Pottawatomi Indians v. Patchak

**567 U.S. 209 (2012) — Supreme Court of the United States — June 18, 2012.** No. 11-246, together with *Salazar v. Patchak*, No. 11-247. Justice Kagan wrote for eight Justices; Justice Sotomayor dissented alone. The assigned account appears in the November continuation at physical pages 168–169, printed pages 1258–1259.

A neighboring landowner could pursue an APA challenge to the Interior Secretary's acquisition of land in trust for a tribe planning a casino. The Court held that the Quiet Title Act's Indian-lands immunity exception did not govern his suit because he claimed no competing property interest. It also held that his neighborhood economic, environmental, and aesthetic interests fell within the acquisition statute's zone. Those threshold holdings allowed litigation to continue; they did not establish that the Secretary lacked authority to acquire the land.

## Acquisition, lawsuit, and procedure

The Match-E-Be-Nash-She-Wish Band had a long history but received formal federal recognition in 1999. In 2001 it asked the Secretary to acquire the Bradley Property in Wayland Township, Michigan, in trust under §5 of the Indian Reorganization Act, then codified at 25 U.S.C. §465. It planned gaming operations to finance economic development, self-sufficiency, government, and social and educational programs. Trust status could satisfy the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act's land requirements. **212 & n.1.**

The Secretary announced approval in 2005 following administrative review and allowed a thirty-day period before acquisition for judicial challenges. Michigan Gambling Opposition sued within that period under environmental and gaming statutes. The Secretary delayed taking title while that litigation proceeded; the organization lost in the District Court and D.C. Circuit. After the circuit ruling, but before acquisition, nearby resident David Patchak brought a different APA challenge. **213.**

Patchak alleged that the Secretary could not acquire the land for this Band because it had not been federally recognized when the IRA was enacted in 1934. He alleged that the planned casino would increase traffic and crime, reduce property values, and permanently alter the area's rural character. He requested a declaration and an injunction preventing acquisition, but asserted **no ownership or other competing interest in the Bradley Property**. The Band intervened to defend the decision. **213.**

After the Supreme Court denied review in the organization's earlier litigation in January 2009, the Secretary took title. That event mooted the request to prevent acquisition, but all parties agreed the continuing suit effectively sought to divest the government of title. The Court subsequently held in *Carcieri* that the Secretary's acquisition authority depended on a tribe being under federal jurisdiction in 1934. This opinion expressly reserved whether the Band met that condition and how federal jurisdiction related to Patchak's allegation about formal recognition. **213–214 & n.2.**

The District Court dismissed without deciding the merits because Patchak's interests were outside the statute's zone. The D.C. Circuit reversed and also rejected the Secretary and Band's alternative immunity argument. The Supreme Court granted their two certiorari petitions to consider both threshold issues. **214–215.**

## APA waiver and the Quiet Title Act

APA §702 generally waived federal immunity for nonmonetary relief challenging official action. It withheld that waiver when another consent-to-suit statute expressly or impliedly forbade the requested relief. The government and Band invoked the Quiet Title Act, which authorized suits over competing property interests but excluded trust or restricted Indian lands. Their position was that Patchak sought to extinguish federal title to precisely those lands, so he could not obtain the same result through the APA. **215–216.**

The Court focused on the kind of claim the QTA addressed, rather than only the practical effect of relief. An adverse owner could not relabel a quiet-title action as an APA case to evade the Indian-lands exception. But Patchak was not an adverse claimant: he wanted the government stripped of title because the Secretary allegedly exceeded statutory authority, not because the land belonged to him or should be transferred to him. **216–220.**

Several statutory features supported that distinction. The QTA's title and related jurisdiction and venue provisions concerned quiet-title actions. Its pleading provision required plaintiffs to identify their own right, title, or interest in the property. Its compensation option allowed the United States to retain property by paying a successful claimant. That option made sense where a claimant established a property right, but payment to an owner would not satisfy Patchak's separate statutory grievance. Earlier QTA decisions likewise involved plaintiffs asserting competing property interests. **217–221 & nn.4–5.**

The Court rejected the claim that the QTA's specific authorization implied a bar against all other challenges involving federal land ownership. A detailed remedy can exclude other persons asserting the same kind of claim, but shared subject matter alone did not turn Patchak's different APA claim into a quiet-title action. The majority acknowledged that litigation could impede tribal investment regardless of whether a plaintiff claimed ownership. Still, it regarded extending the QTA's limitations to the broader category as Congress's decision. **221–224.**

The result was a holding that the APA's general immunity waiver applied to this suit. It did not eliminate the QTA's restrictions for adverse property claimants or decide that every dispute touching Indian trust land could proceed under the APA. The disagreement concerned this particular nonclaimant's statutory challenge to acquisition. **220–224.**

## Zone of interests

The government and Band separately argued that §465 concerned land acquisition, while Patchak's harms came from the land's eventual use. The Court described the APA zone test as relatively undemanding: an interest need only arguably fall within what the allegedly violated statute protected or regulated. Intended benefit to the plaintiff was unnecessary. The question was therefore not whether Congress wanted to promote Patchak's welfare or whether he was a tribal official seeking land. **224–225 & n.7.**

The acquisition provision was closely connected to use. It supported tribal economic life, and the Secretary acquired land with its anticipated economic use in mind. The Department's implementing regulations required consideration of intended uses and potential land-use conflicts; off-reservation business acquisitions also required an anticipated-benefits plan. The Band's application and the Secretary's notice specifically identified a gaming facility. Acquisition was not a transaction detached from the proposed casino. **225–227.**

A neighbor foreseeably affected by that use was therefore a reasonable challenger to the acquisition's statutory limits. Patchak's asserted economic, environmental, and aesthetic interests came within the provision's regulatory scope even though he was not its intended beneficiary. This was the zone holding emphasized in the assigned account. It was not a decision that proximity alone establishes every element of standing for every statute. **227–228.**

## Dissent

Justice Sotomayor would have held the suit barred by sovereign immunity and therefore did not reach standing. She read APA §702's exception as focusing on forbidden relief: Patchak sought to divest the government of Indian trust land, the very consequence Congress carefully limited in the QTA. The QTA preserved federal control through its compensation option, protected Indian lands, and limited who could sue. In her view, those protections could not be bypassed simply by omitting a personal property claim from the complaint. **228–233 & nn.1–3; 236 n.6.**

She also disputed the majority's reliance on a narrow ordinary conception of quiet title, observing that the statute permitted lesser interests such as easements and mineral-exploration rights. Her final concerns were practical: property claimants could recruit nonclaimants to seek the same outcome; delayed challenges could undermine investment and the security of tribal acquisitions; and disputes over hidden property interests could create new pleading incentives and burdens on the government. Those were dissenting objections and predictions, not additional Court holdings. **234–238 & nn.4–8.**

## Judgment and source significance

The Court **affirmed the D.C. Circuit and remanded for further proceedings**. The federal immunity objection and zone-of-interests objection did not bar the suit. It neither invalidated the trust acquisition nor ordered the casino closed. The underlying statutory-authority issue, including the Band's status in 1934, remained unresolved in this judgment. **214 n.2, 228.**

The assignment correctly highlights why a plaintiff need not be an intended beneficiary to satisfy the APA zone requirement. The full opinion adds the separate immunity dispute and its competing views of claims, relief, and congressional remedial design. This brief concerns the 2012 judgment and then-used prudential-standing terminology. It does not certify later developments in Patchak's litigation or present-day statutory numbering and doctrine.
