# West Virginia University Hospitals, Inc. v. Casey
499 U.S. 83 (1991) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts, litigation, and issue

West Virginia University Hospitals operated a hospital in Morgantown near the Pennsylvania border, serving Medicaid patients from southwestern Pennsylvania. In January 1986, Pennsylvania’s Department of Public Welfare announced new reimbursement schedules for those patients’ services. The hospital unsuccessfully challenged the rates administratively on federal statutory and constitutional grounds, then sued Pennsylvania officials under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. Its lawyers hired Coopers & Lybrand and three doctors specializing in hospital finance to assist in preparing the case and testify. The hospital prevailed at trial in May 1988. (85.)

The district court awarded fees under § 1988, including more than $100,000 for expert services. It found those services essential to presenting the case, and the state officials did not dispute that finding. The Third Circuit affirmed the merits judgment but reversed the expert-fee award except for the witness attendance fees authorized by 28 U.S.C. § 1821(b), then $30 per day. Supreme Court review concerned the expert-fee disallowance, not whether Pennsylvania’s reimbursement system had been unlawful. The question was whether § 1988’s authorization of a reasonable attorney’s fee allowed the prevailing hospital to shift testimonial and advisory expert fees to the defendants. (85–87.)

Justice Scalia wrote the March 19, 1991 opinion for Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices White, O’Connor, Kennedy, and Souter. Marshall dissented separately and joined Stevens’s dissent, which Blackmun also joined. The vote was six to three to affirm. The decision construes the version of § 1988 before the later congressional response discussed in the assigned source. (83, 102–103.)

## Costs framework and the majority’s construction

Sections 1920 and 1821 identify taxable costs and limit witness attendance compensation. Crawford Fitting Co. v. J. T. Gibbons, Inc. had required contractual or explicit statutory authority before courts could award a party’s expert witness fees beyond the statutory cap. Advisory experts presented a related but distinct problem: because they did not attend as witnesses, § 1821’s attendance limit did not directly apply, but none of § 1920’s categories authorized a party’s advisory expert expenses. Either way, the hospital needed another source of authority. The majority held that § 1988’s attorney-fee language did not supply it. Nor did describing attorney fees as part of costs enlarge ordinary taxable costs to encompass these expenses. (86–88 & n. 3.)

The Court examined usage across statutes rather than treating the term as a dictionary question alone. Numerous enactments separately authorized attorney fees and expert witness fees, including statutes adopted close to § 1988 in 1976. Other provisions expressly authorized studies, analyses, and advisory services in addition to legal fees. If attorney fees already encompassed expert compensation, those detailed separate authorizations would be redundant. The majority regarded legal and expert fees as established, distinct categories in the legislative vocabulary. (88–92.)

Judicial usage before § 1988 pointed the same way. Courts had sometimes shifted both legal and expert expenses under equitable doctrines, but analyzed them as separate categories. Under the Clayton Act and patent statutes authorizing attorney fees, the prevailing pre-1976 decisions had declined to treat expert fees as part of that authorization. The hospital’s contrary examples were either ambiguous about their authority or relied on a mistaken understanding of precedent. The Court found no persuasive practice under which the enacted term itself included the expert expenses at issue. (92–97.)

## Purpose, precedent, and the limits of judicial supplementation

The hospital argued that § 1988 responded to Alyeska Pipeline Service Co. v. Wilderness Society and should restore pre-Alyeska equitable fee shifting. The majority accepted that some legislators understood the statute as a response to Alyeska, but rejected the inference that it restored the earlier regime in every respect. Section 1988 was broader in some ways, permitting attorney-fee awards without the private-attorney-general doctrine’s former limitations, and narrower in others, covering specified civil-rights provisions rather than every statute. A general desire to encourage enforcement therefore could not establish reimbursement of every expense previously awarded. (97–99.)

The hospital also relied on Missouri v. Jenkins, which allowed compensation for paralegal and law-clerk work. The majority distinguished those services: they had traditionally contributed to attorneys’ work product and been incorporated in hourly charges. Separate billing did not change their legal character. Expert services had not traditionally been folded into lawyers’ hourly rates, and statutory practice repeatedly distinguished them from attorney fees. Jenkins resolved an actual question about the term’s application; it did not license overriding an otherwise established meaning on remedial-policy grounds. (99–100.)

Finally, the hospital argued that Congress would have authorized expert fees had it considered the issue more carefully. The majority rejected judicial completion of an allegedly forgotten provision. Courts may select a permissible interpretation that makes sense within the statutory corpus when language is ambiguous; they may not replace clear language with a more generous policy found in another enactment. A later Congress’s different choice is not proof that an earlier Congress forgot something. The majority’s final note likewise distinguished interpreting the will expressed in a particular enactment from predicting how Congress might later amend it. (100–102 & n. 7.)

## The IDEA passage and its assigned use

Footnote 5 discusses a 1986 conference report on the Handicapped Children’s Protection Act. The report stated that attorney fees as part of costs should include expert expenses and necessary tests or evaluations. Scalia characterized that statement as an apparent effort to depart from ordinary meaning and define a term of art. In Casey, the reference supported the majority’s conclusion that ordinary attorney-fee terminology did not itself include those expenses. The Court was deciding § 1988, not finally resolving reimbursement under the IDEA. (91–92 n. 5.)

The assigned Katzmann account explains how the Second Circuit later read that observation as support for IDEA expert-fee reimbursement and drew an inference from Congress’s post-Casey amendment of § 1988 without a comparable IDEA amendment. That is an account of a later court’s reasoning, not an IDEA holding by the Casey majority. Keeping the difference matters because the assigned reading uses the same footnote to illustrate disagreement over the significance of legislative reports and congressional responses. The account’s broad description of later civil-rights reimbursement also should not erase the particular claims and statutory version Casey itself decided. (Assigned printed 83–84.)

## Marshall and Stevens dissent

Marshall’s separate dissent warned that limiting reimbursement could deny effective access to federal courts even without formally repealing substantive civil-rights protections. If lawyers could not economically represent victims, the enforcement scheme would be undermined. He endorsed Stevens’s fuller analysis and treated the majority’s literalism as frustrating rather than respecting congressional power. (102–103.)

Stevens, joined by Marshall and Blackmun, argued that the statutory language should be read in its established fee-shifting context and through prior decisions construing § 1988. Ordinary litigation costs and the compensatory expenses allowed under fee-shifting provisions were not necessarily identical. He pointed to unchallenged reimbursement of travel and telephone expenses in this case and to Jenkins’s conception of the attorney’s work product. Experts whose work was essential to preparation could substitute for lawyer time, improve quality, and reduce costs, just as paralegals could. Reimbursing one category while excluding the other was, in his view, arbitrary and inconsistent with Congress’s compensatory objective. (103–109.)

Stevens also read the House and Senate reports as intending to remedy Alyeska’s disruption of civil-rights enforcement and restore a practice under which necessary expert expenses could be shifted. Committee-approved examples and hearings emphasized private enforcement and making successful litigants whole. The hospital’s expert work was undisputedly essential, and the dissent identified $104,133 in expert expenses that the majority left the hospital to bear. (109–112.)

The dissent’s final section contrasted purposive interpretations Congress had left intact with restrictive interpretations it had repudiated through legislation. Stevens acknowledged Congress’s authority to correct judicial errors but argued that needless insistence on more precise wording wasted legislative effort and could disregard persuasive evidence of actual purpose. The majority’s contrary response was that later amendments do not establish what an earlier enacted text meant. The disagreement was therefore about the permissible sources and role of interpretation, as well as the practical financing of civil-rights lawsuits. (112–116; 102 n. 7.)

## Judgment and source limits

The Court affirmed the expert-fee disallowance. Experts who testified remained eligible for the ordinary statutory witness fee, but § 1988 supplied no authority to shift their full testimonial or advisory charges. The hospital’s merits victory and entitlement to attorney fees were not reversed. The opinion itself notes that the daily witness allowance had already increased from $30 to $40 under the 1990 amendment; the $30 figure describes the award reviewed, rather than the rate universally applicable thereafter. (86–88 n. 2; 102.)
