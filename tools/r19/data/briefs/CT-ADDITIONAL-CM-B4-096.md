# Orff v. United States
358 F.3d 1137 (9th Cir. 2004) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

The Ninth Circuit decided this appellate stage on February 18, 2004, after argument on February 11, 2003. Judge Clifton wrote for a panel consisting of Judges Noonan, Thomas, and Clifton; the report contains no separate writing or dissent. Reviewing sovereign immunity and subject-matter jurisdiction de novo, the panel affirmed the conclusion that the United States had not waived immunity for these farmers’ claims. It vacated the district court’s earlier merits rulings on appropriative water rights, trust obligations, and surcharges because the court lacked jurisdiction to enter them. The judgment was affirmed in part and vacated in part, with each party bearing its own costs. This brief concerns the assigned 2004 Ninth Circuit decision, not a combined account of different appellate stages. (1138, 1140–1142, 1149–1150.)

## Water project, contract, and dispute

Westlands Water District received water from the San Luis Unit of the federal Central Valley Project under a 1963 contract with the United States. A stipulated judgment in Barcellos in 1986 upheld the contract and required federal performance, resolving litigation arising from the government’s 1978 assertion that the contract was invalid. Individual landowners and water users within Westlands benefited from the deliveries but were not parties to the 1963 district–government contract. (1141.)

The Sacramento River winter-run chinook salmon was listed as threatened in 1990, and the Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta’s delta smelt received that designation in 1993. The federal agency determined that continued project operation jeopardized the species. The Bureau of Reclamation reduced Westlands’ 1993 allocation to fifty percent of its contractual supply under the Endangered Species Act and the Central Valley Project Improvement Act. In earlier litigation, the courts held that Article 11(a) relieved the government of liability for a water shortage caused by statutory mandate, while allowing a separate challenge to the merits of the Bureau’s compliance with the statutes. That earlier contract interpretation supplies background; this opinion did not independently adjudicate the merits of all disputed allocations. (1141.)

## Procedural chronology

Westlands began the present action in May 1993; the farmers and Natural Resources Defense Council intervened. Westlands dismissed its complaint without prejudice in 1995, leaving the farmers to file a second amended complaint. On June 5, 1998, the district court dismissed statutory claims for lack of jurisdiction under the Tucker Act, noting the possibility of an action in the Court of Federal Claims, and rejected the contention that reclamation statutes themselves constituted contracts. Four sets of claims remained: appropriative water rights, trust law, challenged project surcharges under the contract and Constitution, and breach of the 1963 contract. The farmers argued that water rights and trust obligations were incorporated into that contract. (1141–1142.)

The district court initially regarded the farmers as a contracting entity covered by 43 U.S.C. § 390uu’s waiver of sovereign immunity. It granted the government summary judgment on the first three remaining claims but found triable issues on the fourth. On reconsideration on April 12, 2000, it applied Klamath Water Users Protective Association v. Patterson and concluded that the farmers were merely incidental beneficiaries. It then found no applicable waiver and entered final judgment for the government on August 1, 2000. The farmers appealed; Westlands returned as an intervenor to protect its interests, and both Westlands and NRDC filed appellate briefs. (1142.)

## Sovereign immunity, preclusion, and the statutory waiver

The panel applied the rule that the United States cannot be sued without an express waiver, strictly construing the waiver’s scope in the government’s favor. The farmers could not use Barcellos I to preclude the immunity defense. That earlier decision had addressed an alleged ultra vires agency action and the McCarran Amendment, whereas the present case alleged no such ultra vires action and sought private damages rather than a comprehensive adjudication or administration of all claimants’ rights on a stream. The issues were therefore not identical. The later 1993 allocation, based on species designations made after Barcellos I, also involved a different transactional nucleus of facts, defeating claim preclusion. (1142–1144.)

The McCarran Amendment did not cover this suit between particular farmers and the government. Note 3 separately addressed administration of previously adjudicated water rights: the farmers did not identify specific adjudicated rights violated by this allocation, instead asserting rights implicitly incorporated into the contract. General prior adjudication did not convert this private action into the comprehensive proceeding the waiver contemplated. They had also pleaded jurisdiction under § 390uu rather than the McCarran Amendment. (1143–1144 & nn. 2–3.)

Section 390uu permits joining the United States as a necessary defendant to adjudicate contractual rights of a contracting entity and the United States under federal reclamation contracts. The farmers asserted intended-third-party-beneficiary status to qualify as a contracting entity. Applying Klamath, the panel required clear contractual intent to give government-contract beneficiaries enforceable rights. A beneficiary need not be individually named if it belongs to the intended class, but explicit mention of that class is not by itself sufficient. A project may knowingly benefit farmers without granting them a direct right to enforce the district’s contract against the United States. (1144–1145.)

## Why the contract did not establish intended beneficiary rights

Article 15 required payment of district assessments or charges before an individual water user could receive project water, even if the assessment had been held irregular or void. It also required any district–user contracts to subject the user’s supply to the terms of the federal contract. The farmers treated these references to individual users as proof of direct rights against the government. The panel read them instead as conditions on obtaining water through Westlands. The clause described a possible contractual relationship between Westlands and its users; it did not establish a contractual relationship between those users and the United States. (1145–1146.)

Article 11(b) made a payment adjustment the sole remedy for a shortage and referred to the district or anyone claiming water rights by, through, or under it. The panel reasoned that the adjustment concerned money paid by Westlands to the government. The contested language could sensibly refer to an assignee taking over the district’s rights and duties and entering contractual privity, rather than an incidental beneficiary. These farmers had never received such an assignment. Thus neither Article 11(b) nor Article 15 rebutted the government-contract presumption. (1146–1147 & n. 4.)

The panel expressly acknowledged possible disagreement with the Federal Circuit’s H.F. Allen Orchards decision. As it understood that decision, beneficial use of the water and ultimate payment had supported beneficiary status without examination of particular contract language. The Ninth Circuit required clear intent in the precise language and respectfully disagreed to the extent another circuit used a different approach. This acknowledged disagreement is important: the opinion did not present its presumption’s application as an uncontested rule in every federal court. (1147 n. 5.)

## Other asserted routes to enforcement

The farmers’ recordable contracts with the government concerning excess-acreage lands did not solve the problem. Those contracts required sales within ten years and addressed valuation and eligibility for project water. Article 5 subjected their receipt of water to the district contract; only Articles 23, 24, and 25, concerning excess lands, were directly incorporated. The farmers were not suing to enforce those provisions or another term of the recordable contracts. Being a contracting party to those separate agreements did not supply a right to enforce the 1963 contract’s different obligations. (1147–1148 & n. 6.)

The Barcellos stipulated judgment expressly provided that it and the settlement stipulation were not a contract or amendment for § 390uu purposes. Participation in that judgment therefore did not establish the asserted contracting-entity status. Note 7 records a separate district-court ruling that a damages demand exceeding $10,000 could not be brought there on another theory under that judgment, with a possible Court of Federal Claims route; the farmers did not challenge that ruling on appeal, and the panel did not consider it. (1148 & n. 7.)

Finally, the court did not decide whether Westlands was a trustee for the farmers. Even assuming that relationship, the farmers had neither sought judicial compulsion against Westlands nor alleged that the government directly participated in a breach of trust, as required by the exception they invoked to sue in the trustee’s shoes. The assumed trust relationship consequently supplied no demonstrated basis to proceed here. (1148–1149.)

## Vacatur, limits, and significance

The failure to establish a sovereign-immunity waiver deprived the district court of authority from the outset. Its earlier belief that it possessed jurisdiction could not preserve its merits rulings after that defect was recognized. The Ninth Circuit therefore vacated the appropriative-rights, trust, and surcharge rulings as nullities that would bind neither this litigation nor another case. The waiver question did not depend on resolving those claims’ substantive merits. Bell v. Hood, which distinguishes jurisdiction from failure to state a federal cause of action, did not change the result: here the defect was lack of sovereign consent to suit, not simply failure to state a claim. (1149–1150.)

The assigned government-contract account illustrates why intended economic benefit and an enforceable third-party right are distinct. It should not be read as a merits holding that the challenged water reduction satisfied every contractual or statutory duty. This stage affirmed the jurisdictional dismissal and vacated the prior merits adjudications; it did not award damages or foreclose every conceivable proceeding in a different forum. (1148 n. 7, 1149–1150.)
