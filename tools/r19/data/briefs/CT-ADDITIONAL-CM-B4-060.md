# Karl Wendt Farm Equipment Co. v. International Harvester Co.
931 F.2d 1112 (6th Cir. 1991) | Contracts

## Court, divided decision, and exact appellate relief
The Sixth Circuit decided this diversity appeal April 26, 1991; the report records rehearing denied May 21. Judge Nathaniel R. Jones wrote the majority joined by Judge Boggs. Judge Ryan dissented from removing impracticability from the jury. Applying Michigan contract law, the majority reversed the dealer's unsuccessful contract verdict and remanded for a new trial only on damages for International Harvester's breach. It affirmed all other challenged rulings, including rejection of IH's alternative defenses and the deficiency judgment on its counterclaims. This was not a remand to redetermine liability or cancellation of the dealer's outstanding debts. (931 F.2d at 1112, 1114–1115, 1122–1124.)

## Dealer agreement, recession, and sale
Wendt became an IH dealer in Marlette, Michigan, under a fall 1974 Dealer Sales and Service Agreement governing sales, purchase and servicing of equipment, operating requirements, and specified termination procedures. A dramatic farm-equipment downturn and substantial losses led IH to sell its farm-equipment assets to J.I. Case and Tenneco. The base consideration was $246.7 million cash and $161.3 million participating preferred stock. IH described a $479 million paper loss with a tax-credit offset; note 1 preserves a conflicting $497 million record figure and adopts $479 million for the opinion. These transaction-loss figures differ from the broader operating losses discussed in the impracticability evidence. (Id. at 1114 & n. 1.)

The purchaser received access to IH dealers, not the franchise network itself. In roughly four hundred areas containing both IH and Case dealerships, Case offered only one franchise. IH dealers received nearly two-thirds of those franchises, but Wendt did not receive one in its conflicted area. Wendt sued for breach and other theories; only the contract claim survived to trial. IH counterclaimed for debts on equipment and parts supplied on credit. (Id. at 1114.)

The trial court submitted impracticability to the jury, which returned no cause of action on Wendt's contract claim. It denied Wendt judgment notwithstanding the verdict or a new trial. It separately directed verdicts for Wendt against IH's frustration defense, asserted product-discontinuation authority, and implied limitation on contract duration. Both sides appealed their respective adverse rulings. (Id. at 1114–1115.)

## Governing law and why impracticability failed
Contract interpretation received de novo review. State law supplied the directed-verdict and JNOV standards: a directed verdict required an essential failure of proof when evidence was viewed favorably to the nonmovant, and JNOV required insufficient evidence as a matter of law. Federal law governed the new-trial motion, reviewed for abuse of discretion. (Id. at 1115.)

Michigan's highest court had not squarely addressed modern impracticability in this setting. The majority considered its impossibility decisions, an intermediate appellate decision broadening the doctrine, Restatement § 261, and other available state-law evidence. It did not announce that impracticability existed only for UCC sales or categorically predict that Michigan would never recognize it. Rather, even if recognized, it would not excuse these circumstances as a matter of law. (Id. at 1116–1118.)

The Restatement required an event defeating a basic assumption, without the obligor's fault and absent contrary allocation. Extreme shortages can qualify, but ordinary market conditions and a party's financial position generally are not basic assumptions. IH cited losses exceeding $2 million daily, a Fortune 500 decline, and evidence of possible bankruptcy absent sale. The majority held that the downturn and the decision to leave the business did not justify unilateral termination without observing the agreement's procedures. The scale of losses did not change their legal character; proportional hardship had failed to excuse smaller enterprises in Michigan cases. (Id. at 1117–1118.)

The contract specifically allocated termination through stated conditions and procedures. IH could seek mutual termination and share sale proceeds rather than unload the consequence onto excluded dealers. Although IH did not necessarily cause the recession, it chose the asset-sale response. Excusing performance would permit it to avoid franchise obligations while the purchaser selected desirable dealers and others bore the loss. The majority therefore held Wendt entitled to a directed verdict on this defense. (Id. at 1118.)

## Frustration was not established by lost profitability
The majority affirmed rejection of frustration under Restatement § 265. That defense required a principal shared contractual purpose, substantial frustration beyond assumed risks, and an event defeating a basic assumption. The dealership agreement's stated purpose was establishment and governance of the dealer relationship, including sales and service. It was not simply mutual profitability. Treating profitability as every contract's principal purpose would empty the requirement of meaning. Stable markets and finances were not basic assumptions here. (Id. at 1119–1120.)

The relevant frustrating event was also IH's own decision to sell the assets and exit the business. Note 2 explains that sale without complying with termination procedures furnished an additional fault-based reason to reject discharge; economic pressure did not eliminate the choice. (Id. at 1120 & n. 2.)

## Product-line discretion and implied duration
Section 2 allowed IH to add or eliminate listed products, including discontinuing a line or lines, without responsibility to the dealer. IH argued that power to eliminate some lines necessarily permitted elimination of all. The majority rejected that interpretation: product flexibility did not create an alternative route to terminate the relationship, particularly when the same contract contained specific termination provisions. The court acknowledged contrary authority but adopted the reading preserving the agreement's binding force. (Id. at 1120–1121.)

IH also sought an implied term ending every dealership when the manufacturer ceased business. Michigan precedent permitted implied duration where termination provisions were absent, but declined to imply at-will termination where a contract specified termination for cause. Its observations about enterprises eventually ending did not authorize IH to bypass its negotiated procedures. The proposed implication would place all exit risk on the dealer. The directed verdict against that defense was affirmed. (Id. at 1121.)

## Independent debt judgment and preservation
The trial court initially found $253,839.69 owed to IH and required return and commercially reasonable disposition of equipment, including four mistakenly delivered tractors, with later calculation of surplus or deficiency. Wendt initially refused return pending application of the Michigan Farm and Utility Equipment Franchise Act. The trial court said the statute might affect the later deficiency rather than the return duty. After return and sale, Wendt proposed a $180,379.21 deficiency; IH agreed, and judgment entered in that stipulated amount. (Id. at 1114–1115.)

On appeal Wendt sought statutory repurchase credits. The majority held it had not timely renewed that statutory issue during the actual deficiency calculation. Its earlier objection to returning goods did not preserve the later calculation issue, and the entered amount was the amount Wendt itself proposed. The court therefore affirmed this judgment while remanding the distinct dealer-breach damages claim. (Id. at 1122.)

## Ryan's dissent
Ryan considered Michigan likely to adopt Restatement impracticability, but disagreed that the court could reject this evidence as a matter of law. No Michigan authority categorically excluded an unforeseen catastrophic market collapse. Viewed favorably to IH, the evidence showed a sudden nationwide industry collapse, two suppliers' bankruptcies, over $2 billion of IH losses in four years, and a business choice between bankruptcy and sale. Reasonable people could disagree whether this defeated a basic assumption. (Id. at 1122–1124.)

The majority's suggested alternative of consensual termination and sharing sale proceeds did not establish feasibility; assessing whether it occurred to management or could work belonged to the jury. Ryan would leave the verdict undisturbed rather than substitute appellate judgment about the severity of the crisis. His disagreement is an alternative application of the doctrine and review standard, not the controlling holding. (Id. at 1123–1124.)
