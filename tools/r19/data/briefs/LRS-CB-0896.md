## Parties

The Federal Communications Commission and other federal petitioners defended the Commission's indecency determinations and its explanation for changing enforcement policy. Fox Television Stations and affiliated broadcasters challenged findings concerning their broadcasts. Other broadcasters, including CBS, NBC, and ABC, participated in the appellate challenge. The performers whose words generated complaints were speakers in the broadcasts, not the litigants against whom this administrative review imposed personal liability. The case concerns the FCC's regulatory treatment of broadcasters. 556 U.S. at 510–513 (Part II).

## Material Facts

Federal law prohibited obscene, indecent, or profane radio communications. The regulatory regime described by the Court enforced broadcast indecency restrictions between 6 a.m. and 10 p.m.; the opinion's first footnote explains the litigation affecting the statute's broader midnight endpoint. The FCC's indecency definition addressed patently offensive descriptions of sexual or excretory organs or activities when children could be in the audience. After Pacifica, the agency generally distinguished literal descriptions from nonliteral expletives and treated repetition as necessary for actionable expletives. Its broader context analysis considered explicitness, repetition or dwelling, and whether presentation pandered, titillated, or shocked. 556 U.S. at 506–510.

In its 2004 Golden Globes Order, involving Bono's use of a word as an intensifier during an NBC broadcast, the Commission rejected the protection previously afforded fleeting expletives. It reasoned that the word's offensive force derived from its sexual meaning, that isolated usage could still be patently offensive, that categorical immunity could encourage more such broadcasts, and that improved delay technology made deletion easier. It acknowledged contrary earlier staff decisions and Commission statements, disavowed them, and declined to penalize NBC because the earlier policy had not provided adequate notice. That order supplied the policy background; the two broadcasts actually at issue here were Fox's 2002 and 2003 Billboard Music Awards. Id. at 508–512.

Cher used the relevant expletive to express hostility toward critics in 2002. Nicole Richie used both an excretory reference and an expletive during an exchange with Paris Hilton in 2003. Both broadcasts preceded the 2004 policy change. The agency emphasized the gratuitous and shocking language, the entertainment setting, and the substantial audience of children. Although the FCC asserted that aspects of these broadcasts also violated its former policy, it relied on the new policy to eliminate any doubt. Its operative order treated a lack of repetition as a factor against indecency, rather than an automatic exemption. The FCC imposed no forfeiture or other sanction for either Fox broadcast. Id. at 510–513.

## Procedural History

The FCC initially issued its determinations in a 2006 order addressing multiple broadcasts. Broadcasters sought review in the Second Circuit. Because the Commission had imposed no sanctions, the parties had not previously received an opportunity to respond to the particular findings; the agency therefore obtained a voluntary remand to consider their objections. Its Remand Order retained the two Fox indecency findings without assessing penalties. This agency remand occurred before the Supreme Court proceeding and must be distinguished from the later judicial disposition. 556 U.S. at 510–513.

Fox and other participants renewed their challenge. The Second Circuit set aside the orders because the Commission had inadequately explained its change of policy under the APA. Its majority expressed constitutional doubts but did not adjudicate the constitutional challenges. Judge Leval dissented below. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to review the adequacy of the agency's explanation, and reversed the Second Circuit. The Court did not take the further step of resolving the First Amendment challenges in the first instance. Id. at 513, 529–530.

## Issue

Does APA arbitrary-and-capricious review require a heightened justification whenever an agency changes an existing policy, and did the FCC adequately explain its abandonment of categorical protection for fleeting expletives? A distinct question was whether the Court should decide the constitutional validity of the orders despite the lower court's decision to resolve the case solely under the APA. The first questions were decided in the agency's favor; the constitutional questions were expressly reserved. 556 U.S. at 513–522, 529–530.

## Rule and Holding

Section 706(2)(A) requires reasoned decisionmaking and a satisfactory connection between relevant considerations and the agency's choice. A reviewing court does not substitute its preferred policy. A change of policy carries no universally heightened standard of review. The agency ordinarily must acknowledge the change, act within statutory authority, and give good reasons for the new policy. It need not persuade judges that those reasons are superior to the reasons for its old policy. 556 U.S. at 513–516.

The existing policy can nevertheless create additional explanatory obligations. Contradictory earlier factual findings and serious reliance interests must be addressed when relevant. An agency may not ignore them merely by announcing a new preference. Accordingly, the same standard may require a more detailed explanation in a changed factual or reliance setting. Id. at 515–516. Applying that standard, the Court held the FCC's explanation sufficient and its challenged orders neither arbitrary nor capricious. Its ruling concerned the APA explanation, not an adjudication that all applications of the new indecency regime complied with the First Amendment. Id. at 517–522, 529–530.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court reversed the Second Circuit's judgment and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. The broadcasters lost their claim that the FCC's explanation failed arbitrary-and-capricious review. The Court did not order payment of fines, because the FCC had imposed none, and it did not finally uphold the orders against the reserved constitutional objections. The remand permitted the litigation to continue on matters not resolved by this decision. 556 U.S. at 529–530 (Part IV and concluding disposition).

## Reasoning

1. The APA treats initial agency action and a later revision under the ordinary review standard. The Court read State Farm's comparison as addressing rescission versus a failure to act in the first instance. It did not read that decision as imposing a universally stronger burden whenever an agency replaced an earlier policy. The distinction between compelling unlawfully withheld action under § 706(1) and setting aside defective action under § 706(2) helped explain that reading. 556 U.S. at 513–515.

2. Continuity still matters to reasoned explanation. An agency cannot change silently, leave governing rules unexplained, or disregard facts and reliance produced by its prior position. The Court's refusal to rank old and new policies judicially therefore did not authorize unexplained inconsistency. The required explanation depends on the matters the agency actually must confront. A policy founded on contrary prior findings or generating serious reliance may demand more discussion than an initial choice. Id. at 515–516.

3. The FCC acknowledged its change. Its explicit rejection of prior Commission and staff positions, reliance on the Golden Globes Order, and refusal to impose penalties established that awareness. The Court considered its attempt to characterize Cher's broadcast as unlawful even under the old policy potentially unconvincing, but unnecessary to the decision. That questionable alternative account did not erase the clear acknowledgment and independently adequate explanation of the new policy. Id. at 517–518.

4. The agency gave reasons germane to its regulatory task. It could conclude that an expletive's offensive force retained a connection to its sexual meaning and that context-sensitive evaluation was appropriate even for a single utterance. Removing automatic immunity fit its broader refusal to adopt categorical exceptions. It could also predict that immunity for isolated words would encourage more frequent use of those words, while improved screening technology reduced the practical burden of avoiding their broadcast. These were agency judgments the Court found rational, not scientific facts independently established through a trial. Id. at 517–521.

5. The Court declined to demand unobtainable empirical proof. It distinguished failure to produce reasonably available data from a requirement to conduct unrealistic controlled studies of children's exposure to broadcast profanity. The agency could rely on children's tendency to imitate behavior and on Congress's regulatory judgment concerning indecent broadcasts. This discussion did not exempt agencies from examining relevant evidence generally; its rationale concerned the nature of the specific evidence demanded and the permissible predictive judgments involved. Id. at 518–521.

6. Context preserved distinctions among broadcasts. The policy did not automatically condemn every use of particular words. The agency could distinguish an awards show attracting children from other settings, including the film example discussed by the Court. A decision to retain case-specific discretion therefore did not itself establish inconsistency. Pacifica's narrow holding had left additional questions open; it had not created an administrative-law prohibition against any incremental expansion beyond its own facts. Id. at 519–522.

7. The Court separated administrative explanation from constitutional adjudication. It rejected using constitutional avoidance to impose a specially stringent arbitrary-and-capricious standard on otherwise authorized action. Constitutional legality could be challenged directly. Because the Second Circuit had not decided that question, the Court reserved it rather than treating adequate explanation as a constitutional safe harbor. Id. at 516–517, 529–530. The discussion in Part III-E of congressional pressure and small broadcasters is separately identified below because Kennedy did not join that portion.

## Separate Opinions

Voting alignment. Roberts, Kennedy, Thomas, and Alito joined Scalia's opinion in Parts I, II, III-A through III-D, and IV, producing five votes for the controlling analysis and reversal. Only Roberts, Thomas, and Alito joined Scalia in Part III-E. Its discussion of political influence, the institutional character of independent agencies, and some responses concerning local broadcasters therefore had four votes. It should not be quoted as an unqualified majority holding that political pressure alone supplies a lawful explanation. 556 U.S. at 522–529.

Kennedy, concurring in part and in the judgment. Kennedy stressed neutral and rational principles, the agency's legal mandate, its available data and experience, and serious consideration of prior factual findings. He agreed with Breyer that an agency sometimes must explain why it rejected considerations supporting the original policy. He distinguished State Farm because the FCC's earlier position rested on its reading of Pacifica rather than contrary industry findings. Although he did not regard the explanation as exemplary, he found it adequate and joined the Court's reservation of constitutional issues. Id. at 535–539.

Thomas, concurring. Thomas joined the administrative-law disposition while questioning the continued justification for the distinct constitutional treatment of broadcast speech in Red Lion and Pacifica. He criticized their textual foundation and their reliance on technological assumptions concerning scarcity and the special reach of broadcasting. He expressed willingness to reconsider those precedents in an appropriate case. This concurrence neither overruled them nor decided the constitutional question reserved here. Id. at 530–535.

Breyer, dissenting, joined by Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg. Breyer would affirm the Second Circuit and require the FCC to reconsider. He described his approach as applying the same reasoned-decisionmaking standard to the different circumstances created by a policy reversal. In his view the agency inadequately addressed two important matters: the constitutional caution underlying its former reading of Pacifica and the effects of the new regime on local broadcasters unable to afford delay systems. Repeating concerns about offensive language or children's exposure did not explain why the balance had changed. He also objected to accepting a litigation argument supplied by the Solicitor General rather than by the FCC itself. His alternative constitutional-avoidance discussion favored a remand for agency reconsideration, not an immediate holding that the entire regime was unconstitutional. Id. at 546–567 (Parts I–V).

Stevens, separately dissenting. Stevens joined Breyer and emphasized the FCC's institutional independence, regulatory stability, and the difference between emotionally expressive expletives and actual descriptions of sexual or excretory activity. He disputed the FCC's reading of Pacifica and would affirm. His conception of delegated legislative authority and congressional agency must remain distinct from Scalia's four-Justice response. Id. at 539–544.

Ginsburg, separately dissenting. Ginsburg joined Breyer and emphasized the unresolved First Amendment stakes. She distinguished Pacifica's repeated monologue from fleeting emotional expression and stressed that words offensive to some speakers may be ordinary expression to others. Her writing underscored the narrowness of the precedent and the constitutional question still ahead, without supplying a separate majority disposition. Id. at 544–546.

## Full-opinion footnotes and additional qualifications

The eight notes in Scalia’s opinion were read with their continuations. Note 1 explains why the operative indecency window ends at 10 p.m. despite the statute’s midnight endpoint. Note 2 distinguishes the State Farm rescission/nonaction comparison from universal heightened scrutiny. Note 3 separates interpretation of an ambiguous statute under constitutional avoidance from a proposed remand asking the agency to reconsider its own policy in light of constitutional doubts. These last two notes continue across reporter pages 514–515 and 516–517. 556 U.S. at 506 n.1,514–515 n.2,516–517 n.3.

Notes 4–8 belong to Part III-E, joined by four Justices. Note 4 recounts two 2004 congressional oversight hearings and the subsequent Golden Globes Order. Note 5 distinguishes a committee’s oversight wishes from enacted congressional intent; note 6 explains Congress’s APA exclusion. Note 7 expressly denies assuming that Pacifica decided the constitutional validity of the policy now at issue, while recognizing that congressional oversight cannot enlarge the statutory grant. Note 8 rejects importing every notice-and-comment procedural requirement into review of adjudication. These observations do not amount to a five-Justice holding that political pressure alone supplies adequate statutory reasons. Id. at 523–529 nn.4–8.

Thomas’s single starred footnote discusses changed technology for parental blocking of television programs; it supports his willingness to reconsider precedent, rather than announcing an overruling. Kennedy has no judicial footnotes. Stevens’s five notes reproduce the criminal statute, develop his delegated-legislative-power position, contest the evidentiary significance of committee hearings, illustrate his literal/nonliteral distinction, and acknowledge technological change while favoring less expansive indecency regulation. His notes 2 and 3 continue over 540–542. Ginsburg’s starred note preserves the unresolved constitutional questions below and describes the appellate skepticism. Breyer’s full dissent contains no separately numbered judicial notes; its substantial authorities are inline. The counsel/amicus asterisk on 504–505 was also read. Id. at 534*, 539–544 nn.1–5,545*, 546–567.

Scalia’s four-vote response thought local broadcasters’ risk overstated and considered the FCC’s contextual safeguards adequate. Breyer disputed those assurances, stressing that there was no categorical news exemption and that possible equitable treatment did not clearly protect stations unable to afford equipment. Neither writing represents a finding of particular future violations by a named small broadcaster. The dissent’s references to large possible fines address the regulatory risk; this judgment assessed no Fox fine. Id. at 527–529,556–562.

Breyer did not require controlled empirical studies in every case. He faulted the agency for failing to confront existing contrary evidence and considerations already known when it adopted the prior policy. His concern about the Solicitor General’s argument based on the statutory word “any” invokes Chenery: that rationale came from counsel rather than the agency. His constitutional-avoidance alternative would ask the FCC to reconsider, not immediately invalidate the entire policy. Id. at 563–567.

## Complete assigned editorial notes

Note 1, “Subsequent History,” identifies the 2010 Second Circuit vagueness ruling and the 2012 Supreme Court’s as-applied lack-of-notice disposition. It expressly leaves the broader First Amendment issue open. That editorial history concerns later proceedings and does not turn this 2009 APA reversal into a constitutional ruling or count as a fresh complete audit of either later opinion. Assigned 905–906.

Note 2, “Agency Reversing Course,” compares State Farm and Fox and describes later courts recognizing flexibility while requiring an intentional, acknowledged, reasoned departure. It cites Modesto Irrigation, Westar Energy, Philip Morris, Dillmon and National Association of Home Builders. These are attributed casebook comparisons; silent inconsistency is not authorized merely because Fox rejected a universal heightened standard. Assigned 906.

Note 3, “The Role of Political Explanations,” contrasts Scalia’s four-vote discussion with Breyer’s dissent. It distinguishes political oversight justified by statutory considerations from pressure to use considerations outside the agency’s mandate, and distinguishes ideological preferences or priorities consistent with the statute. Its Sierra Club v. Costle comparison and Mashaw discussion are editorial analysis, not an additional holding of Fox. Assigned 906.

Note 4, “Constitutional Considerations in Agency Reasoning,” compares the majority’s refusal of specially stringent APA review with Stevens’s objection to an unacknowledged departure from Pacifica and Breyer’s important-aspect reasoning. The courts retain responsibility for deciding actual constitutional challenges. The Metzger article is a scholarly argument about the connection between administrative and constitutional law, not doctrine adopted in this decision. Assigned 907.

Note 5, “Substance or Process?,” describes Breyer’s focus on the agency’s reasoning process, then questions the line between ensuring that a consideration was addressed and assigning it substantive weight. Its discussion of local broadcasting and judicial skepticism is a teaching question, not a rule giving courts free authority to substitute their preferred regulatory balance. Assigned 907.

Note 6, “DACA Revisited,” runs 907–911. Its later Regents account links Fox’s reliance principle with State Farm’s alternative-policy inquiry and Chenery’s limits on post hoc reasons. It describes reviewability where the program provides a structured process and benefits beyond simple nonenforcement; the distinction between elaborating an original reason and adding new reasons; failure to consider forbearance without benefits; and failure to examine serious reliance interests. Disclaimers of substantive rights could affect the strength of reliance, but did not substitute for agency consideration. The note separately attributes Thomas’s illegality-based objection and Kavanaugh’s view that Nielsen supplied an agency explanation or new rule. It also mentions Sotomayor’s separate equal-protection position. This is the complete assigned later-case account, not a substitute for the separate full Regents audit.
