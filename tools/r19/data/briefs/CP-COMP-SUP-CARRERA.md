# E.M.D. Sales, Inc. v. Carrera

**604 U.S. 45 (2025) · Supreme Court of the United States · January 15, 2025**

## A dispute about the employer’s burden of proof

EMD distributed international food products in the Washington, D.C., metropolitan area. Its sales representatives managed inventory and took orders at grocery stores stocking its products. Several representatives sued in the District of Maryland for unpaid overtime under the Fair Labor Standards Act. EMD did not deny that they worked more than forty hours per week without overtime compensation. It defended on the ground that they fell within the Act’s outside-salesman exemption. 604 U.S. at 48.

The Act ordinarily requires minimum wages and overtime compensation for covered employees, but exempts specified categories of workers. An employer bears the burden of establishing an exemption. The exemption at issue covers an employee working in the capacity of an outside salesman; the applicable regulatory definition concerns making sales as the employee’s primary duty and regularly working away from the employer’s place of business. The Supreme Court’s case concerned the degree of proof needed to establish that exemption, rather than a new definition of outside sales work. Id. at 47–48.

After a bench trial, the district court concluded that EMD had not established the exemption by clear and convincing evidence. It found that the employees primarily carried out the terms of sales already made rather than making new sales themselves, and ordered overtime wages and liquidated damages. EMD argued on appeal that the court should have applied the less demanding preponderance standard. The Fourth Circuit followed its own precedent requiring clear and convincing evidence and affirmed. Although the panel suggested reconsideration by the full court, en banc review was denied. The Fourth Circuit’s approach conflicted with every other court of appeals that had addressed the question. Id. at 48–49.

## Question and holding

What standard of proof must an employer satisfy to show that an employee is exempt from the FLSA’s minimum-wage or overtime-pay requirements?

The usual preponderance-of-the-evidence standard applies. The burden remains on the employer; the decision changes the degree of proof required, rather than shifting that burden to the employee. It also does not establish that these particular representatives actually qualified for the exemption. Id. at 48, 52, 54.

## Why the ordinary civil standard governs

Justice Kavanaugh’s opinion began with the established background rule. When Congress enacted the FLSA in 1938, preponderance was the default standard in American civil litigation, and it remained the default. That standard allocates the risk of error roughly equally between the parties. Courts ordinarily apply it when a civil statute does not specify a different standard. Id. at 49–50, 52.

The Court identified three principal circumstances in which its precedents have required heightened proof. First, Congress can prescribe a heightened standard, including by adopting a term with an established common-law meaning carrying such a standard. Second, the Constitution can require heightened proof, as in specified First Amendment cases, involuntary civil commitment, and termination of parental rights. Third, heightened proof has applied in certain uncommon cases involving unusually coercive government action against an individual, such as expatriation or denaturalization. These examples concern the grounds for departing from the default; they do not make every consequential civil dispute an exception. Id. at 50–51.

None of those grounds applied here. The FLSA specifies no heightened standard for establishing exemptions. The employees acknowledged both that statutory silence and the absence of a constitutional right requiring greater proof. Nor did this private wage litigation involve unusual coercive action by the government. The Court compared it to Title VII employment-discrimination litigation, where preponderance applies despite the importance of the rights involved. Id. at 52.

The employees’ policy arguments did not supply a different legal rule. Their reliance on the public interest in fair wages was insufficient because other employment laws also protect important public interests without requiring clear and convincing evidence. Moreover, the public interest was not entirely on one side: the statute reflected competing interests, and the Court’s task was to apply the enacted law against its established legal background. Id. at 52–53.

The Court also separated nonwaivability from the standard of proof. A right’s protection against waiver does not determine the evidentiary standard used to adjudicate it. Criminal defendants can waive trial despite the prosecution’s demanding burden; some nonwaivable labor rights nevertheless coexist with defenses proved by a preponderance. Thus even assuming some FLSA rights could not be waived, that premise did not establish a heightened burden for proving exemptions. Id. at 53.

Finally, the employer’s control of much of the relevant evidence and the possibility that employees had low incomes did not distinguish this case from Title VII litigation, where comparable circumstances existed but the ordinary civil standard still governed. Id. at 53–54.

## Gorsuch’s concurrence

Justice Gorsuch, joined by Justice Thomas, concurred and joined the Court’s opinion. He emphasized that a court identifying an unstated standard should examine the legal background against which Congress legislated. Preponderance is usually the civil default, but an established common-law rule can occasionally require heightened proof. Courts apply the relevant default unless Congress changes it or the Constitution forbids it. His point therefore cautioned against turning the result into an inflexible rule that statutory silence always requires preponderance regardless of the specific legal background. Id. at 54–55.

The Court was unanimous. Kavanaugh wrote its opinion; Gorsuch’s two-Justice concurrence supplied the only separate writing. There was no dissent and no numbered judicial footnote in either opinion. The published report includes an asterisked counsel and amici note, which does not supply a separate judicial rationale.

## Exact judgment and assigned context

The Supreme Court reversed the Fourth Circuit’s judgment and remanded for further proceedings. The employees asked it to affirm on the alternative ground that they would not qualify as outside salesmen even under a preponderance standard. The Court left that contention for consideration on remand. EMD therefore prevailed on the governing standard of proof, rather than receiving a Supreme Court determination that it owed no overtime or damages. Id. at 54.

The assigned Civil Procedure supplement, printed pages 511–512, presents a captioned editorial account and excerpts of the default rule and three exceptional categories. The complete opinion adds the actual workplace facts, lower-court findings, allocation of the burden, rejection of the employees’ policy arguments, express reservation of the exemption’s application, and Gorsuch’s concurrence. The account is useful both as an FLSA decision and as an illustration of how a court chooses an evidentiary standard when a civil statute is silent.
