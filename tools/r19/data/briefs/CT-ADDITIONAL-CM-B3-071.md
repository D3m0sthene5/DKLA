# Dillingham Commercial Company, Inc. v. Spears
641 P.2d 1 (Alaska 1982) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Chief Justice Rabinowitz wrote the Alaska Supreme Court’s February 19, 1982 opinion, with Connor, Burke, Matthews, and Compton on the panel and no separate writing. The superior court had granted Dillingham summary judgment compelling performance of a lease’s purchase option. It also required Dillingham to pay Spears $20,016.50 in purchase-price interest and $6,000 for insurance premiums, including interest, while awarding Dillingham $6,710 in attorney’s fees and $1,737.52 in costs. Spears challenged specific performance; Dillingham cross-appealed the financial conditions and fees. The Supreme Court affirmed the judgment in its entirety. (1–2, 11.)

## Lease, defaults, and efforts to buy
An earnest-money agreement dated January 15, 1967 provided for sale of Spears’s general-store business, a ten-year property lease, and an option to purchase the property. The March 1 lease required monthly rent of $600 and payment of all taxes and assessments on the premises. The option was exercisable during the lease term so long as the lease was not in default, on one month’s mailed notice and payment of $20,000 toward a $79,800 purchase price. Spears approved assignment of the lease to Dillingham shortly after occupancy began. (2.)

Tax arrears became an express dispute in October 1975, when Spears demanded their payment within ten days and threatened foreclosure on the lease. Dillingham paid the city $16,239.61 that November, but the city advised that additional interest, foreclosure costs, and personal-property taxes remained. Spears’s March 9, 1976 letter again demanded cure, citing delinquent taxes, insurance deficiencies, and late rent. On March 22 Dillingham gave notice of its intention to exercise the option, offering the required $20,000 on May 1. Spears responded that all defaults first had to be cured; Dillingham threatened a specific-performance action. (2–3.)

Further tax demands followed. On June 18 Spears purported to terminate both the lease and option for nonpayment of real-estate taxes, and she then paid the city herself. Dillingham paid further property and personal-property taxes in July and tendered reimbursement to Spears, who refused it and offered continued occupancy only at $1,200 monthly on a month-to-month basis. On August 12 Dillingham again tendered reimbursement, July and August rent, and the $20,000 down payment. Spears refused, and Dillingham sued for specific performance. The court distinguished alleged termination of the lease from the separate question whether defaults prevented exercise of an otherwise surviving option. (3–5.)

## Spears’s notice did not itself terminate the lease
The lease gave Spears remedies available under Alaska law but did not specially authorize termination by unilateral notice for breach. Under Klinger, absent such a special agreement, early termination required the prescribed judicial action and decree. Spears had brought no such action. Language about the tenant’s rights lapsing at expiration “or otherwise,” and language preserving a privilege to terminate despite prior leniency, did not confer a new right to terminate by notice. The former reasonably referred to judicial termination; the latter protected rights that otherwise existed. Thus the June notice had not ended the lease. That conclusion did not eliminate the option’s express no-default condition, which required separate examination. (4–6 & nn. 5–6.)

## Equity relieved the tax and rent defaults
The court upheld relief against loss of the purchase option over the tax arrears. The taxes had been paid and Spears had been offered reimbursement. Dillingham had also paid substantial rent over nine years, with the lease approaching its end. The superior court properly considered these amounts and the parties’ financial consequences in deciding that forfeiture would be inequitable. Alaska’s installment-land-contract decisions supported equitable discretion, but did not make forfeiture impossible in every case: Curry had sustained it in extreme circumstances. Footnote 7 expressly distinguished a purchaser’s equitable ownership under an installment sale from an option holder’s contractual right, while finding no reason for different equitable treatment in this case. (6–7 & n. 7.)

Rent had been late virtually every month. Spears had accepted those payments without objection and asserted the lateness as an obstacle to purchase only when Dillingham sought to exercise the option. The lease required written waivers and said that delay, omission, or waiver of one default would not waive another. Nevertheless, such a clause did not invariably prevent waiver by conduct. Spears’s prolonged acquiescence constituted waiver of the right to claim default from those late payments. The court also independently sustained equitable relief against forfeiture for the late rent. (7–8 & nn. 8–10.)

The decision did not find that every installment had been paid. Viewing the summary-judgment record favorably to Spears, the court assumed two months remained unpaid. In relation to the substantial rent and taxes already paid, and the long absence of objection, that default likewise did not justify forfeiture. Spears’s additional sublease and insurance grounds were not developed in her appellate argument and therefore were waived on appeal; footnote 12 also recounted the trial court’s earlier abandonment, seriousness, and monetary-cure reasons. The appellate waiver ruling should not be turned into an unrestricted merits rule about those covenants. (8 & nn. 11–12.)

## The sale terms were sufficiently established
Spears argued alternatively that unresolved terms required a trial because contemplated deed-of-trust documents had never been prepared and placed in escrow. The superior court had used the original earnest-money agreement’s monthly-payment and interest terms as part of the parties’ total agreement, insofar as consistent with the lease. Spears offered no contrary evidence. The Supreme Court upheld that treatment, rather than holding that a court could invent essential sale terms. Her quotation from Jackson omitted its reference to a disputed oral contract; here an existing written agreement and its established terms left no such factual issue for trial. (8–9 & n. 13.)

## Fees, insurance, and purchase-price interest
The superior court mistakenly used the Rule 82 money-judgment fee schedule as though Dillingham had recovered the $79,800 purchase price. Specific performance had produced no money judgment. Dillingham’s argument that the property’s assessed value was much higher did not cure that mistaken premise. Nevertheless, the $6,710 fee was reasonable and not a clear abuse of discretion; a remand merely to reach the same proper result by the correct route served no purpose. Dillingham abandoned its challenge to the insurance reimbursement in its reply and oral argument. The affirmance does not supply a new merits analysis of that abandoned issue. (9–10 & nn. 14–17.)

Specific performance also required equitable accounting. Had the sale occurred on time, the purchaser would have enjoyed the property and its income while the seller would have had the purchase money. Accounting therefore paired the purchaser’s rents and profits with interest owed to the seller on unpaid purchase money. Dillingham had remained in possession throughout. Its tender and Spears’s wrongful refusal did not entitle it to retain both the property’s benefits and the use of the money free of interest. The court upheld the $20,016.50 interest charge on the full price from August 21, 1976. (10–11 & nn. 18–20.)

Dillingham also sought credit for forty-three $600 payments deposited in trust under a court order. The record did not clearly establish their intended character. The court considered continuing rent during possession the most plausible explanation, and the accumulated $25,800 did not represent a frozen tender of purchase money or full option performance. It therefore afforded no deduction from the interest due. The successful specific-performance claim consequently did not relieve Dillingham of the affirmed financial obligations. (11 & n. 21.)

## Assigned account and source scope
The assigned note on printed page 869 uses this decision as an example of conduct overcoming an anti-waiver clause. That narrow account is accurate, but the full opinion separately addresses termination, equitable relief from option default, established sale terms, and financial accounting. The complete judicial opinion and all twenty-one notes were read in the preserved reproduction and original native report, with the panel, final judgment and notes, and complete assigned page checked visually. There is no separate writing. The report’s heading dates option exercise August 17, while its factual narrative gives the tender as August 12; this brief follows the narrative and does not reconcile that internal discrepancy by inventing an event.
