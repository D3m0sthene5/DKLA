# Texaco, Inc. v. Pennzoil Co.
729 S.W.2d 768 (Tex. App.—Houston [1st Dist.] 1987) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
Justice Warren wrote for a three-judge panel consisting of Warren, Jack Smith, and Sam Bass. The court reviewed a Texas jury judgment against Texaco for knowingly interfering with Pennzoil’s agreement with Getty Oil, the Sarah C. Getty Trust, and the J. Paul Getty Museum. The jury awarded $7.53 billion in compensation and $3 billion in punitive damages. The parties agreed that New York substantive law and Texas procedural law governed. This was an appeal from an actual trial judgment, not a preliminary determination that Pennzoil had merely alleged a contract. (784–787.)

The court rejected Texaco’s challenges to formation, knowing interference, the charge, evidentiary rulings, trial fairness, constitutional objections, and compensatory damages. It nevertheless sustained the punitive-damages remittitur point. Its precise disposition was conditional: if Pennzoil filed a $2 billion remittitur within thirty days, the judgment would be reformed and affirmed for $7.53 billion compensatory and $1 billion exemplary damages; otherwise, it would be reversed and remanded. The opinion also refused to certify the judgment’s propriety under New York law to the Texas Supreme Court. The preserved report notes subsequent denials of rehearing, but supplies no additional rehearing opinion. (768, 864–866.)

## Facts and bargaining chronology
Gordon Getty, as trustee, controlled a block of approximately 40.2 percent of Getty Oil’s shares; the Museum held approximately 11.8 percent. Disagreements between Gordon and Getty Oil management helped create an opportunity for a transaction that would give Gordon and Pennzoil control and potentially divide the company’s assets. Pennzoil began with a public tender offer at $100 per share in late December 1983. A memorandum negotiated around January 1–2 proposed a different arrangement, including acquisition of the Museum’s shares, Pennzoil’s acquisition of a three-sevenths interest, and the Trust’s retention of four-sevenths. If Pennzoil and the Trust could not agree on restructuring Getty Oil by December 31, 1984, the arrangement contemplated a division of assets according to their respective interests. Gordon, the Museum, and Pennzoil signed the memorandum; Getty Oil did not. Getty Oil’s board initially rejected the proposed price. (784–786.)

Price bargaining continued. On January 3 Pennzoil proposed $110 per share plus a contingent payment associated with Getty Oil’s ERC interest. The Museum’s lawyer indicated acceptance of $110 plus a $5 deferred payment if the transaction were firm. Getty Oil’s board voted fifteen to one for a proposal involving that $5 payment. During a recess, Pennzoil’s chief executive Hugh Liedtke accepted the counterproposal, and acceptance was communicated to the board before it adjourned. The participants also addressed management protections, and Pennzoil relied on congratulations and subsequent public announcements as manifestations that agreement had been reached. Getty’s January 4 press release described an agreement in principle, subject to execution of a definitive merger agreement, shareholder approval, and regulatory requirements. Lawyers continued preparing formal implementation documents. Whether those documents were conditions to any obligation or formalities implementing an existing obligation became the central dispute. (785–795.)

Meanwhile, Getty Oil’s investment banker solicited other bidders. Texaco’s advisers assessed how to stop the Pennzoil transaction, identifying the Museum as a crucial initial target and Gordon’s concerns about retaining control as another source of leverage. Texaco offered a higher price, obtained agreements with the Museum and the Trust, and arranged a merger with Getty Oil. The Museum required protection against claims arising from the Pennzoil transaction and refused assurances that its Texaco agreement breached no other agreement. Getty Oil withdrew from the Pennzoil arrangement and proceeded with Texaco. Pennzoil pursued litigation and eventually obtained the Texas judgment under review. These events were extensively disputed at trial; the appellate court assessed whether the evidence supported the jury’s findings rather than resolving every conflict as an original factfinder. (786–787, 796–805.)

## Parties’ positions and controlling questions
Pennzoil contended that the parties objectively intended to bind themselves at the end of the January 3 board meeting, that all essential terms were sufficiently definite, and that subsequent drafting concerned implementation. It further maintained that Texaco knew the facts constituting the agreement and actively induced its breach through a higher competing bid, indemnification, and pressure on the Getty participants. Texaco answered that the memorandum lacked Getty Oil’s signature, the public announcement reserved formal execution, important terms remained unsettled, and the complex transaction ordinarily required a definitive signed agreement. It also denied actual knowledge of an existing obligation and emphasized assurances from lawyers and Getty participants that Pennzoil had no binding contract. (788–805.)

Texaco separately attacked the agreement’s validity, the jury instructions, numerous evidentiary rulings, the judges’ impartiality and qualifications, alleged jury misconduct, and the size and legal basis of damages. These objections did not all receive merits decisions: some were waived through deficient pleading, lack of a requested finding, untimely objections, or new appellate arguments. Keeping those dispositions separate matters because the opinion did not affirmatively decide every suggested defense against Texaco. (805–858.)

## Formation before formal execution
Under the New York law the court applied, intent to be bound depends on objective manifestations in context. An express reservation against obligation before execution can be decisive; absent such a reservation, the anticipation of a later writing does not necessarily prevent present agreement. The court organized its inquiry around four considerations: an express reservation; partial performance; agreement on essential terms; and whether the transaction is of a type ordinarily committed to a formal writing. No single consideration automatically controlled this record. (788–789.)

First, neither the memorandum nor the press announcement conclusively reserved all legal obligation until formal execution. The memorandum’s approval condition concerned Getty Oil’s board, whose January 3 action was the alleged acceptance. The announcement’s reference to a transaction subject to a definitive merger agreement could describe conditions to consummation rather than to formation. Draft clauses concerning execution had to be considered with other language expressing present commitments. The phrase “agreement in principle” was also contested in its business meaning. The jury could choose between the parties’ competing contextual interpretations. (789–792.)

Second, there was little partial performance beyond Pennzoil’s preliminary financing arrangements. The court did not treat this as substantial completed joint performance. But only a short interval separated the claimed agreement and Getty’s withdrawal, making nonperformance less persuasive than in a transaction left unperformed for a prolonged period. (792–793.)

Third, the court found evidence supporting agreement on the essentials: ownership proportions, price, the Museum’s sale, and the restructuring-or-division framework. A proposed change in who would purchase the Museum’s stock responded to tax concerns and could be implementation rather than an unresolved prerequisite. The court likewise treated disputes over the deferred ERC payment, dividend mechanics, a restored price-protection clause, and employee provisions as matters the jury could evaluate against the parties’ contemporaneous manifestations. Changes in a later draft did not themselves establish that no contract had existed. (793–795.)

Fourth, the transaction’s magnitude and complexity favored Texaco’s expectation of a formal signed document. That consideration was substantial but not conclusive: the memorandum, participants’ signatures, board action, and subsequent conduct still permitted the jury to find present assent. The court consequently sustained the finding of an agreement and rejected the separate claim of indefiniteness. Reasonable certainty must permit recognition of breach and a remedy; every implementation detail need not have been spelled out. (795–796.)

## Knowing interference and validity defenses
Knowing interference required knowledge of the facts giving rise to the contractual duty, not an accurate legal conclusion that those facts created an enforceable contract or familiarity with every detail. Accordingly, Texaco could not defeat liability simply by showing that its lawyers described Pennzoil’s arrangement as nonbinding. The jury could weigh those assurances against the documents, public announcements, transaction analysis, contemporaneous communications, indemnity demands, and efforts to prevent the Pennzoil deal from proceeding. The court found legally and factually sufficient circumstantial evidence of knowledge. (796–803.)

Inducement also required more than merely signing an agreement with someone known to have another contract. The evidence supported active persuasion: Texaco planned the sequence of obtaining the Museum and Trust commitments and offered favorable terms and protections that helped draw the Getty participants away from Pennzoil. Getty’s prior dissatisfaction or solicitation of other bids did not necessarily eliminate Texaco’s causal role. Competition and pursuit of one’s own economic advantage did not privilege interference with the existing, nonterminable contract the jury found. (803–805, 829–831.)

Texaco bore the procedural burden of properly raising its affirmative invalidity defenses. Mutual mistake and the statute of frauds failed through pleading and submission defects; the court did not hold on the merits that the unsigned memorandum independently satisfied every statute-of-frauds requirement. The SEC-rule argument was also narrower than a general certification of compliance. The rule concerned outside purchases during an outstanding tender offer, protected the tendering shareholders, and allowed an exemption. On this record Texaco had not established an automatically void agreement that it could invoke to escape liability. The court rejected the fiduciary-duty attack as well: Gordon’s large shareholding did not conclusively establish legal control, the board had valuation evidence, and a later superior bid did not itself prove that the earlier bargain violated fiduciary duties. Its discussion distinguished Revlon on the sequence and circumstances of the competing bids. (805–809, 829–831.)

## Instructions, evidence, and trial objections
The court upheld broad-form submission of intent to be bound and knowing interference. The charge, read as a whole, did not assume a contract, omit the relevant knowledge element, or impose liability merely for negligent failure to investigate. Its knowledge instruction included intentional or willful refusal to ascertain facts or bad faith, which the court distinguished from negligent ignorance. The good-faith instruction concerned performance and implementation after an agreement was found; it did not convert this suit into liability for interference with merely prospective relations. Several objections to language, balance, or emphasis were unpreserved or inadequately briefed. (809–837.)

The evidentiary rulings were assessed for harmful error, not defended indiscriminately. Some business-experience and perception testimony was admissible under the opinion rules. The court regarded several challenged documents or statements as inadmissible or erroneously admitted, but found no reversible harm because the evidence was cumulative, consistent testimony appeared elsewhere, or Texaco itself introduced or used the same material. It also rejected exclusion arguments involving the later SEC discussion, the Delaware pleading, and legal advice, applying relevance, consistency, and cumulative-evidence reasoning. Judicial notes 1 and 2 reproduce the opinion-testimony rule and the relevant SEC restriction. (837–842 & nn.1–2.)

The court also rejected challenges arising from a lawyer’s campaign contribution to Judge Farris, replacement of the ill judge by Judge Casseb, denial of a new-trial hearing, alleged jury misconduct, and Casseb’s retirement qualifications. It found no demonstrated actual bias or pecuniary interest, no prejudicial substitution, and no sufficiently supported outside influence or concealment requiring the requested hearing. A collateral recusal proceeding was not the proper method for attacking the certified retired judge’s authority; the court required quo warranto for that challenge. These are the court’s historical conclusions under the rules and precedents it applied, not a certification of present judicial-disqualification doctrine. The supremacy, commerce, and due-process attacks on the judgment were untimely; the full-faith-and-credit argument failed because the court had applied the governing New York law. (842–859.)

## Damages and conditional disposition
For intentional interference with an existing contract, the court allowed the pecuniary loss of contractual benefits and applicable consequential losses under tort principles. The bargain was intended to obtain access to Getty’s oil reserves, not simply trade a minority stock position. Pennzoil’s experts supported a replacement-cost model: 1.008 billion barrels of oil equivalent multiplied by the $7.47 difference between an estimated $10.87 replacement cost and the $3.40 acquisition cost under the Getty bargain, producing approximately $7.53 billion. Texaco cross-examined the experts but supplied no competing expert testimony. The court upheld the jury’s use of this evidence despite disputes over crude quality, the five-year cost period, development expenses, taxes, and eventual asset division. It treated the lost interest as a present loss and additionally found the requested present-value instruction unpreserved. It upheld prejudgment interest under the New York rule governing recovery for inducing breach. (835, 859–863.)

Punitive damages were legally available on the jury’s finding of intentional, willful, wanton disregard. Economic self-interest, absence of personal hatred, and reliance on counsel were not categorical defenses. Advice of counsel was a relevant factor the jury could consider; the court rejected an instruction that would have made it an automatic bar. The jury’s $3 billion award was not excessive simply because of its approximately forty-percent ratio to compensation. Nevertheless, considering the conduct, deterrence, and the more conservative New York approach, the court concluded that $1 billion sufficed and $2 billion was excessive. That conclusion produced the thirty-day conditional remittitur, not an unconditional appellate award or dismissal. (836–837, 863–866.)
