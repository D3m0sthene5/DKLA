# Spiklevitz v. The Markmil Corporation
136 Mich. App. 587, 357 N.W.2d 721 (1984) | Contracts

## Decision and posture
On August 7, 1984, a unanimous Michigan Court of Appeals panel reversed the limitations-based dismissal of a creditor's claim against purchasers who had promised the sellers to pay their listed debts. The creditor was an intended third-party beneficiary of that new undertaking. Its limitations period began on the new contract, rather than running exclusively from maturity of the sellers' original note. The per curiam panel consisted of Shepherd, P.J., Allen, and assigned circuit judge A. E. Keyes. It reversed and remanded to the district court, with costs to the appellant; it did not enter a final damages award. (587–589, 593; assignment designation at 588.)

## Loan, business sale, and assumption agreement
Spiklevitz loaned money to Vincent P. and Geraldine B. Heron in 1974. Their July 5 note promised $4,800 by January 15, 1975. On April 1, 1980, the Herons sold their business to the defendants. The narrative states that $3,510 remained unpaid, while the attached creditor schedule lists Spiklevitz's debt as $3,510.07. Those are descriptions of the underlying debt, rather than two different judicial awards. (589.)

As part of the purchase price for the building, business, and interest in the Class C license for Vince's Lounge in Detroit, the purchasers executed an assumption agreement. They agreed to pay the obligations on Exhibit A, to hold Vincent Heron harmless, and to indemnify him for liability on those obligations and related litigation expense. Exhibit A expressly listed Spiklevitz. The affirmative payment promise was therefore part of the exchange for the purchase, not an unsupported promise independently made to the creditor. The indemnity protections for Heron did not erase the undertaking to pay the listed creditors. (589.)

Spiklevitz sued the purchasers in district court on June 21, 1981. They asserted that the original note was time-barred and denied that he could enforce their assumption agreement. The district judge treated the original note as the underlying source of the claim, reasoned that the assumption created no additional rights for Spiklevitz, and allowed defendants the original obligors' limitations defense. The judge also observed that the Herons remained obligated on the note. The circuit court affirmed, treating the arrangement as an assignment because recourse against the original obligors remained possible and concluding that beneficiary status had not been intended. The Court of Appeals granted leave to review that affirmance. It noted that the trial disposition, labeled summary judgment, should have been designated accelerated judgment. (589–590.)

## A new promise to pay creates a separately enforceable obligation
The appellate court began with the rule applicable when someone assumes another person's past-due debt before that debt becomes barred by limitations. As between the new promisor and creditor, the relevant period runs from the new undertaking, rather than the original maturity date. The new agreement creates a separate obligation; the court's general discussion says a right of action accrues when that obligation becomes overdue. For this agreement, the court ultimately specified April 1, 1980, its execution date, as the beginning of the period. (591, 593.)

That distinction defeated the lower courts' treatment of the purchasers as merely receiving rights and defenses associated with the old note. The creditor sought to enforce the purchasers' promise made in acquiring the business. His claim did not depend on a release of the original borrowers. Continued recourse against the Herons therefore did not establish that no new obligation existed or that the new promisors necessarily inherited the earlier accrual date. (590–593.)

Michigan's beneficiary statute, MCL 600.1405, permits a person for whose benefit a contractual promise is made to enforce it as if the promise had been made directly to that person. Subsection (1) treats a promise as benefiting a person when the promisor undertakes to give, do, or refrain from doing something directly to or for that person. A business purchaser's promise to discharge specified debts to the seller's creditors is the ordinary creditor-beneficiary arrangement. Spiklevitz's express listing and defendants' promise to pay supplied that relationship. (592.)

The claim that Spiklevitz received no additional rights assumed the disputed conclusion. If the payment promise was enforceable, it supplied additional security; if he was not a beneficiary, it did not. The court refused to use that circular formulation as an initial test of intended-beneficiary status. It held that Spiklevitz was a beneficiary and that the claim on the April 1980 undertaking was timely. (592–593.)

## What the assigned problem changes
The problem at printed page 1174 substitutes a florist, a purchaser, and the seller's mother. It expressly says that the mother's loan was already barred when the business was sold. That is a material change: the actual opinion's stated rule at page 591 concerns a past-due debt that was not yet barred when the new promise was made. The court did not decide that every promise to assume an already-barred debt necessarily revives it for a creditor beneficiary. Its holding supports analyzing the purchaser's separate promise and intended beneficiaries; its express temporal premise must still be addressed before transferring that holding to the assigned variation. The problem's proposed motives for listing the mother's debt are questions for analysis, not factual findings about the Herons.
