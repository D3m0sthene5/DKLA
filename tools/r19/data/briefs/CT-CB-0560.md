# Trident Center v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co.
847 F.2d 564 (9th Cir. 1988) | Contracts | California interpretation law, lender remedies, and sanctions

## A fixed-rate commercial loan became expensive

In 1983 Security First Life Insurance Company and the Los Angeles law firms Mitchell, Silberberg & Knupp and Manatt, Phelps, Rothenberg & Tunney formed Trident Center, a limited partnership, to build an office complex on Olympic Boulevard in West Los Angeles. Connecticut General financed it with a $56.5 million loan at 12¼% interest for fifteen years, secured by a deed of trust. The sophisticated parties negotiated at arm's length from approximately equal bargaining positions. Their detailed documents prohibited the borrower from prepaying principal in whole or part during the first twelve years, through January 1996. Prepayment in years thirteen through fifteen carried a declining fee. Default in the first twelve years gave the lender an option to accelerate and add a 10% prepayment fee. (847 F.2d at 565–566.)

By 1987 market rates had fallen, making refinancing attractive. Connecticut General declined to permit early prepayment. Trident sued in state court for a declaration allowing immediate repayment with a 10% fee. The lender removed and moved to dismiss because the documents expressly forbade voluntary prepayment during the protected period. The district court dismissed and, on its own initiative, sanctioned Trident for filing what it considered a frivolous action. Trident appealed both rulings. This was review of a dismissed complaint, not a trial finding that an oral prepayment agreement existed. (Id. at 566, 570.)

## The written default clause did not give the borrower an option

Judge Kozinski rejected Trident's first theory: that the 10% fee made prepayment an alternative form of performance or a default the borrower could compel the lender to accept. That reading would let the default provision swallow the explicit twelve-year prohibition, contrary to the rule favoring harmonious construction. Multiple clauses assigned the acceleration decision to Connecticut General alone. The lender could declare the debt due, rescind a notice of default, waive noncompliance, or pursue other remedies, including collection of assigned rents. The borrower could stop paying, but could not thereby require the lender to accelerate and accept its preferred settlement of the debt. (Id. at 566–568.)

The first original footnote distinguished this issue from whether a 10% charge would be an invalid penalty for particular defaults. California law on that question was unsettled, and the fee might not be enforceable in every circumstance. That possibility did not create a borrower-held prepayment option. The second footnote emphasized that acceleration is an election, rather than an automatic consequence. The third rejected Trident's claim that California's one-form-of-action rule left the lender only acceleration and foreclosure: the lender could decline a remedy altogether, and certain nonjudicial remedies, such as enforcing an absolute assignment of rents through notice to tenants, were not barred by the rule. These were explanations of the parties' contractual positions, not judgments awarding a fee or executing foreclosure. (Id. at 567–568; original nn.1–3.)

The court further explained why a default could not be judicially converted into cost-free refinancing apart from the designated fee. Default might damage credit, expose the borrower to a trustee's sale timetable, divert rents, and trigger defaults in other loans. Those consequences helped enforce the bargain. The fourth footnote observed the reciprocal interest-rate risk: rising rates could leave the lender equally dissatisfied with its long-term loan. The court found no facial ambiguity giving Trident the asserted right and declined to truncate the lender's remedies. (Id. at 568; original n.4.)

## California law nevertheless required an opportunity to offer evidence

Trident's second theory asserted that the actual bargain permitted prepayment at any time with the 10% fee, even if the writing appeared to provide otherwise. The panel thought the written agreement, considered alone, was not reasonably susceptible to that construction. Under the traditional interpretation rule it described, an unambiguous integrated writing would exclude evidence offered to change that result. But the governing California rule, from *Pacific Gas & Electric*, required preliminary consideration of offered evidence relevant to the parties' meaning, rather than treating the judge's facial reading as conclusive. The Ninth Circuit understood that rule to require an opportunity to present evidence capable of exposing an ambiguity even in these extensively negotiated documents. (Id. at 568–570.)

Kozinski strongly criticized the California approach as creating uncertainty, expense, and incentives for parties disappointed by changed economic circumstances to attack clear language through interested recollections. He questioned its broader premise about the capacity of language to constrain conduct. Those criticisms did not constitute a holding displacing California law. The panel expressly applied *Erie*: a federal court bound by state substantive law could not adopt its preferred traditional rule instead. It therefore reversed to permit the offered evidence to be developed and assessed. The pointed rhetoric belongs to the panel's explanation of its disagreement with the rule, while the actual result applied that rule. (Id. at 569–570.)

The complete opinion's sixth footnote importantly narrows the result. After discovery, Connecticut General remained free to seek summary judgment. Given the clear written wording, it would prevail unless Trident supplied sufficient outside evidence to make its alternative meaning reasonably susceptible and create a genuine factual dispute requiring trial. Reversal did not hold that every claimed intention is admissible for every purpose, that Trident had proved the intended meaning, or that its allegations guaranteed a trial. The assigned excerpt omits this express limitation. (Id. at 570; original n.6.)

## A separate mistake claim and the sanction reversal

The seventh original footnote addressed Trident's alternative demand for rescission based on a unilateral mistake of law allegedly known to Connecticut General and left uncorrected. Despite regarding the allegation as implausible, the panel found it stated a California claim under the cited statutes. That cause also had to be reinstated for eventual summary-judgment or trial resolution. No finding established that the lender actually knew of such a mistake or that rescission ultimately was warranted. (Id.; original n.7.)

The district court's Rule 11 reasoning rested on the premise that no reasonable person, particularly firms of capable lawyers, could misunderstand the documents and therefore the action was brought in bad faith. Because California law allowed the evidence-based challenge, the Ninth Circuit reversed the sanctions along with dismissal. It regarded pursuit of available remedies as understandable with so much money at stake. It expressly reserved the reach of the note's attorney-fee provision, under which the district court apparently also awarded fees. The ninth footnote denied any blanket immunity: a proposed interpretation lacking an objectively reasonable factual basis could still support sanctions even under *Pacific Gas*. (Id. at 570; original nn.8–9.)

## Judgment and source scope

The panel reversed the district court's judgment and remanded to reinstate the complaint and conduct further proceedings under the opinion. Each party was to bear its own appellate costs. Kozinski wrote for the panel comprising himself, Hug, and Alarcon, without a separate writing or recorded dissent. The decision issued May 24, 1988, and was amended July 5; the archived reporter contains the amended opinion. (Id. at 565, 570.)
