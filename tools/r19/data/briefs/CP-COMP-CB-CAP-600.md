# Kindred Nursing Centers L. P. v. Clark

**581 U.S. 246 (2017) — Supreme Court of the United States, May 15, 2017. Civil Procedure.** Justice Kagan wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer, Alito, and Sotomayor. Justice Thomas dissented; Justice Gorsuch did not participate. The 7–1 decision rejected Kentucky’s special clear-statement requirement for agents entering arbitration agreements, but prescribed different dispositions for the two estates.

## Facts and procedural history

Beverly Wellner held a power of attorney from her husband, Joe Wellner; Janis Clark held one from her mother, Olive Clark. Both documents conferred broad authority, but their language differed. Joe authorized Beverly to institute legal proceedings and make contracts concerning real and personal property. Olive gave Janis power to handle all matters affecting Olive or her estate and to sign contracts and agreements in her name. The precise scope of those grants mattered separately from any rule specially disfavoring arbitration. 581 U.S. at 249.

Joe and Olive entered Kindred’s Winchester Centre nursing home in 2008. Using their powers of attorney to complete paperwork, Beverly and Janis each signed an agreement requiring binding arbitration of claims arising from or relating to the resident’s stay. After Joe and Olive died the next year, their estates, represented by Beverly and Janis, brought Kentucky state-court suits alleging that substandard care caused the deaths. The Court did not determine the truth of the care allegations. Kindred moved to dismiss on the ground that the disputes belonged in arbitration. The trial court denied the motions, and the Kentucky Court of Appeals agreed that the suits could proceed. Id. at 249–250.

The Kentucky Supreme Court consolidated the cases and affirmed by a divided vote. Initially construing the instruments themselves, it found the Wellner document insufficient to authorize arbitration, but recognized that the Clark document’s comprehensive delegation covered such an agreement. It nevertheless invalidated both arbitration agreements under an additional clear-statement rule. Because Kentucky’s Constitution specially protected access to courts and jury trial, an attorney-in-fact could waive those rights only if the principal expressly authorized that waiver. Neither instrument supplied the required specific statement. Id. at 250.

The state court described the rule as extending to contracts implicating other fundamental constitutional rights, suggesting future examples involving personal servitude and other extraordinary commitments. Justice Abramson’s state-court dissent, joined by two colleagues, regarded that purportedly general rule as discrimination against arbitration prohibited by the Federal Arbitration Act. The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari. The state dissent is procedural background here; it is distinct from Thomas’s dissent in the Supreme Court. Id. at 250–251.

## Issue and holding

Could Kentucky require arbitration-specific express authority despite an otherwise sufficient power of attorney, or avoid federal preemption by labeling the rule one of contract formation? No. Section 2 of the FAA requires equal treatment of arbitration agreements. A state cannot specially burden those contracts through a facially targeted rule or through a rule directed at their defining features. That protection applies to formation as well as enforcement. Id. at 251–255.

This does not dispense with actual agency authority or generally applicable contract defenses. The Court invalidated the discriminatory clear-statement requirement, while preserving independent interpretation of Wellner’s document and genuinely general state-law rules. Id. at 254 n. 2, 256.

## Equal treatment and the Kentucky rule

Section 2 makes covered arbitration agreements valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, subject to grounds that permit revocation of any contract. Generally applicable defenses, including fraud and unconscionability, remain available. What a state cannot do is create a rule applying only to arbitration or deriving its meaning from the fact that arbitration is involved. A rule may violate that principle even without using the word “arbitration,” if it specially disfavors contracts relinquishing court and jury adjudication—the central features of arbitration agreements. Id. at 251–252.

Kentucky’s requirement fell within that prohibition. The state court justified it by the constitutional importance of access to courts and a jury, but those were precisely the rights ordinarily relinquished by agreeing to arbitrate. Requiring special authorization to surrender them imposed an uncommon barrier on arbitration. Footnote 1 reinforced the inference: the decision did not suggest that an agent needed comparable express authority to settle litigation or consent to a bench trial, although those acts also relinquished court or jury adjudication. Id. at 252–253 & n. 1.

The assertion that other constitutional rights might be covered did not make the rule generally applicable in practice. The state court had not announced comparable limitations on routine agency contracts affecting constitutionally protected property or speech, such as selling furniture or entering confidentiality agreements. Instead, its other examples involved objectionable or fanciful commitments. Pairing arbitration with that exceptional category revealed discrimination rather than meaningful equal treatment. Id. at 253–254.

Footnote 2 expressly allows a state court to announce a new, generally applicable rule in an arbitration case. The problem is not the setting in which a rule originates, but whether it actually operates generally instead of singling out arbitration. Id. at 254 n. 2.

## Respondents’ formation argument

Beverly and Janis principally defended the decision on a different basis: the FAA supposedly governed enforcement only after a valid agreement existed, leaving states unrestricted authority to determine whether agents could form arbitration contracts. The Court rejected that separation. Section 2 refers to validity as well as enforceability, and precedent had already condemned discriminatory use of defenses, such as duress, that concern formation. Id. at 254–255.

Their proposed distinction would also permit easy evasion of the statute. A state could declare every attorney-in-fact incapable of signing an arbitration agreement, even with express authorization, or declare everyone incapable of doing so. Calling those rules formation requirements would not make their targeted destruction of arbitration consistent with equal treatment. The Court’s reasoning concerns discriminatory formation rules, rather than compelling arbitration without any contract or agency authority. Id. at 255.

## Different remedies, dissent, and judgment

For Clark, the state court had already found the document broad enough to authorize arbitration. The clear-statement rule was its sole reason for refusing the agreement, so removal of that rule required enforcement of the Clark–Kindred agreement. The Supreme Court reversed the judgment for that estate. Id. at 256.

For Wellner, the state court had separately read the grant as insufficiently broad. If that interpretation was wholly independent of the discriminatory rule, the Supreme Court’s holding did not disturb it. If the special rule influenced the interpretation, however, the state court had to reconsider the document without that influence. Because the opinion did not establish which was true, the Court vacated the Wellner judgment and remanded for that determination. It did not itself hold that Beverly necessarily possessed authority to arbitrate. Id.

Thomas would have affirmed both judgments because, adhering to his prior position, he considered the FAA inapplicable to proceedings in state courts. His dissent rested on that view of the Act’s reach, rather than joining the majority’s preemption analysis while disputing the particular instruments. Id. at 257.

The exact mandate was **reversed in part, vacated in part, and remanded**. The Court did not determine nursing-home liability or damages. Id.
