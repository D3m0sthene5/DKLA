# McConnell v. Commonwealth Pictures Corp.
7 N.Y.2d 465, 166 N.E.2d 494, 199 N.Y.S.2d 483 (1960) | Contracts

## Decision and posture
The New York Court of Appeals decided this appeal on March 31, 1960. Chief Judge Desmond wrote for a five-judge majority joined by Judges Dye, Fuld, Burke, and Foster. Judge Froessel dissented, and Judge Van Voorhis joined that dissent and supplied a separate memorandum. The court reinstated two affirmative defenses alleging commercial bribery and public-policy illegality. It did not decide after trial that McConnell actually paid a bribe. The allegations were accepted for the limited purpose of testing the defenses’ legal sufficiency. (Complete reproduced opinion, majority opening and concluding paragraphs; voting statement and final order.)

## Facts and lower-court proceedings
Commonwealth agreed in writing to pay McConnell $10,000 if he negotiated an agreement obtaining motion-picture distribution rights for it, followed by a percentage of the distributor’s gross receipts. McConnell obtained the distribution agreement, Commonwealth paid the initial $10,000, and it later refused his demand for further commissions and an accounting. Froessel’s dissent supplies the contractual detail: distribution rights concerned forty western feature films and four serial films associated with Universal Pictures, and the later payment was twenty percent of gross receipts after Commonwealth recouped $100,000 from sixty percent of gross receipts. (Majority, paragraphs beginning “Plaintiff sues” and “Plaintiff negotiated”; Froessel dissent, opening two paragraphs.)

Commonwealth’s answer alleged that McConnell obtained the rights by secretly promising and paying $10,000 to a representative of the producer, without Commonwealth’s or the producer’s knowledge. One affirmative defense characterized the payment as commercial bribery contrary to Penal Law § 439; the other repeated the alleged conduct and invoked public policy. McConnell disputed the allegations, although his motion to strike required treating them as true when deciding whether the defenses could defeat his claim. Commonwealth also asserted counterclaims, including recovery of the initial $10,000, but their sufficiency was not before the Court of Appeals. (Majority, paragraphs beginning “Defendant’s answer” and “The sufficiency”; Froessel dissent, paragraphs beginning “Two defenses” and “These allegations”.)

Special Term struck both defenses because the agreement McConnell sued upon was lawful in itself: it called for negotiating distribution rights, not committing a crime. The court distinguished a lawful agreement performed through misconduct from an intrinsically unlawful contract. The First Department affirmed that part of the order despite a dissent. Commonwealth appealed the ruling striking its defenses. (Majority, opening paragraph and paragraph beginning “The courts below”.)

The assigned account on printed pages 761–762 describes the bribe as an accomplished fact and speaks of the distributor retaining the transaction’s benefit without paying commissions. The reported decision’s procedural qualification is essential: the issue was the sufficiency of pleaded defenses, not a final merits judgment establishing all their allegations.

## Issue and majority rule
Could a defendant plead central commercial bribery in the plaintiff’s performance as a defense to compensation under an agreement that was lawful on its face, even though the defendant had not directed the bribery and the plaintiff might prove its prima facie contract claim without mentioning it?

The majority answered yes. Its concern was whether judicial enforcement would enable the plaintiff to collect the fruits of corruption, rather than whether the defendant had a superior moral claim to the resulting benefit. A party can be denied recovery on a facially valid contract where its performance involves gravely immoral, illegal conduct of the kind alleged here. The rule is bounded: the illegality must bear a direct connection to the obligation sued upon and be central to or a dominant part of the plaintiff’s course of performance. Minor or merely incidental wrongdoing does not automatically erase all contractual rights. (Majority, paragraphs beginning “We take a different view”, “And it makes no difference”, “It is argued”, “There must at least”, and “We point out”.)

## Majority reasoning
Desmond began with the public policy against using courts to obtain the rewards of crime or profit from one’s own fraud. Some earlier cases involved intrinsically illegal agreements, but the majority concluded that the policy would fail if a plaintiff could avoid it merely by drafting a lawful promise and then perform through central corruption. Assuming the defenses were true, McConnell’s claimed commissions resulted from the bribery used to obtain the distribution rights. That connection, rather than facial illegality in the compensation agreement, justified allowing the defenses. (Majority, paragraphs beginning “New York’s policy”, “We must either”, and “Hoy”.)

The court did not make the particular elements of the commercial-bribery statute the exclusive source of the defense. It thought the alleged conduct appeared to fall within § 439 but also relied on the common-law and public-policy treatment of corruption. Sirkin had refused recovery to a seller who secretly paid the buyer’s purchasing agent. Reiner denied compensation for services performed through fraud practiced against a third party. For the majority, bribing the producer’s representative to obtain rights for the distributor did not differ in principle from bribing the purchaser’s representative to obtain a sales transaction. Nor was it decisive that McConnell could establish the elements of his affirmative claim without exposing the bribe: the court would consider the disclosed transaction as a whole when the defense was proved. (Majority, paragraphs beginning “The issue is not”, “Sirkin is the case closest”, “There cannot be”, “In the Reiner case”, and “On the whole case”.)

The majority confronted the objection that an innocent contracting defendant would receive a windfall and that even small illegality might then forfeit valuable compensation. It expressly rejected that unlimited proposition. It recognized a spectrum between peripheral illegality and dominant corruption and declined to announce results for every possible case. The pleaded bribery belonged to the latter category. Even if barring recovery on an otherwise wholly lawful agreement extended the precedents, the public policy against central commercial bribery supported the extension. (Majority, paragraphs beginning “It is argued”, “There must at least”, “We cannot now”, “Consistent with public morality”, and “Perhaps this application”.)

Southwestern Shipping did not compel a different result. The majority described an exception that prevents a mere depository or escrow holder, who was not a party to the underlying transaction, from exploiting illegality to keep money or property held for another. Commonwealth was the contracting defendant in the obligation sued upon, rather than a third-party custodian of money delivered for someone else’s use. The court therefore did not treat that custodial exception as a general rule requiring enforcement whenever a defendant was uninvolved in the plaintiff’s corruption. (Majority, paragraphs beginning “There is no pertinence” and “That exception”.)

## Froessel’s dissent
Froessel would affirm the striking of the defenses. He emphasized that the agreement sued upon was lawful when made, contemplated lawful services, and was not itself obtained through bribery. Commonwealth had retained the negotiated distribution rights and their benefits, which the dissent described as extending over approximately twelve years. The allegations did not establish that Commonwealth’s own agent was corrupted or that the producer challenged the distribution agreement. (Froessel dissent, opening paragraphs and paragraphs beginning “These allegations”, “In the instant case”, and “Here”.)

For Froessel, Sirkin concerned a different situation: the seller procured the very contract sued upon by bribing the purchaser’s agent. He favored a distinction between a contract unlawfully procured or calling for unlawful services and a lawful contract during whose performance the plaintiff later committed wrongdoing. He regarded the majority’s gradations of direct and peripheral, major and minor corruption as unworkable and unsupported by the governing authorities. His hypothetical of a builder bribing an inspector to expedite an inspection illustrated the risk that an owner could retain a building while escaping payment under an otherwise lawful undertaking. That illustration is not a factual finding about McConnell’s transaction. (Froessel dissent, paragraphs beginning “This is not a case”, “In the Sirkin case”, “In the instant case”, “The majority opinion”, and “If a contract was lawfully made”.)

The dissent also read Southwestern Shipping more broadly than the majority: a party unconnected with the separate illegal agreement should not obtain a windfall by asserting that agreement’s illegality. Allowing Commonwealth to retain the benefit of the lawful compensation arrangement while invoking McConnell’s separate dealings with a third party conflicted, in Froessel’s view, with that policy. He would affirm with costs and answer the certified question affirmatively. (Froessel dissent, paragraphs beginning “Moreover”, “There”, “Here”, and final paragraph.)

## Van Voorhis’s separate memorandum
Van Voorhis agreed with Froessel. He acknowledged that courts should not help wrongdoers divide criminal spoils but argued that expanding the rule in this setting might encourage misappropriation and breaches of faith between people who were not themselves in a corrupt relationship. A defendant’s ability to take opportunistic advantage of someone else’s wrongdoing might push enforcement of legitimate obligations outside lawful channels rather than discourage bribery. His concern was the practical effect of the majority’s public-policy choice, not a separate factual conclusion that no bribery occurred. (Van Voorhis memorandum, complete three paragraphs.)

## Exact disposition and significance
The Court of Appeals reversed the appealed order, awarded costs in all courts, remitted the matter to the Appellate Division for proceedings consistent with its opinion, and answered the certified question in the negative. McConnell’s motion attacking the two defenses was to be denied. Whether Commonwealth could prove those defenses remained a subsequent merits question; the counterclaim for return of the already-paid $10,000 was outside this appeal. (Majority final paragraph; final order.)

The assigned commercial-bribery discussion uses the case to expose the tension between refusing judicial aid to central corruption and permitting the defendant to retain a contractual benefit. The holding rests on a direct, dominant relationship between the alleged bribery and the compensation sought. It neither establishes automatic forfeiture for every unlawful act in performance nor resolves the editorial proposal to disgorge and redirect the bribe. The latter appears in the casebook’s separate note, not in the court’s dispositive order.
