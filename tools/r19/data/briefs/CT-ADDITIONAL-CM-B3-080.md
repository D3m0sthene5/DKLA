# Kutzin v. Pirnie
124 N.J. 500, 591 A.2d 932 (1991) | Contracts

## Decision and result
The New Jersey Supreme Court held that the buyers had breached an enforceable real-estate contract, but their breach did not entitle the sellers to retain a deposit exceeding their actual loss when the contract contained no forfeiture or liquidated-damages clause. The court adopted Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 374(1), overruling New Jersey decisions adhering to the contrary common-law rule. It restored the trial court's $17,325 damages award to the sellers and $18,675 repayment to the buyers. Justice Clifford wrote the June 27, 1991 opinion; Chief Justice Wilentz and Justices Handler, Pollock, O'Hern, Garibaldi, and Stein joined, making the judgment unanimous, 7–0. (124 N.J. 500, 502, 516–518.)

## The contract and attorney-review exchanges
Milton and Ruth Kutzin agreed on September 1, 1987, to sell their Haworth house to Duncan and Gertrude Pirnie for $365,000. The sellers' broker prepared a standard realtors' form. The buyers paid $1,000 initially and owed another $35,000 within seven days. The contract did not prescribe forfeiture or liquidated damages; it said that if either party voided the contract, escrow funds would be disbursed under both parties' written directions. (Id. at 502–503.)

The attorney-review provision allowed either side's lawyer to review and disapprove within three days, excluding weekends and legal holidays. Disapproval required notice to the realtors and the other contracting party; notice to the realtors had to be sent by certified mail or telegram or delivered personally. Without proper disapproval, the agreement became legally binding at the period's end. The parties could extend review in writing, and a lawyer could suggest revisions. (Id. at 503.)

The sellers' attorney, Kozinn, approved the agreement except that he wanted to hold the deposit. He communicated with the buyers' broker and sent a September 3 letter, copying the buyers' attorney, Maccarone. The broker transferred the $1,000 to him. On September 4, Maccarone accepted that arrangement, expressed concern about the buyers' protections, and mailed a proposed rider to Kozinn. That proposal did not address disposition of the deposit. On September 10 the attorneys agreed on changes to the rider; Maccarone assured Kozinn that the buyers intended to purchase, and Kozinn received the additional $35,000 the next day. The sellers then left for Florida. (Id. at 503–504.)

A revised rider signed by the buyers was forwarded to the sellers later in September. Shortly afterward the buyers retained a new attorney, who mailed a September 28 notice saying they no longer wished to purchase and requesting the deposit back. The sellers refused and sued for specific performance; the buyers counterclaimed for the deposit, arguing that the contract had been rescinded through attorney review or mutual agreement. While suit was pending, the sellers sold the house to someone else for $352,500 and amended their claim to seek damages alone. (Id. at 505.)

## Trial and appellate judgments
The trial court found a binding, unrescinded contract. It awarded $17,325: the $12,500 shortfall on resale, $3,825 in utilities, taxes, and insurance during the six-month delay, and $1,000 spent on a new basement carpet recommended to improve marketability. It declined claimed lost interest and increased capital-gains taxes and ordered return of the $18,675 deposit balance. The sellers appealed seeking the additional losses or alternatively the whole deposit; the buyers cross-appealed seeking the whole deposit on their rescission theory. The Appellate Division agreed that the contract was enforceable and the interest and tax claims too speculative, but permitted the sellers to retain all $36,000. (Id. at 505–506.)

## Why proposed revisions did not rescind the agreement
The Supreme Court rejected the buyers' argument that communication between lawyers displaced the express notice requirements. The clause was unambiguous. Neither lawyer disapproved the agreement as specified: Kozinn's letter said it was satisfactory subject to the accepted deposit arrangement; it was not certified and omitted notice to the sellers' broker. Maccarone's letter proposed changes without disapproval, was not certified, and omitted copies to both brokers and the sellers. Neither side secured a written extension of attorney review. The contract therefore became binding at the end of the period, modified only concerning custody of the deposit. Refusal to purchase was a breach, allowing the sellers to rescind, resell, and recover resulting damages. (Id. at 507–509.)

The court did not decide whether notice to the other party's attorney would independently satisfy the clause's requirement of notice to that party. It identified direct notice to the party as the better practice, while resolving this dispute on the defects actually present. (Id. at 508.)

## Restitution and the rejection of automatic forfeiture
Traditional New Jersey cases let an innocent seller retain a defaulting buyer's payment regardless of actual damages or the presence of a forfeiture clause. The premise was that a contract breaker should not gain judicial assistance from the wrong. The court reviewed that authority, but also the competing development of restitution against forfeiture. (Id. at 509–512.)

The court explained the asymmetry of the old rule: a buyer who paid nothing before breaching owed compensatory damages, while one who partly performed could lose a sum exceeding the same injury. Retaining that excess imposed a penalty rather than making the seller whole. The opinion traced this approach through Corbin, the first Restatement's § 357, New Jersey authority applying restitution in another contract setting, and the broader modern § 374(1). It reproduced an illustration in which a defaulting purchaser recovered installments less the seller's loss on resale, while recognizing the different situation of a seller obtaining specific performance. Earlier New Jersey deposit decisions had sometimes questioned forfeiture or rested on failure to prove unjust enrichment; with the issue squarely presented, the court now adopted § 374(1) and overruled contrary common-law decisions. (Id. at 512–516.)

The buyers still bore the burden of proving that the deposit exceeded the sellers' breach-related injury. Here the $17,325 loss had been found and was not challenged in the Supreme Court. Subtracting it from $36,000 left $18,675 to return. The deposit's being just under 10% of the price did not change this calculation. There was no categorical 10% entitlement permitting retention of proved excess damages under this agreement. (Id. at 516–517.)

## The reservation for agreed damages and final judgment
The court emphasized that no forfeiture or liquidated-damages clause was before it. Such a clause would implicate § 374(2), which limits restitution when the agreed retention reasonably reflects anticipated loss and difficulties of proof. The opinion did not decide an actual clause's validity or enforceability; it cautioned that labeling a penalty as liquidated damages would not alone cure it. Thus the holding concerned restitution of a proved excess where the parties had not agreed on damages. (Id. at 517–518.)

The Appellate Division judgment was modified to restore the trial court's damages award and affirmed as modified. The sellers recovered their loss; the buyers recovered the remaining deposit despite their breach. This was a final monetary result, rather than an evidentiary remand. (Id. at 518.)
