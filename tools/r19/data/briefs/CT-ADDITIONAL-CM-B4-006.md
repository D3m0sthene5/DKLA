# Coppola v. Kraushaar
92 N.Y.S. 436 (App. Div. 1905) | Contracts

## Court and pleading posture

On March 3, 1905, New York’s Appellate Division, Second Department, unanimously reversed dismissal of a breach-of-contract complaint, even though its claimed wedding losses were too remote. Justice Jenks wrote for Presiding Justice Hirschberg and Justices Woodward, Rich, and Miller. The plaintiff had been dismissed at trial before any testimony was taken. The appellate issue therefore concerned the adequacy of allegations, rather than facts established through evidence. (92 N.Y.S. 436–437, 439.)

## Alleged transaction and loss

Lorenzo Coppola alleged that on January 3, 1902, he ordered two gowns for his betrothed from Isidor Kraushaar, specifying a particular model. He told the defendant that the wedding was set for January 19 and that he was incurring substantial wedding-feast expenses. The defendant allegedly promised delivery by January 18 for $50, receiving $10 immediately. Coppola and his betrothed demanded the gowns on the promised date, but the defendant failed to deliver. The complaint attributed the woman’s breaking off the wedding to that failure and demanded $500 for presents, wine, clothes, and other expenses allegedly rendered useless. These remain pleading allegations; the court had heard no proof of delivery failure, causation, or permanent refusal to marry. (437.)

The complaint did not identify either gown as the bridal gown. That omission mattered because notice that dresses were wanted for a wedding did not itself establish that failure to supply them would cause the ceremony, or the marriage itself, never to occur. The action was against the dress supplier for breach of the delivery contract, not against the betrothed for breach of a promise to marry. (437–438.)

## Why the $500 consequential claim failed

The alleged wedding expenditures were neither an immediate and necessary result of the clothing-contract breach nor a loss shown to have been within the parties’ contemplation when contracting. Explaining the intended use and delivery deadline notified the supplier of the occasion, but did not warn him of the asserted consequence: the bride’s permanent refusal to marry. The court cited the principle that notice of an object does not alone change damages unless it forms the basis of agreement. (437.)

The allegation that the expenditures became wholly useless implied more than a postponement. The court reasoned that gifts, wine, and clothes acquired for a wedding would not necessarily become useless merely because the wedding date changed. To impose these losses, the parties would have needed to contemplate that the marriage would never take place if the dresses were not delivered the day before the ceremony. The bride’s disappointment might be predictable, but permanent refusal to wed was not, absent a specific warning. Applying Hadley and the intervening-agency foreseeability inquiry, the court deemed these damages too remote. It did not conclude that identifying a deadline always makes every downstream consequence recoverable. (437–438.)

## Why dismissal nevertheless was wrong

The absence of recoverable $500 losses did not eliminate the pleaded contract claim. On an objection to legal sufficiency, the allegations and their reasonable implications were accepted; a pleading sufficiently identifying a claim could permit supporting evidence without strict construction against the pleader. Coppola alleged an agreement, his $10 part payment, and nonperformance. Those allegations could support a breach claim even if the damages expressly demanded were unavailable. (438–439.)

The court explained that nominal damages alone would have prevented dismissal. An erroneous damages theory or specification of unrecoverable items did not make an otherwise adequate cause of action demurrable. Moreover, the allegations required an inference of $10 actual loss from the part payment, although they permitted no other actual loss apart from the rejected remote expenditures. The court’s citation to a pleading treatise noted controversy over the same rule for wholly executory consideration; Coppola’s own case involved an actual part payment, so it did not require resolving that broader controversy. (438–439.)

## Disposition and source comparison

The judgment was reversed with costs, all five panel members concurring. The appellate court did not award $500, enter a final $10 recovery, or find the alleged facts proved. It restored the claim notwithstanding the limited possible recovery, observing that the plaintiff’s eventual victory might be small. (439.)
