# Sibbach v. Wilson & Co., Inc.

**Civil Procedure · 312 U.S. 1 (1941) · Supreme Court of the United States · January 13, 1941**

Justice Roberts wrote the Court’s opinion. Justice Frankfurter dissented, with Justices Black, Douglas, and Murphy agreeing with his views, producing a **5–4 division on the rule’s validity**. The Court held Rules 35 and 37 within the rulemaking authority Congress delegated. It nevertheless **reversed the judgment and remanded** because Rule 37 expressly prohibited contempt punishment for refusal to submit to the examination. The distinction between a valid examination rule and an unauthorized imprisonment is essential to the disposition.

## Facts and procedural history

Sibbach sued in the federal district court for Northern Illinois to recover damages for bodily injuries inflicted in Indiana. Wilson denied her allegations and requested a court-ordered medical examination to determine the nature and extent of her injuries. The court ordered an examination by an appointed physician. Sibbach refused, and Wilson obtained an order requiring her to show cause why she should not be punished for contempt. She challenged the court’s authority and asserted that the examination order was void. **312 U.S. at 6–7.**

The two relevant states took different positions. Indiana courts permitted such an examination order, whereas Illinois courts did not; neither state had a governing statute. The district court adjudged Sibbach in contempt and directed that she be committed until she complied or was otherwise legally discharged. The Seventh Circuit held Rule 35 valid and affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari because of the importance of the rulemaking question. **Id. at 7 & nn.2–5.**

## The statute and rules

The 1934 enabling statute authorized the Supreme Court to prescribe federal civil pleading, practice, and procedure, while prohibiting rules that abridged, enlarged, or modified substantive rights. It also protected the constitutional jury-trial right in the merger of law and equity and required the unified rules to be reported to Congress before taking effect. **Id. at 7–8.**

Then-Rule 35 permitted an examination when a party’s physical or mental condition was in controversy. The order required a motion, good cause, notice, and specification of the examination’s details. Then-Rule 37 authorized litigation consequences for refusal, including treating a condition as established, restricting evidence, striking pleadings, staying proceedings, dismissal, or default. Its arrest provision expressly excepted refusal to obey an order for physical or mental examination. These are the historical texts reproduced in the decision, not a verification of the current rules. **Id. at 8–9.**

## Issue and holding on rulemaking authority

Did the examination and discovery rules exceed Congress’s delegated authority because they affected an important interest in bodily privacy?

The majority held they did not. The controlling test was whether the rule genuinely regulated procedure: the judicial process for enforcing substantive rights and administering remedies. The importance of the interest affected did not itself transform a procedural rule into an unauthorized alteration of substantive rights. **Id. at 13–16.**

## The majority’s reasoning

Congress possessed authority to regulate federal practice and procedure and could delegate rulemaking power within statutory and constitutional limits. That authority did not permit courts to use procedural rules to abolish substantive state rights. The majority read the enabling statute as deliberately limited to procedural matters and identified additional limits, including the inability to change statutory jurisdiction by court rule. **Id. at 9–10 & nn.6–9.**

The state-law disagreement exposed a difficulty in Sibbach’s position. If examination was procedural and the federal rule valid, it displaced the former conformity requirement that otherwise might call for Illinois practice. If the exemption instead belonged to substantive law, the majority said the Rules of Decision Act required Indiana law, which permitted examination. Sibbach acknowledged that Rules 35 and 37 were procedural but maintained that substantive rights included important or substantial interests affected by procedure. The majority rejected that proposed distinction within the category of procedural rules. **Id. at 10–11 & nn.10–11.**

Earlier examination decisions did not establish a prohibition against the new rule. *Botsford* had rejected an examination order where no authority for it existed; it did not deny that legislation could authorize the practice. *Stetson* permitted examination under a state statute. Although that decision referred to the Rules of Decision Act, the distinction between substantive and procedural law had not been material because the injury and trial both occurred in the same state. Neither decision required a court to disregard a valid federal rule carrying statutory force. **Id. at 11–13 & nn.12–13.**

An importance-based limitation also lacked a workable boundary. Many procedural changes affect substantial interests, and state practices concerning examinations were not uniform. The majority regarded examinations as methods of finding facts needed to adjudicate the negligence rights already recognized by substantive law. It also emphasized that refusing the examination did not expose Sibbach to imprisonment under the rules properly construed. The relevant measure was the rule’s regulation of judicial procedure, rather than the significance of every interest it touched. **Id. at 13–14 & nn.14–16.**

Finally, Congress had received the rules and retained the opportunity to prevent them from taking effect. Rule 35 had been discussed before congressional committees, and the drafts and advisory materials identified the contrary earlier practice. The absence of adverse congressional action indicated to the majority at least that Congress had found no violation of legislative policy. This supported, rather than replaced, the majority’s analysis of delegated authority. **Id. at 14–16 & nn.17–19.**

## The contempt error and actual judgment

Although Sibbach had not separately assigned the contempt punishment as error in the Seventh Circuit or Supreme Court, the Court noticed it as fundamental plain error. Rule 37’s exception for refusal to submit to examination was explicit. The available remedies were the specified litigation consequences, not punishment as for contempt. The Court therefore **reversed and remanded to the district court for further proceedings consistent with the opinion**. Upholding the rules did not uphold the commitment order. **Id. at 16 & n.20.**

## Frankfurter’s dissent

Frankfurter did not believe that an abstract classification as substance or procedure answered the question of delegated authority. *Botsford* recognized a deeply rooted immunity from bodily intrusion. That immunity was not constitutionally unchangeable, but it should require clear legislative authorization to alter. A general delegation to improve civil procedure did not sufficiently express that policy decision. **Id. at 16–18.**

The absence of imprisonment did not eliminate the rule’s coercion. A litigant refusing examination could lose access to adjudication through dismissal or other litigation consequences. Frankfurter distinguished bodily intrusion from ordinary document discovery and pretrial management. He also rejected inferring legislative approval from Congress’s failure to veto rules that would otherwise automatically take effect; practical legislative conditions made that inference unrealistic. Explicit legislation was required for such a significant change. **Id. at 18.**

His concern extended beyond diversity litigation because Rule 35 applied throughout federal civil litigation, including enforcement of federal rights. Black, Douglas, and Murphy agreed with these views. The dissent therefore challenged the source of authority for the examination requirement itself, rather than merely the district court’s use of imprisonment. **Id. at 18–19.**
