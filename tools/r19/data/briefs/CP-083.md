Provident Tradesmens Bank & Trust Co. v. Patterson
Citation
390 U.S. 102, 88 S. Ct. 733, 19 L. Ed. 2d 936 (Supreme Court of the United States 1968). Argued November
6–7, 1967; decided January 29, 1968. Opinion by Justice Harlan.
Casebook pp. 752–761; source PDF pp. 393–397. Full judicial opinion.
Parties
Provident Tradesmens Bank, administrator of passenger John Lynch’s estate, sought insurance coverage for
its claim against driver Donald Cionci’s estate. Lumbermens Mutual Casualty Company and Cionci’s estate
were defendants; injured passenger John Harris and truck driver Thomas Smith’s representative joined as
plaintiffs. Vehicle owner and named insured Edward Dutcher was the absent person whose interests
produced the Rule 19 dispute. 390 U.S. 104–106; CB 752–754.
Material Facts
Dutcher lent his automobile to Cionci, who drove with Lynch and Harris as passengers. The car crossed a
median and collided with Smith’s truck. Cionci, Lynch, and Smith died; Harris suffered serious injuries.
Lynch’s estate settled a federal tort action against Cionci’s estate for $50,000, but the penniless estate did
not pay. Separate state tort actions by Smith’s representative and Harris remained pending against Cionci’s
estate, Dutcher, and Lynch’s estate. 390 U.S. 104–105; CB 752–753.
Lumbermens’ policy provided $100,000 for claims arising from one accident and covered a person driving
with Dutcher’s permission. Lumbermens denied that Cionci’s alleged detour was permitted. Dutcher also
potentially needed the insurance to cover his own vicarious liability. The coverage plaintiffs sought a
declaration that Cionci had permission; joining Dutcher, a Pennsylvania resident like the plaintiffs, as a
defendant would have destroyed diversity. 390 U.S. 105–109; CB 753–754.
Procedural History
The district court entered judgment for the coverage plaintiffs following directed verdicts for the two estates
and a jury verdict for Harris. The full opinion explains that Pennsylvania’s Dead Man Rule prevented
Dutcher from testifying against the estates, whereas he testified on the Harris claim. The defendants did not
raise Dutcher’s nonjoinder at trial. 390 U.S. 106–107; CB 753–754.
The Third Circuit, sitting en banc, reversed 5–2 on grounds it raised itself: Dutcher was indispensable, and
the federal court should have deferred to the state litigation. It treated joinder as a substantive entitlement
beyond Rule 19 and did not decide the insurer’s state-law appellate arguments. The Supreme Court granted
review of that dismissal. 390 U.S. 107–108; CB 753–754.
Issue
When a person who should be joined cannot be joined without destroying diversity, does his possible
interest require dismissal automatically, or must the court apply Rule 19(b) to the actual prejudice, available
protections, alternative forum, and posture of the litigation? How should that inquiry operate after a fully
litigated judgment?

Rule and Holding
Rule 19 requires a contextual determination whether the action can proceed in equity and good conscience.
Assuming Dutcher should be joined if feasible, his nonjoinder did not justify setting aside this judgment.
The Court identified the interests of the plaintiff, defendant, absentee, and public; courts must evaluate
practical prejudice and consider shaping relief to protect legitimate interests before dismissing. 390 U.S.
108–112, 118–125; CB 754–760.
Indispensability describes the conclusion that the case cannot fairly proceed without a person. It is not an
antecedent classification that substitutes for analysis. Substantive rights may require protection, but Rule 19
validly directs courts to determine whether such rights are actually threatened in the particular litigation. 390
U.S. 118–125; CB 758–761.
Judgment
The Supreme Court vacated the appellate judgment and remanded. The Third Circuit had to decide the
previously unaddressed appellate issues; if the district court’s judgment survived those issues, it should
preserve that judgment while protecting nonjoined persons. The Supreme Court therefore did not finally
resolve every coverage or evidentiary issue. 390 U.S. 128; CB 761 (abbreviated disposition).
Reasoning
Plaintiff and defendant interests depended on timing. Before trial, an adequate alternative forum could favor
dismissal. After trial, the plaintiffs had an additional interest in preserving their judgment and the resources
already spent. The insurer had not objected to nonjoinder and had an opportunity to litigate claims against its
policy. Its belated position could not, by itself, justify discarding the completed adjudication. 390 U.S.
109–113; CB 754–756.
The absentee’s practical exposure required separate analysis. Ordinarily, a nonparty is not legally bound by
the judgment, but that does not eliminate possible practical injury. Here, Dutcher might lose access to policy
proceeds before defending claims against himself. The Court regarded this risk as limited and avoidable. It
did not accept as established the plaintiffs’ assertion that Dutcher faced virtually no possible tort liability.
390 U.S. 110–115; CB 755–758.
The Third Circuit confused an adverse interest under the Dead Man Rule with unavoidable prejudice from
this judgment. A judgment for the insurer would have benefited Dutcher, but that did not establish that a
judgment against the insurer impermissibly injured him. The Supreme Court expressly reserved whether his
failure to intervene could preclude later litigation of permission; it did not announce a general rule binding
every person who could have intervened. 390 U.S. 113–115; CB 756–758.
Relief could protect Dutcher without dismissal. Payment could be withheld while relevant tort claims
proceeded; claimants also represented that they would accept a limitation of their claims to the policy
amount. A court must examine such measures rather than assume that dismissal is the only available
protection. The public interest in efficient, consistent adjudication also included preserving work already
completed. 390 U.S. 115–116; CB 758.
Earlier equity cases did not create a categorical substantive right to have litigation dismissed whenever it
might affect an absentee. Their results responded to particular risks of prejudice and conflicting obligations.
Rule 19 preserved consideration of those risks while requiring a practical examination of the actual dispute.
390 U.S. 118–125; CB 759–761.

Outside the casebook’s abbreviated discussion, the Court also rejected discretionary dismissal in favor of the
state suits. Those suits concerned tort liability and agency; this action concerned permissive use and
insurance coverage. The insurer was not a party to the state actions, and those actions did not offer the estate
an equivalent opportunity to obtain the requested coverage judgment. The completed federal trial also
mattered to that discretionary decision. 390 U.S. 125–128 (full opinion).
Separate Opinions
Justice Harlan delivered the Court’s opinion. The published decision contains no separate concurrence or
dissent. 390 U.S. 104–128.
Notes
For analysis, identify why joinder is required, why it is infeasible, and how each available disposition would
affect the parties and absentee. Then evaluate concrete protective terms and alternative proceedings. The
narrow holding concerns an objection first raised after trial on this record; the broader methodological lesson
is the practical, interest-sensitive operation of Rule 19(b). 390 U.S. 108–116, 118–125; CB 754–761.
Keep subject-matter jurisdiction distinct from the decision whether to proceed without a person. Joining
Dutcher would have defeated diversity, but his absence did not automatically eliminate jurisdiction over the
existing action. Also distinguish formal preclusion from practical impairment: the latter can require
protection even when the former does not apply. 390 U.S. 108–111, 118–125; CB 754–760.
