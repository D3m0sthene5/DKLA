# Mobil Oil Exploration & Producing Southeast, Inc. v. United States
530 U.S. 604 (2000) | Contracts

## Leases bought a regulated opportunity
Mobil and Marathon paid the United States approximately $156 million in up-front bonus payments for ten-year renewable leases issued in 1981. Annual rent and future production royalties were additional obligations. The leases concerned exploration and development off North Carolina's coast. They did not guarantee permission to drill or profitable production. They gave the companies an opportunity to seek permissions under incorporated statutes and regulations, including the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) and Coastal Zone Management Act (CZMA). (530 U.S. at 607–610.)

The sequence mattered. Interior had to consider an Exploration Plan and approve a qualifying plan within thirty days. A drilling permit also required a coastal-zone consistency certification, subject to an affected state's objection and possible Commerce override. Discharge permits and a later Development and Production Plan entailed further approvals. A favorable first-stage decision therefore would not itself authorize the entire project. (Id. at 609–610.)

In 1989 the companies, North Carolina, and Interior agreed on a draft-plan review process. Interior would produce an environmental report and suspend annual rent, approximately $250,000, while specified preparation and state-objection issues remained pending, extending the leases correspondingly. After the draft and an extensive environmental review, the companies submitted their final Exploration Plan and consistency certification on August 20, 1990. Two days earlier, Congress's Outer Banks Protection Act (OBPA) had taken effect. (Id. at 611.)

## New procedures displaced the promised process
OBPA barred plan approvals and drilling permits for at least thirteen months. It required a new Environmental Sciences Review Panel's report, Interior certification to Congress, and an additional congressional-session period. Interior would have to explain differences from the Panel's recommendations. Interior later told Mobil that the submitted plan satisfied existing law and could not properly be disapproved under those requirements, but OBPA prevented approval. It held the plan and suspended the offshore leases instead. (Id. at 611–613.)

After receiving the Panel's report, Interior certified that it had enough information but still awaited further recommended studies. North Carolina objected to consistency in November 1990, and the companies sought Commerce override. Commerce rejected that request in 1994, relying substantially on the new Panel's findings. Congress repealed OBPA in 1996. The Court did not treat the legislation's environmental policy as unlawful; it examined whether its implementation departed from contractual promises. (Id. at 613, 620, 624.)

The companies joined a Court of Federal Claims contract action in October 1992. On summary judgment, that court found repudiation and awarded restitution of the bonus payments. A divided Federal Circuit reversed because the state's consistency objection would have prevented exploration regardless of Interior's failure to approve. The Supreme Court considered both companies' petitions together and reversed and remanded on June 26, 2000. Breyer wrote for eight Justices; Stevens dissented. (Id. at 604–607, 613–614, 624.)

## Restitution required a substantial breach or repudiation
The Court began with ordinary contract principles applicable generally when the Government contracts. Under Restatement §373, repudiation or nonperformance amounting to total breach can support restitution of benefits conferred through performance or reliance. Section 250 defines repudiation through an announced breach serious enough to support total-breach damages; §243 requires substantial impairment of contractual value. This was not a rule granting a refund for every delay or minor breach. The question was whether the promised permission-seeking process had been substantially impaired and whether the companies retained their restitution right. (Id. at 607–608, 614.)

Once that threshold was met, restitution could restore the advance payment even if performance ultimately would have been financially disadvantageous. The Court's land-sale illustration permits recovery of a $20,000 advance after wrongful refusal to convey even though the buyer had agreed to overpay for the land. Its later lottery analogy likewise distinguishes delivery of the purchased opportunity from whether that opportunity would have produced a prize. (Id. at 608, 623–624.)

## Why the Government's incorporated-law defenses failed
The Government contended that the leases' statutory and regulatory cross-references authorized suspension instead of approval. The Court rejected four arguments. An OCSLA suspension provision required a lessee's request, which the Government had not established for the disputed approval delay. An existing regulation permitting suspension for environmental analysis could not import every analysis demanded by a future statute. The leases explicitly included certain future regulations under identified statutes; their general catchall did not incorporate unknown requirements under a later statute such as OBPA. Otherwise the purchased process would become subject to unlimited later change. (Id. at 615–617.)

Nor did the incorporated authority to suspend for serious environmental harm justify this delay. Interior had itself said the plan complied with existing standards. Treating OBPA as a new finding or requirement did not make it part of the earlier contracts. Finally, the regulation Interior actually cited concerned suspensions needed to obey judicial decrees; the Government acknowledged that no such decree existed here and did not rely on that provision in the Supreme Court. (Id. at 617–618.)

OBPA also altered more than a single deadline: it delayed later plans and permits, established another review body, and privileged its findings through the Secretary's explanation requirement. The United States was the contracting obligor, so communicating that it would follow the incompatible statute communicated an intention to depart from the leases. Responding to Stevens, the majority did not accept that generally addressed legislation could never communicate repudiation to contracting parties. It expressly declined to reopen the sovereign-acts defense: the trial court had rejected it and the Government did not contest that determination here. The decision therefore cannot be read as abolishing that doctrine. (Id. at 618–620.)

## The breach was material and was not waived
The thirty-day process was not merely incidental. With multiple discretionary approvals necessary, timely and fair consideration under agreed procedures was a gateway to the companies' potential exploration rights. An announced minimum thirteen-month delay, ultimately at least four years, substantially narrowed that gateway. A plan approval in hand also could matter when seeking Commerce's action. The majority found substantial deprivation of the bargain and repudiation, rather than a technical late response. (Id. at 620–621.)

Urging a repudiating party to perform does not itself waive restitution. Acceptance of significant postrepudiation performance can have a different effect, but the Government's three examples did not establish it. Submitting the plan after OBPA showed what the companies requested, not contractual performance received. Commerce's later response substantially relied on the OBPA Panel rather than providing the consideration the original process required. And lease suspensions were independently due under the 1989 memorandum; the Government did not establish that they were significant performance of the repudiated leases. No retraction was claimed. The companies had not waived their restitution right. (Id. at 621–623.)

The state's objection therefore did not resolve the case. The Government argued that the companies could never have received all permits and suffered no loss from OBPA. That would address compensatory damages for lost performance, but the companies sought restoration of their advance payments following repudiation. Their entitlement did not depend on showing the venture would yield profit or an unconditional right to explore. (Id. at 623–624.)

## Stevens: breach did not justify the full refund
Stevens agreed that failure to approve the qualifying plan within thirty days breached the leases. He disputed repudiation, materiality, and the remedy. His fuller chronology emphasized known North Carolina opposition, federal environmental work, the companies' continued pursuit of approvals, and both sides' efforts to preserve the project. In his view, Interior had considered the plan; its fault was withholding approval, not failing to consider it fairly. Note 3 explains that distinction. (Id. at 624–630 & nn.1–3.)

He viewed legislation as addressed to the public or the Secretary rather than a contractual repudiation communicated to particular lessees. The letters announced suspension rather than cancellation, and substantial continued performance contradicted complete repudiation. His note 4 develops the legislation argument; notes 5–7 identify requested suspensions and dispute the majority's refusal to credit environmental review and continuing lease benefits. He also distinguished an external legal restriction from a change to contractual terms themselves. (Id. at 630–634, 637 & nn.4–7.)

For Stevens, materiality had to be judged at breach in the setting of the entire venture. North Carolina formally objected sixty days after the approval deadline. That independently contemplated barrier, absent override, then prevented drilling. An approval letter was only one of several necessary gateways. The majority's inference about Interior approval affecting Commerce's review was contested, and Stevens noted evidence that OBPA had not impeded the override process. He thought at most modest delay damages were appropriate; a $156 million refund relieved the companies of a known investment risk and was disproportionate. This was the dissent's view, not an alternative operative remedy awarded by the Court. (Id. at 635–639.)

## Exact result and source scope
The Court reversed the Federal Circuit and remanded both cases for proceedings consistent with restitution of the $156 million bonus payments. It did not order drilling, invalidate OBPA, guarantee profitable development, or determine every possible additional expense claim. The judgment protected the purchased contractual process despite uncertainty about ultimate permissions. (Id. at 624.)
