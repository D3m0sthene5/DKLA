Parties
Universal Camera Corporation, the employer, challenged enforcement of an unfair-labor-practice order. The
National Labor Relations Board defended its order requiring reinstatement and back pay for an employee it
found had been discharged because of protected testimony, together with a cease-and-desist direction against
discrimination for filing charges or giving testimony under the labor statute. The employee was the beneficiary
of the Board's order; the reported Supreme Court caption places the corporation and the Board opposite each
other. The trial examiner who initially assessed the evidence was an administrative decisionmaker, not another
litigant. 340 U.S. at 476, 491–493.
Material Facts
The dispute concerned why the employer discharged an employee who had testified in an earlier Board
proceeding. The evidence conflicted. The examiner, who heard the witnesses, accepted the employer's account
and recommended dismissal of the unfair-labor-practice proceeding. The Board rejected the examiner's
findings and reached a contrary conclusion. The resulting dispute therefore implicated both the substance of the
evidence and the significance of the first-hand evaluator's contrary findings. 340 U.S. at 491–496.
The casebook's editorial introduction supplies additional factual detail: the employee held a supervisory
position and had testified at a representation hearing concerning maintenance employees; the employer
asserted refusal to discipline another worker as its reason for discharge. The supervisor pointed to the interval
of nearly a month after the asserted insubordination and to the dismissing officer's earlier accusation that he
had committed perjury at the Board hearing. These details are attributed to the editorial introduction, rather
than presented as newly resolved Supreme Court fact findings. Casebook p. 913.
The Supreme Court itself emphasized that the company's witnesses gave inconsistent testimony and that
evidence showed the dismissing officer had previously been influenced against the employee because of his
appearance at the Board hearing. The Court did not determine anew that retaliation was the actual cause. It
concluded that enforcement could not be rejected outright on the employer's evidence-sufficiency argument
while also requiring proper consideration of the examiner's contrary report. 340 U.S. at 491–492, 497.

Procedural History
After the examiner recommended dismissal, the Board made contrary findings and ordered reinstatement, back
pay, and cessation of the prohibited discrimination. The Second Circuit enforced the order, over Judge Swan's
dissent. Its analysis treated the 1946 APA and 1947 labor-law amendments as leaving the relevant
judicial-review function materially unchanged. It also concluded that a reviewing court could not treat the
Board's disagreement with its examiner as a factor in testing the substituted findings. 340 U.S. at 476–477,
491–493.
The Supreme Court granted review against a background of disagreement among circuits concerning the effect
of the new legislation. It considered this case alongside NLRB v. Pittsburgh Steamship Co., but the companion
is a separate case and is not counted as completed by this brief. The Court explained the whole-record standard,
rejected the exclusion of the examiner's report from the reviewing court's assessment, and vacated the Second
Circuit's judgment. It returned the case to that court for reconsideration without directing a particular
enforcement outcome. Id. at 476, 490–497.
Issue
Under the APA and amended § 10(e) of the National Labor Relations Act, must a court determine substantial
evidentiary support from the whole record, including contrary evidence and the examiner's findings rejected by
the Board? If so, does the examiner's report bind the Board unless clearly erroneous, or instead bear on whether
the Board's decision is adequately supported? A related issue was whether the Supreme Court should itself
conclude that the Board's order could not be enforced on the existing evidence. 340 U.S. at 487–497.
Rule and Holding
Substantial evidence is relevant evidence a reasonable mind could accept as adequate, exceeding a mere
scintilla. Its substantiality must be evaluated in the setting of the entire record, including material that fairly
detracts from the agency's conclusion. A court may set aside a finding when it cannot conscientiously regard
the supporting evidence as substantial in that full context. The test still respects agency expertise and does not
allow a court to replace the agency's choice between two fairly conflicting views simply because it would have
chosen differently in a fresh determination. 340 U.S. at 477, 487–490.
The examiner's report is part of the record and must receive the significance it reasonably deserves. The Board
retains authority to make the final administrative decision and may reject an examiner's findings without
satisfying a clearly-erroneous limitation. Yet rejection does not render the report irrelevant to judicial review.
A contrary conclusion by an experienced examiner who saw the witnesses may reduce the apparent strength of
the evidence supporting the Board, especially when credibility matters. The substantial-evidence standard itself
remains the same. Id. at 492–497.
Judgment
The Court vacated the Second Circuit's judgment and remanded to that court. On reconsideration, the court of
appeals was to give the examiner's report its reasonable evidentiary significance and could reconsider the case
beyond the report's effect. It remained free to grant or deny enforcement under the articulated principles. The
Supreme Court did not itself order dismissal of the unfair-labor-practice complaint, enter judgment that the
discharge was lawful, or finally direct reinstatement. Its statement that enforcement could be permissible was
not a final approval of the Board's order. 340 U.S. at 491–492, 497.

Reasoning
1. The statutory history addressed an overly isolated search for supporting evidence. The original Wagner Act
made Board findings conclusive when supported by evidence, which the Court had construed to require
substantial evidence. In practice, some judicial formulations suggested that finding adequate support
somewhere in the record ended the inquiry, regardless of evidence undermining the conclusion. Frankfurter
described the criticisms that contributed to reform while expressly declining to endorse all accusations against
the Board; some were baseless or reckless. Their importance was their effect on legislative action. 340 U.S. at
477–480.
2. Congress retained the substantial-evidence formula while requiring whole-record review. The Court traced
the Attorney General's Committee, its divided recommendations, the APA, and the Taft-Hartley amendments.
The history contained both assertions of continuity and dissatisfaction with prior practice. More intrusive
formulations, including weight-of-the-evidence alternatives, were considered without becoming the final
standard. The Court read the enacted whole-record language as a purposeful demand for more responsible
scrutiny, while refusing to convert it into independent judicial factfinding. Id. at 480–490.
3. The labor statute and APA called for the same evidentiary-review approach in this setting. Their language
and legislative purpose aligned, and the Senate expressly sought conformity. The Court therefore rejected
divergent substantial-evidence tests depending on which of those two enactments supplied the review
provision. Its discussion concerned the relevant record-based fact review; the reproduced APA provision
separately identifies the proceedings in which substantial-evidence review applies. It does not mean every kind
of agency action is tested under a single evidentiary standard irrespective of the statutory review categories. Id.
at 482 n.15, 485–488.
4. Whole-record review preserves an allocation of responsibilities. Agencies decide matters entrusted to their
specialized experience, and their findings command respect. Courts nonetheless must decide whether the
record reasonably supports those findings. A reviewing judge's contrary preference is insufficient when the
evidence fairly permits either view. Conversely, the presence of some favorable testimony cannot force
enforcement when the entire record prevents a fair assessment that the agency has adequate support. The Court
recognized judgment rather than a mechanical numerical formula as unavoidable in applying this standard. Id.
at 488–490.
5. The Supreme Court's disagreement with the Second Circuit's general description of the amendments did not
automatically require reversal. The lower court had actually considered conflicting evidence, and
inconsistencies in the employer's testimony and evidence of the officer's hostility made enforcement a
permissible possibility. The Court therefore rejected the suggestion that the Board necessarily lost on the
record even before accounting for the examiner. It also emphasized that ordinary application of whole-record
review belongs primarily to courts of appeals, with Supreme Court intervention reserved for rare
misapprehension or gross misapplication of the standard. Id. at 490–492.
6. The Second Circuit created an unnecessary choice between binding effect and irrelevance. It reasoned that
treating disagreement with the examiner as significant would effectively give his report the conclusiveness of a
master's findings. Frankfurter rejected that inference. The labor statute placed final decisional responsibility
with the Board; the APA gave the reviewing agency the powers it would possess in making the initial decision.
Those provisions defeated a mandatory clearly-erroneous restriction on the Board, but they did not remove
initial or recommended decisions from the record. Id. at 492–495.

7. The examiner's first-hand position can have evidentiary value without determining the outcome. The APA
expressly included initial, recommended, and tentative decisions in the record. The reform legislation also
sought more independent and competent examiners. A reviewer should therefore consider an examiner's
opportunity to observe witnesses along with the consistency and inherent probability of their testimony. How
much weight the report deserves depends significantly on the role of credibility in the particular dispute. A
disagreement about demeanor-based credibility may matter differently from a disagreement about an inference
drawn from undisputed documents. The latter comparison explains the principle; the Court did not prescribe a
fixed hierarchy of all possible disagreements. Id. at 493–497.
8. The remedy left the final evidentiary judgment open. The court of appeals had to reconsider with the
examiner's findings included at their proper weight. Because the Court was correcting the reviewing method
rather than deciding the ultimate labor-law merits, it allowed either enforcement or denial on remand. Id. at
497.
Separate Opinions
The report records a short statement that Justices Black and Douglas concurred in Parts I and II but agreed with
the lower court as to Part III. They thus accepted the discussion of whole-record review and the assessment that
enforcement was not necessarily erroneous on the other evidence, while rejecting the majority's treatment of
the examiner-report issue that supplied the remand rationale. The report does not contain an independently
developed explanatory opinion by either Justice. Their referenced agreement with the lower court should not be
expanded into additional arguments that they did not write here. 340 U.S. at 497.
Frankfurter's Part III therefore must be identified as the majority's position rather than a unanimous holding.
Judge Swan's dissent was in the Second Circuit; it is distinct from Black's and Douglas's Supreme Court
participation statement. The dissent by members of the Attorney General's Committee, discussed in the
legislative history, was a committee disagreement and not a judicial separate opinion. Id. at 476, 481–483, 497.
Notes
Three standards occupy different positions in the analysis. Section 10(c)'s preponderance formulation concerns
the Board's administrative determination. Substantial evidence governs the reviewing court's examination of
the relevant agency findings. The proposed clearly-erroneous restriction on the Board's treatment of the
examiner is rejected. Treating those formulations as interchangeable would obscure the allocation of
responsibilities that produces the holding. 340 U.S. at 492–496.
The casebook's note reports that the Second Circuit later refused enforcement and used stronger language
concerning reversal of credibility findings. That subsequent appellate outcome is not the Supreme Court's
judgment, which expressly left both enforcement and nonenforcement available. Casebook p. 918; 340 U.S. at
497. The following notes discuss later NLRB review and begin a different case, Allentown Mack; those
discussions are not separate completed briefs in this edition. Casebook p. 919.
