# Rios v. Davis

**Civil Procedure · CP-142 · 373 S.W.2d 386 (Tex. Civ. App.—Eastland 1963) · November 22, 1963; rehearing denied December 20, 1963**

Rios holds that a prior factual finding does not bind a party in later litigation when it was unnecessary to the prior judgment. The earlier jury had found Rios negligent, but the judgment denied recovery against him because the claimant, Davis, was also negligent. That favorable judgment left Rios no opportunity to appeal the adverse finding. The Court of Civil Appeals therefore reversed the judgment barring his later personal-injury suit and remanded.

The dispute arose from a December 24, 1960 automobile collision. Popular Dry Goods Company first sued Davis for $443.97 in damage to its truck. Davis pleaded Popular’s contributory negligence, joined Rios as a third-party defendant, and sought $248.50 for damage to his own automobile. The jury found that Popular, Rios, and Davis had each been negligent in a manner proximately causing the collision. Applying the then-operative contributory-negligence consequences, the County Court at Law entered judgment denying Popular recovery against Davis and denying Davis recovery against Rios. (373 S.W.2d at 387.)

Rios then sued Davis in district court for $17,500 in alleged personal-injury damages. Davis asserted contributory negligence and relied on the earlier findings and December 17, 1962 judgment as res judicata and collateral estoppel. The district court sustained the preclusion plea and entered judgment for Davis. On appeal, Rios argued that the earlier findings of his own negligence were immaterial because the judgment in that action favored him. (Id.)

Justice Collings agreed. As between Davis and Rios, the controlling reason for the first judgment was Davis’s negligence. Findings that Rios failed to maintain a proper lookout and drove on the left side of the roadway did not supply a basis for denying Davis recovery. Had those findings controlled the result independently of Davis’s own negligence, they would have pointed toward a different judgment. Actual litigation and express jury findings therefore were insufficient: the findings also had to constitute a material basis for the judgment invoked as preclusive. (Id. at 387–88.)

The court linked that requirement to appellate fairness. Rios could appeal a judgment against him, not isolated findings in a judgment in his favor. Even an unsupported finding of his negligence would not have given him a right to appeal this favorable disposition. It would be unfair to make such an unreviewable finding conclusive in the next case. Drawing on Word v. Colley and other authorities, the court located the estoppel in the judgment, not automatically in every verdict answer or factual conclusion. (Id. at 388.)

Davis invoked Rio Bravo Oil, which described preclusion extending to questions directly in issue and passed upon in arriving at judgment. The court rejected the claimed conflict. That authority also limited issue estoppel in a different claim to matters upon whose determination the prior result depended. It did not require giving effect to a finding unnecessary to the prior judgment. (Id. at 388–89.)

The disposition was **reversal and remand**, not a finding that Rios was free from negligence or entitled to damages. Those questions could be litigated in the personal-injury action. The reported opinion contains no separate writings or judicial footnotes and does not state a numerical vote. Its assigned principal passage, printed pages 1296–1297, retains the central essentiality and appeal reasoning. The editors’ subsequent question whether Davis would be bound by the earlier finding of his own negligence is a question for analysis, not an additional holding in this appeal. This brief describes the historical contributory-negligence setting and does not imply that its damage consequences remain current law.
