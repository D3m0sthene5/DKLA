Kirksey v. Kirksey
8 Ala. 131 (1845)
Court, author, and source scope
The Supreme Court of Alabama reviewed a judgment on agreed facts concerning a relative's
promise of housing and land. Justice Ormond announced the court's judgment while
expressly stating that his own view favored the plaintiff. That unusual presentation makes
attribution essential: his personal consideration analysis did not command the court. The
assigned excerpt appears at printed pages 81–82. The complete report at 8 Ala. 131–33
supplies the agreed factual account and the brief disposition; it contains no extended
majority explanation that can properly be reconstructed as though the court wrote one.
Parties and material facts
The plaintiff was the widow of the defendant's brother and had several children. She was
living on public land under a lease that she had held over, was comfortably situated, and
would have attempted to secure the land. The defendant lived in Talladega County,
approximately sixty or seventy miles away. Thus, the plaintiff did not simply move from
homelessness into a gratuitously supplied house. She surrendered an existing situation and
an opportunity she otherwise would have pursued. The record does not establish that she
owned the land she left or identify a sale price she forfeited. 8 Ala. at 131–32.
On October 10, 1840, the defendant wrote a sympathetic letter concerning the death of his
brother Henry and one of the children. He described the plaintiff's circumstances as difficult,
referred to the unhealthy location and poor society where she lived, and advised her to
secure and sell any preference she had in the land. He then offered her a place to raise her
family if she came to see him, adding that he had more open land than he could tend and
wanted her and the children to do well. The letter therefore combines language of family
assistance with language that can be read as making relocation a condition of receiving the
promised benefit. Id. at 132.
Within a month or two of receiving the letter, the plaintiff abandoned her possession without
disposing of it and moved with her family to the defendant's residence. For two years he
provided comfortable housing and land to cultivate. He then required her to move into an
uncomfortable house in the woods and later required her to leave that house as well. Her
claim concerned the failure to continue the promised provision for her family, rather than the
initial failure to admit her when she arrived. Id.
Procedural history
The plaintiff obtained a verdict for two hundred dollars. The parties agreed to the reported
facts and stipulated that the judgment should be affirmed if those facts sustained the action
and reversed if they did not. The appeal was therefore presented as a legal question
concerning the sufficiency of the agreed transaction to support recovery. It was not an
appellate determination that the plaintiff had failed to prove that she moved or suffered
inconvenience. Those events were part of the agreed account. Id. at 131–33.

Issue
Did the widow's loss and inconvenience in abandoning her existing residence and moving her
family in response to her brother-in-law's letter supply consideration for his promise to
furnish a home and land, or was the undertaking a gratuitous promise whose condition she
fulfilled without creating an enforceable bargain? Id. at 132–33.
Holding and actual judgment
The court treated the defendant's promise as a gratuity and held that an action would not lie
for its breach. It reversed the plaintiff's judgment pursuant to the parties' stipulation.
Ormond reported that his colleagues held this view even though he personally inclined
toward finding sufficient consideration in the plaintiff's relocation and resulting loss. The
holding is therefore the rejection of recovery, not Ormond's stated preference for
enforcement. Id. at 133.
The reported disposition does not award the plaintiff compensation for her moving expenses,
preserve the two-hundred-dollar verdict, or order the defendant to provide housing. Nor does
it contain a separate adjudication under a developed promissory-estoppel doctrine.
Describing a reliance-based alternative today is study analysis, not the actual judgment in
1845. Id.
The court's reasoning and the limits of the report
The majority's stated characterization
The majority's reasoning is reported in one concise proposition: it regarded the promise as a
mere gratuity. The report does not set out the colleagues' names in a formal separate-opinion
heading, a multi-element consideration test, or an explanation of why every part of the letter
supported that characterization. A useful brief can identify the interpretive problem, but
should not attribute invented arguments about family policy, damages, or the Statute of
Frauds to this court. Id.
The characterization matters because a condition attached to a gift need not be the
requested price of that gift. A person may have to travel to receive promised assistance
without the promisor seeking the travel as the thing purchased. On that reading, the
plaintiff's move explains how she came to receive the proposed aid, while the underlying
commitment remains gratuitous. This explains the doctrinal significance of the majority's
conclusion; it is an analytical account of the reported characterization rather than a
quotation of an unreported judicial discussion. Casebook 82–83, notes 1–2.
Ormond's expressed disagreement
Ormond considered the plaintiff's loss and inconvenience in breaking up her household and
moving sixty miles sufficient consideration for a promise to provide a house and cultivable
land until she could raise her family. He thus treated the requested change in position as a
legally significant detriment supporting the promised benefit. Nevertheless, he announced
reversal because his colleagues disagreed. The opinion should not be labeled unanimous
simply because only one short signed text appears, and it should not be described as an
affirmance authored by Ormond. 8 Ala. at 133.

Ormond's statement also does not mean that every detrimental response to a promise must
count as consideration. The unresolved interpretive step is whether the detriment was
requested as the exchange or merely occurred in obtaining a proposed gift. The juxtaposition
of his view and the majority's result makes that distinction visible without supplying a
definitive general test that the report itself never articulates. Id.; casebook 82–83.
Study analysis: applying and testing the distinction
The letter contains facts supporting both readings. References to the widow's grief, the
welfare of her children, and the defendant's wish that they do well support an intention to
assist a family member. The conditional invitation and reference to more land than he could
tend allow an argument that he sought her presence and cultivation as something of value to
him. The report does not establish a promise by the plaintiff to work for him or surrender
crops to him, however. A brief should present that possible inference as an argument, not
add an agricultural employment contract to the agreed facts.
The plaintiff's substantial change of position is similarly clear without settling the
consideration question. She moved a family a considerable distance and gave up possession
she might otherwise have secured. Those facts strengthen a reliance argument. They do not
erase the need, under a bargain analysis, to characterize what the defendant sought in
exchange. This is why simply writing that she suffered a detriment and therefore necessarily
gave consideration would reproduce Ormond's position while overlooking the court's
contrary judgment.
For comparison with Hamer, the question is whether the requested act is the substance of
the exchange. For comparison with Ricketts, the question is whether induced reliance can
justify enforcement after ordinary consideration fails. Those comparisons help organize the
course's doctrines, but Kirksey itself resolved only the reported claim by characterizing the
promise as gratuitous. A modern hypothetical changing the legal theory or adding a definite
promise of services requires its own analysis; it is not an alternative holding in the historical
case.
Separate-opinion accounting
There is no separately printed dissent following a majority opinion. Ormond's disagreement
appears within the very passage announcing the majority's result. It has been identified
above as his expressed individual position, while the reversal and gratuity characterization
remain attributed to the court. 8 Ala. at 133.
