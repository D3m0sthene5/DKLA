# DiMichel v. South Buffalo Railway Co.
80 N.Y.2d 184, 604 N.E.2d 63 (1992) | Civil Procedure

## Two appeals and the discovery dispute

Anthony DiMichel alleged that a June 1984 fall while employed by South Buffalo Railway injured him. He sued in New York Supreme Court, Erie County, under the Federal Employers' Liability Act, Safety Appliance Act, and Boiler Inspection Act, seeking $500,000. During discovery he requested any surveillance films or videotapes of him. The railroad resisted without admitting that it possessed any. The trial court initially denied disclosure, then granted reargument after an intervening Appellate Division decision and ordered production of all surveillance materials. The Fourth Department narrowed the order to materials the railroad intended to use at trial and required their production within sixty days or preclusion. 80 N.Y.2d at 190–191 (Part I).

The complete opinion also resolves Poole v. Consolidated Rail Corp. Poole alleged a serious back injury from a defective ladder at work in August 1985. His amended damages demand was $5 million, and trial evidence included alleged impotence. The trial court compelled all surveillance material; Conrail introduced none at trial. The jury awarded $4,152,000. The Fourth Department affirmed, finding the all-material disclosure order too broad but harmless and declining to set aside the verdict. Conrail appealed as of right on the divided appellate disposition. Id. at 191–192 (Part II).

Both railroads appealed orders requiring disclosure of materials intended for use at trial. The plaintiffs had not appealed the limitations on disclosure. The Court of Appeals therefore could not grant the plaintiffs affirmative relief expanding production to every film, regardless of intended trial use. This appellate constraint is essential: affirming the narrower discovery obligation did not adjudicate a properly presented plaintiff's appeal for broader disclosure. Id. at 192 (Part III opening).

## Competing statutory approaches and interests

The four Appellate Division departments had divided over surveillance materials. One approach treated the films as the plaintiff's own statements, unconditionally obtainable under CPLR 3101(e). Other approaches treated them as litigation-preparation materials under CPLR 3101(d)(2), obtainable on a factual showing of substantial need and inability without undue hardship to obtain the substantial equivalent elsewhere. Within the latter approach, departments disagreed over how far those elements followed from the character of visual surveillance itself. Id. at 194–196.

The railroads emphasized impeachment: advance viewing might let a plaintiff tailor testimony to what the investigator captured. Plaintiffs emphasized authenticity: editing, camera angle, speed, lighting, and selective chronology can distort what appears to be an objective record. A filmed unusual or emergency activity can falsely seem ordinary or representative. New York's liberal discovery policy sought to reduce surprise and sharpen issues, but litigation-preparation materials also carried statutory protection.

## The 1992 rule and its application

The court classified surveillance films as litigation-preparation materials protected by a qualified privilege under CPLR 3101(d)(2), rather than treating them as automatically discoverable party statements. It nevertheless held that the plaintiffs here established substantial need and undue hardship. 80 N.Y.2d at 196–197.

The substantial need lay in authenticating potent visual evidence before it influenced the jury. Trial cross-examination alone might not adequately expose distortion. Careful examination could require an expert and a substantial continuance, disrupting the trial, while later disproving an apparently authoritative image might not undo its initial impact. The danger was not a factual finding that these particular defendants had doctored films; it was a reason to give the plaintiffs a fair opportunity to investigate any films to be used against them. Id. at 196.

Nor could the plaintiff's knowledge of his own physical condition supply the substantial equivalent of this particular footage. The film memorialized conditions and a moment that could not likely be replicated. Reviewing it was necessary to identify when it was taken, whether the recorded activity was typical, and how selection or presentation altered the apparent picture. That satisfied the undue-hardship element. Id. at 197.

The court recognized a genuine risk of tailored testimony but rejected the premise that films are necessarily accurate and plaintiffs necessarily dishonest. It balanced that concern by directing that surveillance films be turned over after the plaintiff had been deposed. This sequencing was a feature of the 1992 rule; it has since been superseded as described below. Id. at 197.

In DiMichel, the Appellate Division order was affirmed with costs and the certified question answered affirmatively. The plaintiff could inspect films intended for trial use, if such films existed. The decision did not establish that the railroad possessed films, determine DiMichel's injury damages, or grant a broader all-film disclosure order. Id. at 197–198.

## Why Poole nevertheless required a new trial

The court reversed in Poole because cumulative trial errors deprived Conrail of a fair trial. First, plaintiff's summation repeatedly invited the jury to infer that the unintroduced surveillance films did not help the defense. The court held that a plaintiff could not use disclosed knowledge of surveillance to turn the defendant's later strategic choice not to introduce it into prejudicial jury argument. Disclosure designed to prevent ambush was not permission for a plaintiff to engage in comparable gamesmanship. Id. at 198–199.

Second, the foreman at the accident's time was unavailable because he was undergoing open-heart surgery. The trial court barred the defense from explaining this with medical evidence. Plaintiff's counsel elicited testimony about destruction of the ladder and argued that the foreman's absence reflected reluctance to speak and a defense effort to conceal the facts. The court considered both the argument and exclusion of the real explanation improper; a sustained objection and instruction to disregard part of the argument did not make the cumulative problem harmless. Id. at 199.

Third, the trial court restricted defense expert testimony concerning earnings from alternative employment and had granted plaintiff's protective order preventing a defense vocational examination. Plaintiff's counsel then attacked the vocational expert for failing to perform that very examination and relying on records. Preventing relevant evidence and using a successfully imposed examination restriction to discredit the expert compounded the unfairness. Id. at 199–200.

The Court of Appeals held that denying the mistrial motion was an abuse of discretion and ordered a new trial on both liability and damages, with costs. It did not reach Conrail's other claims or merely reduce the damages amount. The sole judicial footnote disavows Reehil to the extent it said exceptions to improper summation comments present no reviewable legal question. Wachtler wrote; Simons, Kaye, Titone, Hancock, and Bellacosa concurred, while Smith took no part. There is no separate opinion. Id. at 200 & starred footnote.

## Assigned scope and subsequent change

The assigned excerpt occupies PDF pages 51–52, printed pages 42–45, and focuses on DiMichel's surveillance discovery. It omits the separate Poole facts, cumulative trial-error analysis, and judicial footnote. The complete opinion, both appeals and that footnote, was read before drafting. The following editorial note continues onto PDF page 53 and explains the statutory response.

Within a year, the Legislature enacted CPLR 3101(i), requiring disclosure of all covered visual or audio material, including outtakes rather than only portions intended for use. Tai Tran v. New Rochelle Hospital Medical Center, 99 N.Y.2d 383 (2003), held that this also displaced the requirement to wait until after the plaintiff's deposition. The Court of Appeals expressly confirmed both changes in Zegarelli v. Hughes, 3 N.Y.3d 64, 68 (2004), explaining that covered surveillance materials stand with ordinary relevant, nonprivileged discovery. Accordingly, DiMichel is useful for its reasoning about evidence manipulation, preparation, fairness, and appellate scope, but its qualified protection and deposition-first sequencing are historical rules, not the operative New York surveillance-discovery standard.
