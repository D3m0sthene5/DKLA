# Pepsi-Cola Bottling Company of Pittsburg, Inc. v. PepsiCo, Inc.
431 F.3d 1241 (10th Cir. 2005) | Contracts

## Holding and procedural result
The Tenth Circuit distinguished a bottler's rights under its own exclusive appointment from any right to enforce another bottler's agreement. Pittsburg Pepsi could not sue Bottling Group as an intended beneficiary of Bottling Group's territorial promises to PepsiCo. Nevertheless, PepsiCo owed Pittsburg Pepsi a contractual duty to take reasonable steps to protect its exclusive territory, and the evidence raised a triable question whether PepsiCo breached that duty. A separate interference claim against Bottling Group concerning Southeast Kansas Vending also survived summary judgment. The court reversed on those two grounds, remanded them, and affirmed the remaining summary judgment rulings. It did not award damages or find defendants liable after trial. Judge Briscoe wrote for Henry, Briscoe, and Hartz; no separate writings appear. The decision was issued December 20, 2005, although the assigned account labels it 2006. (1241, 1247, 1265–1268.)

## The appointments and changing distribution system
PepsiCo supplied concentrate and controlled the trademarks. Pittsburg Pepsi obtained its original Exclusive Bottling Appointment (EBA) in September 1959 for designated portions of Kansas and Missouri. It agreed not to distribute competing cola products, to purchase its requirements of concentrate, and to market vigorously throughout its territory using its own resources. It retained the right to set its resale prices and agreed to bear advertising expenses. Its appointment required signed written changes, contained a nonwaiver provision, and selected New York law. Later appointments separately covered other beverages, with restrictions on competing products in the corresponding flavor category. Note 1 explains that Pittsburg Pepsi no longer operated a bottling plant, but obtained manufactured products through a bottler cooperative. (1248–1249 & nn. 1–2.)

PepsiCo had emphasized perpetual territorial exclusivity in public statements and established a transshipment enforcement program (TEP) in 1984. Transshipment meant another bottler's product appeared in a territory assigned to a different bottler. PepsiCo could investigate and impose charges payable to the invaded bottler. Later, large national retailers wanted unified purchasing arrangements, while Coca-Cola's consolidation created competitive pressure for PepsiCo to align its own system. PepsiCo used customer development agreements (CDAs) with retail chains and marketplace investment agreements (MIAs) with bottlers to share rebate and advertising costs. Pittsburg Pepsi declined the MIA, avoiding those contractual contributions but also receiving no corresponding marketing funds; PepsiCo nevertheless paid the CDA costs in its territory. (1249–1251.)

In 1999, PepsiCo established anchor bottlers, including Bottling Group, which received a master bottling agreement (MBA) covering portions of forty-one states. Internal strategy materials identified unaligned bottlers and proposed withholding support or new product appointments. Pittsburg Pepsi claimed these tactics sought to weaken independent bottlers for acquisition. The court considered the evidence favorably to Pittsburg Pepsi at summary judgment; it did not adopt that alleged purpose as a trial finding. (1251–1252 & n. 4.)

## Transshipment evidence and litigation
The record addressed three outside purchasers. Grace Energy operated convenience stores in multiple territories. Bottling Group's representatives offered to meet its needs and allegedly gave the impression they could speak for Pittsburg Pepsi. Southeast Kansas Vending (SEK) bought most of its Pepsi products through a Sam's Club supplied by Bottling Group, then resold them in Pittsburg Pepsi's territory. Pittsburg Pepsi repeatedly complained; Bottling Group denied knowledge or sought source information. Sam's Club ultimately told SEK it could not buy Pepsi product for that resale. Smith Foods Vending separately switched away from Pittsburg Pepsi because of pricing concerns and stocked machines in the territory with Bottling Group products; a Bottling Group representative later told Smith to stop transshipping. These relationships did not all receive the same appellate disposition. (1252–1254.)

After an initial dismissal motion was denied, Pittsburg Pepsi filed a six-count amended complaint encompassing contract, fiduciary-duty, beneficiary, interference, conspiracy, and state restraint-of-trade theories. The district court entered summary judgment for defendants on all claims and denied a Rule 59(e) motion. Review was de novo with evidence and reasonable inferences viewed favorably to the nonmovant. Kansas choice-of-law principles led to New York law for the contract and beneficiary claims and Kansas law for the tort claims. Note 6 records abandonment of the Kansas restraint-of-trade and aiding-and-abetting claims. Note 7 concludes that the appointments principally regulated goods sales and were governed by the UCC notwithstanding incidental service obligations. (1254–1256 & nn. 6–7.)

## No perpetual right to every new product or marketing payment
Pittsburg Pepsi argued that nearly four decades of new-product appointments modified its original EBA or formed an implied agreement requiring all future appointments. The court rejected both theories. The original appointment required signed written modification, and no conforming writing established the claimed change. More fundamentally, successive new-product appointments were separate agreements, rather than repeated alterations in performance of one existing contract. An implied-in-fact agreement could not add terms covering a subject already addressed by express appointments; the appellate court affirmed on that analysis rather than the district court's lack-of-consideration rationale. Note 8 preserves the dispute's live damages aspect despite PepsiCo's subsequent offer and Pittsburg Pepsi's acceptance of the disputed appointments: the twenty-one-month withholding period might still have caused injury. (1256–1258 & n. 8.)

The implied covenant could not convert the existing appointments into a promise of all future products. Nor could it require PepsiCo to pay advertising funds contrary to Pittsburg Pepsi's express duty to advertise at its own expense. The only identified marketing funds arose through the MIA, which Pittsburg Pepsi refused to sign. Good faith protected bargained-for benefits; it did not supply additional benefits inconsistent with the agreement. (1260–1262.)

## Reasonable protection of the existing exclusive territory
The territorial claim differed because exclusivity was an express benefit. The EBA's language, the parties' course of conduct, PepsiCo's public explanation of territorial protections, and the implied covenant together required PepsiCo to take reasonable steps against competing bottlers' encroachment. The court did not make the TEP itself an express contractual term or guarantee that no transshipment would ever occur. Failure to enforce that program, combined with failure to use any other protective measure, could instead be evidence that PepsiCo had failed its broader reasonable-protection obligation. (1258–1260.)

Several facts created a genuine dispute. Lawrence simultaneously handled PepsiCo's franchise development and transshipment enforcement and Bottling Group's acquisition efforts. PepsiCo's strategies could encourage Pittsburg Pepsi customers to buy discounted products through large retailers outside its territory. Pittsburg Pepsi also repeatedly complained about transshipment, while the extent of PepsiCo's response and employee training remained unclear. A jury could infer that PepsiCo knowingly allowed encroachment to weaken a franchise it wanted acquired. That permissible inference warranted reversal of summary judgment; it was not itself a finding of bad faith. The corresponding implied-covenant theory was encompassed within this contract claim. (1259–1261.)

## Why the other bottler's contract was not enforceable by Pittsburg Pepsi
Pittsburg Pepsi separately sought to enforce Bottling Group's MBA and its predecessor's EBAs. Applying the New York tests quoted in Piccoli, the court found that PepsiCo itself could enforce those promises. The MBA authorized transshipment charges in addition to other remedies, and the EBA preserved damages and termination rights. Pittsburg Pepsi therefore was not the only person who could recover for breach. (1265.)

The agreements also lacked language showing an intention to grant Pittsburg Pepsi or other bottlers their own enforcement right. Ordinary practice was to report suspected transshipment to PepsiCo, which enforced the territorial restrictions; Pittsburg Pepsi itself followed that practice repeatedly. The resulting territorial benefit was incidental for this enforcement question. The court affirmed summary judgment on the beneficiary claim against Bottling Group even while recognizing Pittsburg Pepsi's direct contract claim against PepsiCo. A commercial benefit from another agreement did not alone establish an intended right to sue on it. (1265–1266.)

## Interference, fiduciary duties, and conspiracy
Under Kansas law, interference required a probable business advantage, defendants' knowledge, but-for causation, intentional misconduct, and resulting damage, with malice understood as actual evil-mindedness or specific intent to injure. The court held that Bottling Group's dealings concerning SEK, the quantities purchased for resale, Pittsburg Pepsi's complaints, and disputed knowledge could permit findings of causation and intentional interference. It reversed only the claim concerning SEK. PepsiCo's broader creation of a favorable climate for outside purchasing did not establish direct interference or the necessary causal link to customers' choices, so its tort judgment remained intact. (1262–1265.)

Note 10 separately addresses interference with Pittsburg Pepsi's contract with PepsiCo. Although the court was inclined to regard that appellate theory as waived, it rejected it on the merits because no evidence showed Bottling Group procured PepsiCo's breach. Pittsburg Pepsi's theory instead described PepsiCo encouraging Bottling Group's conduct. This distinct contractual-interference claim therefore did not accompany the SEK business-relations claim on remand. (1262–1263 n. 10.)

PepsiCo had not consciously assumed a fiduciary role beyond an arm's-length commercial relationship. Ordinary franchise discretion and confidence in fair dealing did not suffice. Pittsburg Pepsi retained independent pricing and information practices, and the record lacked a separate undertaking to act as its fiduciary. The court predicted Kansas law rather than claiming a controlling Kansas decision on this precise franchise relationship. Finally, conspiracy required an independently actionable wrong and multiple participants. With no actionable tort against PepsiCo established on this record, the conspiracy theory failed against it and consequently against Bottling Group. (1266–1268.)
