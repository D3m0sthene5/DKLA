Citation
Lacks v. Lacks, 41 N.Y.2d 71, 390 N.Y.S.2d 875, 359 N.E.2d 384 (1976). Court of Appeals of New York.
Chief Judge Breitel wrote the opinion. Assigned excerpt: casebook, pp. 297–300; related notes, pp. 300–303.
Full judicial opinion. The opinion concerns the difference between a statutory requirement for obtaining
relief and the court’s subject-matter competence to decide the action.
Parties
The husband was the plaintiff in the matrimonial action and sought to preserve his final divorce judgment.
The wife was the defendant, the later movant seeking to vacate that judgment, and the appellant before the
Court of Appeals. Full-opinion addition: the complete caption identifies them as Harold G. Lacks and Irene
R. Lacks. The operative dispute on this appeal was whether the wife could reopen the completed litigation on
the theory that the divorce judgment was jurisdictionally void. Casebook, pp. 297–300; full opinion, caption.
Material Facts
The parties married in New York in 1938. Their marital dispute produced repeated litigation beginning in
1953. On August 10, 1965, the husband commenced the action at issue, seeking a separation on the ground of
cruelty. While that action was pending, New York liberalized its divorce law. At the second trial, the husband
added a request for an absolute divorce, relying on the same allegations and proof that had supported his
request for separation. Casebook, pp. 297–298.
The wife’s later jurisdictional theory rested on Domestic Relations Law § 230. She asserted that the husband
had not resided in New York for a full year before the original action began and that the failure to satisfy that
statutory requirement deprived the court of power to grant a divorce. The legal significance of this assertion,
rather than a fresh factual determination of how long the husband had lived in New York, was the question
the Court of Appeals decided. Casebook, pp. 298–300.
The asserted defect must remain an assertion in the brief. The Court of Appeals expressly declined to assume
that the earlier courts had made a legal or factual error concerning residence. It noted considerable evidence
of the husband’s residence and the possibility that the residence questions already had been considered and
determined. Its point was that even an erroneous determination of the statutory requirement would not make
this judgment void for lack of subject-matter competence. Casebook, pp. 299–300.
Procedural History
After a nonjury trial, the Supreme Court dismissed the separation complaint on June 28, 1967. The Appellate
Division reversed on March 26, 1968, and ordered a new trial. The second trial resulted in a divorce
judgment on March 16, 1970. The Appellate Division affirmed that judgment, with modifications irrelevant
here, on October 26, 1972. Both the Appellate Division and the Court of Appeals denied permission for a
further appeal. Ordinary appellate review of the divorce judgment was exhausted. Casebook, pp. 297–298.
Almost two years after the appellate process ended, the wife moved to vacate the final judgment, invoking
the absence of subject-matter jurisdiction. The governing procedural vehicle was CPLR 5015(a)(4), which
permits relief from a judgment for lack of jurisdiction to render it. This was a postjudgment motion, not
another timely merits appeal. Her characterization of the residence condition as jurisdictional was the basis
for trying to overcome the otherwise final resolution. Casebook, pp. 298–300.

Issue
Does an alleged failure to satisfy the durational residence requirements of Domestic Relations Law § 230
deprive New York’s Supreme Court of subject-matter jurisdiction over a divorce action, so that a final
divorce judgment may be vacated under CPLR 5015(a)(4) after ordinary appellate review has ended? Or is
the residence requirement an element of the right to obtain a divorce that the competent trial court may
determine correctly or incorrectly? Casebook, pp. 298–300.
Rule and Holding
Subject-matter jurisdiction, in the sense relevant here, concerns a court’s competence to entertain the
category of action. It is different from the plaintiff’s ability to establish the substantive conditions necessary
to obtain a favorable judgment. Section 230’s residence requirements regulate the divorce cause of action;
they do not withdraw the Supreme Court’s competence to adjudicate matrimonial causes. An error
concerning those requirements therefore does not make the judgment void under CPLR 5015(a)(4).
Casebook, pp. 298–300; Lacks, 41 N.Y.2d at 74–77.
The wife could not reopen the final divorce judgment on the jurisdictional theory she advanced. The court did
not hold that residence requirements are optional, that every statutory requirement is nonjurisdictional, or that
consent supplies subject-matter power. It held that this particular condition was substantive rather than a
restriction on the court’s adjudicative competence. Casebook, pp. 299–300.
Judgment
The order of the Appellate Division was affirmed, without costs. The wife’s motion to vacate was denied,
and the previously entered divorce judgment remained effective. The affirmance concerns the postjudgment
proceeding; it should not be confused with the Appellate Division’s earlier affirmance of the divorce itself.
Casebook, p. 300; full opinion, disposition.
Reasoning
The label “jurisdiction” cannot do the analysis. The court began by explaining that judicial opinions
sometimes use the word to describe very different limitations: absence of a cause of action, inability to grant
a particular remedy, or a fundamental absence of adjudicative competence. CPLR 5015(a)(4) addresses the
fundamental kind. Calling the residence condition jurisdictional does not establish that it belongs in that
category. Casebook, p. 298.
The consequence of classification explains the need for precision. If an ordinary error in deciding a claim
were treated as a defect in subject-matter competence, the losing party could try to reopen a final judgment
after the normal avenues of review had closed. The usual descriptions of jurisdictional defects as
nonwaivable and rendering judgments void make an overly expansive definition especially disruptive. The
court therefore distinguished the authority to hear the controversy from the correctness of the resulting
decision. Casebook, pp. 298–299.

The source of the court’s authority was broader than the disputed element. New York’s Supreme Court
is a court of general original jurisdiction. The wife’s argument relied on statements that matrimonial relief is
statutory, but the absence of an entitlement to relief under a statute does not necessarily establish the absence
of jurisdiction over actions invoking that statute. A competent court can decide that the claimant has failed to
prove a required fact. It does not lose its competence merely because it decides that factual or legal question
incorrectly. Casebook, pp. 298–299.
Section 230 spoke to maintenance of the action, not withdrawal of competence. The statute provided that
an action for divorce or separation could be maintained only when specified residence conditions were met. It
did not state that the Supreme Court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction over matrimonial causes when a
claimant failed those conditions. Read against the constitutional grant of general jurisdiction, the provision
supplied substantive restrictions on obtaining relief. The court’s conclusion rests on the provision’s legal
function and context, not simply on the absence of one magic word. Casebook, p. 299.
An erroneous judgment and a void judgment are different things. The wife’s interpretation would make
competence depend on the court’s reaching the correct answer to an element of the claim. That collapses
adjudication into jurisdiction: every failure of proof becomes a potential failure of power. The court rejected
that approach. Ordinary legal and factual mistakes must be addressed through the procedures for correcting
such mistakes; they do not acquire the special consequences of jurisdictional defects merely because the
losing party changes their label. Casebook, pp. 299–300.
Finality was the practical concern. The dispute had gone through trial and appellate review and had
produced a judgment of several years’ standing. Treating substantive elements as perpetual jurisdictional
objections would substantially undermine res judicata—the binding effect of completed adjudication—and
the certainty that final judgments are intended to provide. The court did not need to conduct a new residence
inquiry because even the alleged error would not fit the ground for relief the wife invoked. Casebook, pp.
299–300.

Notes
What the case establishes. A rule can be mandatory without being jurisdictional. A plaintiff may be required
to satisfy it to prevail, yet failure to satisfy it may produce an erroneous judgment rather than a judgment
entered by a court without competence. The appropriate question is what the rule restricts and what
procedural consequences follow. This is the point of the comparison to Arbaugh v. Y & H Corp., 546 U.S.
500 (2006), in the casebook’s notes: Title VII’s employee-number requirement was a substantive ingredient,
not a late-stage basis for setting aside the judgment for want of jurisdiction. Casebook, pp. 302–303.
What the case does not establish. The New York discussion of standing and mootness should not be
exported as a rule that Article III standing and mootness are merely substantive elements in federal court.
Lacks interprets the scope of a New York postjudgment remedy and the state court’s competence over
matrimonial actions. The federal system has its own constitutional and statutory jurisdictional limits. The
chapter expressly begins by separating the sources of authority for the different judicial systems. Casebook,
pp. 297–298, 304.
Full-opinion addition: the Court of Appeals separately stated that the federal constitutional connections
needed to make the divorce secure against attack in other states were satisfied. That qualification matters:
deciding that § 230’s statutory duration is not jurisdictional does not eliminate distinct federal constitutional
limits on a state’s authority over marital status. Full opinion, paragraph beginning “Hence, a divorce
judgment.”
Application. Compare a general trial court erroneously finding that the residence condition has been proved
with a specialized court attempting to decide a category of dispute outside its assigned jurisdiction. The first
resembles Lacks; the second may involve a genuine competence problem. Do not answer either question
from the severity of the alleged error alone. Identify the source of the court’s authority and read the purported
restriction in context. Casebook, pp. 299–303.
