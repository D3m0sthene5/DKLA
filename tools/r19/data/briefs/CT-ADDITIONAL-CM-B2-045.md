# McDevitt v. Stokes
174 Ky. 515, 192 S.W. 681 (1917) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and issue

The Kentucky Court of Appeals decided the case on March 9, 1917, in an opinion by Chief Justice Settle. No separate opinion or numerical voting lineup is reported. McDevitt sought $800 as the alleged unpaid balance of a $1,000 promise. The Fayette Circuit Court sustained Stokes’s general demurrer because the petition did not allege sufficient consideration. McDevitt declined to plead further, and the court dismissed the action. The appeal therefore tested the legal sufficiency of the pleaded bargain, not the weight of trial testimony or a jury’s findings. The issue was whether performance already owed to an employer supplied consideration for a third person’s promise to pay a bonus for the same performance. (515.)

## Alleged facts and the promised benefit

McDevitt, an experienced and skilled driver, was employed by Shaw, who owned the mare Grace. Shaw entered Grace in the Kentucky Futurity at Lexington in October 1910, and McDevitt was to drive her. Winning this prestigious trotting race enhanced both the winner’s value and the value of related horses. The $14,000 purse included $10,000 for the winner and $300 for the owner of the winning horse’s dam, along with lesser payments for other finishers and their dams. (515–516.)

Stokes managed a stock farm and practically owned all the stock of its operating corporation, Patchen Wilkes Stock Farm. He was its president and manager. The corporation owned Grace’s sire, Peter the Great, her dam, Orianna, and her full brothers, Vladimir and Kilpatrick. The petition alleged that Stokes, influenced by his interest in the purse payment and the increased value of these horses, promised McDevitt $1,000 if he drove Grace and won the Futurity. McDevitt agreed, drove her, and won. The alleged result was a $300 payment to Stokes or his corporation as owner of the dam, a $10,000 increase in the sire’s value, and $5,000 increases in the value of each of the other three horses. These were allegations offered to establish consideration; the appellate court did not independently appraise the horses or adjudicate ownership between Stokes personally and his corporation. The report identifies $800 as the claimed balance but does not separately narrate the circumstances of payment of the remaining $200. (515–516.)

McDevitt argued that the benefits Stokes obtained, attributable in large measure to his skilled driving, supplied sufficient consideration. The court accepted the general proposition that a legally cognizable benefit to the promisor or detriment to the promisee may support a promise. Its objection concerned what counted as a new legal benefit or detriment in this particular transaction. (516–518.)

## The pre-existing duty rule applied to a third person

McDevitt’s employment by Shaw already obligated him to perform the service offered in return for Stokes’s promise. Performance of that employment obligation would produce the alleged benefits to Stokes even without the additional bonus promise. Nothing in the petition justified inferring that McDevitt would neglect his duty to Shaw if Stokes did not pay him. Thus the court did not equate an unsuccessful race with breach or find that an employee can guarantee victory. It treated the alleged undertaking to drive and win as the same service already required by McDevitt’s employment, rather than an added obligation pleaded in exchange for the bonus. (517–518.)

The court distinguished pecuniary gain from legal benefit. A promise does not acquire consideration merely because, after performance, the promisor’s balance sheet improves. In the court’s explanation, legal benefit involved acquiring a right not otherwise available, while legal detriment involved the promisee relinquishing a right otherwise exercisable. The authorities cited by McDevitt did not involve the same existing obligation and therefore did not undermine this reasoning. The court relied on contract treatises to explain why performance or a promise of performance of an existing legal or contractual duty was insufficient. (517–519.)

Crucially, the court applied the rule even though the existing employment duty was owed to Shaw rather than to Stokes. It cited Ford v. Crenshaw as Kentucky authority, dating to 1822, rejecting an action on a later third-party promise to pay for an act to which the promisee was already contractually and legally bound. Accordingly, the fact that Stokes was an outsider to the employment bargain did not itself create fresh consideration. That is this historical Kentucky decision’s rule; it should not be silently replaced with a later Restatement position or a different jurisdiction’s treatment of third-party promises. (519–520.)

## Why the asserted economic benefit did not change the result

The opinion illustrated the difference between actual and legal benefit through Eblin v. Miller’s Executors. There, a tenant already bound to remain for the relevant rental period threatened to abandon the premises unless the landlord repaired a stairway. The later repair promise, based on the tenant’s agreement not to leave in breach of his obligation, lacked consideration. The court’s discussion also explains the allegations missing from that earlier case’s theory of defective repair; it does not decide a new premises-liability claim in McDevitt’s action. The analogy was that a party gains no new bargained-for legal performance when the promisee merely carries out an existing duty. (520.)

Applied to this petition, the gain to Stokes was incidental to performance that McDevitt already owed Shaw. McDevitt had alleged neither an added responsibility nor a loss resulting from the separate undertaking to Stokes. Therefore the valuable race result and the relatives’ increased market values could not substitute for consideration. The court concluded that Stokes’s promise imposed no enforceable liability on the alleged facts. (520–521.)

## Holding, disposition, and reserved question

The petition failed to state a cause of action because the promised and completed service was already required by McDevitt’s employment. The Court of Appeals affirmed the judgment dismissing the petition after the sustained demurrer and McDevitt’s refusal to amend. It did not award the claimed $800, direct a trial, or decide that the performance had not occurred. Because lack of consideration resolved the case, it expressly declined to decide whether the arrangement also violated public policy. (515, 521.)
