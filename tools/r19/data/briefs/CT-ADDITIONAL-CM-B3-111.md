# L. Albert & Son v. Armstrong Rubber Co.
178 F.2d 182 (2d Cir. 1949) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
The Second Circuit decided the parties’ cross-appeals on November 29, 1949. Chief Judge Learned Hand wrote for a panel that also included Judges Swan and Clark; the report contains no separate opinion. The court sustained the buyer’s rejection of substantially delayed machinery and the denial of most of its claimed reliance losses, but modified the judgment to add interest on a converted motor and permit a foundation-expense setoff, subject to the seller’s opportunity to prove that timely performance would have produced a losing contract. The judgment was affirmed as modified. (182, 184, 191.)

## Facts and procedural history
Albert agreed by an exchange of letters in December 1942 to sell Armstrong four “Refiners,” machines that reconditioned old rubber. The agreement was an indivisible purchase of all four. Two arrived in August 1943; the remaining two arrived on August 31 or September 8, 1945. Armstrong rejected all four in October 1945, on a date the record did not establish. Albert sued for the $25,500 price; Armstrong counterclaimed for breach. The trial judge found that the machines conformed to specifications or could have been brought into conformity with slight trouble and expense, but that the seller’s delay justified rejection. He dismissed both the price claim and the damages counterclaim. He nevertheless awarded Albert the value, without interest, of a 300-horsepower motor and accessories Armstrong put to use on February 20, 1946. (184–185, 188–189.)

The delivery history complicated the buyer’s position. Armstrong repeatedly pressed for performance but accepted the first two machines without objecting to their lateness. Later correspondence and negotiations continued to recognize a subsisting contract. Armstrong installed the first two in August 1944, proposed an unsuccessful resale to Albert in December, and again requested the remaining machines in February and March 1945. Its March 28 letter asked Albert to complete the order and contemplated payment thirty days after all four arrived. Hand treated the previous delay as waived: after that letter, the buyer remained obliged to accept delivery within a reasonable time. The question was whether another five months was reasonable. The machines served a wartime demand for reclaimed rubber, and the end of fighting threatened the temporary shortage on which that business depended. Delivery at the end of August was too late in those circumstances. (185–186 & n. 1.)

## The seller’s acceptance and motor arguments
Albert invoked the Uniform Sales Act’s three forms of acceptance: communicating acceptance, acting inconsistently with the seller’s ownership, or retaining goods beyond a reasonable time. An October 11, 1945 bookkeeping letter acknowledging the amount of Albert’s claim did not establish an intention to accept the machines. A similar earlier inquiry had concerned only the first two, although both parties knew that the contract was entire. Nor was it clear whether the buyer’s rejection preceded or followed the later letter. The seller failed to prove an effective condonation. (186–187 & n. 2.)

Armstrong’s depreciation entry for tax purposes was also insufficient. It might reveal a claimed state of mind about ownership, but it did not interfere with Albert’s ownership or dispose of the machines. The timing of that entry was uncertain as well. The court distinguished an assertion made to the taxing authority from an act against the goods themselves. (187 & nn. 3–4.)

Using the motor was a conversion, but it occurred four months after an unequivocal rejection. It did not retract the rejection and force Armstrong to pay the price of all four machines. Hand considered the statutory provisions and authorities concerning use of goods, stressing the difference between conduct before rejection and a later use of part of rejected equipment. Here the machinery had become substantially valueless for its intended purpose, Albert had left it unclaimed for months, and the motor was salvaged for another use. Those circumstances did not establish abandonment, but they made compelled acceptance of the whole purchase inappropriate. The retention interval before rejection was at most approximately a month, and the seller had shown no additional prejudice from it. (187–188 & nn. 5–8.)

The seller could recover the motor and accessories’ $4,590 fair market value on a quasi-contractual basis. Because that value was ascertainable at the time of conversion, Connecticut law permitted interest from February 20, 1946. Recovery for the converted component remained distinct from recovery of the contract price. (188 & nn. 9–10.)

## Reliance damages and the losing-contract limitation
Armstrong claimed no lost profits. It instead sought $118,478 spent on its reclaim department, $27,555.63 spent on scrap rubber, and $3,000 spent on foundations for the refiners. The trial finding that the late machines were only one possible cause of the department’s failure was not clearly erroneous. Armstrong had disposed of its scrap rubber in April and May 1945 and, so far as appeared, did not attribute the department’s collapse to the missing refiners until its May 1947 counterclaim. A possible causal connection could not support shifting the entire venture’s expenses to Albert. (188–189.)

The foundations stood differently: they were necessary preparation specifically for the promised machines. The buyer had not proved the refiners’ prospective net earnings or eventual scrap value and therefore had not proved an expectation award. Yet failure to establish those figures did not automatically bar reimbursement of the preparatory expenditure. If performance would have been profitable, recovering the foundation cost would compensate a loss caused by breach. If performance would instead have failed to cover the buyer’s outlay, unrestricted reliance recovery would make the seller insure the buyer’s venture. (189.)

Hand resolved that tension by allocating the burden of proving a losing contract to the breaching seller. The promisee may recover its necessary preparatory expenditure, but the promisor may reduce the award by the amount it proves the promisee would have lost with full performance. Uncertainty about the promised performance’s value did not justify placing that entire risk on the injured party when the breach made the inquiry necessary. This rule does not knowingly place the promisee in a better position than performance would have produced. (189–191 & nn. 11–21.)

The opinion did not describe Connecticut precedent as unanimous. It considered potentially conflicting decisions, including the difference between recovering money already paid to a breaching promisor and recovering payments to others. A repayment case might involve disgorgement of a benefit received, whereas the foundation expenditure did not enrich Albert. Hand found the authorities inconclusive enough to adopt the stated burden rule, supported by the Restatement and scholarly treatments of necessary or essential reliance. The assignment’s quotation presents this rule; the actual opinion also explains its causation and evidentiary limits. (189–191.)

## Exact disposition and assigned-source relationship
The modified judgment added motor interest from February 20, 1946. It permitted Armstrong to set off $3,000, with interest from October 1945, but allowed Albert a further hearing to prove and deduct any loss Armstrong would have incurred had the refiners been delivered on or before May 1, 1945. Thus the court did not make an unconditional final $3,000 award or award the department’s entire investment. (191.)
