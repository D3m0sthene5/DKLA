# Shields v. Gross
58 N.Y.2d 338 (1983) | Contracts

## Decision and division
New York’s Court of Appeals held that a child model could not disaffirm unrestricted written consents her mother had executed under sections 50 and 51 of the Civil Rights Law and then obtain statutory relief against publication within their terms. Judge Simons wrote for Chief Judge Cooke and Judges Jones and Wachtler. Judge Jasen dissented in part, joined by Judges Fuchsberg and Meyer, making the principal division 4–3. The March 29, 1983 decision modified the Appellate Division’s order by striking its additional advertising-and-trade injunction, and affirmed as modified, with costs to defendant. The trial court’s narrower, stipulated injunction remained. This account concerns the historical statutory scheme interpreted in that decision. (338, 341, 347, 353.)

## Photographs, consent, and later objection
Brooke Shields obtained modeling work through Ford Model Agency. In 1975, when she was ten, photographer Garry Gross took photographs for a project financed by Playboy Press, initially called Portfolio 8 and later Sugar and Spice. Her mother and legal guardian, Teri Shields, signed two consents before the sessions. The written permission covered use and reuse, publication and republication through different media for any purpose, and waived approval of the finished photographs and eventual use. The majority reproduced the terms in its footnote. (341–342 & star note.)

The photographs appeared in the intended publication and, with mother and daughter’s knowledge, in other publications and enlarged store-window displays. Shields herself later used them in a book after her mother obtained Gross’s authorization. In 1980 she learned of publication in a French magazine and contemplated further uses; she unsuccessfully tried to buy the negatives. In 1981 she sued in tort and contract for damages and a permanent injunction against all further use. (342.)

## Trial findings and appellate positions
Special Term initially issued a preliminary injunction because the asserted limits or invalidity of consent raised factual questions. Following a nonjury trial, the court found the consents unrestricted in time and use and dismissed the complaint. On Gross’s stipulation, however, it permanently barred use in pornographic magazines or publications predominantly appealing to prurient interests and imposed a duty to police that use. The Appellate Division accepted the unrestricted-consent findings but, through several opinions, added a permanent injunction against advertising or trade uses. (342–343.)

Two Appellate Division justices relied on common-law disaffirmance. Justice Kupferman also invoked the General Obligations Law’s child-performer provisions and a possible three-year limit. Justice Asch treated the transaction as a sale of pictures and proposed UCC unconscionability; Justice Carro would have left the trial judgment in place because statutory consent supplied a defense. These were distinct lower-court positions. The Court of Appeals did not decide unconscionability: it had not been raised at trial and the parties had offered no evidence on it. On cross-appeals Gross sought restoration of the trial judgment; Shields sought broader relief, retention of the appellate order, or a new trial. With the underlying findings accepted, the remaining controlling issue was the legal effect of her parent’s consents. (343–344.)

## Statutory consent and common-law disaffirmance
The majority began with New York’s lack of a common-law privacy cause of action. The Legislature had created criminal and civil protections through sections 50 and 51 after Roberson. Section 50 specified written consent by the person depicted or, for a minor, the parent or guardian; section 51 made consent obtained as provided in section 50 a bar to the civil remedy. The majority construed the statutory remedy strictly because it displaced the former common-law position. Once written consent was obtained, use was permissible within its terms. (344.)

The majority acknowledged that an infant ordinarily could disaffirm personal consent or consent given on the infant’s behalf. But the Legislature could alter that right or authorize binding agreements. Here, it had created a new cause of action and prescribed the means of obtaining the minor’s consent to avoid liability. For the majority, making a parent’s statutory written consent binding effectuated that arrangement; the statute needed no additional express prohibition of disaffirmance. Because these consents met the statutory requirements, the common-law right did not undo them. This was a conclusion about the statutory consent class, rather than a declaration that every parent-made agreement binds a child. (344–345.)

## Child-model classification and limits
The majority rejected the claim that lack of prior court approval under General Obligations Law section 3-105 invalidated the consents. As then framed, that provision covered performing artists and professional athletes, and other education and labor provisions distinguished child models from child performers. Judicial approval protected counterparties against later disaffirmance of executory performer contracts; it was not a device for enlarging disaffirmance rights. The majority treated the Civil Rights Law consent mechanism as serving a similar interest in transactional certainty. (345–346.)

Session-by-session work for numerous photographers and comparatively modest fees made the performer approval process impractical for ordinary modeling. The fee here was $450. The majority regarded parental judgment as the Legislature’s substitute for court approval in this setting. It also noted that Shields had not asserted obscenity or pornography, that the trial court found the photographs nonpornographic, and that the stipulated injunction already prohibited use in pornographic publications. It therefore did not decide enforcement of a contract violating that public-policy prohibition. Finally, statutory protection for a photographer extended only as far as consent: a parent could limit authorized uses and a user could not exceed those limits. (346–347.)

## Jasen’s dissent
Jasen would have affirmed the broader Appellate Division protection. He emphasized that Shields sought to prevent future uses, rather than unwind benefits from past publication. In his view, parental consent and common-law disaffirmance were complementary safeguards for children. Requiring written parental consent avoided unauthorized initial exploitation but did not show legislative intent to eliminate the child’s later protection against a parent’s poor judgment or exploitation. The State’s parens patriae role remained necessary when parental protection failed. (347–351.)

The dissent contrasted statutes expressly removing disaffirmance in clearly beneficial transactions with the Civil Rights Law’s absence of express language and photographs’ potential for harmful future use. It regarded omission of models from section 3-105 as leaving common-law protection intact, while that statute’s court approval, term limits, earnings supervision, and power to revise harmful performer arrangements demonstrated safeguards protecting children as well as business. Its sole footnote distinguished disaffirmance of a voidable consent from refusing enforcement of an agreement void from inception for violating public policy. These were dissenting reasons, not controlling holdings. (351–353 & star note at 351.)

## Exact result and source scope
The court removed only the Appellate Division’s further advertising-and-trade injunction and otherwise affirmed as modified, with costs to Gross. It did not eliminate the trial court’s stipulated restriction on pornographic or predominantly prurient publication, award Shields damages, or adopt the lower concurrence’s unconscionability theory.
