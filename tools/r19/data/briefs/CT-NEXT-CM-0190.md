# Delacy Investments, Inc. v. Thurman and Re/Max Real Estate Guide, Inc.
693 N.W.2d 479 (Minn. Ct. App. March 22, 2005) | Contracts

## Facts and financing arrangement
Delacy Investments, doing business as Commission Express (CE), factored real-estate agents’ future commissions. In November 2001, agent Steven Thurman granted CE a security interest in his present and future accounts receivable under a master repurchase and security agreement. CE filed a financing statement. In February 2003, Thurman entered an independent-contractor agreement with Re/Max requiring payment of overhead expenses. Under its terms, he was entitled only to commissions exceeding his past-due obligations; the portion needed to satisfy arrearages belonged to Re/Max. (481.)

In April 2003, Re/Max purportedly acknowledged CE’s interest in a commission from one property, Javelin Avenue. Authenticity of that acknowledgment was disputed, but the court found the dispute immaterial to its decision. The transaction at issue was a different commission, from Keller Lake Drive: on April 22, CE purchased Thurman’s $10,000 receivable, paid $8,000 immediately, and promised $1,000 more when it collected. Re/Max terminated Thurman on June 7 for performance problems, delayed earnest-money deposits, and customer complaints. His overhead arrears were $11,126.38, exceeding the commission. Re/Max applied the commission to those arrears and refused CE’s demand for payment. (482 & nn.1–2.)

## Litigation and issues
CE sued, and Re/Max counterclaimed against Thurman. The district court entered summary judgment for Re/Max because Thurman had no payable commission once the agreed arrears limitation was applied; his assignee could not obtain greater rights. Thurman defaulted and was not a party to the appeal. An earlier appeal was dismissed because the purported final judgment left claims unresolved. After final disposition of all claims in July 2004, the court accepted the present appeal under its unusual procedural circumstances. (482–483 & nn.3–5.)

CE argued that the district court improperly substituted a common-law assignment principle for revised UCC Article 9. It also claimed that notice of its earlier assignment prevented Re/Max from asserting later setoffs or entering a later agreement that diminished CE’s rights. The appellate court reviewed statutory construction de novo and affirmed. A separate attack on whether Re/Max properly moved for summary judgment was not developed in CE’s opening brief and could not be revived in reply. (482–484 & n.4; 486.)

## Governing rules and holding
Minnesota Statutes § 336.9-404(a), implementing UCC § 9-404, separates two categories of limitations on an assignee. Under subsection (a)(1), assigned rights remain subject to all terms of the agreement between account debtor and assignor and to defenses or recoupment claims arising from the transaction that gave rise to the contract. Under subsection (a)(2), other defenses or claims are available if they accrued before the debtor received authenticated notification of assignment. The official comment explains that notice timing does not affect a defense arising from the underlying transaction within (a)(1). (484.)

CE’s claim fell under (a)(1). The Re/Max agreement defined Thurman’s entitlement as the amount remaining after overhead arrears. At the closing, his arrears exceeded the commission, leaving him no collectible balance. Because CE’s rights were subject to that agreement, it likewise had no amount to collect from Re/Max. The familiar rule that an assignee receives no greater rights than the assignor was consistent with Article 9, rather than a competing doctrine displacing it. (483–485, 487.)

## Reasoning and notice argument
A perfected assignment of future receivables does not manufacture a payment entitlement greater than the contract that creates the receivable. The court compared authorities interpreting both former § 9-318 and revised § 9-404, including decisions recognizing contractual recoupment and binding a receivables assignee to an underlying arbitration term. These illustrated the same point: the assignee takes the payment claim with its contractual limitations. The opinion distinguished recoupment arising from the same transaction from setoff based on a broader, independent demand. (484–486 & n.7.)

CE identified three asserted notices: its 2001 financing statement, a March 2003 default notice concerning the Javelin Avenue commission, and the April 2003 Keller Lake assignment. The court stated that Re/Max apparently had notice before the independent-contractor agreement, but did not make notice chronology decisive. Section 9-201 makes a security agreement effective subject to other UCC provisions, and § 9-404(a)(1) supplied such a limitation. CE’s proposed cutoff under (a)(2) did not govern this contract-based defense. The actual commission entitlement remained limited by the agreement even if notice preceded accrual of the defense. (486–487.)

The court added that CE could have arranged contractual protection against unfavorable terms, as a receivables assignee in a comparable case could have restricted the assignor’s ability to enter agreements containing arbitration clauses. It did not hold that CE’s unilateral financing statement could alter Re/Max’s payment obligation. Nor did it extend a prior case concerning an account debtor that paid the assignor after notice and only later claimed setoff; that decision rested on materially different facts. (485–486 & n.6.)

## Judgment and source scope
The Court of Appeals affirmed summary judgment for Re/Max. Judge Halbrooks wrote for a panel with Huspeni and Crippen; no division or separate opinion is reported. The decision left the brokerage owing CE nothing under the assigned commission, while Thurman’s separate default judgment was outside the appeal. (482 n.3; 487.)
