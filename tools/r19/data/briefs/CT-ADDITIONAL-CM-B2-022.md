# Wilberg v. Hyatt
2012 UT App 233, 285 P.3d 1249 | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Judge Roth wrote for the Utah Court of Appeals. Judge Christiansen concurred, and Judge Orme joined the opinion while writing separately about the scope of remand. The court affirmed partial summary judgment against the grandsons’ claim to enforce an oral promise of land, but reversed the rejection, after a bench trial, of their unjust-enrichment claim concerning irrigation installations. It ordered reconsideration of that limited restitution claim, not conveyance of the ranch or an appellate damages award. The rejection of a broader claim for general farm labor was not challenged and remained undisturbed. (¶¶1, 20–22, 26–30.)

## Alleged bargain and contributions
Ross and Wes Wilberg alleged that their grandfather Albert Hyatt promised Ross that Rock Canyon Ranch would pass to the grandsons if they worked for Hyatt until his death and then cared for his son, their uncle, until the uncle’s death. They asserted nearly daily work and management over about a decade without compensation. They also furnished labor and materials for irrigation at Rock Canyon Ranch and labor for a similar system at Hyatt’s Clawson Ranch. Ross used federal irrigation funding available to him subject to a lifetime limit. According to him, using his funding was advantageous because it covered 90 percent of predetermined costs, compared with Hyatt’s 75 percent eligibility. The Clawson materials instead came from funding available to Hyatt and his daughter. These distinctions matter to the benefit conferred; the full opinion does not say that the grandsons paid every irrigation cost personally. (¶¶2–3 & nn.1–2, 24.)

Hyatt sold Rock Canyon Ranch to Clyde Magnuson. The grandsons filed a lien for irrigation contributions and sued both men. Their claims against Magnuson depended on showing that an enforceable earlier contract gave them a superior claim to the land. Partial summary judgment for Hyatt on the contract claim therefore also disposed of the claims against Magnuson. A later bench trial rejected unjust enrichment. The Court of Appeals had already rejected a challenge to the notice of appeal in a prior order; footnote 3 declined to revisit that settled objection without a demonstrated error. (¶4 & n.3.)

## Part performance and evidentiary burden
Utah’s land-transfer statute generally requires a subscribed writing. Equity can enforce a partly performed oral agreement, but the claimant must establish clear and definite contractual terms and clear acts done in reliance on them, ordinarily exclusively referable to the bargain. Acts equally explicable without the agreement do not demonstrate the needed change of position. The doctrine rests on estoppel, including the prospect that refusing enforcement would work a fraud where damages are inadequate. (¶7.)

The court did not treat each requirement as wholly rigid in isolation. Strong independent proof or admission of a definite contract can relax the need for otherwise unexplained reliance acts, provided performance fulfills its terms. When the contract itself is disputed and unclear, exclusively referable conduct becomes vital. Across those formulations, the ultimate standard is clear and convincing evidence. Summary judgment therefore required consideration of whether a reasonable factfinder could satisfy that elevated standard, not merely whether the grandsons disputed a possible inference. (¶¶8–11, 16.)

The grandsons said the central promise was clear, while acknowledging changes in some details. They also relied on later will, trust, and LLC documents. The trust and LLC were never finalized or funded. More importantly, their provisions preserved Hyatt’s control: the LLC gave him 99 percent ownership and sole management authority, including discretion to dispose of land, and the trust authorized him to sell property. His uncontested affidavit explained that those devices allowed inheritance if he wished but preserved his ability to sell. They did not strongly corroborate an unconditional duty to transfer Rock Canyon Ranch at death. The will added little to the issue, as footnote 4 explains. (¶¶12–14 & n.4.)

The asserted work also was not exclusively referable to the alleged bargain. Hyatt had worked extensively on the Wilberg family’s own ranch, allowing use of his machinery and equipment, both before and during the relevant period. The grandsons’ work could reasonably be explained as reciprocal family labor. Thus neither their proof of the contract nor their reliance conduct reached the clarity needed for the exception. The court affirmed the contract summary judgment; it did not simply announce that relatives can never contract about land or that any work on land automatically defeats the statute. (¶¶15–16.)

## Procedural objection to summary judgment
The moving papers set out only six separately numbered factual statements and placed additional supporting facts in the argument section, contrary to Rule 7’s format. A district court has discretion to treat such noncompliance as technical rather than refuse to consider the merits. The grandsons identified no prejudice, such as inability to respond to the additional facts. The appellate court found no abuse of discretion. Their further proposed distinction between requirements for movants and respondents was unpreserved and inadequately supported, so the court did not reach it. This was a procedural ruling independent of the merits of part performance. (¶17 & n.5.)

## Restitution despite an unenforceable land promise
Unjust enrichment requires a benefit the recipient knows or appreciates under circumstances making uncompensated retention unjust. Its measure is the value of the benefit to that recipient, rather than automatically the claimant’s loss or the market price of the claimant’s services. The district court separately assessed general labor and irrigation. Only its irrigation disposition was challenged on appeal. (¶¶18–20.)

The trial court recognized that Hyatt received a benefit from the irrigation but rejected recovery because the grandsons had combined their proof of materials and work on the two ranches without allocating each item to Rock Canyon. That misunderstood the claim. They sought restitution from Hyatt for benefits on both properties, not a recovery limited to one property as though the ranch itself were the defendant. Both installations benefited the same recipient. Even if the Clawson work were not part of the same alleged exchange, its location did not make its benefit to him legally irrelevant. The pleadings and evidence did not restrict the restitution claim to Rock Canyon alone. (¶¶21–22.)

The trial court also said that federal payment meant the grandsons were not out any cash. The appellate court could not tell whether that observation contributed to the denial. If it did, it was erroneous: benefit to Hyatt did not vanish because the resources were government-funded. Moreover, Ross had exhausted much of his limited lifetime entitlement, which he otherwise could have used on his own land. That foregone use explained how he supplied the resource, while the controlling valuation still concerned the benefit received. The court remanded irrigation restitution for reconsideration consistent with these points and denied unsupported appellate attorney fees. (¶¶23–26 & n.6.)

## Orme’s concurrence and assigned problem
Orme agreed with the opinion but read the existing benefit finding as leaving only valuation on remand. He would direct the trial court to fix restitution, rather than undertake an open-ended reconsideration. His fence-painting example distinguishes value conferred from the painter’s acquisition cost, and his sole note contrasts disgorgement of unjustly retained benefits with compensatory damages. That more explicit characterization of the remand is his concurrence; the court’s formal instruction is the reconsideration stated in paragraph 26. (¶¶28–30 & concurrence n.1.)

The assigned ranch problem asks about part performance, estoppel, reciprocal family work, and restitution. The actual opinion analyzes estoppel within part performance rather than deciding a separate general promissory-estoppel cause of action. All thirty paragraphs, the six lead notes, the complete concurrence and its note, all original report pages, and the whole assigned problem were read. This account describes the 2012 judgment without current-law certification; separate source review is tracked in the accompanying register.
