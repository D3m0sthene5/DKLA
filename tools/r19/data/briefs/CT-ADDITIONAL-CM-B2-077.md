# Lawrence v. Cain
144 Ind. App. 210, 245 N.E.2d 663 (1969) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

The Indiana Court of Appeals decided the case on March 24, 1969. Presiding Judge Lowdermilk wrote, with Judges Carson, Cooper, and Sullivan concurring; the reported decision was unanimous, without a separate writing. It reversed a judgment awarding a photography-studio purchaser an injunction and $1,727.50 in damages. The lower court was directed to restate its general findings to conform to the evidence and the appellate opinion and to modify its decree accordingly. Reversal did not excuse the seller's established breach; it corrected the overbroad restraint and unsupported monetary recovery. The report states that no petition for rehearing was filed. (144 Ind. App. at 210, 212–215, 218–220.)

## Sale and successive agreements

The purchaser acquired the Lawrence-Krehe Studio in Marion on May 13, 1965, for $5,500. The seller promised not to compete with that studio in Grant County and not to permit others to use its name there. A same-day supplement and a May 17 supplement followed. The second supplement imposed a distinct promise on the purchaser: she would not compete with the seller for school-photography work at county schools, except Marion High School and Bennett High School. Thus, the actual bargain combined the seller's original restriction with the purchaser's later restriction protecting the seller's reserved school business. (Id. at 212–213, 215, 218.)

In late July or early August 1965, the purchaser let the seller use the studio while she was away to photograph Mississinewa High School students. The seller then sent proofs in envelopes and statements carrying the Lawrence-Krehe name. The court described this as an undisputed breach of the supplemented agreement, despite the permitted studio use. Later in 1965 the purchaser photographed Marion College students. In summer 1966 a heated dispute occurred; she reportedly declared the contract worthless and said she would compete in whichever schools she wished. The record did not explain the cause of that argument. (Id. at 215–217.)

## Claims, judgment, and review

The purchaser sued to enjoin the seller from photography business and solicitation throughout Grant County, including work on community-school yearbooks, and requested $10,000 in damages. The seller pleaded that the purchaser had breached first by soliciting or selling excluded school work. Following a bench trial, the court issued a sweeping permanent prohibition on photography work, studio operation, and solicitation and awarded $1,727.50. (Id. at 213–214.)

The entry's unusual form did not prevent appeal because it intelligibly determined the parties' rights and granted relief. The appellate court noted both the seller's failure to move to modify the judgment and the ordinary rule requiring that procedure to correct a partly valid erroneous judgment. Nevertheless, it expressly elected to address the merits rather than dispose of the case on technical grounds. The seller's new-trial motion challenged the amount of recovery, the legality of the findings, and evidentiary sufficiency. This reported treatment should not be converted into a categorical abolition of the modification-motion requirement. (Id. at 214.)

## Was Marion College a school under this agreement?

The seller argued that the purchaser's college work violated the school-photography restriction and justified treating the contract as breached. The court applied ordinary usage, giving plain contractual language its expressed meaning unless there was a good reason to understand it differently. Although the seller asserted that dictionary definitions would include a college, he did not set those definitions out. The court understood schools in this agreement as local educational institutions below college or university level, rather than all institutions that could generically be called schools. The purchaser therefore did not breach by photographing Marion College students. (Id. at 216–217.)

The seller also relied on the purchaser's statements during the later argument. The court did not decide whether those words themselves amounted to breach. The seller had already breached by using the studio's name on envelopes and letterheads. Applying the rule that a party first responsible for a substantial or material breach cannot invoke the other party's later refusal as a basis to rescind or avoid performance, the court rejected that defense. It neither found the heated statement unobjectionable as a general matter nor construed it as an adjudicated anticipatory repudiation. (Id. at 215, 217.)

## Why the injunction and damages could not stand

The original restriction had to be read with the supplemental reservation. The seller retained exclusive school work except at the two identified high schools, where both could compete. The purchaser could also perform college work under the meaning just adopted. The later reservation necessarily implied permission for the seller to develop photographs and maintain a studio for his allowed school business. Fairly implied terms counted as though expressed. A decree barring every photography activity in the county exceeded the supplemented bargain; injunctive protection could not extend beyond what reasonably protected the purchaser's contractual interests. (Id. at 217–218.)

The damages record also failed. The purchaser offered gross sales for the last seven months of 1965, all of 1966, and the first five months of 1967. Photography was seasonal, with the latter half of the year strongest, and other established and transient competitors operated locally. She supplied no net-income measure and no comparison with the seller's earlier business performance. Damages for breach of a noncompetition promise need not be precisely measurable, but evidence must show an actual impairment of goodwill, loss of value, or lost profits caused by the breach. The court found a complete failure of proof rather than a sufficient basis for the $1,727.50 award. (Id. at 218–219.)

## Disposition and the casebook adaptation

The appellate court reversed with instructions to restate the findings and modify the decree consistently with its opinion. It did not itself draft the revised injunction, award a replacement sum, or declare all contractual restraints invalid. (Id. at 220.)

The assigned drafting problem says the seller promises not to compete for school work and asks whether the seller can photograph college students. The actual supplemental school clause instead restrained the purchaser, and the adjudicated college question concerned her work. That difference is visible in both original texts and is preserved here rather than treating the problem as a verbatim judicial fact statement. The opinion establishes that this agreement's school category excluded colleges; applying that construction to the casebook's changed covenant is a separate exercise, not an additional holding. (C&M printed 565, PDF 258; 144 Ind. App. at 213, 216–218.)
