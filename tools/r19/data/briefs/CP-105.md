# Payne v. S. S. Nabob
302 F.2d 803 (3d Cir. 1962) | Civil Procedure | A pretrial report controls the trial's issues and witnesses

## The theory specified before trial

Hosea Payne brought a personal-injury admiralty action involving the S. S. Nabob, North German Lloyd, and the impleaded stevedore, Lavino Shipping Company. The complete opinion does not narrate the accident in detail. The important facts concerned Payne's own pretrial description of his case. His memorandum relied on a defective winch as the basis for unseaworthiness. Judge Van Dusen's signed pretrial report incorporated that account. The report, reproduced in judicial footnote 2, described brakes that would not hold in neutral and a port winch that had not worked from early that morning. It also recorded the opposing account that Payne and fellow workers caused the injury and that the winches were not defective. These were stated party positions, not findings that either account was true. 302 F.2d at 805-806 & n.2.

At trial, Payne's lawyer added improper loading as an important element of unseaworthiness during the opening statement. The impleaded stevedore objected because that theory was outside the pretrial memorandum and report. The trial judge sustained the objection, excluded two witnesses whom Payne had not listed, and denied a requested continuance. Payne appealed, challenging the applicability and binding force of the pretrial procedure as well as the refusal to permit amendment or delay. Id. at 805, 807.

## Admiralty was subject to the standing pretrial procedure

The Eastern District of Pennsylvania adopted its standing order for pretrial conferences in cases other than protracted cases on October 23, 1958. Payne argued that it did not cover admiralty until local Admiralty Rule 31 took effect on January 1, 1961—after his memorandum, conference, and trial. The full opinion addresses this objection, although the assigned passage omits most of that discussion. Id. at 805.

Judge McLaughlin explained that the standing order's general category of civil cases included admiralty rather than carving it out. The district had actually used that procedure in admiralty matters from the order's adoption, and Rule 16's pretrial procedure had already been brought into admiralty practice through Admiralty Rule 44½ in 1942. The later local rule formally incorporated the district's procedures; the prior accepted practice was not unauthorized merely because the standing order had not separately named admiralty. Payne's counsel had also accepted its authority in this action. This reasoning concerns the historical regime before the later unification of civil and admiralty practice. Id.

## The report's substance mattered more than its title

Payne next argued that a pretrial memorandum only prepared counsel for the conference and exhausted its function there. Because the court had issued a “report” rather than a document titled “order,” he contended that nothing from the pretrial process bound the trial. He invoked *Padovani*'s caution against using pretrial procedure to force unwilling admissions. Id. at 805-806 & n.1.

The court rejected both premises. Payne had voluntarily identified the basis of his claim, and the report recorded that position without objection. He had not been compelled to admit an unwanted proposition. The standing order required a summary of the facts and liability contentions and a list of anticipated witnesses other than rebuttal witnesses. Counsel furnished those materials and attended the conference. The judge then prepared, signed, and filed a report based on them. No objection to its description of the case arose until the opening statement exceeded that description at trial. Id. at 806.

The signed report and its references to the memoranda met Rule 16's requirements in substance. They reduced the action to its essential issues, identified witnesses, removed surplus matters, and permitted the court and opposing parties to prepare for the defined trial. Calling the document a report did not remove its binding effect. This was not a holding that any informal note by a judge has the effect of a final pretrial order; the filed document resulted from the prescribed process and fulfilled its operative purposes. Id.

## Time to amend and the duty to disclose the real issues

Payne characterized the standing order as a request to stipulate and asserted a lack of warning that omitted theories or witnesses would be waived. The court emphasized Rule 16's aim of simplifying issues and counsel's responsibility to disclose fairly what the trial would concern. Payne's liability contentions had been incorporated expressly by reference to his memorandum. The report was filed on September 28, 1959, and trial began on March 14, 1960. During that five-and-a-half-month interval, he made no effort to add an improper-loading theory or additional witnesses. Id. at 806-807.

The court distinguished *Krieger*, which concerned disputed factual issues actually raised at pretrial and improperly resolved through summary judgment. Here the problem was departure at trial from the issues previously disclosed. Payne's own lawyer recognized surprise when requesting a continuance to give the other side time to respond. Avoiding that surprise was a principal reason for requiring disclosure beforehand. Id. at 807.

## Discretion to deny amendment and a continuance

The appellate court acknowledged that refusing amendment was a difficult decision. The trial judge wished to assist, but considered the fairness and institutional consequences of undoing the established procedure at trial. The district's sustained pretrial work had secured cooperation from the bar and relieved a congested trial list. Strict enforcement at this stage protected the efficacy of that system as well as the parties' ability to prepare. The judge denied amendments concerning both liability allegations and witnesses, and treated the continuance request similarly. Id.

The Third Circuit found the judge entirely within his discretion and **affirmed the district court's decree**. McLaughlin wrote for the panel, with no concurrence or dissent in the reported decision. The opinion upholds these trial-management choices in the circumstances described; it does not announce that every request to amend a pretrial order must be refused or that later modification is never available. Nor does its abbreviated account of the injury support adding an invented accident narrative or damages figure. Id.
