# American Horse Protection Association, Inc. v. Lyng
812 F.2d 1 (D.C. Cir. 1987) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, question, and result

Judge Stephen F. Williams wrote for a unanimous panel with Judge Starr and District Judge Green, sitting by designation. The American Horse Protection Association challenged the Secretary of Agriculture’s refusal to begin rulemaking to strengthen regulations against deliberately injuring show horses. The district court granted summary judgment to the Secretary. The D.C. Circuit held that the refusal was reviewable and that the explanations offered did not demonstrate reasoned decisionmaking. It vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to remand to the Secretary for further consideration. It did not order a new regulation or require the Secretary immediately to institute rulemaking. 812 F.2d at 1, 4–8.

## Statutory scheme and existing regulations

“Soring” uses pain to produce an unusually high-stepping gait prized in Tennessee walking horses and certain other breeds. Chains and similar action devices placed on the front limbs can cause intense pain when the horse’s feet touch the ground, prompting exaggerated movement. The Horse Protection Act sought to end the practice by prohibiting the showing or selling of sored horses and authorizing implementing regulations. The statutory definition covered actual pain, inflammation, lameness, or distress, as well as the reasonable expectation of those effects from devices or substances used on the limbs. Id. at 1–3.

The regulations combined a general prohibition with particular equipment restrictions. The general provision forbade any device or practice that caused or could reasonably be expected to cause soreness. Specific provisions prohibited chains exceeding eight or ten ounces, depending on the horse’s age, rollers exceeding fourteen ounces, and certain padded shoes on young horses. Lighter devices were not specifically banned. Violations could carry civil or criminal penalties, but applying the general provision required a showing of actual or reasonably expected soreness. Painkillers could conceal actual soreness, and the rules gave no guidance about when an otherwise unlisted device should be expected to cause it. Id. at 2.

When adopting the rules, the agency recognized that its reasons for allowing lighter equipment might not remain sound. It relied on three test clinics that appeared to exonerate devices below the proposed weight limits. In the final 1979 rule it said it would consider banning all action devices and padded shoes if soring persisted. It also identified a newly commissioned university study that might support further amendments. Those statements made subsequent evidence particularly relevant to the existing regulatory approach. Id. at 2.

## Research, agency correspondence, and the petition

Auburn University conducted the commissioned study between September 1978 and December 1982. Ten-ounce chains produced lesions, bleeding, edema, and inflammation. Eight- and ten-ounce chains and fourteen-ounce rollers caused raw lesions on scarred horses. These reported effects fell within the statute’s definition of soreness. Tests of two-, four-, and six-ounce chains found no harmful effects. Preliminary padded-shoe findings suggested additional problems not suspected during the initial rulemaking. The study therefore supplied grounds for questioning specific regulatory permissions; it did not show that every action device at every weight was harmful. Id. at 2–3 & n.1.

Agency officials had already expressed concern before the study was complete. In May 1981 the Department’s Office of General Counsel warned that regulatory gaps undermined enforcement and the congressional objective. It cited administrative decisions treating equipment outside the specific prohibitions as permissible even when it caused soreness. The APHIS Administrator endorsed the concern and requested recommendations. After Association representatives proposed a complete ban on action devices and pads in early 1982, the Administrator said staff had prepared a justification and were drafting proposed rules. In July he reported that regulations had been drafted but publication was being delayed to observe industry self-regulation. Id. at 3.

At a March 1984 meeting the Association again requested rulemaking. Subsequent correspondence acknowledged an apparent inconsistency between the existing device-weight rules, the law, and the Auburn research. Nevertheless, the agency would await further industry studies. An official also expressed disappointment that industry representatives would not consider lower weights because they thought those weights necessary to retain the desired gait. The court later treated this response as raising concern that the agency misunderstood Congress’s mandate. Id. at 3, 6–7.

There was no formally labeled rulemaking petition in the record. That did not defeat the case. Neither the applicable Department regulation nor APA § 553(e) specified formalities, and the agency’s correspondence showed that officials understood the Association’s requests as petitions. The controversy was therefore the refusal to initiate rulemaking in response to those requests, rather than an adjudication of particular trainers’ enforcement violations. Id. at 5.

## Why the refusal was reviewable, despite Chaney

The Secretary’s decision implicated the distinction between rulemaking refusals and the presumptively unreviewable decisions not to take individual enforcement action discussed in *Heckler v. Chaney*. The latter rested on expertise and priority setting, the usual absence of coercive government action, and the analogy to prosecutorial discretion. The first two considerations could also affect a rulemaking refusal. The prosecutorial analogy was less persuasive, however. Individual nonenforcement decisions are numerous and commonly turn on particular facts; rulemaking refusals are relatively infrequent and more likely to raise legal questions. Id. at 3–4.

The APA also supplied a focal point for review. Section 553(e) allowed interested persons to petition for issuing, amending, or repealing a rule, and § 555(e) called for a brief explanation when such petitions were denied. The court read those provisions as evidence that Congress expected explanation of rulemaking refusals. It concluded that *Chaney* did not displace the circuit’s prior decisions allowing review of these refusals. That conclusion concerned this petition and statutory framework; the opinion did not make every decision about regulatory priorities judicially reviewable regardless of applicable standards. Id. at 4.

Review under § 706(2)(A) remained especially deferential. A refusal to institute rulemaking occupied the high end of the range of deference, and compelled proceedings were appropriate only in exceptionally compelling circumstances. Still, the agency had to consider relevant factors, articulate the factual and policy bases of its decision, and have some record support for its factual assumptions. A request based on a radical change in the factual premises of an existing rule merited particular attention. That principle explained why the Association invoked *Geller v. FCC*, but it did not itself determine the validity or significance of the Auburn study. Id. at 4–5, 7.

## Why the Secretary’s explanation failed

The district court relied on litigation affidavits from the Deputy Administrator of Veterinary Services. One stated that he had reviewed relevant studies and believed retaining the current rules was the most effective means of enforcement. Another cited a generally declining number of alleged violations from 1979 through 1984, despite no generally declining number of horses exhibited and examined. The D.C. Circuit found the first account conclusory: it did not identify the factual and policy reasoning connecting the research to continued use of the existing rules. Even a charitable view of that post hoc explanation could not cure its lack of substance. Id. at 5–6.

The violation figures also failed to establish that the agency had reasoned through the problem. Association affidavits suggested that soring remained widespread, and the agency’s earlier correspondence undercut its benign interpretation of the statistics. In 1982 officials had thought the concerns serious enough to draft amendments. By 1984 the earlier explanation that industry self-regulation deserved a chance was too stale to justify continued inaction. The acknowledged inconsistency with the law and Auburn research demanded an actual explanation. The court did not resolve the underlying prevalence of soring merely by preferring one side’s affidavits. Id. at 6.

The court also corrected a potential misunderstanding of statutory purpose. The Act sought to eliminate soring, not to strike a compromise preserving an artificially enhanced gait produced by pain. Its protection of fair competition concerned trainers who relied on natural breeding and careful training, whose competitors gained an advantage through soring. The legislative-history language cited by the Secretary did not overcome the statute’s prohibitions. Footnote 2 recognized some ambiguity in that isolated passage and suggested it might limit regulation of unrelated abuses; it did not authorize soring to preserve competitive performance. Id. at 6–7 & n.2.

A properly understood general regulatory prohibition would forbid soreness even from unlisted equipment. Yet administrative decisions cited in the record had read the specific prohibitions as permitting soring with devices outside the list. Contrary language in one administrative decision did not demonstrate that the Secretary had adopted a consistent, reasonable understanding. Taken together, the unexplained response to research and possible misunderstanding of the mandate defeated summary judgment. Id. at 7.

## Remedy and assigned significance

The Association sought an order compelling rulemaking. The court instead required renewed agency consideration. Even *Geller* had left the agency to choose the form of its inquiry. Whether Auburn’s findings removed a significant factual premise of the old rules remained a matter for the Secretary’s institutional expertise. He needed a reasonable opportunity to explain the refusal or begin new proceedings. The exact judgment vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded to that court with instructions to remand to the Secretary. Id. at 7–8.
