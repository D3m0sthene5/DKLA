# D & N Boening, Inc. v. Kirsch Beverages, Inc.
63 N.Y.2d 449, 472 N.E.2d 992 (1984) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Judge Jasen wrote for the New York Court of Appeals; Chief Judge Cooke and Judges Jones, Wachtler, Meyer, Simons, and Kaye concurred. The court unanimously affirmed, with costs, the Appellate Division’s order dismissing the complaint under the one-year provision of the statute of frauds. The alleged oral exclusive beverage-distribution arrangement had no permitted route to completion within a year: it could end during that period only through breach, which is not performance. The decision addresses the agreement pleaded, not a factual finding after trial that the plaintiff distributed unsatisfactorily. (452–453, 457–459.)

## Franchise history and the claim
In 1955 Minck Beverages, the principal distributor of Yoo-Hoo, orally granted Joseph Boening and his sons exclusive subdistribution rights in Nassau County and part of Suffolk County. They were required to stop distributing a competing chocolate beverage. Their exclusivity was to continue while they distributed satisfactorily, used their best efforts, and acted in good faith. American Beverage acquired the principal franchise in 1963. After Joseph’s death in 1965, his sons continued through D & N Boening. They sought a writing; American refused but orally agreed to continue on satisfactory-performance terms. (452.)

Kirsch bought American in 1982 and assumed its obligations. That year Kirsch told Boening it would terminate the arrangement and distribute Yoo-Hoo itself. Boening sued American and Kirsch for contract damages or, alternatively, specific performance, alleging unilateral termination without cause. That allegation was central: it asserted an enduring entitlement while satisfactory performance continued, rather than an arrangement allowing the defendants to end it freely. (453, 457–458.)

The defendants separately moved under CPLR 3211(a)(5), relying on General Obligations Law § 5–701(a)(1). Special Term denied dismissal because it considered the arrangement terminable at any time and thus capable of ending within a year. The Appellate Division unanimously reversed because the pleaded agreement could end in that period only through breach. The Court of Appeals agreed. No damages amount or specific-performance remedy was awarded. (453, 459.)

## Narrow construction of the one-year provision
The statute requires an agreement or memorandum subscribed by the party to be charged when the agreement’s terms prevent performance within one year. The court acknowledged the historic antifraud purpose but also the imperfect connection between a contract’s duration and how long after formation litigation might arise. It therefore retained a narrow construction: an agreement is within this provision only if its terms make full performance within a year impossible in fact and law. Expected duration, actual years of performance, or extreme practical improbability of quick completion does not itself supply the statutory requirement. The critical inquiry is what the parties’ terms require or permit. (453–455.)

The court’s catalog of agreements outside the provision illustrates genuine avenues for completion: an option to terminate on seven months’ notice; terms governing possible purchases without binding either party to a transaction; a permitted discontinuation of the underlying business activity; sale of essential leased property; work with no direct or implied timing restriction; and employment terminable for a sufficient business reason. These examples are descriptions of other decisions, not additional terms in Boening’s own franchise. The assigned passage reproduces that catalog. (455–456.)

## Breach is not alternative performance
A cancellation option can make completion within a year possible because exercising it carries out what the contract permits. Breach instead fails to do what the contract requires. Treating any possible breach as completion would effectively eliminate the one-year provision for arrangements that otherwise necessarily continue beyond a year. The court thus distinguished a rightful option to end the relationship from destruction of the relationship through nonperformance. (456–457.)

The sole condition limiting the pleaded subdistributorship was Boening’s obligation to distribute satisfactorily, exert best efforts, and act in good faith. Neither party possessed an independent cancellation option, and no specified event or expiration would automatically conclude their duties. Failure to meet that performance obligation was breach, not an agreed alternative mode of fulfillment. The court construed the arrangement as continuing indefinitely—and beyond the first year—unless Boening failed to perform. Its indefinite duration did not make it an at-will contract. (457–458.)

The sole judicial footnote qualifies the employment example drawn from Weiner. An agreement terminable for just cause is not necessarily outside the statute if “just cause” can only mean the other party’s breach. Boening cannot therefore be reduced either to a rule that every indefinite agreement needs a writing or to a rule that every cause-termination clause avoids the statute. The content of the authorized termination matters. (456 & sole note.)

## Other arguments and limits of the report
The printed points of counsel show Boening arguing that the franchise was distinct from employment or sales-service arrangements and that reliance made invocation of the statute unconscionable. The defendants argued both that the pleaded cause restriction brought the agreement within the statute and that an alternative theory permitting subjective termination at any time would undermine the breach claim itself. Those points are advocacy, not judicial findings. The opinion says only that the plaintiff’s other arguments lack merit; it does not separately develop an estoppel analysis or establish detailed findings about reliance injury. Such reasoning should not be imported from the separate intermediate decision as though it appeared in this Court of Appeals judgment. (450–452, 458.)

## Assigned and historical scope
The assigned account asks how the franchise outcome fits the narrow possible-performance rule. Boening’s answer is that theoretical breach does not count as possible performance under the parties’ terms.
