# Bright v. Bechtel Petroleum, Inc.
780 F.2d 766 (9th Cir. 1986) | Civil Procedure

## Court, question, and judgment

The Ninth Circuit decided the consolidated appeals in Nos. 84-2866 and 85-1730 on January 10, 1986; the preserved report identifies an amendment on February 21, 1986. Judge Beezer wrote for a unanimous panel with Judges Wright and Kennedy. An employee sued his employer in California court for allegedly paying less than his employment contract required, but the shortfall was withholding demanded by income-tax law. The court upheld removal, dismissal of both federal- and state-withholding claims, the district court's $3,300 fee award, and denial of a motion seeking clarification. It also awarded reasonable appellate attorney's fees and double costs. No judge wrote separately. (Opinion ¶¶ 1–7, 17, 22, 32–42.)

The assigned account emphasizes the limits of a plaintiff's control over the complaint: state-law wording could not conceal this claim's actual challenge to federal withholding. The full opinion also addresses employer protection, pendent jurisdiction, litigation sanctions, and the historical deadlines governing postjudgment motions. Paragraph references below follow the preserved OpenJurist reproduction of the actual judicial opinion, not independently mapped reporter pages.

## The withholding dispute and two appeals

Willie D. Bright entered an employment contract with Bechtel Petroleum on November 7, 1983, under a collective bargaining agreement. In January 1984 he supplied a W-4 claiming exemption from federal withholding. Bechtel forwarded it to the IRS. In March the IRS rejected the certificate and directed Bechtel to withhold federal income tax; a California regulation made that federal determination effective for state withholding as well. In 1984 Bechtel withheld $2,486.18 in federal tax and $736 in state tax. Note 5 explains the federal regulations requiring transmission of certain exemption certificates and compliance with an IRS directive rejecting a defective certificate. (¶¶ 2–3 & n. 5.)

Bright sued Bechtel and individual agents in California Superior Court on June 1, alleging breach of contract. Bechtel removed to the Northern District of California and sought dismissal and fees, arguing that the complaint was a frivolous challenge to its compliance with federal tax law. Bright sought remand, now insisting that he wished to litigate only state withholding. On December 4 the district court denied remand, dismissed the action, and awarded $3,300 in attorney's fees. (¶¶ 4–6.)

On December 19 Bright moved for clarification of the grounds of judgment. Before a ruling on that motion he appealed the judgment; after denial he also appealed that order. The court consolidated the appeals. Jurisdiction and dismissal for failure to state a claim received de novo review, while retention of the state claim and the fee award involved discretion. (¶¶ 7–8, 25–27, 33.)

## Why the contract label did not defeat removal

The complaint alleged paychecks smaller than the agreed amounts and described the cause as breach of contract and violation of Christian principles. Bechtel maintained that the actual dispute arose under federal internal-revenue statutes, invoking the then-applicable removal provisions and original jurisdiction under §§ 1331 and 1340. (¶¶ 10–11 & n. 1.)

The court began with the well-pleaded-complaint principle: a federal question had to arise from the claim rather than merely from an anticipated defense. Plaintiffs normally controlled their choice of claim and forum. But the panel's artful-pleading doctrine prevented concealing a claim's true federal nature through state-law terminology. Note 2 illustrated that approach with a prior state-contract pleading that actually asserted a federally governed labor dispute; note 3 allowed the removal petition to clarify the action presented by the complaint. These propositions describe the court's historical reasoning in this case, rather than a conclusion that every contract dispute involving a federal regulation is removable. (¶¶ 12–14 & nn. 2–3.)

The factual materials mattered. Bright had sent two letters threatening contract litigation because Bechtel obeyed the IRS directive. Both challenged that directive's legality and cited federal revenue statutes. One referred to an earlier Ninth Circuit decision dismissing a similar withholding suit as frivolous. Affidavits also identified substantially identical wording in another Bechtel employee's recently dismissed complaint. Note 4 explained why the unusual shared phrasing was significant. (¶¶ 14–15 & nn. 3–4.)

Bright's later assertion that only state tax was at issue did not match the complaint or that history. The panel viewed the attempted narrowing as another effort to evade the federal court handling similar withholding suits. It therefore held removal proper. This was a determination about the actual claim supported by the record, not simply acceptance of an employer's federal defense as sufficient. (¶¶ 16–17.)

## Federal withholding did not breach the contract

Section 3402 imposed withholding duties when applicable regulations required them, and Bechtel had acted in accordance with those regulations. Circuit precedent recognized that an employer satisfied its contractual payment obligation by withholding taxes as required and paying the balance. Section 3403 further made the employer liable to the Treasury for the withheld payment while protecting it against a claim by another person for that payment. (¶¶ 19–20 & n. 5.)

The panel also treated the suit as one restraining collection through withholding and applied the Anti-Injunction Act, § 7421(a). It upheld dismissal of the federal-withholding claim on this combination of mandatory compliance, statutory protection, and the tax-collection restriction. The opinion did not adjudicate Bright's ultimate tax liability or declare all possible employer withholding misconduct immune from suit. Its premise was withholding fully in accordance with the governing requirements. (¶¶ 19–22.)

## The related state claim remained within the court's power

Because the district court dismissed the entire case without remanding any portion, the panel understood the state-withholding claim to have been dismissed too. Federal jurisdiction over the removed federal claim brought power to hear related pendent state claims; dismissal of the federal claim did not itself destroy that power. Both withholdings arose from a common nucleus of operative facts: the IRS directive triggered them through the California regulation, and allocation of the funds between governments did not turn the withholding into unrelated transactions. Judicial economy supported resolving the same employer-liability question. Retaining the state claim was not an abuse of discretion. (¶¶ 23–27.)

Bright invoked the general obstacles to federal adjudication of state tax disputes and argued that California could not delegate withholding authority to the federal government. The court distinguished a challenge to the state taxation system from the employer-contract issue actually before it. Even if the regulation were invalid, Bechtel's reliance on its command was justifiable. The panel did not decide the California regulation's constitutionality. Note 6 separately identifies the Tax Injunction Act and the circuit's restrictions on state-tax refund actions. (¶ 28 & n. 6.)

A California decision involving similar federal and state withholding claims supported dismissal: absent a contractual promise to refrain from withholding, payment of the lawful balance discharged the employer's obligation. In addition, California's contract law excused performance made impossible by operation of law. A party did not have to disobey a regulation to avoid a breach claim; note 7 explained that regulations had legal force and private agreement could not evade them. The state claim was therefore properly dismissed. (¶¶ 29–32 & n. 7.)

## Fees, clarification, and the precise outcome

The panel found the action frivolous, in bad faith, and intended to harass. The record included another nearly identical Bechtel employee's suit and a finding that such litigation formed part of a campaign to discourage employers from withholding by making compliance expensive. Note 8 contrasted Bright's demand exceeding $100 million with approximately $3,222 withheld. The $3,300 district fee award was a permissible deterrent, not compensation for an independently established tax underpayment. (¶¶ 33–35 & n. 8.)

The clarification motion also failed. Even construing it generously as a motion under Rule 52(b) or Rule 59(e), December 19 was outside the then-applicable ten-day period following the December 4 judgment. The panel added that even a timely motion was properly denied because Rule 52(a) did not require findings and conclusions when deciding motions. This is the deadline the 1986 opinion applied, not a statement of present rule deadlines. (¶¶ 37–38.)

For the frivolous appeal, the panel invoked § 1912 and Appellate Rule 38, awarded reasonable attorney's fees and double costs, and allowed Bechtel fifteen days after filing of the decision to submit its fee declaration under the specified fee-calculation factors. The closing judgment was affirmed. The opinion did not supply a final dollar amount for the additional appellate award. (¶¶ 39–42.)
