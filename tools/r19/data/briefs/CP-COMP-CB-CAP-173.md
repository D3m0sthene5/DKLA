# Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis

**519 U.S. 61 (1996) · Supreme Court of the United States · December 10, 1996**  
**Civil Procedure — improper removal followed by dismissal of the nondiverse party**

The district court should have remanded when diversity was incomplete. Nevertheless, its final merits judgment survived because the nondiverse defendant had been dismissed before trial and no jurisdictional defect remained at judgment. Justice Ginsburg wrote for a unanimous Court. CivPro assigns the developed account in PDF page 217, printed page 390, Note 4, before the contrasting *Grupo Dataflux* account. The holding did not retroactively make the original removal proper or treat the plaintiff’s preserved objection as waived.

## Why Lewis’s settlement did not immediately produce diversity

James David Lewis, a Kentucky citizen, sued in Kentucky state court on June 22, 1989, after injuries sustained while operating a bulldozer. He asserted state-law defective-manufacture, negligent-maintenance, warning, and warranty claims against Caterpillar, incorporated in Delaware with its principal place of business in Illinois, and Whayne Supply, a Kentucky corporation with its principal place of business there. Kentucky citizenship on both sides prevented complete diversity. **64–65.**

Liberty Mutual, incorporated and headquartered in Massachusetts, intervened as a plaintiff seeking reimbursement for workers’ compensation benefits it had paid on behalf of Lewis’s employer. Its subrogation claims ran against both defendants. Lewis settled his own claims against Whayne Supply within a year, but Liberty Mutual’s claim against that company remained. **65–66.**

Caterpillar removed on June 21, 1990, one day before the then-applicable one-year removal limit. Its notice assumed that Lewis’s settlement would produce Whayne Supply’s dismissal and complete diversity. Lewis promptly sought remand, pointing to Liberty Mutual’s unresolved claim. The district court denied that motion without addressing the argument, treating Lewis’s settlement as dispositive. **65–66.**

In June 1993, Liberty Mutual settled with Whayne Supply, and the latter was dismissed. Complete diversity then existed. After a six-day trial in November, the jury found for Caterpillar, and the court entered judgment. The Sixth Circuit vacated that judgment because complete diversity had been lacking at removal. It did not reach Lewis’s other appellate issues. **66–67 & n.2.**

The Court also explained that Caterpillar’s third-party claim against Lewis’s Kentucky employer did not defeat jurisdiction over Lewis’s claims against Caterpillar. The employer was adverse only to Caterpillar, and Lewis asserted no claim against it. Jurisdiction over additional claims required their own assessment rather than treating every person named in the lawsuit as adverse to every other person. **66–67 n.1.**

## The removal error was accepted, not excused at the outset

The Court reviewed the historical statutory framework: §1332’s complete-diversity rule, removal of actions within original jurisdiction, the forum-defendant restriction, the thirty-day removal periods, and the one-year diversity-removal limit. Jurisdictional defects differed from procedural removal defects, for which remand requests had a thirty-day deadline. **68–69 & nn.3–6.**

For this review, the Court accepted two givens: the district court wrongly treated Whayne Supply as effectively gone when removal occurred, and diversity was incomplete then. It expressly did not decide Caterpillar’s separate request to disregard Liberty Mutual’s subrogation claim or Whayne Supply’s citizenship; that question fell outside the grant of review. **70 & n.7.**

Earlier cases, *American Fire & Casualty Co. v. Finn* and *Grubbs v. General Electric Credit Corp.*, favored preserving some judgments despite improper removal, but neither controlled this exact posture. In *Finn*, jurisdiction remained deficient at judgment. In *Grubbs*, no party had objected before trial. Lewis, by contrast, had promptly but unsuccessfully sought remand and then appealed the adverse judgment. **70–73 & nn.8–9.**

## Cure of jurisdiction differed from waiver or cure of the removal violation

Once Whayne Supply was dismissed, diversity was complete before trial and remained complete at judgment. That cured the jurisdictional deficiency. It did not erase Caterpillar’s statutory failure to remove a case fit for federal adjudication at the time of removal. **73.**

Nor had Lewis waived the objection. His timely remand motion was enough to preserve it. A denial of remand was not independently final and appealable as of right, and Lewis did not have to pursue a discretionary §1292(b) interlocutory appeal to preserve eventual review. A contrary requirement would induce routine interlocutory certification requests inconsistent with the final-judgment system. **74 & nn.10–11.**

Lewis had a concrete statutory concern, not merely a technical complaint: proper adherence to the removal rules would have kept this case in state court. By the time Whayne Supply actually left, the historical one-year limit had expired. The Court treated the implications of that limit as fairly included in the question presented despite the timing of Lewis’s argument on that point. **75 & nn.12–14.**

## Why the completed judgment nevertheless stood

The controlling consideration was the cost of undoing a completed diversity trial that applied state substantive law and ended with all federal jurisdictional requirements satisfied. Finality, efficiency, and economy strongly disfavored sending that case back for renewed state-court proceedings. The Court drew support from *Newman-Green*, which allowed dismissal of a dispensable nondiverse party to avoid unnecessary loss of completed litigation. **75–76.**

This reasoning had an essential boundary: if a jurisdictional defect remained at the end, the judgment would have to be vacated. Here none remained. Preserving the judgment therefore did not authorize a final merits adjudication without subject-matter jurisdiction. **76–77.**

Lewis predicted that the rule would invite premature removal in hope that diversity later materialized. The Court expected district courts to enforce the statutory requirements when removal was attempted and considered defendants unlikely to benefit from a gamble that a defect would escape detection and disappear before judgment. It did not describe its holding as permission for knowingly improper removal. **77–78.**

## Judgment and source limits

The Supreme Court **reversed the Sixth Circuit and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion**, **78**. This removed the jurisdictional basis for vacating the district judgment; it did not resolve Lewis’s other appellate issues that the Sixth Circuit had left undecided. The decision was unanimous, with no separate writing or appendix. **63.**
