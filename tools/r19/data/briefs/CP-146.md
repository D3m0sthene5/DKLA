# Bernhard v. Bank of America National Trust & Savings Association

**Civil Procedure · CP-146 · 19 Cal. 2d 807, 122 P.2d 892 (1942) · March 6, 1942; rehearing denied April 2, 1942**

Bernhard rejects mutuality as a condition for invoking issue preclusion. Due process protects the person to be bound by requiring participation or privity in the first proceeding; it does not require that the new party invoking the judgment also have been bound by it. The bank could therefore use a probate court’s final finding of ownership against the estate, whose same interests had already been represented in the probate hearing.

Clara Sather, an elderly woman in failing health, moved into Charles and Mrs. Cook’s home in 1933. She authorized Cook and her physician to draw jointly against her Los Angeles commercial account. Cook separately opened a San Dimas account styled “Clara Sather by Charles O. Cook,” without authorization from Sather to that bank. Later, in the presence of bank officers, Cook, and her physician, Sather signed by mark an authorization transferring $4,155.68 from her Los Angeles savings account for credit to her own account at the San Dimas bank. The bank credited the Cook-styled account. Cook withdrew the balance, placed it in an account for himself and his wife, and later moved it to another bank in their names. (19 Cal. 2d at 809.)

After Sather died that November, Cook became executor. Years later, when the probate court required an account, he submitted one that omitted the transferred money and resigned. Helen Bernhard and other beneficiaries objected. Following a hearing, the probate court settled the account and expressly determined that Sather had given the deposit to Cook during her lifetime. After his discharge, Bernhard became administratrix with the will annexed and sued Bank of America, successor to the San Dimas bank. She claimed the estate remained entitled to the deposit because Sather never authorized its withdrawal. The bank pleaded both consent and the preclusive probate determination. The trial court accepted the latter ground and entered judgment for the bank. (Id. at 809–10.)

Bernhard challenged the defenses and admission of the probate record. She also disputed the probate court’s jurisdiction because Cook had resigned before the objections were filed; an intervening California decision established that the probate court had jurisdiction in that situation. On appeal she contested preclusion and the validity of the gift. Her central objection was that the bank had neither been a party nor been in privity with a party in probate, and would not have been bound had the finding gone the opposite way. Traditional mutuality doctrine would deny it the benefit of the judgment for that reason. (Id. at 810–11.)

Justice Traynor distinguished two questions that the traditional formula combined: who may assert a judgment and against whom it may operate. No person can lose rights through a judgment without the notice and opportunity for participation required by due process. Consequently, the person against whom preclusion is invoked must have been a party or privy in the earlier litigation. But someone else’s absence from that litigation does not erase the first party’s fair adjudication. A litigant who already had the opportunity to contest an issue should not reopen it merely by selecting another adversary. Existing exceptions for derivative liability—such as principal and agent or employer and servant—already reflected that reasoning despite the absence of conventional mutuality. (Id. at 811–13.)

The court stated three pertinent inquiries: whether the issue was identical, whether there was a final judgment on the merits, and whether the person against whom preclusion was asserted was a party or privy to that adjudication. It overruled Estate of Smead, Silva v. Hawkins, and People v. Rodgers to the extent inconsistent with this approach. The court’s use of the broad term “res judicata” includes the issue-preclusion application at stake here; this was not simply a second identical claim against the same defendant. (Id. at 813.)

Ownership of the money was identical in both proceedings and had been conclusively adjudicated on the merits when the probate court settled the account. Bernhard’s formal change from objecting beneficiary to administratrix did not change the represented right. The beneficiaries had sought recovery for the estate’s legatees and creditors in probate; the administratrix now sought recovery for those same interests. The probate order bound them, and Bernhard could not avoid it by litigating in the new formal capacity. The court did not independently retry whether Sather made a valid gift. (Id. at 813–14.)

The judgment for the bank was **affirmed unanimously**. Traynor wrote; Chief Justice Gibson and Justices Shenk, Curtis, Edmonds, Houser, and Carter concurred, with no separate writings or judicial footnotes. The assigned principal passage spans printed pages 1327–1329; its later hypothetical questions and discussion of federal developments are editorial material. The result illustrates nonmutual defensive issue preclusion while retaining the participation requirement for the party bound. This historical brief does not claim that every possible later application, including offensive use, automatically follows without further analysis.
