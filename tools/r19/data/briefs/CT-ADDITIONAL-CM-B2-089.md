# Hamann v. Crouch
211 Kan. 852, 508 P.2d 968 (1973) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and disposition
The Kansas Supreme Court decided the case on April 7, 1973. Justice Kaul wrote for the court; the complete report contains no separate opinion, judicial footnote, or numerical voting lineup. A teacher appealed the denial of reinstatement and the limits on his damages, while college officials cross-appealed the finding of liability. The court reversed with directions to enter judgment for the college defendants. Because the teacher accepted leave knowing the college's conditional meaning of its policy, the college owed neither reinstatement nor damages. (852, 856, 861.)

## Employment, leave, and the competing meanings
Fredrick C. Hamann had taught at Garden City Community Junior College since 1965. His March 17, 1969 contract covered nine months beginning September 1 at a salary of $9,928, payable in twelve installments. His assignment covered biological sciences and mathematics, with biological sciences his primary field. The contract incorporated the college's rules and policies. Its leave regulation said professional employees granted leave would, if possible, be reinstated in positions similar to those held when leave began. Hamann read the qualification as applying only to similarity: the college had to give him a job, although it might differ from his former job. The college understood reinstatement itself to depend on an available position. (853–854, 860.)

Hamann independently applied for a year's teacher training at Oak Ridge Associated Universities without consulting college officials. After Oak Ridge accepted him on May 20, he took the leave policy to his neighbor, attorney Fleming, who advised him and suggested how to request leave. At that point Hamann did not know the college's interpretation and consequently did not tell Fleming about it. His May 23 request expressed anticipation of returning to his present position but did not expressly state his competing interpretation. The detailed chronology identifies a May 26 letter granting leave according to the policy. The introductory narrative instead calls May 23 the grant date; the fuller account distinguishes the request and subsequent letter. (853, 857.)

After receiving the grant letter, Hamann spoke with President Crouch and board chairman Edwin S. Hooper. Their conversations fully informed him that reinstatement depended on an available position. He did not tell either official that he interpreted the provision differently. His response to Hooper was that, under that interpretation, he did not feel they had a leave policy at all. He nevertheless accepted leave and went to Oak Ridge, acknowledging at trial that he knew he was taking a chance. He did not return to Fleming with this newly learned condition. The court therefore treated the lawyer's earlier advice, given without knowledge of the college's expressed meaning, as of little value to the actual dispute. (857, 859–860.)

In February 1970 Hamann asked to return for the following academic year. Before March 15 the college notified him that no position was available. He unsuccessfully applied for other teaching jobs and later found nonteaching work. A Kansas-National Education Association representative investigated and received a board hearing; Hamann considered that representation proper and raised no complaint about the hearing. He filed suit on June 12, seeking damages or mandamus compelling a contract for 1970–71. He maintained that leave continued his employment contract and that he remained on staff. The defendants maintained that the agreed leave relieved both sides of their existing employment obligations and created only conditional rights under the leave policy. (853–854.)

## Trial decision and appellate issues
The trial court found that the parties dealt at arm's length, that the college consistently granted leave under its policy, and that Hamann knew its interpretation when he accepted. Nevertheless, because the college drafted both the policy and contract form, the trial court construed the ambiguity against it. That construction guaranteed reemployment, with similarity required only when possible. It awarded $9,928 less $3,600 in mitigation, a $6,328 judgment, while refusing reinstatement. The $3,600 represented what the court found Hamann could reasonably have earned with diligent effort during the relevant academic year; its finding of actual earnings for that period was $2,491.63. These figures are distinct from the opinion's earlier account of particular summer and fall jobs. (854–856.)

The trial court also found no exactly similar position available without changing schedules, creating additional classes, or dismissing another teacher. It reasoned that the college could have planned for his return by hiring replacements for only one year had it understood reinstatement to be obligatory. Hamann challenged denial of reinstatement, the use of nonprofessional employment in calculating mitigation, and denial of additional damages including attorney fees. The college's cross-appeal contended that his knowledge bound him to its meaning, that the court could not substitute its own interpretation, and that it erred in treating employment outside his teaching qualifications as obligatory. The appellate court identified the first contention as dispositive. (855–856.)

## Reasoning: known meaning controls this ambiguous bargain
Both sides conceded ambiguity. The Supreme Court accepted that ambiguities ordinarily may be construed against a drafter, but held that the trial court had misapplied that rule to these facts. Contract interpretation seeks the parties' intention consistently with legal principles. Where actual ambiguity exists, oral testimony showing an operative interpretation is admissible and can be controlling. A party who knows the meaning the other party intends cannot accept on that basis while silently reserving a different interpretation and later demand that a court enforce the uncommunicated version. The court cautioned that this principle cannot serve as a device for writing a new contract; the facts must warrant its application. Here both the findings and Hamann's testimony established precisely the required knowledge and acceptance. (856–859.)

Leave was discretionary, not an unconditional contractual entitlement. Hamann could have rejected the college's stated conditions or communicated his own position. His acceptance instead bound him to the conditional understanding. Granting and accepting leave released him from his obligation to teach for the ensuing year and released the college from its corresponding employment obligations. His remaining rights therefore arose from the leave regulation, rather than an unchanged teaching contract or the continuing-contract statute, K.S.A. 72-5410 et seq. The court acknowledged that such a policy gave only limited benefits, but explained that a small college could not necessarily absorb the staffing consequences of a guaranteed return. That practical limitation did not authorize a court to substitute a stronger promise for the one knowingly accepted. (858–860.)

The absence of a suitable vacancy involved enrollment, classroom space, faculty qualifications, budgets, and schedules. The dean testified that neither the biological-science position nor a combined full teaching load was available without discharging someone else. No finding or evidence supported fraud, bad faith, arbitrariness, or capricious action. The trial court consequently could not replace the board's administrative judgment with its preferred staffing arrangements. The amicus authorities about continuing employment and procedural due process did not change the result: the leave bargain controlled, notice was timely, and Hamann did not allege denial of procedural due process. (860–861.)

## Scope and assigned comparison
The court eliminated liability and directed judgment for the defendants. It did not separately establish that all teachers must mitigate through any available nonprofessional work, resolve an independent attorney-fee entitlement, or announce that every ambiguous employer policy favors the employer. The holding rests on actual knowledge of the other party's interpretation, silent acceptance, and the absence of an available position or misconduct. (856, 858–861.)
