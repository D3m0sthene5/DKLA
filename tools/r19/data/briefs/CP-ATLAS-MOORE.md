# Moore v. Moore

**Civil Procedure — 391 A.2d 762 (D.C. 1978), No. 12312. District of Columbia Court of Appeals, August 29, 1978. Judge Ferren wrote the opinion on a panel with Judge Gallagher and Superior Court Judge Moultrie, sitting by designation. No separate opinion appears.**

## A custody action expanded after trial

Reuben and Sidney Moore married in the District of Columbia in 1968, and their daughter Jessica was born in 1973. After the marriage deteriorated, Sidney took Jessica to her parents’ home in Schenectady, New York, in December 1975. Reuben sought custody there. The New York court orally awarded temporary custody to Sidney and visitation to Reuben. In February 1976, Reuben, assisted by a detective, took Jessica from her maternal grandfather and brought her to Washington. Sidney followed, but Reuben refused to return the child, and she filed a D.C. habeas proceeding seeking custody. Reuben then took Jessica on a trip to Europe and the Bahamas. After his return, Sidney and her parents took the child from him and returned to Schenectady. 391 A.2d at 765–766.

In April 1976, Reuben opposed the habeas petition and filed the custody suit at issue here. Sidney initially challenged D.C. jurisdiction. After the New York court suggested resolving the custody issues in D.C., she withdrew that objection and answered. The Family Division of the Superior Court tried the case in October 1976. Id. at 766.

At the end of trial, the court orally awarded Sidney custody and $500 per month in child support, and gave Reuben visitation conditioned on a $7,500 bond. It did not initially award spousal support, recognizing that there was no affirmative pleading requesting it. It reserved the attorney-fee issue for motion papers. A week after trial, Sidney moved under the domestic-relations counterpart of Rule 15(b) to conform the pleadings to the evidence by adding a counterclaim for custody, child support, separate maintenance, and counsel fees. Id.

The February 1977 written order granted the amendment and awarded custody, $500 monthly child support, another $500 monthly in separate maintenance, and $5,916.65 in counsel fees. It retained the visitation bond. Reuben appealed after the court denied his stay and new-trial motions. He argued that Sidney had requested no affirmative relief before trial and that he lacked notice and an adequate opportunity to contest the counterclaim’s subjects. He also challenged the basis for the custody findings and the monetary awards. Id. at 766–768.

## Rule 15(b): actual litigation can expand the pleaded issues

The D.C. domestic-relations rule incorporated a civil Rule 15(b) identical to the then-existing Federal Rule. Matters tried by express or implied consent were treated as though pleaded; an amendment could formalize that litigation even after judgment. Conversely, failure to make a formal amendment did not erase an issue actually tried by consent. The court interpreted the rule to favor decisions on the real controversy while preserving fair notice and an opportunity to respond. Id. at 768–769 & n.3.

For implied consent, the trial record had to show that the opposing party recognized the introduction of the unpleaded matter and had an adequate chance to litigate it or cure surprise. Evidence relevant specifically to the new issue, rather than merely to already pleaded matters, could support that inference. A failure to object could also matter, but incidental evidence that only inferentially suggested another issue was not enough by itself. This required an issue-by-issue inquiry, rather than treating all posttrial additions alike. Id.

## Custody and child support were properly before the court

Reuben knew the court would determine who should have custody, rather than merely reject or accept his request without deciding Jessica’s placement. Sidney’s answer asserted that Jessica’s best interests favored her custody; both parents introduced evidence about their qualifications. The New York court’s suggestion to resolve custody in D.C. and Sidney’s earlier habeas petition further supplied notice. Adding a counterclaim for custody after trial therefore caused no prejudicial surprise and fell within the court’s discretion. Id. at 769.

Child support also had been tried by implied consent. Its determination was inherent in resolving a custody contest centered on the child’s best interests, and Sidney had introduced evidence of Jessica’s financial needs without a relevance objection. These considerations together supported notice and consent even though no pretrial support claim had been filed. The court could permit the amendment and decide entitlement to child support. That conclusion did not, by itself, establish that the particular $500 amount was adequately supported. Id.

## Visitation security and fees were permissible subjects, too

Reuben conceded that visitation belonged within the custody determination. Although Sidney’s amendment motion did not specifically request a visitation bond, the trial court could fashion complete relief protecting Jessica’s interests. Reuben’s history of taking her away supported requiring security against another removal. The appellate court approved the authority to require a bond, while separately reviewing whether the amount had a sufficient factual basis. Id. at 769–770.

For attorney fees, D.C. precedent permitted an award to a parent who obtained legal assistance to protect the child, treating that assistance as a necessary expense for the child rather than simply shifting fees to the prevailing adult. Evidence of D.C. counsel expenses had been admitted without objection. The judge also reserved decision, received a motion and opposition, and held a hearing. On that record, Reuben had an opportunity to contest the issue, so the fee amendment was permitted without prejudicial surprise. Id. at 770.

## Separate maintenance exceeded the issues tried by consent

Spousal support was different. Neither the original pleadings nor the nature of this custody suit put a claim for Sidney’s own maintenance at issue; the parties’ marital relationship was not being litigated. Although evidence about her finances had been admitted, it was also relevant to custody and child support. Its admission therefore did not uniquely signal that she was pursuing a separate award for herself. The record supplied no other basis for finding adequate notice and implied consent. Id. at 770 & n.5.

The trial court abused its discretion by permitting that amendment and awarding separate maintenance. This is the limiting application of the same rule that allowed the other additions: evidence supporting an existing issue does not automatically establish consent to try every new claim to which it might also be relevant. The appellate court reversed the maintenance award. Id. at 770, 772.

## The custody award survived, but the financial determinations required more support

Reuben’s other attacks on custody failed. The record did not show reliance on a presumption favoring mothers or the parent already possessing the child. Because no maternal or “tender years” presumption was used, note 2 reserved his constitutional and statutory objections to such a presumption. His argument that Sidney had misled the court about a written New York order also failed: the written order was excluded on authentication grounds, and the judge expressly disclaimed reliance on it. Note 1 explains that the written order was entered a day after Reuben took Jessica, although the earlier oral ruling existed. The history of removal could be relevant without finding that he violated a written decree. Id. at 766–767 & nn.1–2.

The evidence supported placing Jessica with Sidney. But the court’s findings did not adequately establish the parents’ financial abilities for child support, counsel fees, or the bond’s amount. Support depended on the child’s needs and both parents’ ability to pay; fees also required consideration of the services and their relationship to the child’s interests; the bond needed a connection to pecuniary ability. Gross income, public assistance, a business property transaction, joint home ownership, and family loans did not supply a sufficiently complete picture of disposable income and legitimate obligations. Id. at 770–772.

The final disposition was therefore mixed: **custody affirmed; separate maintenance reversed; child support, counsel fees, and visitation rights and bond remanded for further proceedings, findings, and conclusions**. The existing monthly child support and bond requirements remained effective pending final resolution. The appellate court did not cancel all relief to Sidney or approve every dollar of the trial order. Id. at 772.
