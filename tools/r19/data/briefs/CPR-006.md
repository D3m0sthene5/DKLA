Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown
Citation
Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown, 564 U.S. 915 (2011). United States Supreme Court,
decided June 27, 2011. Justice Ginsburg wrote for a unanimous Court. The Court reviewed a North Carolina
Court of Appeals judgment sustaining personal jurisdiction over three foreign subsidiaries in a
wrongful-death action.
Full judicial opinion.
Parties
The plaintiffs were the parents of Julian Brown and Matthew Helms, acting as administrators of their sons'
estates. The boys and their parents were North Carolina residents. The defendants included The Goodyear
Tire and Rubber Company, an Ohio corporation, and three indirect subsidiaries organized and operating in
Luxembourg, Turkey, and France. Only the three foreign subsidiaries contested personal jurisdiction and
petitioned for Supreme Court review. The American parent operated in North Carolina and did not challenge
the state courts' authority over it. Its participation did not itself establish jurisdiction over each separately
incorporated subsidiary.
Material Facts
In April 2004, Julian and Matthew, thirteen-year-old soccer players, died in a bus accident outside Paris
while traveling toward the airport. Their parents alleged that a defective tire manufactured at the Turkish
subsidiary's plant caused the crash. The claims challenged the tire's design, construction, testing, and
inspection. The accident, manufacture of the allegedly defective tire, and the tire's sale occurred outside
North Carolina. The particular tire was a type not distributed in that state.
The subsidiaries manufactured tires primarily for European and Asian markets. They had no North Carolina
place of business, employees, bank accounts, or registration to do business. They did not design or
manufacture tires there, solicit business there, or directly sell or ship tires to North Carolina customers. Some
of their other tires nevertheless reached North Carolina through distribution by other Goodyear affiliates.
Those shipments comprised a small fraction of the subsidiaries' production: tens of thousands of tires out of
tens of millions manufactured during the relevant period. Many were specialized tires obtained by special
order. The jurisdictional question concerned what those indirect sales established about the subsidiaries
themselves. 564 U.S. at 918-22.
Procedural History
The parents filed wrongful-death claims in the North Carolina Superior Court for Onslow County. The
foreign subsidiaries moved to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction. The trial court denied the motions,
allowing the claims against them to proceed despite the absence of a connection between their North
Carolina tire sales and the accident.

The North Carolina Court of Appeals affirmed, 199 N.C. App. 50, 681 S.E.2d 382 (2009). It recognized that
specific jurisdiction was unavailable because the alleged injury did not arise from or relate to the
subsidiaries' North Carolina contacts. It instead found general jurisdiction, reasoning that the subsidiaries
had placed tires into an organized distribution system that carried them to North Carolina and had not taken
affirmative steps to prevent distribution there. The court also emphasized the state's interest in providing its
residents a forum and the difficulty of litigating abroad. These considerations allowed the intermediate court
to treat product distribution as support for adjudicating an unrelated foreign accident.
The North Carolina Supreme Court denied discretionary review, 364 N.C. 128, 695 S.E.2d 756 (2010). The
United States Supreme Court granted certiorari to review the intermediate appellate judgment. The issue was
the constitutionality of general jurisdiction; neither liability for the accident nor damages had been
adjudicated. Before the Supreme Court, the parents also urged that all Goodyear entities should be treated as
one enterprise. The Court declined to consider that argument because they had not timely raised it below or
in their opposition to certiorari. 564 U.S. at 922-23, 930-31.
Issue
Does a foreign subsidiary become subject to general personal jurisdiction in North Carolina because some
tires that it manufactures abroad enter the state through affiliated distribution channels, when the lawsuit
concerns a different tire and a fatal accident in France? May contacts potentially relevant to specific
jurisdiction support adjudication of any claim against the manufacturer?
Rule and Holding
A state may exercise general, or all-purpose, jurisdiction over a corporation when its affiliations with the
state are so continuous and systematic that the corporation is essentially at home there. Specific jurisdiction
instead requires an affiliation between the forum and the underlying controversy. The subsidiaries' limited,
indirect tire distribution did not make them at home in North Carolina and could not support jurisdiction over
these unrelated claims. The Court identified a corporation's place of incorporation and principal place of
business as paradigm corporate homes, while using Perkins to illustrate sufficiently substantial operations
elsewhere. 564 U.S. at 919, 923-29.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the North Carolina Court of Appeals. North Carolina could not exercise general
jurisdiction over the three petitioning subsidiaries on this record. The ruling removed the asserted basis for
keeping those subsidiaries in this lawsuit. It did not adjudicate the negligence claims or decide the American
parent's liability, and it did not hold that the parents lacked any other forum.

Reasoning
The Court began with the difference between a forum's power over a particular dispute and its power to hear
every dispute against a defendant. Product distribution can create a contact relevant to a claim arising from
the product's presence or use in the forum. It does not automatically create a corporate home. Treating the
circulation of products as sufficient for general jurisdiction would make a substantial manufacturer
answerable on any claim wherever its products were sold. That result would erase the more demanding
connection required for all-purpose authority.
The North Carolina courts had imported stream-of-commerce reasoning into the wrong jurisdictional
inquiry. Whether a defendant directed or expected products to reach a state may matter to specific
jurisdiction. Here the plaintiffs had already conceded the missing link between the forum transactions and
the accident. Their sons' residence supplied a strong reason for wanting to litigate locally, but it supplied no
additional connection between the foreign subsidiaries and North Carolina. A state's interest in its residents
could not independently establish the necessary defendant-forum relationship.
The Court compared the subsidiaries with the defendants in Perkins and Helicopteros. In Perkins, the
corporation's president actually managed the company's affairs from Ohio while wartime conditions
interrupted operations abroad. Ohio functioned as the center of its activities. In Helicopteros, substantial
purchases and related training visits in Texas did not support general jurisdiction. The subsidiaries' North
Carolina contacts were far removed from the management activities in Perkins and insufficient even though
some actual sales reached the forum.
Finally, jurisdiction had to be assessed over the parties before the Court. The parent company's North
Carolina operations did not eliminate the subsidiaries' distinct legal identities. Because the parents' enterprise
theory was forfeited, the Court did not decide the circumstances in which corporate separateness might
properly be disregarded. The result therefore cannot be read as a categorical ruling that affiliates' contacts
can never be attributed to one another; it rejects these subsidiaries' amenability to unrelated suits on the
theory actually preserved. 564 U.S. at 927-31.
Separate Opinions
There were no concurrences or dissents. All nine Justices joined Justice Ginsburg's opinion.
Notes
The case's use of “foreign” includes both corporations from another state and corporations from another
country. General jurisdiction turns on the corporation's relationship to the particular forum, rather than
foreign nationality alone. Daimler develops the at-home limitation further, while Ford illustrates why a
manufacturer may still face specific jurisdiction when its extensive local market activities relate to the
models involved in local injuries. The foreign accident here lacked that relationship. The forfeited enterprise
argument also shows why preserving an alternative jurisdictional basis in the trial and appellate proceedings
matters.
