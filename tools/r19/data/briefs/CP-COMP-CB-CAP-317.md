# Danner v. Anskis

**256 F.2d 123 (3d Cir. 1958) · United States Court of Appeals, Third Circuit · June 3, 1958 · Civil Procedure**

**Assigned account:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 386, printed page 739: complete Note 4 through its questions, before the 740 marker. The opinion's ancillary-jurisdiction reasoning is historical; the textbook's supplemental-jurisdiction question is a later editorial comparison.

**Decision in one sentence.** Rule 13(g), as the Third Circuit then understood it, did not authorize plaintiffs jointly suing an outside defendant to crossclaim against each other on an independent claim arising from the same accident; the proposed claim also lacked an independent diversity basis because its adverse parties were all Pennsylvania citizens. 256 F.2d at 124.

## Facts and procedural path

The Danner and Weintraub families filed suit October 1, 1954, against William Anskis after a two-car collision in Bucks County, Pennsylvania. Mrs. Danner drove the car occupied by three Danner children and their mother and two Weintraub children and their mother. The complaint attributed the collision to Anskis's negligent driving and sought personal-injury damages. All plaintiffs were Pennsylvania citizens; Anskis was a New Jersey citizen, supplying the original suit's diversity basis. Id. at 123–124.

On April 1, 1957, Anskis was defaulted for failing to appear, plead, or defend. Three weeks later, the Weintraub plaintiffs attempted to crossclaim against **Mrs. Danner and her husband**, alleging that Mrs. Danner's negligent operation caused the same injuries. The district court granted the Danners' motion to dismiss because the claim was not a proper Rule 13(g) crossclaim and lacked another federal jurisdictional basis. The Weintraubs appealed. Id. at 124.

The original defendant's default did not turn the shared plaintiffs' positions into an ordinary claim and responsive crossclaim against them. Nor did allegations of either driver's negligence settle whose conduct actually caused the accident. The appeal concerned the permissible federal procedural vehicle, not an adjudication that Mrs. Danner was negligent.

## How the court interpreted crossclaims

Judge Maris reproduced Rule 13(g)'s authorization of a claim against a coparty arising from the transaction or occurrence underlying the original action or a counterclaim, or relating to property involved in the action. Although the Weintraubs' proposed claim plainly concerned the same collision, the court treated transactional connection as insufficient by itself. Id.

It understood the crossclaim device as ancillary to a claim **already asserted against the crossclaimant**. A defendant could crossclaim against a codefendant about the underlying transaction. A plaintiff facing a defendant's counterclaim could crossclaim against a coplaintiff about that counterclaim's transaction. But the Weintraubs faced no such previously pleaded claim against themselves. Their affirmative claim against the Danners was independent of the families' shared affirmative complaint against Anskis. Id.

The court connected that procedural construction to federal jurisdiction. It reasoned that an unrestricted reading could extend district-court jurisdiction beyond the federal judicial power, contrary to Rule 82. Merely placing two Pennsylvania plaintiffs in a diversity case against a New Jersey defendant did not authorize a separate controversy between those plaintiffs. If treated as an independent suit, the proposed Danner claim still lacked diverse adverse parties. Id. The holding therefore was not that a plaintiff could **never** use Rule 13(g); the opinion expressly described the counterclaim situation in which a plaintiff could use it.

## Limitations issue and judgment

The district court also relied on the Pennsylvania limitations period. The Third Circuit expressly declined to determine that issue because its procedural and jurisdictional grounds sufficed. It noted that more than the statutory two-year period had passed before service of the crossclaim and that, had dismissal been reversed, the Danners could raise the defense in their responsive pleading under Rule 12. Id. That observation is not an appellate holding finally establishing the limitations defense against every possible state action.

The court **affirmed the district court's dismissal order**. Maris wrote for the panel of Maris, Kalodner, and Staley; the report contains no separate writing or dissent. Id. at 123–124. It did not disturb the original defendant's default, decide the personal-injury merits, or award damages on the proposed crossclaim.
