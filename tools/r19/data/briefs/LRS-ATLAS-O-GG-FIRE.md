# Lujan v. G & G Fire Sprinklers, Inc.
532 U.S. 189 (2001) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and disposition

On April 17, 2001, a unanimous Supreme Court reversed the Ninth Circuit’s judgment invalidating California’s statutory system for withholding wages and penalties from public-works contract payments. Chief Justice Rehnquist explained that, when ordinary judicial process is available to resolve this contractual dispute, that process supplies due process. The Court assumed without deciding both state action and a protected property interest in G & G’s claim for payment. It did not decide whether the subcontractor had complied with the wage and recordkeeping requirements or whether the withheld amounts were substantively correct. (190–191, 195–199.)

The holding concerns a disputed contract claim that could be fully protected in an ordinary breach-of-contract suit, rather than a presently exercisable entitlement to occupy property or pursue a licensed occupation. It therefore does not establish that every property deprivation may proceed without an administrative hearing merely because damages might later be available. (195–197.)

## Public-works requirements and withholding

G & G installed fire sprinkler systems as a subcontractor on California public projects. Contractors and subcontractors had to pay the prevailing wage set by the State. Under the law governing the events, the contractor was liable for unpaid wage differences and statutory penalties; the public awarding body had to include the requirement in its contract. Related provisions covered payroll records and hours. The awarding body withheld wages and penalties forfeited under contractual stipulations and the Code, and the prime contractor could withhold corresponding sums from its subcontractor. (191–192 & nn.1–2.)

In 1995, the Division of Labor Standards Enforcement determined that G & G had failed to pay prevailing wages and to keep or provide required payroll records on three projects. It instructed the awarding bodies to withhold the assessed amounts from the contractors. The contractors then withheld payment from G & G, which stated that the total exceeded $135,000. The route of payment and withholding matters: the State’s notices acted through public awarding bodies and prime contractors, while G & G sought money under its subcontractual arrangements. (192–193.)

The governing Code permitted the contractor or an assignee to sue the awarding body on the contract for alleged breach in withholding payment. Suit had to be brought within ninety days after completion and acceptance, and the money remained withheld pending the result. The statutory remedy was described as exclusive for the contractor or its assignees. Those were the provisions challenged; the Court’s third note explains that an amended administrative-review scheme, with an impartial hearing officer, written decision, and judicial review, would take effect July 1, 2001. Those later procedures had not been available during the challenged withholding and were not the basis of the Court’s constitutional conclusion. (192–193 & n.3.)

## Federal action and two Ninth Circuit judgments

G & G brought a section 1983 action in the Central District of California seeking declaratory and injunctive relief. It contended that withholding notices issued without any hearing deprived it of property without due process. The District Court granted summary judgment, declared the specified Code provisions unconstitutional, and enjoined enforcement against G & G. A divided Ninth Circuit panel affirmed in 1998, concluding that the company had a property interest in full payment and received neither a predeprivation nor a postdeprivation hearing. (193–194.)

After American Manufacturers Mutual Insurance Co. v. Sullivan, the Supreme Court vacated that judgment and remanded for reconsideration. Sullivan had rejected state action involving a private insurer’s disputed medical payment and had also rejected an entitlement to payment before the treatment was found reasonable and necessary. The Ninth Circuit nevertheless reinstated its judgment in 2000. It distinguished the mandatory withholding directed by state officials and explained that its concern was the absence of any hearing, not an entitlement to immediate payment while a hearing was pending. The Supreme Court’s 2001 decision reviewed that reinstated appellate judgment. (194–195.)

## Assumed interest and the difference from present possession

Rehnquist assumed that withholding occurred under color of state law and that the company had a protected interest in its claim for payment of the sort recognized in Logan. The Court resolved the case through adequacy of the opportunity to pursue that claim, rather than denying an interest at the threshold. Its inquiry was whether the claim’s merits could be fairly judged through state judicial process. (195.)

The Ninth Circuit had relied on decisions concerning seizure of a house, suspension of a racehorse trainer, suspension of a bank president, and wage garnishment. In those cases, the person was denied an entitlement to exercise present dominion over property or continue an occupation. G & G instead had a claim that it had satisfied its contractual terms and was therefore entitled to payment. The State disputed compliance. An ordinary contract suit could fully protect that claim. Due process is context-dependent and does not require one inflexible set of procedures in every situation. (195–197.)

## Available suits, delay, and the burden of challenge

The contractor’s ability to assign its statutory cause of action supplied a means for a subcontractor to sue the awarding body. Nothing in the record established that a contractor would refuse an assignment. G & G acknowledged that it had sued awarding bodies in state court over withheld payments on earlier projects; the Court inferred that it had used assignments as the Code required. That history supported the practical availability of the remedy, without deciding the merits of this dispute. (197.)

G & G objected that the money could remain withheld through several years of litigation. The Court recognized hardship but treated delayed payment at a lawsuit’s conclusion as ordinary for a contested contract claim. The delay did not extinguish the cause of action or deprive G & G of the chance to establish the amount owed. (197.)

Even without an assignment, a standard contract suit against the prime contractor appeared available. The statutory exclusivity language applied to the contractor and assignees, not necessarily to an unassigned subcontractor. California’s J & K Painting decision had refused to infer that failure to obtain an assignment barred every other avenue of relief. The Supreme Court expressly described its state-law conclusion as tentative: California courts had final authority over that question and the defenses a contractor might raise. G & G had not demonstrated that this route was closed and, as the party attacking the statute, bore the burden of establishing unconstitutionality. (197–199.)

## Judgment and assigned limits

The judgment was reversed. There were no concurrences or dissents, no award of the withheld funds, and no final determination of the underlying wage or contractual dispute. The decision upheld the challenged historical scheme on the availability of ordinary judicial process, rather than on the new administrative procedures not yet effective. (193 n.3; 199.)
