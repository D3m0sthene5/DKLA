# Mckesson v. Doe

**592 U.S. 1, 141 S. Ct. 48 (2020). Supreme Court of the United States, November 2, 2020.** Per curiam. Justice Thomas dissented without a written explanation; Justice Barrett did not participate. Seven participating Justices supported the disposition. Citations below use the preserved five-page official slip opinion. Assigned account: CivPro PDF 280, printed 524–525, within Note 4 on certification.

## The alleged protest conduct and the injured officer

DeRay Mckesson organized a demonstration in Baton Rouge protesting a police shooting. According to the allegations recounted by the Court, protesters occupied the highway outside police headquarters at his direction. While officers made arrests to clear it, an unidentified person threw concrete or a similar object, striking Officer Doe in the face and causing severe injuries, including lost teeth and brain trauma. Doe sought damages from Mckesson on the theory that negligent organization of the protest caused the assault. The complaint did not identify Mckesson as the person who threw the object; the theory attempted to connect his organizing conduct to another person’s violence. These were allegations in a claim under review, not findings establishing that Mckesson caused the injury. (Slip 1–3.)

The district court dismissed the negligence claim as barred by the First Amendment. A divided Fifth Circuit panel reversed. Although Louisiana generally recognized no duty to protect others from third-party criminal acts, the panel reasoned that Mckesson could have breached a duty not to negligently precipitate such a crime by directing the protest onto a highway where confrontation was foreseeable. It also concluded that the First Amendment did not bar liability for consequences of tortious activity allegedly directed by him. Judge Willett disputed both the state-law duty analysis and the constitutional conclusion. Rehearing en banc was denied on an eight-to-eight division, accompanied by additional disagreements about state law and the First Amendment. Those circuit votes describe the history; they are not the Supreme Court’s vote. (Slip 1–3.)

## Why the Court did not decide the constitutional question

The petition asked whether the Fifth Circuit’s theory of personal liability violated the First Amendment. Mckesson argued that negligently directing a protest onto a highway, even if punishable as a misdemeanor, did not make him personally responsible for the violence of someone merely attending. The Court recognized that protected expressive activity requires precision about both the grounds for damages and the people who may be held accountable. But constitutional scrutiny mattered only if Louisiana tort law permitted recovery in the first place. The uncertain state-law premise made deciding the First Amendment issue premature. (Slip 3.)

The Court therefore focused on certification, a procedure by which a federal appellate court can obtain the state supreme court’s authoritative answer to dispositive state-law questions. Louisiana’s rules permitted certification when controlling state precedent was absent, either on the federal court’s initiative or a party’s motion. The opinion stressed that certification is not obligatory simply whenever state law is unsettled. Federal judges ordinarily are competent to decide state law, and certification can increase expense and delay. The choice generally lies within sound federal judicial discretion. (Slip 3–4.)

## The two questions requiring state guidance here

Two features together made this an exceptional case calling for certification before constitutional adjudication. First, the proposed duty presented novel state-law judgments involving moral, social, and economic considerations: the fairness of liability, development of precedent, and society’s direction. A federal prediction about how Louisiana would balance the value of protest against the consequences of denying damages was particularly unnecessary when the Louisiana Supreme Court was available to answer. Second, guidance would determine whether the supposed state-law conflict with the First Amendment was real rather than hypothetical. The novel tort claim heightened the concern about prematurely invalidating state law. (Slip 4–5.)

The Court identified two questions: **whether Mckesson could have breached a duty of care in organizing and leading the protest, and whether the alleged assault was a particular risk within the scope of protection of that duty, assuming one existed**. The second was not merely a repetition of the first. Even if organizing the protest implicated a duty, it did not follow automatically that an intentional assault on an officer was the kind of harm Louisiana would treat that duty as protecting against. The earlier dissent had questioned whether highway-obstruction rules primarily protected motorists instead. State guidance on these questions could eliminate or change the constitutional dispute. (Slip 2, 4.)

## Exact disposition and retained limits

The Court **granted certiorari, vacated the Fifth Circuit’s judgment, and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion**. It concluded that the circuit should have sought Louisiana Supreme Court guidance on those two questions before venturing into this unsettled tort theory. The Court acknowledged that Louisiana might ultimately announce the same duty the Fifth Circuit had predicted. It expressed no view on whether the circuit should certify or itself resolve other state-law issues raised on remand. (Slip 5.)

The result neither reinstated the district court’s constitutional analysis as a final holding nor established that Mckesson owed a duty, caused the injuries, or was constitutionally immune. Thomas’s dissent appears only as a participation notation, with no rationale to attribute. Barrett’s nonparticipation likewise appears on the final page. There are no judicial footnotes or appendices in the complete slip opinion. The assigned passage uses this decision as a concrete illustration of avoiding a constitutional ruling through authoritative state-law guidance; its quoted explanation corresponds to the Court’s limited reasoning, not a universal duty to certify uncertain questions.
