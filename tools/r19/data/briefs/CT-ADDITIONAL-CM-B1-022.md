# Harrington v. Harrington
365 N.W.2d 552 (N.D. 1985) | Contracts

## Court and result
Justice Gierke wrote the North Dakota Supreme Court's April 1, 1985 opinion. Chief Justice Erickstad, Justice Vande Walle, District Judge Larry M. Hatch, and assigned Surrogate Judge Vernon R. Pederson concurred, producing a unanimous five-member decision without separate writings. The court affirmed dismissal of Nancy Harrington's mortgage foreclosure action and the order requiring her to satisfy two mortgages on her former husband's farm. His agreement to devise the farm to their son supplied consideration despite reserved powers and contingencies affecting what the son might ultimately inherit. The court separately upheld specific performance after examining procedural objections and equitable fairness. (552, 554, 556–558.)

## Settlement, refinancing, and testamentary agreement
Nancy and Gerald divorced in 1981. Their property settlement gave Gerald the farm in exchange for $150,000 to Nancy, consisting of a $10,000 initial payment and fifteen annual installments. Gerald signed a promissory note secured by two farm mortgages. He made the initial payment but only $3,000 in later payments. Financial difficulties then led him to seek refinancing, which Nancy's encumbrances prevented. Late in 1981 he asked her to release the mortgages. Their son Ronn farmed with Gerald, who had remarried and whose current wife was pregnant. Nancy worried that Ronn might not inherit the farm and sought an inheritance commitment in return for giving up her secured debt. (554–555.)

Gerald's attorney prepared an agreement signed on December 30, 1981. Nancy would satisfy both mortgages and release liability on the underlying note. Gerald would make Ronn the sole devisee of the farm land Gerald had owned before January 1, 1978. The contract did not guarantee receipt of an unchanged, debt-free farm. Ronn would inherit subject to encumbrances and would owe Gerald's widow twenty-five percent of the crops annually while she had farm-related debt. Gerald could convey a portion to continue farming if future economic exigencies required it, and Ronn had a first option to buy land sold. (555–556.)

On April 1, 1982 Gerald made a holographic will devising the land to Ronn in accordance with the agreement, conditioned on Nancy's performance. He obtained refinancing and continued farming with Ronn. Nancy nevertheless did not satisfy the mortgages and brought foreclosure on October 3, 1983. The district court held that she was contractually bound to release the mortgages and debt, dismissed her complaint, and required satisfaction. Its judgment allowed Gerald's attorney to apply for a court-ordered satisfaction if she refused. The first note records that the Farmers Home Administration disclaimed an interest, the Security State Bank of Hannaford appeared, and the remaining defendants did not appear. (555–557 & n.1.)

## Existence of consideration and illusoriness
Nancy argued that Gerald retained unlimited freedom to convey or encumber the land, making his promise worthless and illusory. The court distinguished existence of consideration from adequacy. Contractual detriment concerns surrender of a legal freedom, rather than whether the promisee is certain to receive a valuable practical result. A promise is illusory if its apparent commitment disappears on examination because the promisor has committed to nothing. Gerald's undertaking was different: he surrendered his otherwise available freedom to choose another devisee. That legal restriction supplied consideration for Nancy's release even though future circumstances could reduce the devise's value. (555–556.)

The third note importantly limits the reserved powers. Gerald had to act in good faith under the cited statute and could not convey or encumber the farm simply to evade the promised devise. The permission for economic necessity therefore was not unrestricted discretion to defeat the entire arrangement. The main text also rejected the proposition that contingencies affecting the nature or extent of Ronn's inheritance erased Gerald's present legal detriment. (556 & n.3.)

Nancy separately emphasized the current wife's statutory elective share, which could encompass some or all of the land if Gerald died. The wife had not waived that right, as the fourth note records. Nevertheless, the possibility of her later election did not eliminate Gerald's commitment or make it illusory. The fifth note reports Gerald's testimony that a $500,000 life-insurance policy would pay the bank's mortgages, leaving open the possibility of a debt-free inheritance. That was testimony and a possible outcome, not a finding that an eventual inheritance necessarily would be unencumbered or immune from the widow's rights. (556 & nn.4–5.)

## Pleading and adequacy of legal relief
Nancy contended that the satisfaction order improperly granted specific performance because Gerald had not pleaded the compulsory counterclaim required by Rule 13(a). His answer did not label such a counterclaim, although its prayer asked that she be required to satisfy the mortgages. The sixth note confirms that the requested performance arose from the same transaction and was properly compulsory. Rule 15(b), however, permits treatment of an unpleaded issue tried by express or implied consent as if pleaded. The court concluded that Nancy's failure to release the mortgages had been raised at trial and decided, so Rule 13(a) did not bar its litigation. (557 & n.6.)

She also argued that Gerald had failed to demonstrate inadequate legal remedies. The absence of an explicit specific-performance request was not determinative where the pleadings sufficiently notified the opponent of the right asserted and relief demanded. Damages would not accomplish the desired removal of the encumbrances. The court regarded specific performance as competent relief for a need monetary recovery could not meet. (557.)

## Equitable fairness and final disposition
Existence of consideration did not end the equitable inquiry. The second note expressly permits scrutiny of its adequacy when specific performance is requested. The court reproduced North Dakota's statutory limits, including inadequate consideration, unjust or unreasonable terms, improper procurement of assent, and qualifying mistake. Nancy repeated the risks associated with future conveyances and the widow's election as reasons the consideration was inadequate. (555 n.2, 557–558.)

The court applied its rule that mere inadequacy does not defeat specific performance absent unfairness or overreaching; gross imposition, undue influence, or a bargain shocking the conscience can justify denial. It reviewed the trial court's decision for abuse of discretion. Although the agreement was not a model of balanced bargaining, Nancy had not shown the necessary inequity. Gerald's warning that financial constraints might force him and Ronn to stop farming may have made her feel pressure, but the trial court and appellate court did not find undue influence or coercion. The Supreme Court therefore affirmed the judgment requiring mortgage satisfaction. It did not award a present farm interest to Ronn or adjudicate an eventual probate distribution. (558.)
