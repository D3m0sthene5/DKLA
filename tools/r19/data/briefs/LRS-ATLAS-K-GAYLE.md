## Facts and procedural chronology

On February 16, 2001, authorities arrested Rohan Ingram at a hotel in Plattsburgh, New York, suspecting that he had entered the United States illegally from Canada. They found numerous firearms in boxes in his hotel room. A superseding indictment charged conspiracy to export defense articles on the United States Munitions List, conspiracy to travel with intent to engage in illegal acquisition of firearms, and possession of a firearm by a person with a qualifying prior conviction under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(a)(2). Gayle and Richardson appear in the caption, but Ingram is the appellant whose Canadian conviction presents the question here. Original ¶¶ 1–3.

The predicate was Ingram’s 1996 Canadian conviction for using a firearm in commission of an indictable offense under Canadian Criminal Code § 85(1)(a). The offense carried a maximum imprisonment term of fourteen years. He had actually received less than a year, but the district court held that the statute refers to the maximum punishment, not the sentence imposed; he did not challenge that ruling on appeal. The disputed element was the foreign court’s status, rather than the punishment threshold. Original ¶ 10 & n.2.

Ingram moved to dismiss the possession count because his conviction occurred outside the United States. The Northern District of New York denied the motion, treating the statutory reference to any court as unambiguously including foreign tribunals. United States v. Ingram, 164 F. Supp. 2d 310 (N.D.N.Y. 2001). A jury found him guilty on all three counts on October 5, 2001. He received seventy-eight months’ imprisonment, three years’ supervised release, and a $300 assessment on January 30, 2002; the court denied his acquittal motion on February 5. The panel reviewed statutory interpretation de novo. Original ¶¶ 3–4, 10.

## Statutory question and competing positions

Section 922(g)(1), enacted as part of the Gun Control Act of 1968, prohibits specified firearm transactions and possession by someone convicted in any court of a crime punishable by imprisonment exceeding one year. Would Ingram’s Canadian conviction satisfy that predicate? The Government supported the district court’s broad reading. The Third, Fourth, and Sixth Circuits had included foreign courts. The Tenth Circuit’s contrary Concha decision arose in the sentencing-enhancement setting, but depended on the same § 922(g)(1) predicate language. Original ¶¶ 5–11 & n.3.

The Government’s position had textual force: an unrestricted modifier can include courts of many kinds, and earlier decisions had included military courts. Congress’s concern with dangerous firearms possession could sensibly extend to persons convicted abroad of violent offenses. The panel nevertheless found ambiguity after considering the phrase in its statutory setting. Original ¶¶ 12–20, 26.

## Holding and interpretive reasoning

The panel held that § 922(g)(1)’s reference encompasses domestic convictions and excludes foreign convictions. It did not infer that Congress lacked constitutional authority to reach the latter; it determined that Congress had not expressed that intent sufficiently in this statute. Original ¶¶ 25–27.

First, the panel read the predicate provision together with § 921(a)(20). That definition excludes federal or state business-regulation offenses, including antitrust violations, and certain state misdemeanors. Including foreign courts without adjusting these expressly domestic exceptions would produce a troubling asymmetry: a foreign antitrust conviction could prohibit firearm possession even though the equivalent domestic conviction would not. The anomaly cast doubt on the supposedly inevitable broad reading of any. Original ¶¶ 15–19.

Second, an unqualified statutory reference to an official or governmental act can implicitly refer to the enacting government’s own institutions. A state authorization for a police officer to arrest does not necessarily authorize foreign police. Correspondingly, court can take a domestic domain even without an express geographic modifier. At the same time, the panel acknowledged reasons to include foreign violent-crime convictions. These competing plausible readings supported ambiguity rather than establishing the domestic construction by text alone. Original ¶ 20.

Third, the panel consulted authoritative legislative history to resolve that ambiguity. The Senate Judiciary Committee’s report described felony in federal and state terms. The Conference Report adopted the House’s punishment-based formulation instead of the Senate’s felony terminology, without rejecting the domestic limitation or discussing foreign convictions. Taken together, the panel considered those materials evidence of an intended domestic scope. This was an inference from the enacted bill’s legislative history; the Court did not pretend that the Senate wording itself became the final statutory text. Original ¶¶ 21–25 & nn.5–6.

The seventh note added on rehearing addressed the Government’s objection that earlier versions of the firearm prohibition mattered more than legislative activity surrounding the 1968 Act. The panel declined to disregard the authoritative history surrounding the enactment containing the ambiguous term. That note reinforces the methodology without changing the announced judgment. Amended Appendix A, n.7.

Finally, foreign convictions create questions the statute does not answer: whether judgments reached through procedures inconsistent with basic standards of justice should count, or whether convictions for religious disobedience or criticism of government should trigger the prohibition. Those concerns helped explain why the apparently broad phrase was doubtful in context. They did not establish a case-by-case screening rule under which acceptable foreign convictions would count. The holding excludes foreign predicates as a class. Original ¶¶ 26–27.

## Agency regulation and limits

ATF’s regulation expressly included federal, state, and foreign offenses. After supplemental briefing, both parties agreed that ATF’s interpretation of a criminal statute was not entitled to Chevron deference, even if ambiguous. The panel also noted that the agency’s explanation relied on courts’ existing constructions; neither that explanation nor those decisions accounted for the legislative materials identified by the panel. Original n.4. This is the opinion’s historical treatment of that regulation and the parties’ agreement, not a certification of current administrative-deference doctrine.

The panel discussed the Tenth Circuit’s use of lenity but resolved its own ambiguity through statutory context and legislative history. It should not be reduced to a holding that every initially ambiguous criminal provision automatically favors the defendant before other interpretive tools are used.

## Exact judgment and relationship to Small

The panel **reversed Ingram’s conviction on the felon-in-possession count and remanded for resentencing on the remaining counts**. Original ¶ 28. The other conspiracy convictions survived. Note 1 records a separately filed order affirming the district court’s refusal to suppress the firearms evidence; this opinion does not develop that separate ruling’s reasoning. No judge wrote separately on the foreign-predicate question.

The assigned account describes Small v. United States, 544 U.S. 385 (2005), as endorsing the domestic-only interpretation. Small involved a different defendant and a Third Circuit judgment; it was not Supreme Court review of Ingram’s appeal. The separate Small brief supplies its judgment, different reasoning, and dissent. This brief verifies the reported Gayle opinion, its amendment, and the assigned account; it does not certify later statutory changes or subsequent treatment.
