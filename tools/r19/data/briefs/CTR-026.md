# Specht v. Netscape Communications Corp.
306 F.3d 17 (2d Cir. 2002). U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit. Sotomayor, J., joined by McLaughlin and Leval, JJ. Docket Nos. 01-7870, 01-7872, and 01-7860; decided October 1, 2002. Affirming 150 F. Supp. 2d 585 (S.D.N.Y. 2001). | Contracts

## Parties

Plaintiffs / appellees: Christopher Specht, John Gibson, Michael Fagan, Sean Kelly, Mark Gruber, and Sherry
Weindorf, individually and on behalf of proposed classes. Gibson, Fagan, Kelly, Gruber, and Weindorf were software
users; Specht operated a website offering files connected to the WhyWeb internet service. Defendants / appellants:
Netscape Communications Corporation and its corporate parent, America Online, Inc., collectively Netscape, providers
of Netscape Communicator and the SmartDownload plug-in.

## Procedural History

The plaintiffs filed three related putative class actions in the Southern District of New York. They alleged that
SmartDownload secretly transmitted information about downloads to Netscape and thereby violated the Electronic
Communications Privacy Act, 18 U.S.C. §§ 2510 et seq., and the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1030.
The district court kept the actions separate and stated that its arbitrability opinion applied equally to all three.

Netscape moved under Federal Arbitration Act § 4 to compel arbitration and stay the judicial proceedings. It invoked
an arbitration clause in the SmartDownload license, an arbitration clause in the Communicator license, and
direct-benefit estoppel against Specht. After weeks of discovery, the record included affidavits, plaintiff depositions,
witness declarations, screenshots and other visual exhibits, oral argument with a computer demonstration, and
supplemental briefing.

The district court denied the motion. It ruled that the SmartDownload page failed to provide reasonable notice and an
unambiguous assent mechanism; the Communicator license governed a distinct product and transaction; Fagan's
shareware route supplied still less notice; and Specht received no direct contract benefit capable of binding a
nonsignatory. Netscape timely appealed under 9 U.S.C. § 16, and the district court stayed the underlying actions. The
Second Circuit consolidated the appeals on August 10, 2001. Jurisdiction rested on § 16(a)(1)(B), which permits an
immediate appeal from an order denying a motion to compel arbitration.

The court of appeals reviewed denial of arbitration, contract formation, scope, and the direct-benefit theory de novo;
subsidiary factual findings were reviewed for clear error. California law governed formation because the district court
selected it and that choice became fixed on appeal. Federal arbitration law required the court to decide whether an
arbitration agreement existed before applying rules favoring arbitration of disputes within an existing clause. The
Second Circuit affirmed in full.

## Material Facts

Communicator was Netscape's browser program. SmartDownload was a separate plug-in that enhanced downloading.
The complaints alleged that Communicator placed a cookie on a user's computer and SmartDownload placed a separate
identifying Key. When SmartDownload handled a file download, it allegedly sent Netscape the file's internet address
together with the cookie and Key, allowing Netscape to associate a user's identity with online download activity.

Five user plaintiffs downloaded Communicator from Netscape. During installation, each automatically saw a scrollable
Communicator license and had to click 'Yes' before installation could finish. Selecting any other course aborted
installation. The visible assent mechanism therefore tied an identified contract to identified conduct. The
Communicator license defined its covered programs as Communicator and Navigator, contained an integration clause,
and required binding arbitration in Santa Clara County for disputes relating to that agreement. SmartDownload
operated as a separate plug-in outside those defined programs.

Gibson, Gruber, Kelly, and Weindorf obtained SmartDownload from a Netscape page captioned 'SmartDownload
Communicator.' The visible screen promoted the software and displayed 'Start Download' beside a tinted 'Download'
button. Clicking that button immediately began the download. The screen presented the click solely as the act that
started the download.

A reference to SmartDownload's license appeared on the next screen below the download button, visible only after
scrolling. It invited the user to review and agree to the license before downloading and using the software. That
invitation linked to a separate 'License & Support Agreements' page, which then linked to the full product-family
license. The full license stated that clicking an acceptance button or installing or using the product constituted consent
and contained the arbitration clause. Netscape placed no acceptance button in the SmartDownload download sequence
and supplied no later notice while the plug-in operated.

The four Netscape-site users swore that they never saw the license reference when they clicked to download. Weindorf’s attached screenshot showed a partially visible, almost illegible reference; the other three screenshots showed no reference. (306 F.3d at 23 n. 10.) Some acknowledged
in depositions that their memories lacked every detail of the historical display and that monitor configuration could
affect what appeared on one screen. The record established the page's design and the relationship between the visible
download invitation and the submerged terms notice.

Fagan stated that he acquired SmartDownload from ZDNet, an unrelated shareware website. ZDNet showed only a link
for more information, which led to a Netscape page containing another link to the license. This sequence permitted a
user to complete the download while every reference to license terms remained outside the download path.

Specht never obtained or used SmartDownload. Visitors who already had SmartDownload used it to download
WhyWeb-related files from his site. Netscape argued that the plug-in increased download success and thereby
increased Specht's chance to earn commissions from later WhyWeb subscriptions. Specht stated that he received no
commissions from those files.

## Question Presented

Four issues: (1) Did clicking SmartDownload's visible 'Download' button objectively adopt license terms referenced
below that button when the page assigned the click only the function of starting a download? (2) Did the extensive
motion record permit decision as a matter of law under FAA § 4? (3) Did the separate Communicator arbitration clause
reach statutory claims based on SmartDownload? (4) Did Specht receive a direct benefit from a Netscape license
sufficient to require him, as a nonsignatory, to arbitrate?

## Holding

SmartDownload formation: A reasonably prudent user in this interface would understand the visible button as an
instruction to download free software. The interface placed the terms reference below the visible screen and provided
an assent mechanism only for the separate Communicator software. The SmartDownload arbitration clause therefore
remained outside the user plaintiffs' contracts. FAA procedure: Netscape asked the district court to decide the
uncontroverted formation question as a matter of law, and the developed evidentiary record functioned as a hearing
under § 4; the existing record supported final decision. Communicator scope: The statutory claims concerned conduct
by a separate plug-in and fell outside Communicator's agreement. Specht: The hypothesized commission benefit was
indirect and speculative, leaving direct-benefit estoppel unavailable. Disposition: Denial of the motion to compel
arbitration and stay litigation affirmed.

## Reasoning

Formation precedes arbitration: FAA § 2 makes written arbitration provisions enforceable as contracts, subject to
generally applicable contract rules. A court must establish the existence of an agreement containing the arbitration
clause before it applies the federal policy favoring arbitration. State contract law supplied the formation rules, while the
FAA supplied the motion, stay, hearing, and appellate framework.

California assent rule: Agreement may be manifested by spoken words, writing, or conduct. Conduct expresses assent
when the actor intends the conduct and knows or has reason to know that the other party may infer assent from it. The
objective inquiry includes both the act and the transactional context that gives the act meaning. A user's click can adopt
terms when the interface reasonably alerts the user that terms exist and that the click signifies agreement to them.

Notice and interface design: Netscape's visible page praised the product and invited an immediate download. The
license reference appeared after the functional endpoint of that invitation, on a later screen reached by scrolling. The
mere presence of additional page content, reflected by the scroll bar, told a user only that the page continued. It gave
the user no reason to expect that the unseen area contained contractual notice. The pathway to the actual license then
required two hyperlinks.

Meaning of the click: An observer could see each user intentionally click 'Download.' The page assigned that act
solely the function of acquiring the plug-in. The click therefore objectively manifested a request for software; adoption
of the submerged license required a separate, conspicuous signal connecting the act to the terms.

Clickwrap comparison: The Communicator process displayed license text and made installation depend on clicking
'Yes.' The act, its legal meaning, and its consequence appeared together. SmartDownload separated the action from
notice and supplied no assent control. The comparison demonstrated a technically available and legally effective design
for expressing assent. The court's notice-and-manifestation rule applies to every online format.

Paper, shrinkwrap, and online history: Paper cases often charge a recipient with terms contained in or conspicuously
referenced by a document the recipient knows has contractual significance. Shrinkwrap cases such as ProCD and Hill
enforced terms when purchasers received the terms with the product and retained or used it after a stated
review-and-return period. Online delivery carries the same contract principles. The interface must provide contextual
signals equivalent to the notice and choice those transactions supplied. A submerged reference beneath an immediate
free-download invitation supplied neither signal.

FAA § 4 hearing: Section 4 calls for a summary trial when formation is genuinely in issue. Netscape had urged the
district court to decide reasonable notice on uncontroverted facts and asked the judge to resolve the question as a matter
of law. The parties had already completed targeted discovery and assembled an unusually full record with sworn
testimony and visual demonstrations. Appellate courts ordinarily decline a theory first advanced on appeal, and the
record required a finding that Netscape failed to establish adoption.

Scope of the Communicator clause: Communicator's arbitration language was broad, so a presumption of arbitrability
governed disputes related to that agreement. The contract expressly covered Communicator and Navigator and used an
integration clause to define its subject matter. The complaints attributed the interception and transmission to
SmartDownload, a separate product. Resolving those statutory allegations required no construction of the
Communicator license or determination of rights under it. The claims were therefore collateral to that agreement and
outside its clause.

Direct-benefit estoppel: A nonsignatory who knowingly receives a direct, concrete benefit under a contract containing
an arbitration clause can be estopped from accepting the benefit while resisting the clause. Netscape's theory required
unknown users with valid licenses to visit Specht's site, use SmartDownload, obtain WhyWeb files, subscribe later, and
generate commissions. The asserted advantage depended on multiple contingencies, and the alleged contracts conferred
no intended benefit on Specht. That chain lacked the tangible contractual benefit required for estoppel.

Exam rule: For electronic assent, identify the precise screen, the precise user act, the notice visible before that act, the
language linking conduct to legal consequences, and the accessibility of the terms. Analyze formation of the
term-bearing agreement before scope. Treat nonsignatory doctrines as a third inquiry requiring an existing predicate
contract and a direct relationship to its benefits.
