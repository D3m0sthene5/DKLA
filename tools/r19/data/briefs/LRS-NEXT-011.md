# Myers v. United States

**272 U.S. 52 (Oct. 25, 1926) · Legislation and the Regulatory State · LRS-NEXT-011**

The Supreme Court upheld a first-class postmaster's removal without the Senate's consent and rejected his claim for the salary he would otherwise have earned during the rest of his term. Chief Justice Taft treated the power to remove executive officers appointed by the President with Senate consent as constitutionally vested in the President. Congress could not make the exercise of that power depend on obtaining another Senate vote. Holmes, McReynolds, and Brandeis dissented, arguing that Congress could attach enforceable tenure conditions to this inferior statutory office. The judgment affirmed the Court of Claims, but rejected that court's conclusion that Myers had waited too long to sue. (272 U.S. at 106–108, 176–178, 239–242.)

## Facts, claim, and procedural posture

Frank S. Myers was appointed first-class postmaster at Portland, Oregon, on July 21, 1917, by the President with the Senate's advice and consent, for a four-year term. Section 6 of the Act of July 12, 1876, specified that postmasters of the first three classes were to be appointed and could be removed by the President with Senate consent, and would hold office for four years unless sooner removed or suspended according to law. The controversy therefore involved an express statutory restriction on removal, rather than a statute silent about tenure. (Id. at 106–107.)

On January 20, 1920, Myers was asked to resign. He refused. The majority dates his removal to February 2, 1920, when the Postmaster General acted at the President's direction. Myers petitioned the President and the Senate's Post Offices Committee on February 10, requesting an opportunity to answer any charges. He continued protesting to the Department through the end of the appointed term, took no other occupation, and received no compensation for other service. The President made a recess appointment of Jones in August 1920; Jones took office on September 19. That executive replacement did not supply Senate consent to Myers's removal during his term. (Id. at 106–108.)

Myers filed his salary action in the Court of Claims on April 21, 1921. A supplemental petition after the term expired on July 21 sought $8,838.71 for the period following removal. The Supreme Court appeal was prosecuted by his administratrix. The action sought money, rather than a judicial order restoring him to office. McReynolds's separate recital uses February 3 as the removal and salary-claim starting date; the chronology above follows the majority's February 2 date and does not silently reconcile the report's difference. (Id. at 106–108, 178, 187.)

The Court of Claims rejected the claim on the ground of laches, reasoning that a dismissed officer had to assert his rights promptly. The Supreme Court agreed with the general prompt-action requirement but found Myers had satisfied it. He continuously sought reinstatement. A committee hearing depended on a notice of removal or nomination of a successor being sent to the Senate, yet neither occurred during the three Senate sessions before the end of his term. He sued shortly after the Sixty-sixth Congress ended and three months before his term expired. The Solicitor General conceded orally that laches had not been shown, without formally confessing error. Consequently, the constitutional question could not be avoided: if the statutory consent requirement was valid, the administratrix was entitled to the unpaid salary; if it was invalid, the President's removal was effective and the judgment against her could stand on that different ground. (Id. at 107–108.)

## Issue, positions, and governing constitutional provisions

Could Congress require Senate consent before the President removed an inferior executive officer whom the President had appointed with Senate consent? Myers relied on the express postal statute. Arguments opposing unilateral removal emphasized Congress's power to create inferior offices, define their terms and compensation, and prescribe the machinery for carrying federal powers into execution. They also relied on the absence of an express constitutional removal clause and on the protection of fixed tenure discussed in *Marbury v. Madison*. The government answered that Article II supplied an exclusive presidential removal power that legislation could not surrender to the Senate. The Court also acknowledged Senator George Wharton Pepper's presentation, undertaken at its request as a friend of the Court, of arguments against its eventual conclusion. (Id. at 108–109, 125–131, 139–144, 158–164, 176–177.)

Article II, section 1, vests the executive power in a President. Section 2 prescribes presidential nomination and appointment with Senate advice and consent, while allowing Congress to vest appointment of inferior officers in the President alone, courts of law, or department heads. Section 3 requires faithful execution of the laws. The Constitution expressly provides removal through impeachment, and Article III protects constitutional judges' tenure during good behavior, but it contains no general express provision governing ordinary removal of executive officers. The majority therefore resolved the case through text, structure, necessary implications, and historical practice. (Id. at 108–110.)

## Majority's reasoning

Taft read the Executive Vesting Clause as a grant of authority, rather than simply a label for a branch. A President personally responsible for faithful execution must work through subordinate officers. Selecting those officers and dismissing officials whose ability, judgment, loyalty, or performance no longer warrants confidence are executive functions. Without the latter power, responsibility would remain with the President while control over the people executing the laws could reside elsewhere. The Court contrasted Congress's grant of enumerated legislative powers with Article II's general vesting of executive power and treated express qualifications of that grant as limits to be construed within their stated scope. (Id. at 115–118, 131–134, 151–152, 163–164.)

The Senate's participation in appointment did not imply participation in removal. A rejected nominee could ordinarily be replaced by another suitable candidate. A Senate veto over removal, by contrast, could force the President to retain an incumbent whom experience had shown to be unsuitable. The President and his administrators were also better placed to assess defects revealed by an officer's actual service. For Taft, removal followed the President's appointment authority; it did not follow the Senate's narrower authority to advise and consent. Extending the Senate's appointment check by implication would substantially enlarge its control over executive administration. (Id. at 119–125.)

Congress still could create an office, define its duties and jurisdiction, prescribe reasonable qualifications, establish its duration, and set compensation. The majority distinguished those legislative choices from choosing the incumbent and controlling removal after an office existed. It rejected an analogy to inferior federal courts, whose particular jurisdiction Congress distributes among tribunals: creating an executive office activated the Constitution's appointment and removal allocation, rather than giving Congress a continuing power to redistribute that allocation at will. Qualifications themselves could not become a disguised legislative designation of the officer. (Id. at 126–131.)

The 1789 debate over establishing the Department of Foreign Affairs supplied the majority's principal historical support. The House first considered express language making the secretary removable by the President. Benson's amendments substituted language dealing with custody of departmental records when the President removed the secretary, and eliminated the original express removal clause. Taft concluded that this sequence intentionally recognized an existing constitutional power, avoiding the implication that Congress had granted a revocable statutory power. The Senate's division was resolved by Vice President Adams's vote, and President Washington signed the measure. The judicial footnote at page 115 identifies differing contemporary accounts of the Senate's precise tally; the interpretive argument does not depend on concealing that variation. (Id. at 111–115 & n.*.)

Taft gave this early congressional construction substantial weight because it addressed organization of the new government, involved participants in the Constitutional Convention, and was followed by longstanding executive, legislative, and judicial practice. Congressional interpretation was not automatically conclusive; the Court agreed with the substantive reasons supporting it. Its account of later authorities treated *Marbury*'s discussion of irrevocable appointment as unnecessary to the jurisdictional judgment there, and relied on cases such as *Parsons* to reject a fixed term as an unconditional entitlement to remain despite presidential removal. The dissents sharply disputed both that treatment of *Marbury* and the majority's description of historical agreement. (Id. at 136–156, 175–176.)

The majority also addressed the Tenure of Office Act and later statutes apparently restricting removals. It regarded the Reconstruction-era legislation as a departure from the earlier constitutional construction, and presidential objections and later repeal as reasons not to treat that departure as settled acquiescence. A President's approval of legislation containing a restriction did not necessarily establish constitutional acceptance: approval could reflect the value of an appropriation act whose removal provision was a rider. Practice mattered in light of actual opportunities for objection, as well as the views of all branches. The Court held the 1867 Act invalid insofar as it prevented presidential removal of executive officers appointed with Senate consent, and applied the same conclusion to the postal restriction governing Myers. (Id. at 164–176.)

## Qualifications and questions left open

The majority recognized that presidential control of an officer's work could vary with the duties Congress assigned. Some particular statutory decisions might be committed to the officer's own discretion. In a quasi-judicial proceeding affecting individual interests, the President could not properly dictate the particular decision. Nevertheless, Taft maintained that the President could assess an officer's completed decisions when deciding whether the officer's discretion had generally been exercised intelligently and wisely, and remove the officer on that assessment. The opinion thus distinguished controlling an individual adjudication from evaluating continued service; it did not claim every statutory decision was simply the President's personal decision. (Id. at 132–135.)

The inferior-officer exception depended on the appointment arrangement. Under *United States v. Perkins*, when Congress vested appointment in a department head, it could restrict that head's removal authority. A department head had no independent constitutional appointment prerogative overriding those statutory conditions. Congress could not use the exception to draw removal participation to itself or either chamber, and Myers's presidential appointment with Senate consent had not been replaced by the alternative arrangement. The Court expressly left undecided whether vesting appointment of an inferior officer in the President alone would permit additional congressional removal regulation. It also reserved removal questions concerning judges outside Article III, whose functions presented different considerations from those of executive officers. (Id. at 154–164, especially 158, 160–162.)

## The three dissents

Holmes's brief dissent concentrated on Congress's control over its statutory creation. Congress could abolish the office, determine its duration and pay, and transfer appointment of this inferior officer to another authorized actor. Holmes saw no constitutional reason it could not also protect an incumbent's tenure until Congress or the Senate assented to removal. The duty to execute the laws required the President to operate within the laws and the powers Congress lawfully supplied. He expressly agreed with McReynolds's and Brandeis's conclusions. (Id. at 177.)

McReynolds emphasized the particular case: an inferior postmaster appointed for a definite term under an explicit removal restriction. He distinguished an assumed removal power when neither Constitution nor statute restricted tenure from the much stronger claimed power to disregard a positive statutory restriction. Performing an ouster might be executive action, but prescribing when it could occur was legislative. Congress's postal and Necessary and Proper powers, and its ability to entrust postmaster appointments to the department head, undermined a claim that unrestricted presidential removal was indispensable. (Id. at 178–188, 191–193.)

His extended historical analysis rejected the majority's reading of 1789 as a clear constitutional settlement. The debate concerned a superior officer without a fixed term; participants held divergent views, and later statutes supported congressional control over tenure. He treated *Marbury*'s determination of a protected legal right to the office as a necessary step in deciding that case, rather than disposable dictum. He also read *Parsons*, *Perkins*, and *Shurtleff* as consistent with statutory authority to restrict removal. Broad references to executive power could not import undefined royal prerogatives into a government of limited powers. In his view, judgment should go for the administratrix. (Id. at 193–239.)

Brandeis framed the dispute more narrowly still: could the President ignore the consent condition for this inferior civil officer while the Senate was in session? He did not regard removal of military officers, cabinet officers, or removal during a recess as necessary questions. Legislative authority to prescribe tenure included authority to specify its ending conditions. Any implied executive authority should be limited to what the constitutional duty required; suspension could address immediate disloyalty or neglect without establishing an uncontrollable power permanently to remove an inferior officer. (Id. at 240–247.)

Brandeis's extensive notes documented state arrangements, federal tenure laws, presidential compliance, removal through appointment of successors, and restrictions on nominations. Those examples supported his claim that effective government and congressional restrictions were compatible. He interpreted the 1789 votes as coalitions of members with different constitutional premises, not as a majority declaration that Congress had no removal-regulation power. He also traced tenure protections to resistance to the spoils system, contesting an account that reduced them to the Johnson controversy. His constitutional conclusion was that separation of powers deliberately tolerates friction to restrain arbitrary power. The President's duty was to use the lawful means Congress provided, not to claim whatever authority might maximize efficiency. (Id. at 248–295, especially 283–287 & nn.71–75, 291–295.)

## Judgment, assigned scope, and use

The Court affirmed the judgment against the administratrix. Taft wrote the Court's opinion; Holmes, McReynolds, and Brandeis each wrote a dissent. The outcome upheld Myers's removal and defeated his remaining-term salary claim because the statutory Senate-consent condition was unconstitutional, rather than because his objections were untimely. (Id. at 107–108, 176–178.)
