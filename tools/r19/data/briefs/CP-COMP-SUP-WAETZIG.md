# Waetzig v. Halliburton Energy Services, Inc.

**604 U.S. 305 (2025) · Supreme Court of the United States · February 26, 2025**

## A dismissed lawsuit and an unsuccessful arbitration

Gary Waetzig sued his former employer, Halliburton Energy Services, in the District of Colorado. He alleged that the company had terminated him because of his age, violating the Age Discrimination in Employment Act. Halliburton asserted that he had to arbitrate the claim. Waetzig agreed to arbitration and voluntarily dismissed his federal action rather than requesting a stay. Halliburton had not served an answer or moved for summary judgment, so Waetzig’s notice under Rule 41(a)(1)(A)(i) ended the case without a court order. Because this was his first dismissal of these claims, the dismissal was presumptively without prejudice. 604 U.S. at 307–308.

Waetzig lost in arbitration after a telephonic hearing and an award granting Halliburton summary judgment. He maintained that the arbitrator had failed to comply with procedural requirements in the arbitration agreement. Rather than file a new action challenging the award, he returned to the old federal docket and asked the district court to reopen the dismissed case and vacate the award. When the court questioned its ability to consider that request, Waetzig invoked Rule 60(b). Id. at 308–309.

The district court issued two distinct orders. First, it reopened the action, holding that the voluntary dismissal was a final proceeding eligible for Rule 60(b) relief. It found that Waetzig had carelessly dismissed the case under the mistaken belief that the court would retain jurisdiction over the arbitration agreement, rather than seeking a stay or administrative closure. It relied on Rule 60(b)(1) and additionally found a basis under Rule 60(b)(6), reasoning that *Badgerow v. Walters* affected Waetzig’s ability to bring a new case. Second, after reopening the action, it separately vacated the arbitration award. Id. at 309 & n.1, 311.

The Tenth Circuit reversed. It held that a voluntary dismissal without prejudice was neither a final judgment nor a final order because no court judgment or order was required. It also considered the dismissal outside the category of a final proceeding, which it believed required a judicial determination with finality. That conclusion conflicted with decisions of other circuits. Id. at 309–310.

## Question and holding

Does a voluntary dismissal without prejudice under Rule 41(a) count as a final judgment, order, or proceeding from which a district court may grant relief under Rule 60(b)?

Yes. It qualifies as a final proceeding. The Court did not decide whether such a dismissal also qualifies as a judgment, because its conclusion about proceedings resolved the issue. Eligibility for relief under Rule 60(b) does not itself establish that the requirements for granting relief are satisfied in a particular case. Id. at 311–312, 315 n.2, 319.

## Reopening and jurisdiction over the arbitration motion

Halliburton argued that the district court lacked jurisdiction to vacate the arbitration award. The Court distinguished that issue from the antecedent question whether Rule 60(b) permitted reopening the original action. *Badgerow* requires an independent jurisdictional basis for federal review of an arbitration award, and *Kokkonen* indicates that Rule 60(b) cannot create jurisdiction that otherwise does not exist. Those propositions did not determine whether the dismissal fell within Rule 60(b)’s scope. The Court accordingly addressed the first order, reopening the case, while leaving jurisdiction over the second order for consideration on remand. Id. at 310–311.

This separation is central to the decision. Reopening a case under Rule 60(b) is not a ruling that every new motion filed on that docket necessarily falls within federal jurisdiction.

## Why the dismissal was final

Justice Alito’s unanimous opinion used text, context, and history. The word final entered Rule 60(b) in 1946. Contemporary legal dictionaries described it as terminating, completed, or conclusive. A voluntary dismissal without prejudice fits that meaning because it completes the particular lawsuit, even if the plaintiff can pursue the same claims in a later action. The Advisory Committee’s explanation also indicated that the word excluded interlocutory determinations, which remain subject to revision during an ongoing case. Id. at 312–313.

Once the plaintiff dismisses the action, however, the court no longer exercises its ordinary authority over an ongoing case. Rule 60(b) supplies an avenue for relief from the act that ended it. Treating a voluntary dismissal as neither interlocutory nor final would create a third category unsupported by the rule’s history. The possibility of refiling therefore did not prevent finality for purposes of reopening this terminated action. Id. at 313.

The Court declined to import the meaning of finality used in appellate-jurisdiction statutes. Under § 1291, finality limits appeals that otherwise would interrupt trial proceedings as a matter of statutory right. Rule 60(b) instead concerns discretionary relief in a case that has already ended. Those contextual differences gave the Court no reason to assume the rule adopted appellate finality’s requirements. Id. at 313–315.

## Why the dismissal was a proceeding

The term proceeding has a broad legal meaning encompassing steps in conducting litigation, including docket filings. Other Civil Rules likewise use proceedings to cover the activity in a lawsuit generally, as when a court stays further proceedings for failure to comply with discovery or pay costs. Id. at 315–316.

Halliburton argued that the neighboring words judgment and order limited a proceeding to judicial action or a conclusive determination of rights. The Court accepted that neighboring words matter but found that this context supported the opposite conclusion. A formal judicial determination would already be an order. Reading proceeding to require the same thing would deprive it of independent significance. The sequence judgment, order, or proceeding instead moves from narrower to broader categories. Id. at 316–317.

Halliburton’s suggested distinction between dismissals with and without prejudice also failed. Both occur through a filing. And dismissal without prejudice can have serious legal consequences; here, the limitations period apparently prevented Waetzig from continuing through a newly filed action. That observation did not turn the dismissal into a merits adjudication, but reinforced that its procedural effect could warrant relief under the rule. Id. at 318.

The historical source of Rule 60(b) supported the same reading. The rule was modeled on a California provision authorizing relief from judgments, orders, or other proceedings. Before the federal rule’s enactment, California’s Supreme Court interpreted that provision to reach voluntary dismissals, with the presence or absence of prejudice immaterial to eligibility for relief. Later federal amendments retained the three categories, and the addition of final did not exclude a voluntary dismissal that terminated the action. Id. at 318–319.

## Exact disposition and assigned context

The Court reversed the Tenth Circuit and remanded. It expressly declined to decide whether the district court properly granted Rule 60(b) relief to Waetzig or possessed jurisdiction to vacate the arbitration award. Those questions remained for the lower courts to the extent the arguments had been preserved. The decision therefore restores the possibility of reopening; it does not itself sustain the vacatur of the arbitration award or decide the age-discrimination claim. Id. at 319–320.

Alito wrote for a unanimous Court. There were no separate opinions. The complete opinion’s two numbered footnotes address the district court’s additional Rule 60(b)(6) rationale and the reserved question whether the dismissal also constitutes a judgment. An asterisked amici note in the published report is distinct from those judicial notes.

The assigned supplement, printed page 512 within physical PDF page 345, presents a short captioned account emphasizing finality and the breadth of proceeding. The complete opinion supplies the two-order procedural sequence, independent-jurisdiction reservation, lower court’s alternative grounds, distinction from appellate finality, California statutory history, and express limits on the relief awarded. These details prevent the rule of eligibility from being mistaken for automatic relief or automatic federal jurisdiction.
