# Corl v. Huron Castings, Inc.
450 Mich. 620, 544 N.W.2d 278 (1996) | Contracts

## Court, issue, and result

On March 1, 1996, the Michigan Supreme Court held that unemployment compensation had to be deducted from damages for breach of the employee's just-cause employment agreement. Justice Riley wrote for Chief Justice Brickley and Justices Mallett and Weaver. Justice Cavanagh dissented, joined by Justice Levin; Justice Boyle wrote a separate dissent. The 4–3 court reversed the Court of Appeals and remanded for entry of an award consistent with the deduction. The employee's liability verdict was not undone: the dispute concerned the amount recoverable after benefits already received. (450 Mich. at 623–625, 639–640, 650.)

## Employment, stipulation, and lower-court rulings

William Corl worked for Huron Castings from July 1981 until his May 1988 termination. He asserted rights under Toussaint, alleging a legitimate expectation that his employment could be terminated only for good cause. Before trial, the parties stipulated that damages were $16,500 after a $6,200 deduction for unemployment benefits, and reserved for the judge whether the award should be increased by that $6,200 if Corl won. Thus, the underlying stipulated total was $22,700; the benefits question did not require a jury to estimate additional injury. (Id. at 623–624 & nn. 1–2.)

The jury found for Corl, and judgment was initially entered for $16,500. The judge then added $6,200 under Pennington, despite considering the result illogical. The Court of Appeals likewise affirmed in an unpublished memorandum because it viewed Pennington as binding. The Supreme Court granted the employer's appeal to reconsider that rule and the governing damages principles. (Id. at 624–625 & nn. 3–5.)

## Contract rather than tort damages

The majority treated Toussaint rights as enforceable in contract. The employer's duty to discharge only for cause arose from the employment undertaking, not a tort duty imposed generally by law. The usual contract remedy aimed to put the employee in the position full performance would have produced, rather than punish breach. Unemployment benefits had already replaced part of the wage loss, so allowing the full stipulated damage amount without deduction would exceed that compensatory position. The collateral-source rule, which ordinarily prevents a tortfeasor from benefiting from compensation received from other sources, did not govern this common-law contract claim. (Id. at 625–628.)

The majority distinguished federal labor and discrimination remedies. Gullett upheld the labor board's discretion to decline a deduction as a means of implementing the governing statute's policies; it did not compel the same measure in an ordinary contract action. Other contract authority had deducted social-security and annuity payments to avoid a recovery exceeding what performance would have provided. Statutory remedial discretion and common-law contract compensation were therefore not interchangeable. (Id. at 628–632.)

## Pennington and legislative developments

Pennington had rejected unemployment-benefit deductions in an employment-contract case. The majority regarded that discussion as unnecessary to its disposition and thus dicta, but also explained why it should not control even if binding. Its analogy to a personal-injury collateral-source decision did not account for the contract character of the claim. Its premise that unemployment payments were not in lieu of wages had also been undermined by later legislation coordinating unemployment and workers' compensation for identical periods. The court inferred recognition of both as wage-loss replacement. (Id. at 632–637 & nn. 24–31.)

The majority applied a stare-decisis principle permitting departure when a clear error or changed considerations would perpetuate injustice. It rejected the dissent's legislative-acquiescence inference: the workers' compensation coordination provision itself showed changed legislative treatment of duplicative wage-loss payments. Because the damage award here arose under judicially administered contract law, the court considered it unnecessary to await an amendment specifically addressing this award. That was the majority's inference and justification, rather than an express statutory directive governing Corl's contract damages. (Id. at 632–637.)

Corl also argued that deduction would confer a windfall on the employer. The majority responded that unemployment financing tied an employer's contributions in part to claims charged to its experience account. Arguments that costs were ultimately passed to consumers and employees did not change the result; on that reasoning, the public could likewise bear the added burden of unreduced damages. It saw no policy reason to extend the tort collateral-source rule here. Its final instruction required subtracting benefits already received, restoring the $16,500 figure dictated by the stipulation. (Id. at 637–639.)

## Cavanagh and Levin's dissent

Cavanagh would affirm. He disagreed that Pennington's instruction was merely incidental: it supplied a legal rule intended to guide the remanded proceedings, even though the earlier aggregate verdict could not stand. He also rejected the majority's characterization of an isolated sentence as declining to reach the deduction issue. Its context concerned the plaintiffs' requested immediate enlargement of the verdict, rather than whether the deduction principle mattered at a new trial. (Id. at 640–643.)

For him, repeated legislative amendments without disapproval of Pennington demonstrated acquiescence. Express coordination of workers' compensation, but not employment-contract damages, showed that lawmakers knew how to require offsets when desired. He further disputed treating all contracts as fungible. Employment supplied benefits and security beyond wages; wage recovery alone did not necessarily restore the position performance would have provided. On both precedent and remedial grounds, he opposed the deduction. Levin joined that dissent. (Id. at 643–650.)

## Boyle's separate dissent

Boyle likewise would affirm but supplied additional reasons. Although recovery under Toussaint was limited to contract damages, she viewed its doctrinal foundation as partly equitable and fault-based rather than wholly conventional contract law. The label therefore did not automatically answer the collateral-benefit question. Wage-based labor remedies could make a claimant whole without considering every collateral loss and benefit. (Id. at 650–653.)

She also regarded full deduction as an overcredit: employer contributions were not dollar-for-dollar reimbursement of what this employee received, and the state, rather than the employer, paid the benefits. The record offered no method for determining the employer's true contribution. Giving the employer the entire deduction could also foreclose reimbursement of the unemployment fund. The legislature could prescribe credits or recoupment, but had not done so; those allocation and valuation questions were, in her view, especially appropriate for legislative choice. (Id. at 653–654 & nn. 2–3.)
