# Campos-Chaves v. Garland
144 S. Ct. 1637 (2024) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, question, and result

On June 14, 2024, the Supreme Court decided consolidated Nos. 22-674 and 22-884 by a 5–4 vote. Alito wrote for Roberts, Thomas, Kavanaugh, and Barrett; Jackson dissented for Sotomayor, Kagan, and Gorsuch. A noncitizen who received a proper paragraph (2) notice for the hearing he missed could not obtain rescission of an in absentia removal order on the notice ground in § 1229a(b)(5)(C)(ii), even though the initial notice to appear lacked a hearing date and time. The Court affirmed the Fifth Circuit in Campos-Chaves, reversed the Ninth Circuit in Mendez-Colín, and vacated and remanded the Ninth Circuit judgment in Singh because an alternative rescission argument remained unaddressed. (Majority 1–2, 7–8, 16–17 & n. 2.)

## Three factual records and procedural paths

Campos-Chaves, an El Salvador national, entered without inspection near Laredo in 2005. Three days later the government served a notice to appear charging removability and naming the immigration court's address, but leaving the date and time to be set. A notice of hearing sent a few months later specified September 20, 2005, at 9 a.m. He did not attend or explain his absence; the immigration judge found removability established by the required evidence and ordered removal in absentia. Thirteen years later he sought reopening based on Pereira and the defective initial notice. The judge and BIA rejected that ground, and the Fifth Circuit denied review because he did not dispute receipt of the later hearing notice. (Majority 4–5.)

Singh, an Indian national, entered by climbing a fence near Calexico in 2016. Several weeks later he received a notice charging removability but listing the hearing date and time as TBD. Five days afterward the government sent a specific hearing notice to his supplied address. In October 2018 the court sent another notice setting November 26 at 1 p.m. He did not attend, but the government lacked his file, so the court rescheduled to December 12 at 9 a.m. and again sent notice. He missed that hearing too and was ordered removed in absentia. In April 2019 he sought rescission because the initial notice did not comply with Pereira. The judge and BIA rejected the motion, but the Ninth Circuit held that absence of a single-document notice to appear was enough to permit rescission. Rehearing en banc was denied over a twelve-judge dissent. (Majority 5–6.)

Mendez-Colín, a Mexican national, attempted entry at San Luis in 2001 with two other noncitizens while falsely claiming U.S. citizenship. His notice to appear named the immigration court but left hearing time and date to be set. He attended the hearing scheduled by a later notice. Further hearings followed in 2002, with notices given to him or his attorney and attendance at all those hearings. After sustaining removability, the judge scheduled September 15, 2003, at 9 a.m. for his intended cancellation application, sending notice to counsel. Counsel sought withdrawal days before the hearing for lack of client contact but attended; the judge granted withdrawal while retaining counsel to serve any in absentia order. The judge found the claims for relief abandoned and ordered removal in absentia. Two reopening motions failed, and the BIA deemed the appeal from the second withdrawn when Mendez-Colín was removed during its pendency. About fifteen years later he sought reinstatement based on the defective initial notice. The BIA refused; the Ninth Circuit granted review in an unpublished decision alongside Singh. (Majority 6–7.)

## Statutory setting and opposing positions

Paragraph (1) of § 1229(a) requires a notice to appear containing specified information, including the hearing's time and place and consequences of nonappearance. Niz-Chavez requires the paragraph (1) information in a single document. Paragraph (2), for a change or postponement, requires written notice of the new time or place and nonappearance consequences. If the government establishes the required notice and removability by clear, unequivocal, and convincing evidence, a noncitizen who fails to attend must be ordered removed in absentia. (Majority 2–3.)

Rescission is available on specified grounds: exceptional circumstances causing nonappearance, raised within 180 days; government custody with nonappearance through no fault of the noncitizen; or nonreceipt of notice in accordance with paragraph (1) or (2). These cases concerned the third ground. The government conceded that none of the initial notices complied with paragraph (1), but argued that each later received the proper paragraph (2) notice for the missed hearing. The noncitizens read the disjunction distributively: absence of a valid paragraph (1) notice, or absence of a valid paragraph (2) notice, independently permitted rescission. They also argued that a paragraph (2) change notice required an earlier compliant paragraph (1) notice. (Majority 3, 7–8, 13–14.)

## Majority reasoning

The ordinary disjunctive meaning of “or” meant that either type of notice could satisfy the notice condition. Context supported that reading. The nearby provision authorizing in absentia removal also referred to paragraph (1) or (2), and required proof of “the written notice,” singular. Construing rescission to require both notices would make an order validly obtainable on one notice but defeasible for failure to supply both, an incongruity Congress was unlikely to intend. (Majority 8–10.)

This did not mean that any notice of any hearing sufficed. The relevant notice was the one for the missed hearing at which removal was ordered. A changed-hearing notice superseded the earlier notice for scheduling purposes. The rescission requirement corresponded to the government's notice burden for that particular hearing. For example, receipt of an initially compliant notice would not prevent rescission if the government later changed the hearing date without telling the noncitizen. The other rescission grounds similarly excused nonappearance for reasons not attributable to the noncitizen; the relevant-notice interpretation fitted that structure. (Majority 11–13.)

Each later notice here supplied the specific time of the missed hearing and the consequences of nonappearance. The majority rejected the argument that there could be no change or new time unless an old specific time had been supplied. Changing could mean altering, modifying, switching, or replacing; a notice could change a TBD designation to an actual hearing time. Congress referred to any change. A new time could mean a recently supplied or novel time without replacing an earlier specific time, just as a new baby need not replace an old baby. Mendez-Colín's added individual hearing also changed his proceedings' schedule. (Majority 13–15.)

Pereira's observation that paragraph (2) presupposed a prior time-and-place notice did not resolve this question. Pereira concerned the stop-time trigger for cancellation eligibility; the meaning of paragraph (2) was not at issue, and that observation was supplementary dicta. Niz-Chavez's recognition that paragraph (2) permits amending an inconvenient chosen time remained true without excluding other uses of paragraph (2). Neither decision was overruled concerning the initial notice or stop-time rule. (Majority 15–16 & n. 1.)

The government remained obligated to issue a proper notice to appear, including charges and other information important to presenting a case. A recipient of only a later hearing notice still had to attend and could raise the initial notice's defects there, allowing the judge to reschedule to cure prejudice. The specific rescission provision did not permit indefinite challenges based on defects that could have been raised at a hearing the recipient skipped. (Majority 16.)

## Jackson's dissent

Jackson treated the paragraph (1) notice as indispensable and paragraph (2) as supplementary. All the first notice's listed contents were mandatory, and many were not repeated in a hearing-change notice. Pereira and Niz-Chavez had established that missing time-and-place information meant there was no compliant initial notice, and later backfilling did not transform it into one. The majority's focus on whichever notice concerned the missed hearing severed the statutory sequence. (Dissent 1–8.)

A change notice was like a construction change order: it altered selected terms of an existing instruction without replacing the instruction itself. Reading the words in context, a change, postponement, and new time presupposed a previously set time. The modifier any covered different kinds of changes, not absence of an initial scheduled hearing. The conditional and narrower paragraph (2) notice was not an equivalent alternative to paragraph (1); “or” reflected that a supplemental notice sometimes would be needed and sometimes not. Thus even accepting an either-notice formulation, none of these noncitizens had received either a compliant initial notice or a valid supplemental one. (Dissent 8–14 & n. 2.)

Jackson rejected dismissal of the prior cases' reasoning as dicta. Their analysis concerned the same notice provisions, confirmed the statutory sequence, and deserved respect even if not formally binding here. Note 1 assumed rescission was the issue presented but questioned whether deficient notice also undermined the original order under the government's proof burden; note 3 made the parallel point. (Dissent 3 n. 1, 14–16 & n. 3.)

Her historical account traced notice obligations through the 1952 Act, mandatory in absentia procedures introduced in 1990, and the 1996 scheme. Congress sought efficient removal while retaining notice safeguards and incentives for both parties to comply. The government's fear of reopening many orders resulted from its own systemic noncompliance and did not justify changing that balance. Jackson warned that the majority supplied no adequate limit for notices lacking counsel information or even charges, which later hearing notices did not contain. Requiring attendance to protest the government's defect shifted a burden Congress placed on the government onto unfamiliar noncitizens. She would permit seeking rescission under these circumstances, while expressly reserving whether rescission was automatic or discretionary. (Dissent 17–23 & nn. 4–6.)

## Exact disposition and assigned scope

The Fifth Circuit judgment was affirmed and the Ninth Circuit's Mendez-Colín judgment reversed. Singh's judgment was vacated and remanded because the Ninth Circuit had not reached his separate exceptional-circumstances argument under § 1229a(b)(5)(C)(i); the Supreme Court did not decide it either. (Majority 16–17 & n. 2.)
