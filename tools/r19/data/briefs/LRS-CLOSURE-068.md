# Murray’s Lessee v. Hoboken Land and Improvement Co.
59 U.S. (18 How.) 272 (1856), decided February 19, 1856 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Rival land titles traceable to a defaulting customs collector

The case arose through ejectment litigation between parties claiming under Samuel Swartwout, a former New York customs collector. Plaintiffs claimed through an execution levied April 10, 1839. Defendants claimed through a marshal’s June 1, 1839 sale under a Treasury distress warrant. Under the May 15, 1820 statute, the government’s lien arose upon levy and recording in the district court. That levy preceded the judgment supporting plaintiffs’ title, making the warrant’s validity decisive for priority. Three cases presenting the same questions came from the New Jersey circuit court on certificates of division between its judges. (272, 274.)

The special verdict found that Swartwout had served as collector for eight years before March 29, 1838. On November 10, 1838, Treasury accounting officers audited and certified a balance of $1,374,119.65 due from him; the Solicitor of the Treasury issued the warrant for that balance. Plaintiffs did not claim irregularity in the statutory steps preceding issuance. They argued that the procedure authorized by Congress was unconstitutional. The case therefore concerned title resulting from administrative collection, not a new criminal prosecution of the collector. (275.)

## Constitutional challenges and posture

Plaintiffs argued that determining indebtedness and issuing execution were judicial acts entrusted exclusively to Article III courts. Treasury officials lacked both judicial status and a connection to an Article III court that could support even ministerial judicial work. They also argued that seizure without ordinary judicial proceedings violated Fifth Amendment due process. Their counsel challenged the absence of hearing and jury trial, the burden placed on the debtor, and the absence of an oath supporting the warrant. (273, 275–276.)

Justice Curtis’s opinion treated the due-process and Article III issues together: could the government enforce a customs collector’s administratively determined balance without exercising Article III judicial power, yet provide due process? The Court acknowledged that if these Treasury acts were exercises of that judicial power, they would be void. It did not simply equate an executive officer’s application of law to facts with constitutional judicial power. (275–276, 280–281.)

## Due process constrained Congress, but did not always require an ordinary trial

The Court traced due process to Magna Carta’s law-of-the-land guarantee and Coke’s understanding of it. It considered the Constitution’s separate jury guarantees in explaining why the Fifth Amendment used due-process terminology. The fact that Congress enacted a process did not make it constitutionally sufficient: the Amendment constrained legislative, executive, and judicial action, and Congress could not define away that restraint by calling its chosen procedure due process. (276.)

The Court’s historical test had two parts. First, examine the Constitution for a conflicting provision. Second, consult settled English common-law and statutory procedures before migration, as accepted and used under American civil and political conditions. The pertinent tradition was collection of public revenue debts, particularly balances due from revenue receivers, rather than ordinary private debt litigation. (277.)

English law had long used summary measures against Crown debtors, with special accounting and execution practices for revenue officers. Magna Carta itself regulated, rather than eliminated, collection against their goods and lands. Curtis reviewed exchequer records, auditors, inquisitions without notice, and procedures resembling the federal distress warrant. Colonial and early state laws used comparable distress processes against defaulting collectors, including seizure and sometimes imprisonment. Early federal revenue acts repeated that approach. The Court treated this longstanding practice and legislative construction as substantial evidence that the 1820 mechanism was due process for this kind of public debt. (277–280.)

The opinion acknowledged that due process generally involves opposing parties, regular allegations, an opportunity to answer, and a trial before a judge. But the established public-revenue exception showed that this was not universal. Its conclusion was tied to ascertainment and recovery of balances due from a customs collector, rather than a declaration that historical executive practice could replace adjudication of every private dispute. (280.)

## Revenue administration and matters involving public rights

Judgment about law and facts appears throughout administration; that broad descriptive sense of “judicial” does not establish that an act necessarily exercises Article III power. Congress’s revenue powers included choosing collection officers, accounting duties, security, and appropriate means to recover public money still held by them. The power did not end when the collector first received the funds. The Court distinguished the revenue side of the English exchequer from its ordinary judicial side, avoiding an inference that the name “court of exchequer” made every accounting operation judicial. (280–282.)

Plaintiffs argued that because the 1820 statute allowed the collector to ask a district court to review indebtedness, the subject necessarily belonged to courts alone. The Court rejected that binary. The government could sue its debtor or consent to a suit testing the debt. Providing review waived governmental immunity on Congress’s stated terms; it did not prove that administrative collection was constitutionally unavailable without that consent. (282–284.)

Curtis added important boundaries. Congress could not withdraw from judicial cognizance a matter inherently belonging to a common-law, equity, or admiralty suit, or turn an inherently nonjudicial matter into judicial business. But some matters involving public rights were susceptible to judicial determination while Congress could choose whether and how to provide a federal judicial remedy. Claims to lands in ceded territories illustrated that category. In private litigation, an executive determination validly committed to executive authority could also be conclusive on a particular issue. These passages explain the Court’s reasoning; they do not supply an unrestricted license to transfer all private-rights adjudication to executive officers. (283–285.)

## Review, warrant objections, and certified answer

As justification for the marshal’s levy, the lawfully issued warrant conclusively established the recited facts and authority. Congress nonetheless allowed a court to determine the debt and restrain collection within the amount judicially found. Allowing the collector to file a bill and provide security after collection began did not undermine the initial need for summary action: the levy secured payment while the debt could be tested. Congress judged when that collection mechanism was necessary. (285.)

The Fourth Amendment objection also failed. The warrant was a civil debt-collection execution, with no search authority beyond the ordinary execution process; its statutory name did not transform it into a search warrant requiring the asserted oath. As for failure to levy on corporate shares before land, the shares did not appear to be goods or chattels subject to levy at that time. The marshal’s return establishing levy under the warrant was at least prima facie evidence against the claimed irregularity arising from other available property. (285–286.)

The Court **answered the third certified question affirmatively**: the warrant and proceedings could transfer Swartwout’s title against plaintiffs’ lessors. This made the first two questions, concerning a mortgage and its authorization, immaterial; the Court returned no answer to them. The same opinion disposed of the companion certificates. This was a certified-question disposition, not a reported affirmance of a final lower-court judgment. No separate judicial writing or numerical vote is stated. (272, 286.)
