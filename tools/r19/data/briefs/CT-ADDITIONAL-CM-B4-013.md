# Spang Industries, Inc., Fort Pitt Bridge Division v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.
512 F.2d 365 (2d Cir. 1975) | Contracts

## Court and dispute
The Second Circuit decided this appeal on February 26, 1975. Judge Mulligan wrote for Judges Waterman, Hays, and Mulligan; no separate opinion appears. Fort Pitt challenged a highway contractor’s offset for expenses caused by late steel delivery and sought correction of interest on its unpaid contract balance. The court sustained the damage offset but required recalculation of interest and separate amended judgments against the contractor and its surety. (365–367, 371–372.)

## Facts and timing of the agreement
Torrington won a New York highway reconstruction contract that included a 270-foot, two-span bridge over the Battenkill River near the Vermont border. Before bidding, it obtained Fort Pitt’s quotation for fabrication, supply, and erection of about 240 tons of structural steel at 27.5 cents per pound. A September 5, 1969 confirmation expressly left delivery to mutual agreement. Fort Pitt had project specifications showing work would begin October 1, 1969, with overall completion by December 15, 1971. (366, 368.)

On November 3, in response to Fort Pitt’s inquiry, Torrington specified late June 1970 for steel delivery and erection. Fort Pitt said November 12 that it was tentatively scheduling accordingly. It later asked whether June remained valid and received confirmation. Not until January 29, 1970 did it announce that expansion and other delays would prevent meeting that date. After unanswered requests and Torrington’s threat to cancel, Fort Pitt promised early August delivery. It missed that date as well: small parts shipped August 21, but heavy structural components were shipped only from August 24 through September 4. (366–367.)

Fort Pitt also failed to notify Syracuse Rigging, its unloading and erection subcontractor, of the first shipment. When steel began arriving at the railhead around September 1 and the railroad demanded unloading, Torrington had to do that work until Syracuse arrived September 8. Enough steel was available for erection only by September 16. Erection finished October 8, and the bridge was ready for concrete October 28. State specifications ordinarily required pouring at forty degrees Fahrenheit or warmer; special permission was obtained to pour at thirty-two degrees. With freezing weather imminent, Torrington completed a crash pour lasting until 1 a.m., incurring overtime, equipment, and concrete-protection expenses. (367.)

## Procedure and parties’ positions
Fort Pitt sued Aetna, which had provided Torrington’s labor-and-material bond, for the unpaid subcontract balance. That federal action was transferred to the Northern District of New York. Torrington separately sued Fort Pitt in state court for delay damages; removal brought that action to the same federal court, where the cases were consolidated. After a three-day bench trial, the court found Fort Pitt in breach and allowed Torrington a $7,653.57 damage offset against the amount due. A judgment for Fort Pitt against Torrington and Aetna followed; later amendment left uncertainty about Aetna’s obligation. Fort Pitt’s third-party action against Syracuse ended in Syracuse’s favor, and no appeal from that judgment was before the court. (367 & n. 1; 372 n. 9.)

Fort Pitt did not contest the trial court’s factual findings, breach determination, or rejection of its delay excuses. It argued that the expenses were special damages outside the parties’ contemplation in September 1969. Because the overall project could run until December 1971, it maintained that it could not then foresee Torrington’s need for steel in 1970, and subsequent knowledge could not expand its liability under Hadley v. Baxendale. Torrington responded that the relevant knowledge existed when the parties subsequently fixed the reserved delivery date. (367–369.)

## Knowledge when an open performance date is fixed
The court accepted the governing rule that breach damages must be reasonably anticipated at contracting and that notice of unusual circumstances ordinarily must precede or accompany that commitment. It nevertheless rejected Fort Pitt’s application. Where the agreement expressly reserved the performance date for later mutual determination, knowledge of consequences was assessed when the parties agreed upon that date. Fort Pitt accepted a June 1970 requirement pursuant to the original agreement. It could not treat the September writing as authorizing reliance on a 1971 delivery date after accepting a different one. Its contemporaneous behavior—initial acceptance followed much later by explanations for nonperformance—reinforced that conclusion. (368–369.)

The court left open whether the September contract was already valid despite the unspecified date or became valid only when that material term was settled. Under either characterization, Fort Pitt knew the required delivery timing when it became committed to it. This was not unilateral expansion of liability by later notice unrelated to a contractual reservation. Note 3 also distinguished the generally rejected tacit-agreement embellishment to Hadley from the requirement of reasonable contemplation. (369 & n. 3.)

## Foreseeable construction consequences and reasonable mitigation
Fixing the knowledge date did not automatically establish recovery. The court next examined the type and scale of the loss. An experienced bridge fabricator would understand the ordinary sequence from steel erection to pouring a concrete deck. It knew the northern location, had the specifications, and had sold bridge steel to Torrington before. Delayed delivery beyond summer predictably jeopardized concrete work before freezing weather. The trial court found that prolonged cold would otherwise force postponement until June 1971. (369–370 & nn. 4–6.)

Torrington’s response avoided a potentially much greater loss through prompt, reasonable mitigation at modest cost. The offset covered unloading occasioned by Fort Pitt’s notification failure and premium time, additional equipment, and work-protection costs associated with the accelerated pour. The trial court had reduced Torrington’s original $23,290.81 claim because it did not prove that supervisory costs, overhead, and some equipment expenses were directly attributable to the delay. The appellate court therefore did not approve every expense incurred on the project or award the contractor’s hypothetical profits from a longer postponement. (370–371.)

Note 7 states that reasonable expenditures to avoid foreseeable breach harm may themselves be recovered, whether the mitigation attempt succeeds or fails. Here the successful crash program reduced serious loss to relatively small expenses. The court considered those expenses reasonably anticipated by an experienced supplier, rather than the kind of unusual, uncommunicated loss that Hadley excludes. It affirmed their use as an offset against Fort Pitt’s contract price. (370–371 & nn. 7–8.)

## Interest and exact disposition
Fort Pitt remained entitled to statutory interest on overdue sums. Torrington made partial payments in February and September 1972 after litigation began, but those payments did not extinguish the debt. Fort Pitt had demanded interest in its counterclaim. Accepting partial payments without protest therefore did not waive interest; the rule applicable to full payment of principal did not control. Payments had to be applied first to accrued interest and only then to principal. (371–372.)

The district court also needed to account for the statutory rate change from seven and one-half percent to six percent effective September 1, 1972, applying the appropriate rate to each period. Because later amendment of the judgment had obscured Aetna’s obligation, the appellate court directed amended, separate judgments against Torrington and Aetna after interest was recomputed. It affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded, with no appellate costs. There was no final new interest total fixed by the appellate mandate. (372 & n. 9.)
