# Louisiana Power & Light Co. v. City of Thibodaux

**360 U.S. 25 (1959). Supreme Court of the United States, June 8, 1959.** Justice Frankfurter wrote the Court’s opinion. Justice Stewart separately concurred in the judgment. Justice Brennan dissented, joined by Chief Justice Warren and Justice Douglas: a 6–3 judgment, with Stewart’s separate rationale. Assigned account: CivPro PDF 280, printed 524, within Note 3 on uncertain state law.

## The condemnation dispute and the unusual stay

The City of Thibodaux began a Louisiana state-court proceeding to expropriate land, buildings, and equipment owned by Louisiana Power & Light, a Florida corporation. The company removed the action to the Eastern District of Louisiana on diversity grounds. Louisiana Act 111 of 1900 appeared to authorize municipal acquisition of utility property through eminent domain, but no Louisiana court had interpreted its application to a situation like this one. An opinion of the state attorney general had concluded that a city lacked the asserted authority in a strikingly similar situation. After a pretrial conference, the district judge ordered a stay on his own initiative so the Louisiana Supreme Court could first interpret the statute. (25–26, 30.)

The Fifth Circuit reversed, reasoning that the procedure was unavailable in an expropriation case and that exceptional circumstances were absent in any event. The Supreme Court granted review of whether the stay was permissible. Although the certiorari petition also questioned the stay order’s appealability, the Court expressly excluded that question from its grant; this decision did not resolve it. (26 & n.1.)

## Issue and holding

Could a federal diversity court temporarily retain but stay this condemnation action pending a prompt authoritative determination of the uncertain state statute? The Court held that it could. It reversed the Fifth Circuit and reinstated the stay. The decision did not authorize dismissal merely because state law was difficult, decide that the city could condemn the property, or set compensation. (27–31.)

The majority accepted the general rule that difficulty of state law alone does not justify surrendering federal jurisdiction, as Meredith had held. It distinguished that case’s dismissal from this district judge’s retained-case postponement. More fundamentally, eminent domain involved a sovereign prerogative. Determining whether a city possessed delegated condemnation authority addressed the allocation of governmental power between state and city, through locally varying statutes understood in local settings. The federalism considerations supporting stays in conventional equity cases were therefore relevant even though this condemnation proceeding was legally classified as a suit at common law. (26–28 & n.2.)

## Why authoritative state interpretation mattered

The apparent statutory grant and contrary attorney-general opinion left the district judge with a genuine uncertainty. A federal prediction might govern only this dispute, while a later Louisiana Supreme Court interpretation governed all subsequent litigants differently. The majority regarded obtaining the state court’s controlling interpretation as a sensible discharge of judicial duty, not evasion of it. The special combination of uncertain condemnation authority and governmental relationships justified the judge’s discretion. (28–30.)

The retained jurisdiction supplied a practical limit. The parties were expected to seek a Louisiana declaratory judgment promptly and obtain state supreme-court review. If no declaratory proceeding was promptly begun, or no authoritative answer arrived within a reasonable time, the district court retained complete control and could itself decide the statutory question. If the taking ultimately was sustained, the district court would determine compensation. The Court thus approved postponement for the best informed decision, rather than permanently sending the parties away or guaranteeing an indefinite stay. (29–31.)

Stewart’s short concurrence emphasized the particular circumstances: conscientious deferral for clarification of a controlling statute of highly doubtful meaning lay within allowable discretion. He distinguished the same-day Mashuda decision, which rejected dismissal and involved clear state law with factual questions remaining. His position did not treat every eminent-domain case as a reason to defer adjudication. (31.)

## Brennan’s dissent: jurisdictional duty and concrete delay

Brennan viewed abstention as an extraordinary, narrow exception to Congress’s direction that federal courts decide properly invoked diversity cases. Traditionally, the reasons were avoiding an unnecessary serious constitutional ruling or avoiding interference with a delicate state governmental process. Neither existed here, he argued. The company’s constitutional objections were insubstantial; deciding ordinary state law did not disrupt a regulatory apparatus, impede taxes, or enjoin state officials. The city itself sought federal adjudication after removal. Federal resolution would implement state policy rather than frustrate it. (31–36.)

For the dissent, the question was not whether the district judge exercised discretion sensibly, but whether he had discretion to abstain in these circumstances at all. Eminent domain was not more inherently sovereign than other governmental powers routinely considered in federal court. Meredith, in his view, could not be distinguished merely because the present case used a stay: Meredith had required federal adjudication, rather than directing a retained-case referral. Brennan’s fourth footnote expressly challenged the majority’s stay-versus-dismissal distinction. (36–41 & n.4.)

The dissent also criticized the practical consequences. The utility had removed the city’s original state action, then benefited from a federal stay requiring the city to initiate a new state proceeding. The dispute had already been delayed more than two years. The city estimated that another two years could pass before an authoritative state answer, after which compensation litigation would still resume federally. Brennan attributed a strategic benefit from delay to the party retaining possession, while recognizing that the majority itself characterized the judge’s action as conscientious. His constitutional and statutory history, including the full statute in footnote 7, supported the conclusion that judges should not curtail diversity jurisdiction because they considered it undesirable or found state law hard. He would have affirmed the Fifth Circuit. (41–44 & nn.5–7.)

## Judgment and assigned distinction

The Court **reversed the Fifth Circuit and reinstated the district court’s stay**, preserving federal authority to act if prompt state clarification failed. (30–31.) The assignment asks whether a stay differs from dismissal; here that distinction is part of the majority’s justification and the dissent’s express disagreement. Neither the case nor that editorial question supports a blanket rule that uncertain state law permits federal nondecision. This brief addresses the 1959 judgment, without asserting later developments in abstention doctrine. Independent-review status appears in the accompanying register.
