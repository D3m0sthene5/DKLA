# 1600 Walnut Corp. v. Cole Haan Company Store
530 F. Supp. 3d 555 (E.D. Pa. 2021) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Judge J. Curtis Joyner signed the memorandum and accompanying order March 29, 2021; both were filed March 30 as documents 46 and 47 in civil action 20-4223. The court granted the landlord's Rule 12(b)(6) motion and dismissed all six amended counterclaims with prejudice. It did not then award the landlord rent or resolve the entire action: the order set deadlines for summary-judgment motions and responses. This stage applied the agreed lease language to the tenant's pleaded facts, rather than adjudicating facts after trial. (Official memorandum pp. 1, 3–4, 8; order pp. 1–2.)

## Lease terms, closure, and the requested declarations
Cole Haan entered a long-term commercial lease with 1600 Walnut in 2004; in 2014 they extended it through March 31, 2025. A force majeure clause excused delayed performance for strikes, labor trouble, material shortages, power failure, restrictive governmental laws or regulations, riots, insurrection, war, and other reasons not the delayed party's fault or beyond its reasonable control. But the clause expressly preserved the tenant's duty to pay rent, apart from a delayed commencement date, and excluded lack of funds or financing as grounds for relief. The lease also provided automatic termination if the entire premises was taken by eminent domain or conveyed in its place. (Memorandum pp. 1–2.)

Cole Haan permanently vacated in March 2020 and stopped paying rent. The governor's March 23 COVID-19 order prohibited operation of the store. Philadelphia retailers could reopen with restrictions after June 5, but Cole Haan did not reopen or resume payments. The landlord sought rent arrearages, additional rent, late fees, interest, attorneys' fees, and costs. The tenant sought six declarations: discharge or alternatively abatement for frustration of purpose; discharge or alternatively abatement for impossibility or impracticability; discharge for failure of consideration; and contractual termination because the government restrictions allegedly constituted a taking. (Id. pp. 2–3.)

The action was within diversity jurisdiction, and the parties agreed Pennsylvania law applied. Counterclaims received the same pleading review as claims. The court accepted well-pleaded allegations as true, viewed them favorably to Cole Haan, and considered integral documents and admitted facts without converting the motion into a trial of competing factual accounts. The lease's legal effect nevertheless defeated relief. (Id. pp. 3–4.)

## Express risk allocation displaced the proposed common-law relief
Pennsylvania parties could allocate contractual risks broadly. The court would turn to common-law impossibility and frustration to allocate risk only if the contract had not already done so. Here, the force majeure clause specified both excusing events and an exception keeping rent payable. Lack of funds was separately excluded. The relevant issue was therefore whether the COVID-19 interruption fell within that agreed allocation. (Id. p. 5.)

The governor's restrictive retail orders were the most obvious proximate cause of nonperformance and were expressly covered as restrictive governmental laws or regulations. Cole Haan instead argued that the pandemic itself lay outside the clause: the listed events supposedly were manmade and short-lived, while COVID-19 was naturally occurring. The court rejected that reading. It regarded the pandemic as belonging with the listed life-altering national events such as war, riots, and insurrection and thus within the broad catchall. The clause was unambiguous and construed as a matter of law. Coverage did not free the tenant from rent; it triggered the allocation expressly preserving that obligation. (Id. pp. 6–7.)

Because the contract allocated this contingency, frustration, impossibility or impracticability, and failure of consideration did not supply discharge or abatement contrary to the agreed terms. This reasoning did not determine that government closures never frustrate commercial leases. It enforced this lease's rent exception and catchall on the pleaded facts. (Id. pp. 5–7.)

## No termination under the eminent-domain provision
The tenant's sixth counterclaim characterized the restrictions as a regulatory taking or inverse condemnation, bringing them within automatic contractual termination for eminent domain. The court relied on the Pennsylvania Supreme Court's treatment of the governor's orders as a valid exercise of police power rather than eminent-domain power. It therefore rejected the asserted taking and the resulting contractual termination claim. The order was a decision about the tenant's counterclaim, not a compensation judgment against the government. (Id. pp. 7–8.)

## Exact order and assigned scope
The separate two-page order dismissed every amended counterclaim with prejudice, granted leave to file the landlord's sur-reply, and required summary-judgment motions within thirty days, with oppositions within fourteen days after service. These further deadlines confirm that dismissal of the counterclaims was not a money judgment deciding all remaining claims. (Order p. 2.)
