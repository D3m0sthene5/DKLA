# Riggs v. Palmer

115 N.Y. 506, 22 N.E. 188 (N.Y. 1889)

## Court, opinion, and assigned scope
The New York Court of Appeals decided the case on October 8, 1889. Judge Earl wrote for the court. Judge Gray dissented, with Judge Danforth concurring in his dissent. The assigned four-page Class 3 reading abridges the majority and dissent. The full judicial report supplies the precise form of the judgment, the interests of other family members, and the dissent's fuller account of statutory revocation rules.

The case concerns a beneficiary who murdered the testator to secure an inheritance. The central disagreement is how a court should read apparently comprehensive statutes governing wills and descent when their literal operation would allow the killer to obtain the property. The majority relies on legislative purpose and background common-law principles; the dissent relies on the enacted rules and the limits of judicial power. Both positions need to be distinguished from the separate criminal prosecution. 115 N.Y. at 508–20.

## Parties and material facts
Francis B. Palmer executed a will in 1880. He left small legacies to his two daughters, Mrs. Riggs and Mrs. Preston, and the residue of his real and personal property to his grandson, Elmer E. Palmer. The gift to Elmer was subject to support for Elmer's mother, Susan. The will also made a conditional gift over to the daughters if Elmer survived Francis but died under age, unmarried, and without issue. These provisions matter because the controversy concerns particular interests under a validly executed instrument, rather than a general competition among unidentified heirs. Id. at 508.

Francis later married Mrs. Bresee. Their antenuptial agreement entitled her to support on his farm during her life in place of dower and other claims against his estate, with that support charged upon the farm. The daughters' ultimate recovery therefore could not properly be described as an unencumbered transfer of everything Francis owned. The court's decree preserved the relevant support obligations. Id. at 508, 514–15.

Elmer lived with his grandfather and knew the contents of the will. At age sixteen, he poisoned Francis in 1882. The findings established that he acted to prevent Francis from changing the will and to obtain immediate enjoyment of the property. The court thus dealt with an intentional killing causally connected to the desired inheritance. It did not decide the consequences of every form of homicide or every beneficiary's unrelated misconduct. Id. at 508–09.

Elmer was convicted of second-degree murder and was confined in a reformatory when the civil action was brought. The civil court did not have to determine criminal guilt anew or infer his motive from a mere accusation. The relevant facts had been found by the referee and were accepted in the appeal. Id. at 514–15.

## Procedural history
Francis's daughters brought the civil action against Elmer and the administrator, seeking to prevent him from taking the estate through his crime. The case was tried before a referee. The resulting judgment dismissed the complaint, and the General Term affirmed. The General Term of the Supreme Court in the Third Judicial Department acted on an order made February 5, 1887. The daughters appealed to the Court of Appeals. The issue was whether the law permitted Elmer to take despite the facts established below. Id. at 506, 508–09, 514–15.

The Court of Appeals reversed the judgments below. Because the findings already supplied the facts necessary for relief, it directed entry of a new judgment without ordering a new trial. That direction is more specific than a generic statement that the court “invalidated the will.” Id. at 514–15.

## Issues
Did the statutes governing wills and descent require the court to recognize Elmer's inheritance because the will was validly executed and had not been revoked in a manner specified by statute? Alternatively, could the statutes be construed against a background legal principle that a person may not obtain property by his own crime, even without an express statutory slayer provision?

The case also raised a remedial question: would excluding Elmer impose an additional punishment or divest property already belonging to him, or would it prevent his wrongful acquisition of property in the first place? Id. at 509–15.

## Rule and holding
A beneficiary who intentionally murders the testator to prevent revocation and obtain the estate may not take the property under the will or through intestate succession. The majority acknowledged that a literal application of the statutory language favored Elmer, but construed that language in light of its purpose and fundamental common-law maxims. The legislature's general rules protecting testamentary disposition were not understood to authorize acquisition of the estate through the beneficiary's murder of the testator. Id. at 509–14.

This is the majority's construction of the law applicable to these facts. It is not a holding that a court may disregard any statute whenever its application appears morally unattractive. The opinion attempts to identify a sufficiently established legal principle and a sufficiently clear conflict with the purpose of the statutory scheme to justify the result. Gray disputed both the method and its compatibility with the legislative rules. Id. at 515–20.

## Actual judgment
The court reversed the General Term's judgment and the judgment entered on the referee's report, without a new trial. It directed that Elmer and the administrator be restrained from using estate property for Elmer's benefit; that the devise and bequest to him be declared ineffective to pass title; and that Elmer be declared to have no interest in the estate. The daughters were to be declared its true owners, subject to the charges for Susan and for the widow under the antenuptial agreement. Costs in all courts were imposed on Elmer. Id. at 514–15.

The surviving charges are part of the judgment. The court did not simply cancel every provision of Francis's will or treat the other beneficiaries' interests as nonexistent. Nor did it order additional imprisonment or amend the criminal sentence.

## Majority reasoning
### Literal language and the purpose of testamentary statutes
Earl began by conceding the difficulty for the daughters: if the statutes were applied literally and without qualification, the property would pass to Elmer. He then asked what the statutes were designed to accomplish. Their object was to allow owners to dispose of property at death and carry out their lawful final wishes, not to create an incentive for a beneficiary to kill the owner before those wishes could change. Id. at 509–10.

The majority used a counterfactual inquiry into legislative intention. Had lawmakers expressly considered the case of a beneficiary who murdered the testator to prevent revocation, Earl reasoned, they would not have intended the beneficiary to take. He illustrated the method with familiar examples in which broad statutory language is confined because literal application would defeat the enactment's reason. Those illustrations are interpretive analogies; they are not additional holdings about the facts of Palmer's estate. Id. at 509–11.

This reasoning treats statutory purpose as capable of limiting general words. It does not depend on finding ambiguity in the identity of Elmer or the formal validity of the instrument. The difficulty arises precisely because the ordinary formal rules, considered in isolation, appear to favor him.

### Background maxims as part of the legal system
The majority also offered a common-law basis for the result. Legal rules operate against principles forbidding a person to profit from fraud, acquire property by his own wrong, or found a claim on his own crime. Earl regarded those principles as governing the construction and operation of statutes unless the legislature displaced them. Id. at 511–12.

On that understanding, an express slayer exception was unnecessary. The inheritance statutes did not expressly abrogate the background maxim, and the court should not treat their silence as affirmative authorization of a criminally procured benefit. This is analytically different from inventing a special rule because the individual litigant is unsympathetic: the majority describes an existing general legal principle that applies to the transaction. Id.

### Analogies to fraud and insurance
Earl relied on decisions refusing to enforce rights procured through wrongful conduct, including the refusal to allow recovery on life insurance after the claimant feloniously caused the insured's death. A will obtained through fraud or undue influence likewise would not receive ordinary effect. In his view, the fact that Elmer's instrument was originally valid did not require the legal system to assist his subsequent criminal effort to make it immediately operative. Id. at 512–13.

These analogies do work only if the common-law principle travels across doctrinal settings. Gray's dissent directly challenged that move: a testamentary disposition is not a contract whose enforcement depends on reciprocal obligations, and the legislature had prescribed formal rules for altering its operation.

### The court's account of legislative silence
The majority denied that it was merely filling an omitted case with a rule of its own. It reasoned that the legislature could have assumed established maxims would prevent the murderer from benefiting, and referred to the civil law's treatment of similar misconduct as confirmation that the result was not novel. Id. at 513.

The distinction is important in explaining the opinion. Earl did not say that every statutory omission grants a court a free hand. He argued that the relevant prohibition already existed as part of the law with which the statutes should be read. Whether that account is convincing is the methodological controversy the case presents.

### Acquisition, forfeiture, and punishment
Earl answered the objection that the civil result added a penalty to the criminal law. Before the killing, Elmer had no vested ownership of the grandfather's estate. Refusing to let him acquire it by murder therefore did not confiscate existing property; it prevented acquisition through the crime. Id. at 513–14.

The court also refused to allow Elmer to obtain the same benefit as an heir rather than a devisee. Changing the doctrinal route to the property would not eliminate the connection between the crime and its fruits. The judgment accordingly excluded his interest in the estate, rather than merely rejecting one wording of his claim. Id.

## Gray's dissent, joined by Danforth
### A comprehensive statutory scheme must control
Gray accepted the moral repugnance of the killing. His disagreement concerned legal authority. The legislature had specified how wills were made, altered, and revoked, and the will satisfied those requirements. Murder by a beneficiary was not one of the prescribed modes of revocation. Courts could not add a new one because they believed the legislature ought to have done so. Id. at 515–18.

The dissent thus located fidelity to legislative intention in the enacted scheme, rather than in a hypothetical answer lawmakers might give to a morally extreme case. A court that substitutes its own judgment about a desirable exception risks becoming the lawmaker in an area already comprehensively regulated.

### Intended revocation is not legally effective revocation
Gray stressed that a testator's intention to revoke does not itself revoke a will. The statutory act must occur. He discussed decisions in which even interference with an intended alteration or destruction did not permit courts to treat the formal revocation requirements as satisfied. Those examples supported his view that the law deliberately values an ascertainable rule over reconstruction of what a deceased person would probably have done. Id. at 518–19.

On that reasoning, the finding that Elmer killed Francis to prevent a change was not enough. It showed why Elmer acted, but it did not establish a legally completed revocation or identify with certainty the replacement disposition Francis would have chosen.

### Contract analogies and the danger of a judicially written will
Gray rejected reliance on contract and insurance cases as a sufficient basis for changing a testamentary gift. A will does not rest on a promise by the beneficiary to perform some obligation. Its effect follows from the testator's valid act and the governing statutes. The majority's appeal to general maxims therefore could not bypass the specific statutory conditions. Id. at 519–20.

He also questioned the destination of the property. Once a court departed from the will, it would have to decide who should take instead, thereby risking a judicially created disposition that the testator never made. For Gray, that consequence reinforced the need for legislation rather than case-specific adjustment.

### Civil deprivation and the criminal penalty
The dissent viewed deprivation of the testamentary benefit as an additional consequence not provided by the criminal or probate statutes. However justified such a rule might be as policy, the legislature should specify it. Gray would have affirmed the judgment below with costs. Id. at 519–20.

## Study synthesis and limits
The decision places two accounts of legal fidelity in direct conflict. Earl treats purpose and common-law maxims as part of the statutory scheme itself. Gray treats the scheme's formal completeness and specified modes of revocation as limits on judicial supplementation. A useful comparison must explain those rival accounts; “the majority used fairness and the dissent ignored it” is inadequate.

Keep the procedural and property details attached to that debate. The court acted on established findings, entered a directed civil judgment without a new trial, preserved the support charges, and denied acquisition rather than modifying the criminal sentence. Those details explain both what the majority actually did and why the dissent regarded it as an exercise of power belonging to the legislature.

This brief describes the 1889 decision. It does not present the opinion as a complete statement of contemporary statutory slayer law or resolve later questions about accidental homicide, insanity, joint ownership, or constructive trusts that were not decided here.


## Additional original-opinion details

The appeal was submitted on June 21, 1889 and decided it on October 8. The original report identifies Philo Riggs as guardian ad litem in the formal caption; Earl identifies Mrs. Riggs and Mrs. Preston as the testator’s daughters and plaintiffs. Those descriptions belong to different parts of the report and should not be collapsed into a claim that Philo himself was a daughter. The reporter’s headnote confirms that Danforth and Gray dissented; Danforth’s concurrence at the end is concurrence with Gray’s dissent. 115 N.Y. at 506, 508, 520.

Earl’s interpretive illustrations include a manor judge who cannot decide his own dispute despite broadly conferred jurisdiction, a barber whose bloodletting escapes a street-bloodshed prohibition, and necessary or charitable activity permitted on the Sabbath. These illustrate interpretation by statutory design. His separate common-law argument invokes Armstrong’s insurance recovery prohibition and wills affected by fraud, undue influence or public policy. He also expressly rejects Owens v. Owens, which permitted dower to a wife convicted as an accessory before the fact to her husband’s murder. This discussion supports his rationale; the present judgment concerns Palmer’s estate and does not adjudicate a separate widow’s dower claim. Id. at 509–14.

Gray’s intended-revocation discussion identifies Dan v. Brown’s requirement of an outward act, Gains v. Gains’s forcibly retained will, Leaycraft v. Simmons’s withheld instrument and proposed codicil, and Clingan v. Mitcheltree’s fraudulent prevention of destruction. In each analogy, intention or interference does not substitute for the statutory act. These are authorities discussed in the dissent, rather than additional holdings of the majority. Gray’s Roman-law comparison describes escheat as punishment and grounds a possible slayer rule in legislation. Id. at 517–20.
