# Shubin v. William Lyon Homes, Inc.
101 Cal. Rptr. 2d 390; formerly 84 Cal. App. 4th 1041 (2000) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and disposition
The California Court of Appeal, First District, Division Four, decided this appeal on November 14, 2000. Justice Sepulveda wrote; Presiding Justice Hanlon and Justice Reardon concurred without separate writings. The court reversed the order denying the employer’s motion to compel arbitration and directed the trial court to sever the words “or administrative” from the final sentence of the arbitration clause and enforce the remainder. Procedural unconscionability was established, but only a removable part of one provision was substantively unlawful. The original reported text was subsequently omitted from pages 1041–1063 on direction of the California Supreme Court by order dated March 14, 2001. This brief reconstructs the assigned historical decision and does not present the removed opinion as currently published precedent. (Opinion introduction and part III; reporter omission notice.)

## Employment, allegations, and signing circumstances
William Lyon Homes hired Kristy Shubin as a receptionist in April 1998 and promoted her to escrow coordinator in December. In January 1999, she informed Doug Bauer, the president of the employer’s Northern California region, that she was pregnant. She alleged that Bauer and marketing/sales coordinator Connie Newbury then reacted negatively and hostilely, that she was demoted to receptionist, and that Bauer refused accommodations for physician-imposed pregnancy work restrictions in March. After obtaining a Department of Fair Employment and Housing right-to-sue letter, she filed a sex- and pregnancy-discrimination and harassment complaint in July 1999. The opinion’s footnote 1 identifies the complaint and motion documents as the factual sources; the court did not determine whether the alleged discrimination occurred. (Part I.A & n. 1.)

The employer relied on a two-page employee agreement containing the arbitration clause. Shubin declared that office manager Kathy Sampson brought several documents to her desk late in the day, said they needed to be signed for headquarters, stood by, and indicated where to sign or initial. When asked what the papers were, Sampson described them as company records showing Shubin’s hiring and promised to explain them later. Shubin said she had no time to read everything, felt she had no choice, received no copies, and was never told she was agreeing to arbitration. Sampson recalled giving her the agreement to read and sign, said Shubin never requested additional reading time, and did not recall promising a later explanation. (Part I.C.)

Footnote 3 preserves the disputed signing date: Sampson said April 14, the first workday; Shubin thought approximately two weeks later. Her signature was undated, and William Lyon signed for the employer on May 18. The court did not resolve that discrepancy. In May 1999, after Shubin filed her administrative claim, a different office manager asked her to sign another agreement. Shubin requested permission to take it home, read it, consult counsel, or receive a copy; those requests were refused. The appellate court treated that later incident as corroborating an inference about the employer’s document-signing practices, not as a replacement for proof concerning the first agreement. (Parts I.C, II.E.2 & n. 3.)

## Contract, motion, and review
The clause required arbitration before an employment-law arbitrator selected under JAMS/Endispute rules for disputes concerning employment or the agreement, expressly including discrimination and harassment. It excepted workers’ compensation, unemployment, taking or use of confidential information, and claims under the confidentiality provision. That provision required confidentiality and use of information exclusively for the company, both during and after employment. The final sentence allowed the responding party to recover its costs, expenses, and attorney fees if the other party pursued a legal or administrative action concerning a matter within the arbitration clause. (Part I.B & n. 2.)

The employer first supplied a copy to Shubin’s lawyer with an August 1999 letter proposing arbitration if discussions failed. Counsel objected to procedural and substantive unconscionability. The employer’s September motion argued that the clause covered discrimination, preserved ordinary remedies, did not shorten limitations or expressly limit discovery, and contained exceptions for both sides. Shubin challenged the signing process, confidentiality carveout, incorporated deposition restrictions, arbitration costs, and penalties for using other forums. (Parts I.C–D.)

On October 8, 1999, the trial court denied arbitration, finding unequal court access, discovery limits especially harmful to the employee, and the possibility that she would owe the employer’s costs and fees even after prevailing in court or an administrative forum. Armendariz had not yet been decided. Footnote 4 records that the trial court also cited Maciejewski, apparently without knowing that the Supreme Court had granted review two days earlier and deferred that case pending Armendariz. On appeal, legal enforceability was reviewed de novo, while declaration-based factual findings received substantial-evidence review. A refusal to sever because unconscionability permeated the agreement was subject to abuse-of-discretion review. (Parts I.D, II.A & n. 4.)

## Procedural unconscionability and statutory safeguards
The agreement was adhesive despite its brevity: a standardized employment condition with no realistic negotiating opportunity. The employer’s reliance on a signer’s obligation to read was insufficient. Rosenthal concerned fraud in execution and lacked evidence of actual conduct pressuring the signer; here Sampson’s words and conduct supplied such evidence. The trial court could credit Shubin’s declaration and infer a hurried process with no meaningful opportunity to read or propose modifications. The court therefore upheld procedural unconscionability without deciding that every unread employment agreement was invalid. (Part II.E.1–2.)

Applying Armendariz, the appellate court distinguished defects that interpretation could address from those requiring severance. Neutrality was unchallenged, and no unlawful limitation of statutory remedies was claimed. Agreement to arbitrate FEHA rights implied consent to discovery adequate to vindicate them, overcoming the asserted default deposition restriction. Incorporation of JAMS rules was not express contrary language foreclosing that inference. A written decision revealing essential findings and conclusions was likewise required by construction, while the precise review standard was premature. The employer also had to bear costs unique to mandatory arbitration. Incorporation of the JAMS default cost rules was not treated as a specific allocation defeating that obligation. Those features did not supply grounds to reject arbitration. (Part II.D.1–5, II.E.3.a & nn. 5–7.)

## Mutuality, administrative access, and severance
The court rejected the trial court’s unequal-access analysis. Unlike the employee-only clause in Armendariz, this confidentiality exception did not expressly distinguish employer and employee. Although confidentiality disputes would typically concern the employer, the employee could also go to court for declaratory relief over whether disclosure violated her obligations. Workers’ compensation and unemployment exceptions involved statutory administrative processes rather than an employer concession of litigation rights. The court found sufficient mutuality in this particular wording. (Part II.E.3.b.)

The costs-and-fees penalty required closer analysis. For a court action on a contract, the appellate court reasoned that Civil Code section 1717 required bilateral recovery by the prevailing party, curing the proposed reading that a successful employee must pay the losing employer. But that statute concerned court actions and did not appear to reach administrative proceedings. An employee legitimately pursuing a discrimination complaint before the state enforcement agency could still face the contractual penalty. Armendariz expressly preserved access to that agency. The penalty would chill that access and was substantively unconscionable to that extent. The court did not invalidate every contractual cost provision or hold that any voluntary departure from arbitration was protected. (Part II.E.3.c & n. 8.)

Only the administrative component of the final sentence was unlawful. Deleting “or administrative” could remove it without adding obligations or undermining the agreement’s central purpose. That differed from Armendariz’s multiple defects and nonmutual scope, which could not be cured without rewriting. The court therefore reversed and ordered that deletion and enforcement of the remaining agreement. It did not affirm the complete denial of arbitration, adjudicate the discrimination merits, or find procedural unconscionability sufficient by itself. (Parts II.F, III.)
