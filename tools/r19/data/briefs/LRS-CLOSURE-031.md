# City of Arlington v. FCC
569 U.S. 290 (2013) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and procedural posture

On May 20, 2013, the Supreme Court affirmed the Fifth Circuit and held that the then-governing *Chevron* framework applied to an agency’s interpretation of a statutory ambiguity concerning the scope of its authority. Scalia wrote for Thomas, Ginsburg, Sotomayor, and Kagan. Breyer concurred in part and in the judgment, supplying a more contextual explanation of delegated interpretive authority. Roberts dissented, joined by Kennedy and Alito, and would have vacated and remanded for an independent judicial determination of that authority. The judgment was therefore 6–3, with five Justices joining Scalia’s opinion. (290, 292, 307–308, 312, 328.)

## Statutory setting and regulatory facts

Wireless service requires towers and antennas whose placement is ordinarily subject to local zoning. The Telecommunications Act of 1996 imposed federal limitations on state and local siting decisions. Section 332(c)(7)(B)(ii) required action on an application within a reasonable period after filing. A nearby saving clause preserved state and local authority except for the limitations in that paragraph, while § 332(c)(7)(B)(v) authorized an affected person to challenge inconsistent action or inaction in court. Separately, § 201(b) gave the FCC general authority to prescribe rules implementing the Communications Act, including its later-added provisions. (293–294.)

In July 2008, CTIA-The Wireless Association asked the FCC to clarify the reasonable-time requirement, citing lengthy delays in local proceedings. In November 2009, the Commission issued a declaratory ruling finding that unreasonable delays obstructed wireless services and competition. It set presumptive, rebuttable periods of 90 days for applications to place antennas on existing towers and 150 days for other applications. These were not categorical deadlines immune from contrary circumstances. (294–295 & n. 1.)

## Litigation and question presented

Arlington and San Antonio challenged the ruling in the Fifth Circuit. They argued that the saving clause and express judicial remedy demonstrated that Congress withheld FCC authority to interpret these siting limitations. The Fifth Circuit applied *Chevron* to the agency-authority question, found the relevant statutory interaction ambiguous, and upheld the FCC’s construction. It separately upheld the timeframes as reasonable statutory interpretations. The Supreme Court granted review limited to whether courts should apply *Chevron* to an agency’s determination of its own jurisdiction; it did not undertake a separate reconsideration of the substantive reasonableness of the deadlines. The companion petition came from the New Orleans City Council’s telecommunications committee. (290 n. *, 295.)

## Majority: no categorical jurisdictional exception

Scalia began with the familiar two-step approach: a court must enforce a clear congressional answer; when the administered statute leaves an ambiguity, it accepts the agency’s permissible construction. He described this as a background rule Congress could use when legislating. A proposed distinction between jurisdictional and nonjurisdictional interpretations would undermine that rule because every agency interpretation concerned the limits of authority Congress supplied. (296–298.)

The majority distinguished agency powers from judicial jurisdiction. Congress can define which cases courts may hear, while an erroneous decision within that jurisdiction remains a judicial judgment. For an agency, Congress prescribes both whether it can act and how it must act. An unauthorized application of power and an assertion of power not granted both exceed statutory authority. Relabeling one interpretive problem jurisdictional did not justify a different standard. (297–298.)

Scalia illustrated the point with hypothetical common-carrier statutes: one directly gave an agency jurisdiction over unreasonable conditions; another prohibited those conditions and separately authorized implementing rules. Both required interpretation of the same operative concepts, and their equivalent substance should not produce different deference merely because the grants were framed differently. Prior decisions likewise applied *Chevron* to statutory coverage, counterclaim authority, wetlands permitting, preemption, and express restrictions on agency powers. Even decisions rejecting expansive assertions, such as *Brown & Williamson* and *MCI*, had used the framework to enforce statutory limits. The framework did not guarantee the agency victory. (298–304 & nn. 2–3.)

The majority rejected a separate federalism objection. Congress had itself imposed a federal reasonable-time requirement upon local authorities. The interpretive question was whether federal judges or the FCC would elaborate that requirement, not whether local authorities could disregard it altogether. (305.)

## Delegation prerequisite and application

Scalia agreed that *Mead* required congressional authorization for an agency to determine the matter in the manner used. The disagreement concerned what established that authorization here. Congress had unambiguously given the FCC general authority to administer the Communications Act through rulemaking and adjudication, and this interpretation was issued in exercising that authority. The majority rejected a further provision-by-provision inquiry requiring more specific evidence of delegation for each ambiguity within the agency’s substantive field. It found the statutory prerequisites satisfied and affirmed. (305–307.)

This did not authorize an agency to disregard a clear statutory boundary or resolve an ambiguity beyond its permissible range. Scalia stressed rigorous enforcement of both limits. His separate discussion of governmental structure characterized agency rulemaking and adjudication, despite their legislative and judicial forms, as exercises of executive power under the Constitution. That characterization responded to Roberts’s institutional concerns; it did not decide an appointments or removal dispute. (304–305 n. 4, 307.)

## Breyer’s concurrence

Breyer agreed that jurisdictional labels did not supply a useful distinction. He nevertheless emphasized an independent judicial inquiry into whether Congress meant the agency’s interpretations to carry the force of law. Ambiguity could indicate delegation without invariably establishing it. Relevant considerations included text, structure, history, the interstitial character of the question, agency expertise, complexity, and the question’s relationship to ordinary agency responsibilities. An interpretation lacking delegated controlling authority might still persuade under *Skidmore*. (308–310.)

Here, the FCC’s broad authority, the open-ended reasonable-time language, the technical and administrative character of the question, and useful agency expertise favored delegation. The saving clause expressly excepted the relevant paragraph, and the judicial-review clause provided a remedy without negating agency interpretation. Both fit a scheme giving local authorities, the Commission, and courts distinct roles. Breyer would uphold the FCC’s authority on those contextual grounds and joined the judgment and compatible portions of the majority opinion. (310–312.)

## Roberts’s dissent

Roberts disputed the premise that his approach required sorting important jurisdictional questions from ordinary regulatory details. The antecedent question was whether Congress delegated controlling interpretive authority over this ambiguity. A court should answer that independently before deferring to the agency’s resulting interpretation. He grounded that responsibility in the judiciary’s duty to decide law, the APA, and *Chevron*, *Adams Fruit*, *Mead*, and *Gonzales*. (312, 316–322.)

A general grant would often establish delegation, but not inevitably for every provision. Statutes could distribute responsibilities among agencies or exempt particular matters. Courts had to enforce those allocations rather than let an agency bootstrap itself into authority. Roberts maintained that ordinary statutory construction could identify delegation without a new arbitrary test. His concern extended to preserving the boundary between Congress and the executive amid the practical accumulation of regulatory powers in administrative bodies. (312–315, 322–327.)

He did not conclude that the FCC lacked interpretive authority. He concluded that the Fifth Circuit erred by deferring on that preliminary question. He would vacate its decision and remand for the court to decide delegation independently in the first instance. (328.)
