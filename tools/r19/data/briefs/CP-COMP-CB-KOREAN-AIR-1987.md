# In re Korean Air Lines Disaster of September 1, 1983

**829 F.2d 1171 (D.C. Cir. 1987) — September 25, 1987 — Civil Procedure**

Judge Ruth Bader Ginsburg wrote for the panel of Judges Ruth Bader Ginsburg, Stephen F. Williams, and Douglas H. Ginsburg. Judge Douglas H. Ginsburg also concurred, joined by Judge Williams. All three joined the Court’s opinion and judgment. Assigned: CivPro PDF physical page 231, printed pages 418–419. The complete appellate report, its adopted July 25, 1985 district opinion, 664 F. Supp. 1463, and May 7, 1987 clarification, 664 F. Supp. 1488, supply the following historical account.

## Facts, competing claims, and interlocutory posture

A Soviet aircraft shot down Korean Air Lines Flight 007 on September 1, 1983. Wrongful-death actions against Korean Air Lines were consolidated for pretrial proceedings in the District of Columbia under 28 U.S.C. § 1407. The tickets contained the notice of limited liability in eight-point type, although the Montreal Agreement specified ten-point type. Plaintiffs sought partial summary judgment denying the carrier the $75,000 Warsaw/Montreal liability limitation. The Second Circuit had accepted an argument that noncompliance with the ten-point requirement deprived a carrier of the limitation, while the District of Columbia court independently rejected that interpretation. Whether an MDL court had to apply the federal-law interpretation of the circuit from which an action originated therefore mattered directly to plaintiffs’ potential recovery. (829 F.2d at 1172–1173.)

Chief Judge Aubrey E. Robinson Jr. denied the plaintiffs’ motion on July 25, 1985. He also rejected their request for immediate payment of $75,000: that figure was a maximum, not a guaranteed minimum, and individual damages remained disputed. After certification under § 1292(b), the D.C. Circuit allowed an interlocutory appeal. Because the scope of the district court’s ruling was initially unclear, the appellate court remanded for clarification on January 30, 1987. Robinson’s May 7 memorandum explained that his interpretation applied to all the categories of actions, including those originating in New York, rather than exempting the New York cases in deference to Second Circuit law. (829 F.2d at 1172–1173; 664 F. Supp. at 1477–1478, 1488–1490.)

## The adopted decision on the liability limitation

The appellate court expressly adopted the district court’s underlying opinion, so the merits were not left undecided. That opinion distinguished the Warsaw Convention’s rules from the later Montreal Agreement. Warsaw originally limited recovery to approximately $8,300, subject to its own defenses and exceptions. Montreal was an intercarrier special agreement raising the limit to $75,000 and expressly waiving Article 20 defenses; it was not itself a negotiated treaty amendment. It required ten-point notice but did not state that violating that requirement forfeited the liability limit. Plaintiffs invoked ticket nondelivery precedent and argued that an inadequate eight-point notice should count as no delivery. Alternatively, they disputed treaty coverage because Korea had adhered to the Hague Protocol. Robinson relied on Article XXIII’s adherence provision and concluded that the United States and Korea shared treaty obligations at least as to the Convention’s unamended portions. (664 F. Supp. at 1464–1469.)

For the district court, the decisive textual distinction was between failure to deliver a ticket and an irregularity in a delivered ticket. The plaintiffs had received tickets; the treaty did not equate every defect with nondelivery. Cases involving tickets delivered too late to permit a passenger to arrange insurance did not establish that this particular typographical failure triggered the same consequence. The Montreal Agreement’s express waiver of Article 20 defenses also showed that the parties could agree to a waiver, but no express waiver of the damages limitation appeared here. Reading an additional forfeiture into the agreement would alter the negotiated exchange of increased limits and reduced defenses. (1467, 1472–1476.)

Robinson acknowledged substantial criticism of the old aviation-industry protections and the appeal of full compensation. His answer was institutional: dissatisfaction did not authorize judges to rewrite a treaty still maintained by the political branches. The government could enforce domestic operating requirements, including the type-size rule, without courts creating an unstated treaty sanction. He declined to follow the Second Circuit’s ten-point bright line and held that Korean Air Lines could invoke the $75,000 limit. That did not award $75,000 to anyone. Plaintiffs still had to prove individual damages, and material disputes concerning their amounts precluded immediate payment. The order denied partial summary judgment, recognized the carrier’s entitlement to the limitation, and denied the immediate-payment request. (1470–1478.)

## The appellate issue: whose interpretation of federal law governs?

Plaintiffs urged that Van Dusen v. Barrack’s preservation of transferor law should carry Second Circuit precedent into an MDL proceeding. The Court rejected the extension. Van Dusen concerned defendant-initiated transfer of diversity litigation under § 1404(a) and preserved the transferor state’s law, including its choice-of-law rules. Its Erie-based purpose was to prevent a federal transfer from producing a result unavailable in the state courts where the action was filed. That justification did not confer a comparable entitlement to a particular circuit’s interpretation of one body of federal law. The Court did not decide the then-unresolved plaintiff-initiated transfer question, noted in footnote 4. (829 F.2d at 1173–1175 & nn.4–7.)

A federal venue rule selects a convenient location; it does not create a substantive right to a favored federal interpretation. Each federal court must apply federal law according to its own reasoned judgment, subject to controlling appellate review. Other circuits’ decisions deserve careful consideration but are not binding merely because an action began there. The Court distinguished legitimate variation caused by different state laws from divergent federal rules applied inside one consolidated proceeding. Automatic adherence to every transferor circuit would institutionalize the very inconsistency centralization sought to reduce. The Court preferred independently reasoned, informed uniformity, with the Supreme Court able to resolve a genuine federal conflict, to uniformity produced by mechanical repetition. (1174–1176.)

The resulting rule was precise: an MDL transferee court should consider the transferor circuit’s federal precedents but is not bound by them under stare decisis. That holding did not displace transferor state law in diversity cases. Nor did the Court purport to decide conclusively how every transferor court must treat an MDL ruling after return. It thought law-of-the-case principles should bind later proceedings, while expressly acknowledging that it was not positioned to have the last word on that future problem. It affirmed the determination that Korean Air Lines could invoke the Warsaw limitation as raised by Montreal. (1176.)

## Douglas H. Ginsburg’s concurrence

The concurrence joined the Court’s opinion while examining the practical risks more fully. It agreed that Van Dusen’s state-law rationale did not govern the federal-law question and found no congressional instruction in § 1407 that the transferee judge should simulate the transferor judge’s rulings. Temporary transfer gives the receiving court real authority over the transferred proceedings; the statute’s specific deposition provision did not impose an unexpressed general rule requiring origin-circuit law. The concurrence also distinguished a genuinely preclusive prior determination from a circuit precedent that had never decided the transferred parties’ own claims. (1177–1179 & nn.2–7.)

The difficult possibility was a case returning to the Second Circuit after an MDL ruling contrary to that circuit’s precedents. The concurrence did not pretend that a controlling decision had already settled the collision between circuit precedent and law of the case. It identified safeguards instead: many MDL cases do not return for trial; many pretrial rulings are not outcome determinative; interlocutory appeal and law-of-the-case principles can reduce repeated litigation. Its suggestion that a dissatisfied party might have to seek interlocutory review was a proposed solution, not a newly announced procedural requirement. (1179–1181 & nn.15–17.)

Applying each origin circuit’s interpretation from the outset was not costless. It would guarantee divergent treatment during coordinated discovery and settlement, require repeated analysis of many circuits’ authorities, and sometimes require predictions where an origin circuit had not spoken. Those burdens were unlike the justified effort to ascertain different states’ laws under Erie. They undermined the efficiencies Congress sought through MDL coordination. (1181–1184.)

Finally, the concurrence considered possible legislation: requiring origin-circuit law throughout; giving an MDL ruling effect when reviewed elsewhere; or transferring the whole case for trial and appeal as well as pretrial proceedings. It preferred the second approach over the first and identified practical costs of the third. These were legislative alternatives and recommendations, not features already enacted by § 1407. Its discussion of then-existing trial-transfer practices likewise describes the historical setting, not present authority. (1184–1186.)

## Assigned significance and source limits

The assigned account captures the contrast between transferor state law and independent interpretation of federal law, then uses the concurrence to question the structure of MDL coordination. Its later Lexecon questions are editorial discussion, not part of the 1987 judgment.
