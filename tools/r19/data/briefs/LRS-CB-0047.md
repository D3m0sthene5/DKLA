States. Argued November 30 and December 1–2, 1891; decided February 29, 1892. Justice Harlan delivered
the Court's opinion. Justice Lamar, joined by Chief Justice Fuller, disagreed with the delegation holding but
concurred in the judgments. Assigned excerpt: Legislation and the Regulatory State casebook pp. 47–52,

Parties
Marshall Field & Co. challenged customs duties paid to John M. Clark, collector at Chicago, on woolen goods
and silk embroideries. The cases considered together also involved Boyd, Sutton & Co.'s duties on silk and
cotton laces and Herrman, Sternbach & Co.'s duties on colored cotton cloths. Those proceedings included the
United States and, in the Boyd matter, the New York collector. The importers sought relief from assessments
made under the Tariff Act of October 1, 1890. Their strategy was to show that defects elsewhere in the act
invalidated the statute under which their goods were taxed. 143 U.S. at 650–651, 662–667.
Material Facts
The 1890 act imposed the duties in question but also contained several provisions the importers attacked. Its
reciprocity section generally allowed specified sugar, molasses, coffee, tea, and hides to enter free of duty.
Beginning January 1, 1892, the President was to suspend that treatment as to a producing country if he found
that country's exactions on American products reciprocally unequal and unreasonable. During suspension, the
statute itself prescribed the duties to be collected on the listed goods. The President could determine how long
suspension should last. Id. at 680–681, 692–693.
The act also authorized bounties from the Treasury for specified domestic sugar production. The importers
argued that Congress lacked authority to make those payments and that the bounty provisions were so
connected with the tariff system that invalidating them would invalidate their own duties. The Court did not
decide whether Congress possessed the disputed bounty power. Id. at 694–697.
A different objection concerned enactment. The enrolled act, signed by the presiding officers of both houses,
approved by President Harrison, and deposited with the Secretary of State, lacked a tobacco-rebate provision
the importers asserted had passed both chambers. They proposed to establish the discrepancy through
legislative journals, committee materials, and other congressional documents. This was an allegation about the
authenticated act's conformity to the passed bill, not a finding by the Supreme Court that the alleged omission
actually occurred. Id. at 668–670.
Procedural History
The importers protested their assessments to the Board of General Appraisers, which sustained the collectors.
The respective federal circuit courts affirmed. The Supreme Court considered the resulting cases together and
separately addressed the enrollment objection, the reciprocity delegation, and the sugar-bounty challenge. Id. at
662–667.

Issue
Could legislative journals and related papers be used to impeach the duly authenticated enrolled act by showing
that Congress had passed a provision missing from it? Did the reciprocity section unconstitutionally transfer
legislative or treaty-making power by allowing the President to trigger specified duties after judging foreign
treatment of American products? Finally, would any invalidity of the domestic sugar bounties invalidate the
separate provisions imposing the importers' duties? 143 U.S. at 667–670, 680–682, 694–695.
Rule and Holding
A bill authenticated by the presiding officers of both houses, approved by the President, and deposited in the
public archives was conclusive against the proposed attempt to show through legislative journals and related
materials that it had passed in a different form. This evidentiary conclusion did not prevent courts from
deciding whether the substance of the authenticated statute conformed to the Constitution. The Court reserved
questions concerning matters the Constitution specifically required to be entered in the journals. Id. at
671–672, 679–680.
The reciprocity section did not transfer legislative or treaty-making power. Congress prescribed the
contingency and the duties that would follow. The President ascertained and proclaimed the contingency in
execution of that policy; once he found the specified unequal treatment, suspension was obligatory. His
discretion over duration remained, in the majority's view, discretion in administering the statutory policy. Id. at
692–694.
The Court declined to decide the constitutionality of the sugar bounties because the customs-duty provisions
were legally independent and would remain operative even if the bounties were invalid. A common general
protective objective did not establish that Congress intended these distinct provisions to stand or fall together.
Id. at 695–697.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed each of the judgments sustaining the assessments. The importers obtained no
refund on the constitutional theories presented. Lamar and Fuller agreed with those results despite rejecting the
majority's conclusion that the reciprocity section was valid, because they considered that section severable
from the duties relevant to these importers. Id. at 697, 700.
Reasoning
On enrollment, the Court accepted the premise that officials could not constitutionally enact a bill Congress
had never passed simply by signing and publishing it. The question was what evidence a court should treat as
authoritative in determining passage. The majority therefore addressed proof of enactment rather than
dispensing with the constitutional requirement that both houses pass the bill presented to the President. Id. at
669–670.
The Journal Clause did not make every journal entry conclusive proof of every step in enactment. The
Constitution left substantial discretion over the fullness and manner of recording proceedings, and it did not
require bills and all amendments to be reproduced in full. Official attestation of the enrolled bill communicated
the houses' assurance that the authenticated measure had passed. Presidential approval and archival deposit
completed that authentication. The Court thought respect for the coordinate branches and legal stability
required accepting it against the attempted impeachment. Id. at 670–673.

The opinion also considered the comparative risks of error. Journals assembled during legislative business
could contain omissions or mistakes, and allowing them to defeat authenticated enactments would expose
established laws to repeated collateral challenges. State decisions supported that concern, while contrary cases
often involved distinctive state constitutional requirements. Gardner v. Collector was distinguishable because it
used journals to supply an omitted year of approval, rather than contradict the existence or terms of a duly
authenticated statute. Id. at 673–680.
The holding preserved two limits. Courts remained able to test the authenticated act against substantive
constitutional restrictions, as the Court itself then did. It also expressly left unresolved how legislative validity
might be affected by failure to record matters the Constitution specifically ordered entered in the journals. A
claim that legislative proceedings are wholly immune from judicial inquiry would extend beyond the question
actually decided. Id. at 671–672, 679–680.
On delegation, Aurora supplied a judicial precedent for legislation whose operation depended on a presidential
proclamation of later circumstances. The majority treated revival and suspension as applications of the same
principle: Congress could enact a legal consequence while entrusting identification of the triggering event to
the executive. That precedent supported the characterization of the reciprocity mechanism as conditional
legislation. Id. at 682–683.
The majority then surveyed numerous congressional enactments concerning embargoes, nonintercourse,
discriminatory tonnage duties, reciprocal trade, and unsafe imports. Presidents and other executive officials had
repeatedly determined whether circumstances justified operation or suspension of congressionally specified
restrictions. Harlan gave this long legislative practice substantial interpretive weight, while acknowledging that
the examples differed in how closely they matched the challenged section. The opinion's review of those
statutes supports its constitutional reasoning; it does not make every historical executive measure
independently binding precedent. Id. at 683–692.
The operative statutory construction was central. Congress selected the goods, enacted the policy of reciprocal
treatment, prescribed the relevant foreign exactions, and fixed the alternative duties in advance. Harlan
interpreted the President's authority to deem foreign treatment unequal and unreasonable as authority to
examine and find the specified condition. Once that condition existed, the President had a duty to proclaim
suspension. The majority therefore located the decision that suspension should occur in Congress. Id. at
692–693.
The President still exercised judgment, including discretion over duration. The Court regarded that judgment as
enforcement of an enacted policy rather than discretion to choose what the law should be. Its distinction was
between making the governing law and determining the circumstances in which the law's existing alternatives
operated. The same explanation disposed of the treaty-making objection: the provision implemented
congressional legislation, rather than conferring authority to substitute an executive treaty for legislative action.
Id. at 693–694.
The bounty issue was resolved through severability. The relevant inquiry was whether the provisions were so
dependent that Congress evidently would not have enacted the valid part without the invalid one. Import duties
and payments to domestic sugar producers had different legal operations. The words and scope of the act did
not show that one depended on the other merely because both formed part of a general economic program. The
importers therefore could not defeat their own assessments by challenging the bounty appropriation. The Court
avoided deciding the broader General Welfare Clause issue. Id. at 695–697.

Separate Opinions
Justice Lamar, joined by Chief Justice Fuller, accepted the judgments but rejected the reciprocity section as an
unconstitutional delegation. They agreed that Congress could use purely contingent legislation of the kind
upheld in Aurora. In their account, that earlier statute specified both an ascertainable event and the
consequences that followed, leaving the President an executive task of identifying the event. Id. at 697–699.
They read the 1890 section differently. Deciding what foreign treatment was reciprocally unequal and
unreasonable, and deciding how long a suspension was just, required the President to make policy judgments
about America's commercial relations. The breadth of the affected American products and the duration left to
presidential discretion made the authority legislative in their view. The statutory language that the majority
treated as fact-finding thus supplied the principal basis of the separate opinion's objection. Id. at 699–700.
Lamar also disputed the force of the historical examples. Most had not received judicial approval, and
legislative practice could not overcome a clear constitutional prohibition. He and Fuller nevertheless
considered the reciprocity provision insufficiently connected to the rest of the tariff act to invalidate all other
provisions. Their concurrence in the judgments depended on that separability, not acceptance of the majority's
delegation analysis. Id. at 698, 700.
Notes
The disagreement demonstrates why stating only that the President found facts is incomplete. Both opinions
accepted some conditional legislation; they disagreed over whether this statute's evaluative language and
duration discretion left legislative choices to the President. A useful comparison identifies those precise
features and explains how each opinion characterized them. Id. at 692–694, 699–700; casebook pp. 49–52.
The full opinion presents three distinct controls: evidence of enactment, constitutional limits on statutory
power, and severability. The enrolled-bill holding did not validate every substantive provision, and agreement
in the ultimate judgments did not establish unanimity on delegation. The bounty question was left undecided on
its merits. Those distinctions prevent turning a decision sustaining particular import assessments into an
unqualified endorsement of every component of the act. Id. at 671–672, 694–700.
