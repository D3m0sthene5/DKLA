# Exercycle of Michigan, Inc. v. Wayson
341 F.2d 335 (7th Cir. 1965) | Contracts

## Decision and relief
On February 12, 1965, the Seventh Circuit affirmed a $22,324.50 lost-profit judgment for a distributor whose exclusive Michigan territory had been invaded by another distributor. The plaintiff could enforce the competing distributor's promise to the manufacturer not to sell outside its assigned territory because that provision primarily protected the other distributors, rather than benefiting them only incidentally. Judge Kiley wrote for a unanimous panel with Circuit Judge Schnackenberg and District Judge Grant. This was review of a judgment after factfinding on conflicting evidence, not a ruling that bare allegations survived dismissal. (335–337.)

## The territorial network and competing sales
Exercycle Corporation of New York sold machines through a national network of territorial distributorships. Each contract gave an exclusive sales territory and included a clause restricting the distributor's sales to its assigned area. Exercycle of Michigan's franchise covered Michigan and the region within fifty miles of Toledo, Ohio. Its contract began October 15, 1955. Wayson and Hale & Hearty's December 21, 1955 contract covered Greater Chicago and nearby cities. They sometimes received nonexclusive rights elsewhere, but never in the plaintiff's territory. (336.)

The trial court found that defendants began selling in Michigan in 1957 and made 123 sales there. Plaintiff protested in early January 1958; in November 1958, the manufacturer reminded defendants that Michigan belonged exclusively to plaintiff and that other distributors could not sell there. Defendants nevertheless continued. The trial court also found that their territorial restriction was intended to protect plaintiff's exclusive rights. (336.)

## Accounting action and appellate objections
Plaintiff brought a diversity action for an accounting, asserting beneficiary rights under defendants' contract and malicious interference with its business and contract rights. It obtained the lost-profit judgment. While the suit was pending, it surrendered its own franchise; note 1 states that this mooted the requested injunction. The affirmed remedy was therefore the monetary judgment, not an ongoing injunction protecting a franchise that plaintiff still held. (336 & n. 1; 337.)

Defendants disputed diversity jurisdiction, the credibility of plaintiff's principal witness, its beneficiary status, and the sufficiency of damages proof. The complaint alleged an amount exceeding the then-applicable $10,000 jurisdictional threshold and demanded more than $50,000. The actual recovery exceeded $10,000, so the jurisdictional objection failed. The court was applying the historical threshold, not today's amount-in-controversy requirement. (336.)

Any inconsistencies in president Donald Harris's testimony went to credibility for the trial court to resolve. The findings about protests, continued sales, and the protective purpose of the territorial clause were supported by evidence and were not clearly erroneous under Rule 52(a). The court accordingly retained those findings as the foundation of its legal conclusion. (336.)

## Primary contractual protection of a third party
Defendants argued that their agreement did not contemplate third-party benefits and that they did not know the contents of other distributors' agreements. The court rejected that position. Defendants knew of the manufacturer's nationwide distribution system and the limits of their own franchise; they therefore knew or should have known that sales beyond those limits intruded upon another distributor's area. Their professed ignorance of the precise other contracts did not undo the territorial provision's protective purpose. (336–337.)

The district court's finding that the restriction protected other distributors, including plaintiff, established a primary benefit rather than an incidental one. That purpose supplied plaintiff's right to sue and recover as a third-party beneficiary. Plaintiff was not merely someone economically affected by the manufacturer's network; it was within the class directly protected by defendants' promise against cross-territory sales. The court confined its conclusion to this clause, these findings, and this distribution arrangement. It did not require plaintiff to prove assignment of the manufacturer's contract. (337.)

## Proof of lost profits
Defendants characterized the damages as speculation. The trial court found that absent defendants' 123 Michigan sales, plaintiff would have made at least that many additional sales. Defendants' records established total retail proceeds of $56,715; evidence placed plaintiff's selling costs at $34,390.50. The difference was $22,324.50, the judgment amount. The calculation concerned plaintiff's foregone profits, not automatic disgorgement of defendants' gross revenue. (337.)

Evidence supported plaintiff's ability to make the displaced sales: it maintained offices in five Michigan cities, employed eight salesmen across its territory, advertised there, and had a president who had sold 1,000–1,200 machines. Uncertainty in proving profits with perfect precision did not entitle the wrongdoer to escape liability where this record supported a reasonable inference. The court upheld the award as adequately grounded rather than merely conjectural. (337.)
