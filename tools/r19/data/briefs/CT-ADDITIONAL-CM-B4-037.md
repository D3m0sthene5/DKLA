# United States v. Spearin
248 U.S. 132 (1918) | Contracts

## Court, cross-appeals, and result
Justice Brandeis delivered the Supreme Court’s December 9, 1918 opinion affirming the Court of Claims’ $141,180.86 judgment on the Government’s appeal and Spearin’s cross-appeal. The United States contended that recovery should be limited to $7,907.98; Spearin sought another $63,658.70. Neither obtained the requested change. Justice McReynolds took no part, and the report contains no dissent or other separate opinion. The paired captions identify both directions of appeal, not two different substantive judgments. (132–133, 138–139.)

## Specified sewer work and undisclosed drainage conditions
Spearin undertook, for $757,800, to build a dry dock at the Brooklyn Navy Yard according to government plans and specifications. A six-foot brick sewer crossed the selected site and had to be diverted before construction could proceed. The Government prescribed the substitute section’s dimensions, materials, and location. Spearin complied with every specified requirement, and the Government accepted that work as satisfactory. The substituted sewer lay approximately thirty-seven to fifty feet from the excavation, partly within his working area, and connected outside that area to a seven-foot sewer emptying into Wallabout Basin. (133–134.)

Approximately a year after relocation, a heavy rain coincided with high tide. Backwater pressure broke the new sewer and flooded the excavation. Investigation disclosed a dam in the connecting seven-foot sewer, diverting much of the water into the six-foot line. Neither the city plans nor the Government’s supplied plans showed the obstruction; the relevant government officials did not know it existed. They did, however, know the low site had experienced sewer overflows in prior years, a history not communicated to Spearin. The decision thus distinguishes ignorance of the dam from the Government’s knowledge of earlier flooding. (134.)

Before contracting, Spearin superficially examined the premises and requested engineering-office information about conditions and probable cost. He made no special examination of the sewer or inquiry into the danger of flooding and had no information on that subject. The question was whether general inspection duties made him bear the adequacy risk despite compliance with the Government’s specific sewer design. (134–135.)

## Suspension, repudiation, and annulment
After the break, Spearin notified the Government that the existing sewer arrangements threatened the project. He would resume only if the Government paid or assumed the existing damage and either removed the future danger through changes or assumed responsibility for losses caused by insufficient sewer capacity, location, and design. Repair alone was estimated at $3,875, but continuing under the existing condition endangered both his and government property. The Government insisted that remediation was his responsibility. (135.)

After fifteen months of investigation and unproductive correspondence, the Secretary of the Navy annulled the contract and took the plant and materials on the site. Other contractors later completed the dry dock under substantially changed plans. The Government first discontinued use of the intersecting sewer and then reconstructed it with different dimensions, shape, and materials to eliminate the danger from internal pressure. Spearin had spent $210,939.18 and received $129,758.32; the Court of Claims also found $60,000 of profit he would have earned through completion. (135.)

## Implied adequacy warranty and general contractor duties
Ordinarily a contractor promising a possible undertaking for a fixed price assumes unforeseen performance difficulties and is not entitled to more compensation simply because they arise. Building on a selected site generally carries risks such as soil subsidence. But the Court distinguished mandatory compliance with owner-prepared plans: the contractor is not responsible for consequences of defects in those plans and specifications. (136.)

The specified sewer was integral to the dry-dock contract and preparation of its foundation, not a collateral contract supported by separate consideration. By prescribing the sewer’s character, dimensions, and location, the Government impliedly warranted that compliance would yield an adequate sewer. Without that particular undertaking the existing system’s risk might have fallen on Spearin; the actual contract allocated it differently. (136–137.)

The usual clauses requiring site examination, plan checking, and responsibility until completion did not negate that warranty. Site inspection did not require searching the locality’s history at his peril to determine whether the Government’s specified sewer would work. Checking plans did not oblige him to judge their adequacy for their intended purpose. The general responsibility clause did not abridge rights arising from specific contractual provisions. All three clauses are reproduced in notes 1–3 and were considered rather than ignored. (137 & nn.1–3.)

Nor did the Navy’s statutory writing requirement or the parol evidence rule exclude the warranty: it arose by implication of law, rather than an unwritten collateral representation offered to vary the agreement. The warranty breach, followed by the Government’s repudiation of past and future responsibility, justified Spearin’s refusal to proceed. He was not obliged to restore the sewer and continue at his own peril. He could have terminated at that point, but did not; the Government’s later unjustified annulment made it liable for the resulting breach loss. (137–138.)

## Both damages challenges failed
The Government’s $7,907.98 proposal represented proceeds from plant it sold plus the value of plant retained. It rested on the rejected premise that Spearin should have repaired the sewer and resumed despite the Government’s denial of responsibility for safe sewer conditions. Once annulment was wrongful, he was entitled to compensation for losses caused by that breach, rather than merely the recovered or retained plant. (138.)

Spearin’s request for another $63,658.70 asserted that the lower court had used the value of work, materials, and plant reported by a naval board instead of subtracting government payments from his proper expenditures. The Supreme Court acknowledged that language in the findings lent some support to this characterization, but read the lower court’s opinion as correctly adopting and applying the expenditure-and-profit measure he invoked. The $60,000 anticipated profit was already included. It affirmed the $141,180.86 judgment, leaving both parties’ requested alterations denied. (133, 135, 138–139.)
