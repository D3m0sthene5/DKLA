## Parties and Material Facts

Friends of the Earth (FOE), Citizens Local Environmental Action Network, and later Sierra Club were citizen-plaintiffs. Laidlaw operated a wastewater-treatment facility in Roebuck, South Carolina, discharging into the North Tyger River under an NPDES permit. The permit limited pollutants including mercury. Laidlaw repeatedly exceeded mercury limits and committed monitoring and reporting violations. 528 U.S. at 175-178. FOE gave the required 60-day notice of intent to sue. Laidlaw then asked the state environmental agency, DHEC, to sue it; the District Court later found Laidlaw sought the state suit to preclude the citizen action. DHEC and Laidlaw settled for $100,000. FOE nevertheless filed, alleging ongoing permit violations and seeking declaratory and injunctive relief plus civil penalties payable to the United States. Id. at 176-178. Members testified that pollution concerns changed their behavior: they curtailed swimming, fishing, picnicking, birdwatching, and other recreational use near the river and believed their property or enjoyment was diminished. The District Court ultimately found no demonstrated environmental harm from the violations, but the Supreme Court emphasized that Article III injury focuses on injury to plaintiffs' interests, not proof of ecological damage as an independent standing element. Id. at 181-185.

## Procedural History

The District Court rejected Laidlaw's standing and preclusion defenses. After trial it found numerous violations and an economic benefit from noncompliance, imposed a $405,800 civil penalty, but denied declaratory and injunctive relief because Laidlaw had achieved substantial compliance. FOE appealed the penalty amount but not the denial of equitable relief. The Fourth Circuit assumed initial standing but held the case moot because only civil penalties remained and those were payable to the Treasury. It vacated and ordered dismissal. The Supreme Court reversed and remanded. Id. at 178-180, 195.

## Issues

Did FOE have standing when members curtailed recreational and aesthetic use because of reasonable concerns about discharges? Could civil penalties payable to the government redress those injuries through deterrence? Did Laidlaw's later compliance and facility closure moot the case? How does the burden for standing differ from the burden for mootness caused by voluntary cessation?

## Rule and Holding

Standing requires concrete injury, causation, and likely redressability. FOE established injury through members' diminished recreational, aesthetic, and economic interests. The relevant injury was to the members, not a requirement to prove environmental damage. 528 U.S. at 180-185. Civil penalties can redress ongoing or threatened injury by deterring future violations even though money is paid to the government. Steel Co. did not control because that case involved wholly past violations; FOE sued over violations ongoing when the complaint was filed. Id. at 185-188. A defendant's voluntary cessation does not moot a case unless it is absolutely clear the challenged conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. The defendant bears this formidable burden. Laidlaw's compliance did not automatically moot the controversy, and the later closure required factual consideration on remand. Id. at 189-195.

## Judgment

The Court reversed the Fourth Circuit and remanded. It did not finally determine the effect of the facility's closure or resolve the attorneys' fees question. Id. at 195.

## Reasoning

### Injury in fact

Members described specific uses of the North Tyger River and specific ways their reasonable concerns about Laidlaw's discharges curtailed those uses. This was materially different from indefinite "some day" travel intentions in Lujan. The Court rejected the idea that plaintiffs also had to prove actual environmental degradation to establish standing; requiring that would make standing harder than proving some merits violations under the Clean Water Act. Id. at 181-185.

### Civil penalties and redressability

The Fourth Circuit treated penalties payable to the Treasury as incapable of redressing private injury. Ginsburg instead focused on deterrence. A substantial penalty makes future noncompliance less attractive and therefore reduces the threat that caused members to avoid the river. The remedy need not transfer money to the plaintiff to redress an injury. Id. at 185-188.

### Standing versus mootness

Standing is measured when the complaint is filed. Mootness asks whether later events have eliminated the live controversy. The Court rejected a simplistic equation between the two. A plaintiff bears the initial standing burden; a defendant claiming its own voluntary conduct mooted the case bears the heavy burden of making recurrence unreasonable to expect. Id. at 189-194.

### Voluntary cessation

If stopping challenged conduct automatically mooted a case, a defendant could stop during litigation and resume after dismissal. Laidlaw's post-filing compliance therefore did not erase the controversy. The facility's later closure might matter, but the record did not permit the Supreme Court to decide conclusively whether recurrence was impossible, especially because Laidlaw retained its NPDES permit and the issue had not been fully resolved below. Id. at 193-195.

## Separate Opinions

### Stevens

Stevens agreed with the Court and added that a civil penalty judgment already entered should not be vacated merely because later events make prospective relief unnecessary. He emphasized the difference between mootness before judgment and an effort to erase a valid penalty afterward. Id. at 195-197.

### Kennedy

Kennedy joined the majority but wrote to flag difficult questions about whether private citizen suits seeking public penalties comport with Article II and historical practice. He did not resolve those questions because they were not properly presented. Id. at 197.

### Scalia, joined by Thomas

Scalia disputed injury and redressability. He argued that the District Court found no environmental harm and that members' subjective concerns should not create injury without an objectively demonstrable threat. He also rejected deterrence of future violations as sufficient redress for civil penalties payable to the government and warned that the majority loosened Steel Co. and Article III limits. Id. at 198-215.

## Doctrinal Significance

Laidlaw sharply separates standing from mootness and makes voluntary cessation difficult for defendants to use. It also recognizes deterrence as redress for civil penalties where violations were ongoing when suit began. The case does not hold that citizen suits for wholly past violations satisfy Article III.

## Complete original-opinion clarifications

Standing must be established separately for each requested form of relief. The Court did not infer penalty standing merely from standing to seek an injunction. Instead it held that imposing penalties for violations ongoing when suit began was likely to deter continuing or future violations that threatened these members. It acknowledged that deterrence can become too insubstantial or remote and expressly declined to define those outer limits. Steel Co.’s treatment of wholly past violations therefore remained distinct. 528 U.S. at 185–188.

The members described particular locations and activities near the North Tyger River, not an abstract interest in enforcing the statute. The majority credited reasonable concerns tied to concededly ongoing illegal discharges; it did not hold that an unsupported subjective fear always establishes injury. It distinguished both indefinite future travel intentions and mere conclusory use of unspecified areas. Scalia disputed the adequacy of these members’ evidence after trial, including their deposition testimony and the later finding of no demonstrated environmental harm. This is a disagreement about injury and proof, not a majority finding that mercury violations caused proven ecological damage. Id. at 181–185,198–201.

Laidlaw’s 1987 permit became effective January 1. The District Court found 489 mercury-limit violations between 1987 and 1995 and 13 such violations after the citizen complaint, along with 13 monitoring and 10 reporting violations during that period. The last recorded mercury violation occurred in January 1995. The January 22,1997 judgment found an economic benefit of $1,092,581 but imposed a $405,800 penalty considering the statutory factors and total deterrent effect. References to substantial compliance since August 1992 do not mean the District Court found perfect compliance throughout that interval. The judgment anticipated possible fees; it did not actually award them. Id. at 176–179,195.

FOE sent notice April 10,1992; the state settlement was reached June 9, before expiration of the sixty-day period, and FOE sued June 12. The District Court’s determination that the state had not diligently prosecuted permitted this suit to proceed at that stage. The Supreme Court did not finally resolve that separate statutory defense. Scalia expressly notes that it remains open on remand. Laidlaw’s facilitation of the state complaint is evidence discussed by the majority, not a holding that a defendant-assisted state action necessarily fails the diligent-prosecution requirement. Id. at 176–178,211 n. 4.

Both substantial compliance and closure might moot the case if Laidlaw satisfies the heavy voluntary-cessation burden. Neither was conclusively resolved here. The facility’s closure occurred after the Fourth Circuit’s decision; its effect, along with compliance, presented disputed factual questions for remand. The Supreme Court reversed the appellate dismissal, rather than finally reinstating an unalterable penalty or finding recurrence impossible. Denial of injunctive relief does not itself establish that no future violations remain for penalties to deter: a court may regard an injunction as excessively intrusive while penalties provide adequate deterrence. Id. at 189–194.

The majority distinguished the plaintiff’s initial standing burden from a defendant’s voluntary-cessation burden. Its discussion of sunk costs concerned costs to the judicial system, not a litigant’s desire to recoup expenses. It did not authorize retaining cases after the parties plainly lack a continuing interest. Footnote 6 also explains that even a subsequent mootness finding would not necessarily justify vacating a district judgment when mootness resulted from the voluntary act of the party that lost below. Id. at 190–194 nn. 5–6.

The majority did not decide whether the catalyst theory remained available for fees. The District Court had stayed the fee-petition deadline pending appeal, and no order granting or denying fees was before the Court. Any request for costs and fees belonged first in the District Court. This is an express reservation, not approval or rejection of the catalyst theory. Id. at 194–195.

## Separate opinions: additional limits and agreements

Stevens wrote alone. His view is broader than protection of an already-entered judgment: he would treat the civil-penalty claim as surviving voluntary compliance after filing even if compliance occurred before judgment and made related prospective relief moot. He analogized penalties to punitive damages and stressed that later events cannot retroactively invalidate a valid monetary judgment. His starred footnote identifies supporting appellate cases. These are his additional reasons, distinct from the majority’s decision to leave the compliance and closure questions open. Id. at 195–197.

Kennedy also wrote alone. He joined the Court while reserving Article II questions concerning private recovery of public fines and possible delegation of executive power. Neither the certiorari petition, lower-court treatment, nor the parties’ briefing specifically developed those questions. They were reserved for another case. The assigned editorial bracket on 1224 incorrectly says Stevens was joined by Kennedy; the original report’s participation statement and both opinion headings identify separate solo concurrences. Id. at 171,195,197.

Scalia’s dissent, joined by Thomas, has three principal objections: inadequate proof of injury; lack of sufficiently particularized and likely redress from Treasury penalties; and the majority’s broader distinction between standing and mootness. On redressability he distinguishes the general availability of penalties from the marginal deterrence supplied by an additional actual penalty in this case. He contends that relief designed to deter generally cannot satisfy the constitutional requirement that the plaintiff’s injury be redressed. His structural criticism invokes Article III’s limits. He expressly declines to decide Article II because that question was not argued, despite discussing its relevance to executive control. Id. at 198–210.

Scalia conditionally agrees with the majority’s practical remand on voluntary cessation: assuming initial penalty standing, which he rejects, leaving the fact-sensitive compliance question open is sensible. His disagreement with the standing/mootness discussion does not mean he rejects the ordinary voluntary-cessation rule. He views its burden as an evidentiary presumption within a continuing live controversy, rather than a relaxation of Article III. His notes 4–6 separately preserve diligent prosecution, reject Stevens’s damages analogy because penalties are not compensation to the plaintiff, and explain the Fourth Circuit’s possible formulations of its jurisdictional conclusion. Id. at 210–215.
