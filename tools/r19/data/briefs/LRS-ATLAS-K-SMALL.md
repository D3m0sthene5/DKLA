## Facts and litigation

In December 1992 Gary Small shipped a water heater from the United States to Okinawa, purportedly as a gift. Suspicious Japanese customs officers opened it and found two rifles, eight semiautomatic pistols, and 410 rounds of ammunition. A three-judge Japanese court convicted him of weapons-control and customs offenses on April 14, 1994. Each offense was punishable by imprisonment exceeding one year. He received a five-year sentence with credit for 320 days already served, was paroled on November 22, 1996, and completed parole on May 26, 1998. These detailed facts appear in the dissent’s record account. 544 U.S. at 395–396. The assigned account and syllabus say he served five years; the fuller judicial chronology distinguishes the sentence imposed from the time actually spent imprisoned.

On June 2, 1998, one week after parole ended, Small bought a pistol from a Pennsylvania dealer. A later search found another pistol and over 300 rounds of ammunition. Federal authorities prosecuted him under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), which bars specified firearm possession by a person with a qualifying prior conviction. He pleaded guilty while reserving the foreign-predicate statutory argument. The Western District of Pennsylvania rejected it, 183 F. Supp. 2d 755, and the Third Circuit affirmed, 333 F.3d 425. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve a circuit disagreement: the Third, Fourth, and Sixth Circuits included foreign convictions, while Gayle in the Second Circuit and Concha in the Tenth excluded them. Id. at 387, 395–396.

## Question and holding

Does § 922(g)(1)’s reference to someone convicted in any court of a crime punishable by imprisonment exceeding one year include a foreign conviction? The Court held that it includes **domestic convictions only**. Small’s Japanese judgment could not supply the disputed predicate. The judgment was one of statutory interpretation, not an assessment that his Japanese conviction was procedurally unfair or that his particular offenses were harmless. Id. at 387, 394.

## Majority reasoning: contextual breadth and a domestic assumption

The word any ordinarily has broad force, but its domain depends on context. A promise to see any film does not necessarily extend to films showing in every city. The Court’s earlier cases likewise did not treat that modifier as fixing scope independently of the surrounding law. Breadth within a category does not answer whether the category includes foreign tribunals. Id. at 388.

The majority began with the ordinary assumption that Congress generally legislates with domestic concerns in mind. It expressly acknowledged that the established presumption against extraterritorial application **does not directly apply**: the Government was prosecuting firearm possession in the United States, not conduct abroad. Nevertheless, the Court considered a similar domestic assumption appropriate for determining which prior convictions this domestic prohibition incorporates. Id. at 388–391.

Several practical considerations supported that choice. Foreign judgments can rest on conduct permitted or encouraged here, procedures inconsistent with American understandings of fairness, or punishments substantially more severe than domestic law would impose. As a group, they therefore identify dangerous persons for purposes of American law less reliably than domestic convictions. The statute also supplies no coherent mechanism for prosecutors or courts to screen particular foreign judgments for acceptability. Such individualized refinement would be textually unsupported, difficult for officials unfamiliar with foreign law, and uncertain for people deciding whether they could lawfully possess a firearm. Id. at 389–390.

These concerns did not dictate the result or establish an inflexible clear-statement requirement. The majority described its assumption as an aid where Congress likely had not considered the problem and other evidence was approximately balanced. Language, context, history, or purpose could rebut it. Id. at 390–391. This qualification matters: Small did not declare that a statute can refer to foreign facts only if it expressly uses the word foreign.

## Statutory structure, history, and purpose

The Court found no convincing rebuttal. The provision did not mention foreign judgments or address a subject, such as immigration or terrorism, in which they would be especially pertinent. Several neighboring provisions expressly used federal or state categories. If foreign convictions counted generally, equivalent offenses could receive inconsistent treatment. A Canadian antitrust conviction would disable firearm possession while a New York equivalent would fall within the business-offense exception. Foreign counterparts to qualifying state misdemeanors could count although the domestic offenses were exempt. Conversely, domestic-violence misdemeanors and serious-drug enhancement predicates were defined domestically, leaving foreign equivalents outside those particular provisions. The accumulated distinctions suggested that Congress had not considered the foreign-conviction question. Id. at 391–392.

The legislative history was **neutral**, not affirmative proof of deliberate exclusion. A Senate version used federal and state felony language; the Conference Committee adopted the punishment-based version referring to any court. The change simplified the definition and avoided varying state uses of felony. The history did not show that the revision addressed foreign convictions. The majority therefore treated silence as consistent with the domestic assumption rather than adopting Gayle’s stronger inference of a domestic legislative intent from the reports themselves. Id. at 393.

Congress’s purpose of keeping guns from dangerous persons did support the Government: a person convicted of a serious foreign crime might be just as dangerous as a domestic offender. But the Government reported only roughly ten to twelve foreign-predicate prosecutions since 1968. The majority considered that limited experience further evidence that Congress had probably paid no attention to the question. The statute and history provided insufficient grounds to displace the domestic assumption. Congress remained free to change the result by amendment. Id. at 393–394.

## Thomas dissent, joined by Scalia and Kennedy

The dissent would have applied the natural expansive meaning of any court and affirmed. The Japanese tribunal was a court with authority to convict Small of qualifying serious crimes. Other firearms provisions expressly limited their reach to federal or state law; their specific wording made the absence of such a limit in § 922(g)(1) significant. Thomas accepted that context matters, but saw nothing in this context limiting the class of courts geographically. Id. at 394–398 & nn.1–4.

He rejected the majority’s domestic assumption as an invented clear-statement rule. Extraterritoriality doctrine regulates where federal prohibitions apply, not whether a domestic prohibition can use foreign facts. Here the conduct being punished was domestic, and incorporating a foreign conviction created no conflicting foreign obligation. Thomas also objected that neither party had presented the majority’s new quasi-extraterritorial assumption. Notes 5 and 6 elaborate the absence of briefing and dispute the majority’s characterization of the remaining evidence as balanced. Id. at 399–401.

The dissent considered foreign serious convictions sensible proxies for dangerousness, especially Small’s actual arms-smuggling offenses. The majority’s examples of repressive foreign laws were selective; note 7 collected foreign murder, rape, kidnapping, weapons, and other violent offenses that could reasonably inform a possession disability. The rarity of prosecutions weakened predictions of widespread unfair applications, rather than disproving Congress’s practical reason to cover them. Id. at 401–403 & n.7.

As to statutory anomalies, Thomas acknowledged the asymmetries but considered them potentially rational choices to proceed incrementally with familiar domestic exceptions. They were not absurd in the strong sense required to disregard clear language. The majority’s categorical exclusion created its own serious disparity: people convicted abroad of grave violence could possess firearms while someone convicted domestically of a comparatively minor qualifying offense could not. Id. at 403–405.

Finally, Congress’s failure to anticipate an application demonstrates statutory breadth rather than ambiguity when the text is clear. Thomas rejected reliance on silence in legislative history and, alternatively, thought the removal of federal and state limits in Conference supported expanded coverage. His discussion expressly recognized objections to using unenacted historical material at all; for those who did use it, he considered this change evidence for the Government. Id. at 405–407. That was part of a shared dissent, not a separate Scalia concurrence.

## Exact disposition and course use

The Supreme Court **reversed the Third Circuit’s judgment and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion**, 544 U.S. at 394. It did not adjudicate the validity of the Japanese conviction, decide whether Small was dangerous, or announce a case-specific fairness exception. This is a different defendant and judgment from Gayle, although its domestic-only construction agrees with Gayle’s result.

The paired decisions expose disagreements over the weight of legislative silence, the domain of broad words, and the permissible use of default assumptions. The assigned author’s retrospective comment about drafting an alternative Gayle result illustrates his interpretive deliberation; it is not an additional Supreme Court holding. The full source also prevents conflating an analogy to extraterritoriality with direct application of that canon. This brief verifies the historical opinion and assigned passage, without certifying subsequent treatment or current statutory amendments.
