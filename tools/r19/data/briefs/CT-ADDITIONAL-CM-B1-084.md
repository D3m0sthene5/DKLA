# Mechanical Plant Services, Inc. v. Dresser-Rand Co.
116 F.3d 1474 (table), No. 96-1896 (4th Cir. June 27, 1997) | Contracts

## Transaction and litigation
Mechanical Plant Services supplied precision millwrights who built and repaired metal surfaces and rotating machinery. Dresser-Rand requested a labor quotation in 1990. MPS’s March 7 quotation included a clause barring Dresser-Rand for one year from employing MPS workers who had been terminated, released, or voluntarily quit, or using them through another labor broker or vendor. Violation purportedly required payment equivalent to 2,000 manhours per employee at the straight-time invoice rate. (Official slip op. 2–3.)

Dresser-Rand’s March 19 purchase order expressly took exception to that term. MPS refused to acknowledge the exception the next day. Dresser-Rand’s March 26 letter said it did not intend to employ MPS’s active employees but had a problem signing the clause and proposed discussion. Telephone negotiations followed. MPS vice-president Wayne Henry eventually told Dresser-Rand manager R. M. Jennings that no employees would be sent unless the clause was included. Nevertheless, MPS began supplying workers without a document signed by Dresser-Rand. For the ensuing five-year relationship MPS sent a form containing the clause each time labor was ordered, Dresser-Rand never signed, and MPS continued supplying workers for which Dresser-Rand paid. (Id. at 3.)

MPS alleged Dresser-Rand hired its employees in violation of the clause and sought $901,280 in liquidated damages in a diversity action. The district court granted Dresser-Rand summary judgment because MPS’s performance accepted an offer excluding the clause. The Fourth Circuit affirmed in an unpublished per curiam opinion, over Judge Hamilton’s dissent, on a panel with Chief Judge Wilkinson and Judge Wilkins. The decision was argued June 4 and decided June 27, 1997. The original expressly stated its nonbinding status under then-applicable Local Rule 36(c). The reporter citation is a table citation; the substantive pinpoints here refer to the preserved official seven-page opinion. (Id. at 1–4.)

## Formation issue and majority reasoning
The issue was whose proposed terms governed a relationship in which both sides performed while repeatedly disagreeing over a restriction. MPS argued that Dresser-Rand’s acceptance of labor accepted MPS’s forms, including the clause. Dresser-Rand maintained that it consistently rejected the clause and that MPS’s subsequent provision of labor accepted the counteroffer omitting it. (Id. at 2–4.)

The majority did not treat absence of a signature as automatically fatal. North Carolina law recognized enforceable agreements and acceptance through a course of conduct without an executed writing. The question was what conduct meant in light of the parties’ communications. Dresser-Rand’s initial express objection and continued refusal to sign were consistent throughout the relationship. Its receiving and paying for labor did not, in the majority’s view, establish agreement to a term it had consistently rejected. (Id. at 4.)

MPS, by contrast, supplied labor despite knowing of that continuing objection. Its ultimatum was never accepted, yet it performed anyway. The majority interpreted that course of conduct as willingness to transact without the disputed restriction, effectively accepting Dresser-Rand’s counteroffer. The result therefore turns on the court’s interpretation of express resistance combined with performance; it does not hold that sending the physically last form always settles a dispute, or that every unsigned term necessarily fails. (Id. at 4–5.)

North Carolina’s suspicion of restraints on employee mobility reinforced the majority’s caution. The opinion cited the writing requirement for employment contracts with noncompetition provisions and strict requirements for restrictions on former employees’ business activities. Although this business-to-business clause did not directly restrict workers, it would indirectly prevent MPS millwrights from moving to customer employment. MPS acknowledged that purpose. The affected workers were not parties to the agreement constraining their opportunities. The majority considered that policy consistent with its formation conclusion; it did not separately adjudicate this clause’s reasonableness as to time or territory. (Id. at 5; compare dissent 6–7 & sole note.)

## Hamilton’s dissent
Judge Hamilton thought the record supported two equally plausible interpretations and therefore required a factfinder. Dresser-Rand’s consistent objection could support the majority’s view. But after its March 26 letter, MPS expressly insisted that performance depended on inclusion of the restriction. Dresser-Rand then accepted the employees’ services while MPS’s written terms unquestionably continued to include it. A reasonable jury could infer acceptance of MPS’s offer from those acts. Choosing which side had fired the effective last shot was, for Hamilton, choosing between reasonable factual inferences on summary judgment. (Id. at 5–6.)

His sole footnote also challenged the majority’s policy inference. He agreed North Carolina was suspicious of restraints but emphasized that the rule concerned unreasonable restrictions. The party seeking enforcement bore a burden that included reasonableness in time and territory. The district court had never assessed those matters, so general caution about mobility could not substitute for the missing evaluation. Hamilton would have vacated the summary judgment and remanded, including for consideration of Dresser-Rand’s other summary-judgment arguments. That proposed remedy was the dissent’s position, not the court’s operative result. (Id. at 6–7 & sole note.)
