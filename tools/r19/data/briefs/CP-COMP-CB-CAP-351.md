# Nutraceutical Corp. v. Lambert

**139 S. Ct. 710 (2019) — Supreme Court of the United States, February 26, 2019.** Justice Sotomayor wrote for a unanimous Court. Civil Procedure: Rule 23(f), mandatory claim-processing rules, and equitable tolling. Assigned account: CivPro, physical PDF 437–438, printed 847. All judicial pinpoints below refer to the preserved official ten-page slip opinion, separately paginated from its syllabus.

## Facts, chronology, and the requested appeal

In March 2013 Troy Lambert sued Nutraceutical in the Central District of California, alleging that marketing a dietary supplement violated California consumer-protection law. The district court initially certified a consumer class but reconsidered and decertified it on February 20, 2015. Rule 23(f) allowed Lambert fourteen days to request the Ninth Circuit's permission for an immediate appeal. This decision concerns that request's timing, not whether the marketing was unlawful or class treatment otherwise appropriate. **Slip 1–2.**

At a March 2 status conference, ten days after decertification, Lambert said he wanted to file a reconsideration motion in the near future. The district court directed him to file it no later than March 12. Neither counsel nor the court discussed an appeal. Lambert filed the reconsideration motion on March 12, twenty days after decertification. The district court denied it on June 24, and Lambert filed his Rule 23(f) petition on July 8, fourteen days after that denial but more than four months after the original class order. **1–2.**

Nutraceutical objected that the petition was untimely. The Ninth Circuit nevertheless allowed it, reasoning that Rule 23(f)'s deadline was nonjurisdictional and thus susceptible to equitable tolling. It considered Lambert diligent because he announced his intention within the initial fourteen days and followed the district court's schedule. Having allowed the appeal, it reversed decertification on the merits. The Supreme Court reviewed whether equitable tolling could support the late permission petition. **2–3 & n.1.**

## Holding: nonjurisdictional does not mean equitably adjustable

The Court agreed that Rule 23(f)'s time limit is a nonjurisdictional claim-processing rule. Its source was a procedural rule rather than a statute. Unlike a jurisdictional limitation, it could therefore be waived or forfeited by the opposing party. But Nutraceutical had properly raised it, and the Court rejected the Ninth Circuit's inference that every nonjurisdictional deadline permits equitable relaxation. **3–4 & nn.2–3.**

Some claim-processing rules are mandatory when invoked. Whether a deadline permits equitable tolling depends on the governing text, not solely its classification as jurisdictional or nonjurisdictional. Courts cannot override a rule's clear restriction simply because a litigant acted diligently, was reasonably mistaken, or otherwise deserves sympathy. The issue here was the court's authority to excuse lateness in the face of a preserved objection, not a requirement to raise the deadline on its own whenever a petition was late. **4.**

## How the rules foreclosed tolling

Rule 23(f) required a petition within fourteen days, and Appellate Rule 5(a)(2) required a permission petition within the specified time. Deadline language alone does not always defeat tolling, because other procedural provisions may authorize flexibility. Here, however, the appellate rules addressed this filing specifically. Appellate Rule 2 permitted suspension of provisions for good cause except as limited by Rule 26(b). Rule 26(b) generally allowed extensions but expressly prohibited extending the time to petition for permission to appeal. That carveout covered Lambert's filing and prevented the equitable exception the Ninth Circuit had used. **4–5.**

The Court reinforced that reading with *Carlisle* and *Robinson*. Those cases involved a criminal-rule extension provision that likewise prohibited enlarging specified deadlines. They treated the restriction as controlling even where a court ordinarily had authority to relax time limits. The comparison supplied a textual and precedential reason for inflexibility, rather than assuming that every appellate deadline is formally jurisdictional. **5–6.**

## Lambert's counterarguments and the Court's answers

Lambert proposed separating extensions granted before expiration from permission granted afterward. Rule 26(b)'s general language addressed both actions, while its prohibition used the word “extend.” He argued that the prohibition therefore left room to excuse an already late filing. The Court found *Robinson* had rejected the same distinction: allowing the late filing effectively enlarged the prohibited period regardless of when permission was given. Arguments from rules governing appeals as of right carried little weight because Rules 5 and 26 specifically addressed discretionary permission petitions. **6–7 & n.4.**

He also invoked the Rule 23(f) committee's statement that a court of appeals could grant or deny a petition on any persuasive consideration. That broad discretion concerns whether a certification ruling deserves interlocutory review, not whether an otherwise untimely petition may be accepted. The limited availability of immediate review is consistent with an unforgiving filing requirement. General equitable preferences cannot overcome the relevant rules' explicit language. **7–8 & n.5.**

Finally, Lambert pointed to lower-court decisions allowing petitions within fourteen days after resolution of a reconsideration motion itself filed within fourteen days of the original certification order. He treated that practice as proof of tolling authority. The Court distinguished it conceptually: a timely reconsideration motion can affect when an order is final for appeal purposes, and thus when the clock begins, rather than suspending an already running time period through equitable tolling. Lambert's written motion came after the initial period. His earlier remarks did not constitute an oral motion; the transcript described leave to file and a future intention. **8–9 & n.6.**

## Reservations and exact judgment

The Court did not decide every possible reconsideration or filing problem. Note 7 expressly reserved the effect of a motion actually filed within the fourteen-day window. It also reserved a situation in which the district court misled a litigant about the appellate deadline—something it said had not happened here—and whether an insurmountable filing impediment could require a different result. Those reservations limit how broadly the no-tolling holding should be restated. **9 n.7.**

Lambert separately argued that his March 12 reconsideration motion was timely under other rules or the district court's schedule and restarted the appeal period, or that denial of reconsideration independently qualified as a new certification order under Rule 23(f). The Ninth Circuit had not decided those theories, and they lay beyond the question presented. The Supreme Court left them for that court on remand if preserved. It therefore **reversed and remanded**, rather than finally declaring every possible basis for the petition unavailable or affirming decertification on its merits. **9–10.**

## Reading the assignment

The textbook correctly distinguishes a claim-processing deadline from a jurisdictional one and identifies Appellate Rule 26(b)'s prohibition. Its reconsideration discussion describes the Court's explanation of the distinction between the clock's starting point and tolling. The full opinion adds important precision: note 7 reserves the effect of an actually timely reconsideration motion, and the final section leaves Lambert's other preserved timeliness theories open. The decision forecloses the particular equitable-tolling approach used below; it does not supply a comprehensive ruling on all reconsideration scenarios.
