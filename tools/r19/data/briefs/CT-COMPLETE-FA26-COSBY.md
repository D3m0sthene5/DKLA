# Commonwealth v. Cosby
252 A.3d 1092 (Pa. 2021), No. 39 MAP 2020, decided June 30, 2021 | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
The Pennsylvania Supreme Court reviewed the Superior Court’s December 10, 2019 affirmance of William Henry Cosby Jr.’s convictions and September 25, 2018 judgment of sentence for three counts of aggravated indecent assault involving Andrea Constand. It held that due process required enforcement of a former district attorney’s unconditional nonprosecution decision after that decision intentionally induced Cosby’s reasonable, detrimental reliance in civil depositions. The Court vacated the convictions and sentence, discharged Cosby, and barred future prosecution on these particular charges. Justice Wecht wrote for a four-Justice majority joined by Justices Todd, Donohue, and Mundy. Justice Dougherty, joined by Chief Justice Baer, agreed that there had been a due-process violation but would suppress the deposition evidence and allow prosecution to continue. Justice Saylor dissented from discharge. Thus six Justices accepted a due-process violation, but only four accepted the majority’s scope of the violation and discharge remedy. The Court did not decide the separately granted question concerning other-acts testimony under Pennsylvania Rule of Evidence 404(b). Majority slip op. 1–2 & n.3, 38–47, 78–79 & n.35; Dougherty 1, 4–9; Saylor 1–5.

## Facts and chronology
Constand worked in Temple University’s athletics department and met Cosby in 2002 through his relationship with the university. Their contacts included career advice and several visits during which, according to her testimony, he made unwelcome sexual advances. In January 2004, at his Cheltenham home, he gave her three blue pills and wine. She described impaired vision, weakness, difficulty speaking, and intermittent unconsciousness, followed by digital penetration and his use of her hand to masturbate himself while she could not resist. She continued some subsequent contacts and attempted to ask what pills he had given her. After returning to Canada, she reported the incident to police in January 2005. Her complaint reached authorities in Pennsylvania. The Supreme Court recounted her trial testimony; it did not treat the later due-process decision as a finding that the alleged assault had not occurred. Majority 3–8.

Montgomery County District Attorney Bruce Castor investigated with detectives and his deputy. He assessed a prosecution as unlikely to succeed because of delayed reporting, inconsistencies he perceived in Constand’s accounts, lack of corroborating forensic evidence, subsequent contacts with Cosby, and anticipated obstacles to admitting other complainants’ accounts. Those were Castor’s assessments of the prosecutorial evidence, not independent findings that Constand was untruthful. Cosby had voluntarily spoken to police with counsel, maintained that the encounter was consensual, and said that he had supplied Benadryl. After the police interview, Cosby voluntarily supplied pills that laboratory testing confirmed to be Benadryl; the later home search found no evidence investigators considered corroborative of Constand’s allegation. Majority 8–10.

Castor decided not to prosecute. As he later testified, he intended to eliminate the threat of prosecution so that Cosby could not invoke the Fifth Amendment in a civil action by Constand. He communicated his position to criminal attorney Walter Phillips, who conveyed it to civil counsel John Schmitt. On February 17, 2005, Castor issued a signed press release describing the investigation as concluded, explaining that a conviction was unattainable on the available evidence, and declining to authorize charges. Its final paragraph said that he would not publicly elaborate on his analysis because of the possible civil action and that he would reconsider “this decision” if necessary. Whether this reservation concerned charging or public elaboration became an important interpretive dispute. No formal immunity order or bargained written nonprosecution agreement was executed. Constand and her attorneys did not assent to such an arrangement and learned of the charging decision from a reporter. Majority 10–13, 19–29, 61–64 & n.25.

Constand sued in federal court on March 8, 2005. Cosby sat for four depositions on counsel’s understanding that the nonprosecution decision removed the risk supporting a Fifth Amendment claim. He never invoked that privilege. Although counsel resisted some questions on other grounds, a civil-court order required answers about Constand. He repeated his consent defense but also gave inculpatory information about supplying drugs to other women before sexual activity. Constand settled the civil suit for $3.38 million. The records were initially sealed, then unsealed following a media request in 2015. The majority inferred that both the disclosures and the substantial civil settlement reflected detrimental reliance. The official opinion’s page 13 says “March 8, 2015” for filing the civil suit, but page 26 and the assigned excerpt correctly give 2005; the former is an evident internal date error. Majority 13–15 & nn.6–9, 26, 66–67, 76–78.

Castor’s successors reopened the investigation in 2015 and did not accept that his decision barred prosecution. Correspondence between Castor and then-District Attorney Risa Ferman showed disputes over what he had promised, whether the restriction concerned prosecution or use of civil admissions, and whether Constand’s lawyers had accepted the arrangement. Castor’s accounts were not consistent on all those points. The eventual charges concerned the January 2004 encounter with Constand, not a general immunity from liability for every alleged encounter with other women. Majority 15–18, 26–29 & nn.10–14.

## Proceedings and competing arguments
Cosby sought dismissal through a January 11, 2016 habeas petition. At the evidentiary hearing, Castor and Schmitt described the nonprosecution decision and reliance; Constand’s civil lawyers disputed the claimed agreement and their purported assent. The trial court found that there had been no formal agreement or enforceable promise of immunity, that Castor had exercised ordinary unilateral charging discretion, and that his later accounts were equivocal. It also considered reliance unreasonable given the lack of writing and the availability of experienced counsel. It rejected dismissal, and later refused to suppress the deposition evidence. Interlocutory efforts did not stop the prosecution. Majority 18–30.

The first trial in 2017 admitted testimony from one other alleged victim and ended in a hung-jury mistrial after seven days of deliberation. The second trial in 2018 admitted testimony from five other alleged victims, concerning incidents in the 1980s. The trial court treated those accounts as a common plan or scheme relevant to consent, rather than solely propensity evidence. The jury convicted on all three charges; the court imposed three to ten years’ imprisonment and designated Cosby a sexually violent predator. The Superior Court affirmed both the evidentiary rulings and the rejection of the nonprosecution claim. Its reasoning included the absence of judicially approved statutory immunity and the unreasonableness of relying on a press release without a formal agreement. Majority 31–46 & nn.16–19.

The Supreme Court granted review of the other-acts evidence and the nonprosecution issue. Cosby sought enforcement of Castor’s commitment against successor prosecutors; alternatively, he sought suppression of the civil deposition evidence. The Commonwealth relied on ordinary prosecutorial discretion, the trial court’s factual and credibility findings, the lack of a formal promise or statutory order, and the alleged unreasonableness of reliance. The remedy question ultimately separated the majority from Dougherty and Baer: whether the constitutional harm was prosecution after induced civil reliance, or the use of the resulting evidence at a criminal trial. Majority 47–52, 68–78; Dougherty 4–9 & n.4.

## Governing principles and holding
Supported trial-court factual findings bound the Supreme Court, but legal conclusions and inferences drawn from those facts remained subject to its own review. The majority accepted the absence of a formal immunity agreement and the characterization of the 2005 action as unilateral prosecutorial discretion. It nevertheless held that those findings did not resolve the distinct constitutional consequences of intentional, detrimental reliance. Its holding did not depend on reversing the trial court to find an ordinary bargain or a statutory immunity order. Majority 48–52, 68 n.27.

Prosecutors have broad discretion to charge, withhold charges, select offenses, and discontinue cases, but exercise that power within the constraints of federal and state due process. A prosecutor’s decision ordinarily can be conditioned, changed, or revoked; declining charges does not invariably create a permanent defense to prosecution. Here, however, an unconditional decision publicly made with the purpose of inducing reliance, followed by reasonable reliance to the detriment of the privilege against self-incrimination, required enforcement as a matter of fundamental fairness. A successor’s assumption of office did not eliminate the responsibility of the prosecutorial office. Majority 52–60, 78–79.

Contract doctrines informed this constitutional inquiry. The majority drew on cases enforcing prosecutorial assurances in plea and related contexts, including Santobello, Zuber, and Martinez, while recognizing that unilateral declination differs from a consummated bargain. It stated the promissory-estoppel elements as conduct the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or inaction, actual responsive action, and injustice if the assurance is not enforced. The foundation of relief remained due process under the Fourteenth Amendment and Article I, Section 9 of the Pennsylvania Constitution, rather than a finding of an ordinary contract. Unconsummated offers ordinarily need not be enforced, but detrimental reliance can create constitutional consequences. Majority 53–60 & nn.21–23.

## Majority’s application and remedy
The majority read the entire February 2005 press release as a final, unconditional decision concerning this prosecution. It interpreted the later reservation of reconsideration as referring to public commentary about the decision, rather than to the charging decision itself. It separated the trial court’s rejection of a formal bargained promise from the objective effect of the public announcement and the established purpose of obtaining civil testimony. It stressed that a press release alone would not necessarily create a due-process entitlement: the intended and actual detrimental reliance supplied the constitutional consequence. Majority 61–64 & n.25.

The Fifth Amendment privilege can apply in a civil proceeding whenever answers may expose the witness to criminal punishment. The majority concluded that the assurance removed the risk on which Cosby and counsel understood that privilege to depend. His prior voluntary police interview and repetition of an exculpatory account did not establish that he would freely supply inculpatory information without that assurance. It rejected the trial court’s inferred waiver and concluded that the four depositions supplied clear evidence of reliance, including disclosures that weakened his civil defense and contributed to the settlement. Majority 64–68 & nn.26–27.

That reliance was reasonable. A contrary rule would require a defendant to distrust an elected prosecutor’s public statement and disregard experienced counsel’s advice. No legal rule required the assurance to be recorded or embodied in a written agreement before reliance could be reasonable. The majority also rejected using wealth, education, number of attorneys, or supposed sophistication to vary the entitlement to rely on counsel. Castor intended the reliance and did not correct it during the succeeding decade. Majority 68–72.

Specific performance was not an automatic contract remedy merely because reliance occurred. The Court instead asked what equity and fundamental fairness required in the particular circumstances. It distinguished Stipetich, where police lacking authority promised nonprosecution and the Court had suggested suppression of resulting evidence. Here the elected district attorney had made the decision, and the harm extended beyond the criminal trial: the induced testimony handicapped Cosby in the civil case and contributed to a substantial settlement before the later prosecutions. Suppressing testimony at a third criminal trial could not undo those consequences or restore the promised position. The majority therefore required discharge and a bar against prosecution on these charges. It described the circumstances and remedy as rare and severe, and did not announce that every defective prosecutorial assurance requires dismissal. Majority 72–79 & nn.28–35.

## Separate opinions
Dougherty, joined by Baer, agreed that the prosecutor’s inducement, reasonable reliance, and later use of the damaging evidence violated due process. He expressly rejected any suggestion that a district attorney possesses a unilateral, permanent pardon-like power over future prosecutors. In his view, the constitutional violation occurred when the induced deposition evidence was introduced at trial, rather than merely when a successor brought charges. Suppression would cure that injury and restore the relevant position without an irrevocable prosecution bar. He relied on Stipetich, Peters, Parker, Gallego, and Blue to argue that excluding evidence, rather than preventing an otherwise valid prosecution, was the appropriate response to the defective nonprosecution undertaking. He expressed no opinion on the remaining other-acts issue because the majority’s discharge rendered it moot. Dougherty 1–9 & nn.1–6.

Saylor read the press release as an ordinary announcement of a present discretionary decision, carrying an inherent possibility of reconsideration. He found the express reconsideration language ambiguous and disputed the majority’s treatment of the trial court’s supported credibility findings concerning a promise and reliance. He also considered claimed reliance on an unwritten, unrecorded, vague undertaking unreasonable. He dissented from discharge, but did not simply endorse all the trial rulings: he expressed substantial reservations about the remote other-acts evidence and a tentative inclination to order a new trial on that separate ground. That inclination was not a decided evidentiary holding. Saylor 1–5 & nn.1–3.
