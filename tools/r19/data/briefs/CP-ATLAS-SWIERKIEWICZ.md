# Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N. A.

**Civil Procedure · CP-ATLAS-SWIERKIEWICZ · 534 U.S. 506 (2002) · February 26, 2002**

Swierkiewicz holds that an employment discrimination plaintiff need not plead facts establishing a McDonnell Douglas prima facie case. That framework organizes proof; it is not an extra pleading requirement. The complaint described the alleged discrimination, the events leading to discharge, relevant dates, and the ages and nationalities of relevant participants, and satisfied Rule 8(a). The Court **reversed the Second Circuit and remanded for further proceedings**. It did not find unlawful discrimination or establish entitlement to damages. (534 U.S. at 510–515.)

Akos Swierkiewicz was a Hungarian native, fifty-three when he filed his complaint. He began working for Sorema, a New York reinsurance company principally owned and controlled by a French parent, in April 1989 as senior vice president and chief underwriting officer. Nearly six years later, chief executive François Chavel allegedly demoted him to marketing and services and assigned most of his underwriting work to Nicholas Papadopoulo, a thirty-two-year-old French national like Chavel. About a year later, Chavel said he wanted to energize the department and made Papadopoulo chief underwriting officer. Swierkiewicz alleged that Papadopoulo had only one year of underwriting experience, compared with his own twenty-six years in insurance. (Id. at 508.)

The complaint alleged subsequent isolation, exclusion from decisions and meetings, and unsuccessful attempts to discuss the problem. In April 1997, Swierkiewicz sent Chavel a memorandum explaining his grievances and requesting severance. Two weeks later, company counsel offered resignation without severance or dismissal. Chavel fired him when he refused to resign. Swierkiewicz sued for national-origin discrimination under Title VII and age discrimination under the ADEA. The Court accepted these allegations as true when reviewing dismissal, as judicial note 1 expressly explains; it did not treat them as findings after evidence. (Id. at 508–509 & n.1.)

The Southern District of New York dismissed for failure adequately to plead circumstances supporting an inference of discrimination. The Second Circuit affirmed under its precedent requiring factual allegations establishing the prima facie case: protected-group membership, job qualification, an adverse employment action, and circumstances supporting an inference of discrimination. The Supreme Court granted review to resolve the circuit conflict described in note 2. (Id. at 509–510 & n.2.)

Justice Thomas distinguished those evidentiary elements from a complaint’s function. McDonnell Douglas addresses the order and allocation of proof, and later decisions describe the prima facie case as raising an evidentiary inference. The Court had not made that showing a condition of pleading. At this stage, the question was whether the claimant could offer evidence in support of the claim, rather than whether he would ultimately prevail. (Id. at 510–511.)

Two features made transposing the framework especially inappropriate. Direct evidence can support a discrimination claim without proving every prima facie element; a plaintiff might obtain that evidence only in discovery. Requiring more at pleading than might eventually be necessary at trial would therefore be incongruous. Moreover, the prima facie formulation is flexible and context dependent, rather than rigid or ritualistic. Before discovery, the precise appropriate formulation may not even be apparent. (Id. at 511–512.)

The Court instead applied Rule 8(a)(2)’s short-and-plain-statement requirement and Conley’s fair-notice formulation. It emphasized discovery and summary judgment as mechanisms for identifying disputed issues and disposing of unsupported claims. Exceptions requiring specificity existed, but the Court would not judicially add employment discrimination to them. Rule 9(b) expressly addressed fraud and mistake, while neither employment discrimination nor municipal § 1983 liability was enumerated. Judicial note 3 reproduces the historical Rule 9(b) language; note 4 uses the then-existing official negligence form to illustrate the simple pleading contemplated by the rules. The opinion also invokes former Rules 8(e)(1), 8(f), and 84 in their historical forms. (Id. at 512–514 & nn.3–4.)

Swierkiewicz’s complaint met the standard. It identified the protected grounds of the alleged termination and supplied a narrative, dates, and comparative ages and nationalities. Those facts gave Sorema notice of both the claims and their grounds and described legally available relief under Title VII and the ADEA. The Court did not hold that an unexplained accusation must always suffice. If a pleading inadequately specifies its allegations, Rule 12(e) permits a more definite statement; Rule 56 can address claims lacking merit. (Id. at 514.)

Sorema argued that permitting conclusory discrimination allegations would burden courts and invite unsubstantiated suits. The Court responded that the Federal Rules contained no special heightened requirement for those claims. A new specificity requirement had to come through amending the rules, rather than judicial interpretation. Pleading sufficiency was distinct from the likelihood of ultimate recovery. On that reasoning, it reversed and remanded, leaving further proceedings and the factual merits open. (Id. at 514–515.)
