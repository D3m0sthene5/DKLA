# Hewitt v. Hewitt
77 Ill. 2d 49, 394 N.E.2d 1204 (1979) | Contracts

## Court and result
The Illinois Supreme Court decided this case on September 19, 1979. Justice Underwood delivered the court’s opinion; no separate writing or judicial footnote is reported in the complete reproduced opinion. The court reversed the appellate court and affirmed the Champaign County circuit court’s dismissal of Victoria Hewitt’s amended complaint. The decision concerns the enforceability of property claims arising from the pleaded relationship, rather than a finding after trial that a property-sharing promise was or was not made. (77 Ill. 2d at 49, 52–54, 66.)

## Relationship and alleged undertaking
Victoria alleged that she and Robert lived together from 1960 to 1975 in a family-like relationship and had three children. In June 1960, while both were students at Grinnell College in Iowa, she became pregnant. According to her account, Robert told her that they were husband and wife, that no formal ceremony was necessary, and that he would share his life, future, earnings, and property with her. They announced to their parents that they were married and thereafter represented themselves publicly as husband and wife. These are allegations or matters described in testimony, not findings resolving the existence and terms of the claimed contract. (Id. at 52–54.)

Victoria said that she devoted her efforts to Robert’s professional education and establishment in pedodontia, obtained financial assistance from her parents, and contributed her particular skills to his practice. Although she received payroll checks for that work, she put them into a common fund. She also performed homemaking, parenting, and social activities supporting his professional reputation. She alleged that he had originally possessed no funds but, through their efforts, came to earn more than $80,000 annually and to accumulate substantial property held either jointly or in his name alone. Her demand was for one-half of the profits and property accumulated during their relationship. (Id. at 52–54.)

## Claims and procedural course
Victoria initially sued for divorce. At the hearing on Robert’s motion to dismiss, she admitted that they had never obtained a marriage license or participated in a marriage ceremony. The circuit court found that no ceremonial or common-law marriage existed. Robert admitted paternity, so the court said she need not institute a separate Paternity Act proceeding to have child support determined; it directed greater specificity about the property she sought to divide. Child support was not at issue in the Supreme Court, and the opinion does not eliminate the children’s support rights. (Id. at 52–54.)

The amended complaint asserted four related grounds. Robert’s express promise and their joint efforts supposedly entitled Victoria to half the property in equity. Their conduct alternatively established an implied property-sharing contract. His allegedly fraudulent assurances that she was his wife supported imposing a trust over property acquired through her services. Finally, her reliance and lifelong contributions made retention of the resulting benefits unjust enrichment. The circuit court dismissed these claims on the ground that Illinois law and public policy required a valid marriage for the property relief sought. (Id. at 53–54.)

The appellate court reversed. It emphasized that the parties had lived an outwardly conventional and respectable family life for more than fifteen years, with the absence of valid marriage as the principal defect. Because that conduct did not openly offend public policy, it held that the complaint stated a claim on an express oral agreement. The Supreme Court granted leave to appeal and rejected that approach. The earlier appellate opinion is discussed here as the procedural and analytical position under review, rather than independently certified as a separately read complete judicial source. (Id. at 54–55.)

## Competing contract and policy positions
Victoria maintained that enforcement of an express bargain would not itself confer property rights simply because people cohabited. The appellate court had relied substantially on California’s Marvin decision, which recognized express contracts between unmarried partners except insofar as founded explicitly on sexual services and also contemplated implied agreements and appropriate equitable remedies. Underwood distinguished that broad contract approach from the Illinois appellate court’s apparent limitation to couples whose lives resembled conventional marriage: the California claimant, he suggested, might fail the latter test despite an otherwise enforceable bargain. (Id. at 55–57.)

More fundamentally, the court refused to regard the issue solely as ordinary contract enforcement between these litigants. Recognizing express agreements would likely carry with it implied-contract, constructive-trust, and other equitable claims for the more common relationships lacking an express bargain. The resulting litigation would require scrutiny of domestic arrangements. The court therefore viewed the requested change as a decision about the legal status of nonmarital relationships and its possible consequences for marriage, inheritance, support, children, and other incidents of family life. Its questions about those consequences explain its policy concerns; they are not holdings deciding each collateral right. (Id. at 56–58.)

## Illegality and the statutory marriage scheme
Illinois precedent and traditional contract authorities treated bargains for future illicit cohabitation or sexual intercourse as unenforceable. The court acknowledged that cohabitants could nevertheless make valid agreements about genuinely independent matters whose consideration did not include sexual relations. It also described other jurisdictions’ decisions treating pooled earnings or homemaking services as severable consideration. But it resisted using that characterization to recast the intimate, marriage-like arrangement alleged here as an ordinary arm’s-length bargain. Its result does not state that every commercial agreement between two people who happen to cohabit is void. (Id. at 58–61.)

Even assuming that the traditional rule should change, the court considered the legislature the institution suited to assess sociological information and policy alternatives. This was the institutional choice highlighted in the assigned quotation. The recently enacted Illinois Marriage and Dissolution of Marriage Act did not expressly address nonmarital cohabitation, but the court inferred a policy against the mutual property rights claimed from several features of the statutory scheme. (Id. at 60–61.)

First, the Act identified solemnization and registration, preservation of marriage, and protection of family relationships as its purposes. The court feared that property security resembling marriage could make a private alternative more attractive, including arrangements unavailable to married spouses. Second, the Act expressly reaffirmed the invalidity of Illinois common-law marriages contracted after June 30, 1905. The pleaded declarations of marital intent, assumption of the status, and sustained public representation would have established such a marriage under the former law. Giving the arrangement marriage-like property consequences threatened to restore indirectly what the legislature had abolished. The court found unpersuasive Victoria’s distinction between her requested one-half share and the different inheritance rights of an actual spouse. (Id. at 61–63.)

Third, the court read the legislature’s then-recent retention of fault grounds for dissolution, in contrast to California’s no-fault framework, as evidence of a strong state interest in marriage rather than a private relationship terminable at will. Fourth, the Act recognized a putative spouse only through a marriage ceremony and good-faith belief in a valid marriage, with that status ending upon knowledge of invalidity and common-law marriages excluded. The court inferred that this carefully limited protection, adopted while Marvin had received publicity, was inconsistent with broadly granting mutual property rights to knowingly unmarried cohabitants. These are the court’s interpretations of the 1977 scheme in its 1979 decision, not assertions about contemporary Illinois law. (Id. at 63–64.)

## Holding, limits, and disposition
The court expressly declined to suggest that Victoria’s position was entirely without merit. Nevertheless, it held her claims unenforceable because they conflicted with the public policy implicit in the statutory marriage scheme against mutually enforceable property rights for knowingly unmarried cohabitants. It reversed the appellate judgment and affirmed the circuit court. It neither awarded Robert damages nor remanded for a trial to determine an otherwise enforceable property-sharing agreement. (Id. at 64–66.)

The two assigned accounts use the decision for related purposes: the earlier restitution note illustrates a historical policy obstacle to relief between cohabitants, while the later public-policy discussion emphasizes the court’s preference for legislative assessment. The book’s descriptions of later approaches, Watts, and the Restatement are editorial comparisons; they do not form part of Hewitt’s holding. Nor does the opinion’s discussion of societal risks prove empirical findings about all unmarried households. The complete judgment’s focus is the pleaded marriage-like arrangement, the policy it inferred from the then-existing Illinois statutes, and the institutional source of any change.
