# Levin v. Halston Limited
91 Misc. 2d 601, 398 N.Y.S.2d 339 (1977) | Contracts

## Court and limited disposition
Judge Burton S. Sherman decided the defendant’s motion in the Civil Court of the City of New York, Special Term, New York County, on October 3, 1977. He dismissed the first cause of action, seeking mental-suffering damages, and directed judgment accordingly. The report does not dispose of the separately requested return of the purchaser’s deposit. This is a trial-court ruling on the sufficiency of that damages claim, not an appellate affirmance or a final finding that the dress had been properly made. (601–603.)

## Purchase and alleged failure
Mrs. Myron Levin of Highland Park, Illinois, wanted a custom-made Halston dress to wear at her daughter’s wedding. In February 1975 she visited the defendant’s New York showroom, selected a dress as a possibility, had measurements taken, and received fabric samples. At the end of April she ordered it and sent a $335 deposit. She alleged that the defendant lost her measurements, necessitating another flight to New York; she then returned a third time in June for a fitting. The dress arrived before June 20, as promised. (601.)

Her letter described an uneven hem, an ill-fitting neckline and capelet, poor packing, and dismay that such workmanship carried the designer’s label. She could not return to New York to have it remade with fewer than six weeks until the wedding. The dress apparently traveled back and forth between Illinois and New York; payment of the remaining $345 was stopped. She hastily obtained another dress for the occasion and never wore the disputed one. These are the purchaser’s allegations and account, considered favorably for this motion; the court did not adjudicate each claimed defect. (601–602.)

She sued for return of the deposit and mental anguish. Halston moved to dismiss the mental-anguish cause for insufficiency. Her personal letter and the importance of her daughter’s wedding made the claimed disappointment understandable to the judge. Sympathy and the genuine importance of the event nevertheless did not answer whether this contract claim supported that category of recovery. (602.)

## Rule and reasoning
The judge applied the general rule that mental suffering caused by breach of contract was not recoverable. He described the few exceptions recognized by the authorities he cited as arising in fiduciary or quasi-fiduciary contractual relationships, such as hotel and guest, common carrier and passenger, or amusement park and patron, coupled with tortious conduct. None applied to this transaction. The report does not hold that commissioning a personalized dress created a fiduciary relationship or establish tort liability for its alleged defects. (602.)

He also invoked Hadley’s limitation of contract damages to natural and probable consequences reasonably foreseeable at formation, concluding that the claimed mental suffering did not qualify here. The analysis is brief: it states that conclusion without identifying negotiations about an emotional-damages undertaking or making detailed findings about what the designer knew. It should not be expanded into an unsupported factual holding that the designer was unaware of the wedding, nor into a ruling that every foreseeable emotional reaction is compensable. (602.)

The resulting judgment dismissed the first cause for mental suffering as a matter of law. It did not direct repayment of the deposit, determine replacement-dress expenses, or impose a completed damages award on either side. The separate deposit request remains outside the disposition reported in this opinion. (602–603.)

The original instead identifies the purchaser as the intended wearer of the dress at her daughter’s wedding. The assigned dismissal shorthand refers to the emotional-distress cause; the actual report makes its limited scope clear. The original forum is the Civil Court, although the assignment abbreviates it as City Court.

The entire three-page original report, including the purchaser’s letter, judicial reasoning, and continuation of the disposition on page 603 before the next caption, was read in native text and all three images.
