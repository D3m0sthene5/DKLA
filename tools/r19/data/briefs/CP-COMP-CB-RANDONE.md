# Randone v. Appellate Department of the Superior Court of Sacramento County

**5 Cal. 3d 536, 96 Cal. Rptr. 709 (1971) — Supreme Court of California, August 26, 1971; opinion modified September 23, 1971 as printed.** Justice Tobriner wrote the unanimous opinion, joined by Chief Justice Wright and Justices McComb, Peters, Mosk, Burke, and Sullivan. Civil Procedure: prejudgment attachment, notice and hearing, and necessities of life. Assigned: CivPro PDF 601–602, printed 1189, Attachment Note 1.

## Concrete facts and procedural path

A collection agency sued Joseph and his wife Randone, individually and doing business as Randone Trucking, on February 16, 1970. It alleged that it held an assigned debt for $490 in legal services, plus $130 interest. On March 17, it obtained a writ of attachment from the municipal court clerk and levied on the Randones’ checking account. The bank withheld the account’s $176.20 pending an order releasing the attachment. No judicial determination of the alleged debt preceded that loss of access. **541–542.**

On March 31, the Randones moved to dissolve the attachment as a deprivation of property without due process. Their affidavit stated that unemployment insurance was their sole source of income and sought an expedited hearing because of the hardship. The municipal court heard the motion on April 3 and denied it. The superior court’s appellate department affirmed without a written opinion on October 29 and refused certification to the Court of Appeal on November 5. **542.**

Having exhausted that appellate route, the Randones sought an original writ in the California Supreme Court. The court issued an alternative writ because the constitutional question was important, lower courts disagreed, and municipal-court proceedings could otherwise evade its review without certification. Exercising original jurisdiction necessarily entailed the conclusion that an adequate ordinary remedy was lacking. The question was whether maintaining this attachment constituted an abuse of discretion. **542–543.**

## The challenged statutory procedure

The court examined Code of Civil Procedure § 537, subdivision 1, rather than every conceivable attachment mechanism. The provision generally authorized attachment in qualifying unsecured express or implied contract actions for direct payment of money. Once the plaintiff filed the required declaration and undertaking, a clerk could issue the writ without judicial scrutiny, prior notice, or an opportunity for the debtor to contest it. The creditor selected the property. With earnings excluded by a 1970 amendment, the scheme still reached almost every other kind of property. **540–541 n.1, 543–544.**

The levy deprived a debtor of use even if the property, as with a bank balance, stayed physically where it was. Restraint could last three years unless released earlier. Debtors could post security, prevail in the suit, or seek an exemption, but those remedies put the initiative and burden on them. Exemption proceedings could entail substantial delay during which necessities remained unavailable. **544–547 & nn.4–7.**

This was the central practical defect: a creditor’s unadjudicated contract claim could immediately remove assets needed for living and litigating, even where no extraordinary danger justified acting before hearing the debtor.

## Parties’ arguments and issue

The agency argued that *Sniadach v. Family Finance Corp.* concerned the distinctive problem of wage garnishment, which this statute no longer allowed. It also contended that general attachment imposed lesser harm and served important interests in collection, credit availability, and preventing debtors from hiding or removing assets. The Randones relied on the broader due-process principle that significant property deprivations ordinarily require notice and a meaningful prior opportunity to be heard. **546–548, 555–557.**

The court had to decide both whether that principle extended beyond wages and whether the statute confined summary seizures to conditions sufficiently exceptional to justify them. It separately considered the especially severe consequences of attaching necessities.

## Sniadach expressed a general due-process principle

The court rejected the wage-only reading. Its recent *Blair v. Pitchess* decision had already applied the same reasoning to prejudgment claim and delivery of personal property. Federal decisions concerning welfare termination and access to judicial process reinforced the general requirement of an opportunity to be heard before a significant deprivation, subject to exceptional countervailing interests. California’s own notice-and-hearing decisions likewise formed a broader tradition. **548–551.**

The critical property interest was use during the period before judgment. Describing attachment as temporary did not eliminate that interest or the hardship caused by its loss. Earlier attachment authority had underestimated those effects. The potentially lengthy restraint here was not an insignificant interruption that could be dismissed merely because title had not finally changed. The court also explained why the older unexplained *McKay v. McInnes* affirmance did not overcome the principles developed in *Sniadach*. **551–552 & nn.13–14.**

## Ordinary collection interests did not establish an emergency

The court acknowledged that summary action can be justified. Banking and public-health precedents involved urgent public protection, officials charged with public responsibilities, and statutes tailored to an immediate need. Historical nonresident attachment also served a jurisdictional function under the then-prevailing jurisdictional framework, with potentially less impact on daily necessities. These examples illustrated relevant considerations, rather than demonstrating that every private creditor’s interest was equivalent to a public emergency. **552–555.**

Section 537(1) lacked that tailoring. It made security available broadly in contract litigation without proof that delay would frustrate collection. The agency’s claim that ordinary attachment was essential to affordable, widely available credit was unsupported on the record. Even accepting a possible effect on credit practices did not establish an overriding public interest or justify sacrificing debtors’ constitutional process protections. **555–556.**

An actual danger of flight or concealment could support a carefully limited summary remedy. But this statute required no particularized facts demonstrating such danger, and none had been shown here. Bank deposits’ general mobility likewise did not establish an actual emergency for every account holder. **556–557 & n.20.**

## Necessities created an additional constitutional defect

The statute’s breadth also allowed attachment of property essential to daily life. Deposited earnings and bank funds needed by an unemployed family could be as vital as wages still held by an employer. Household essentials, tools, and other assets central to subsistence could produce comparable hardship. Creditor control over which property to seize allowed selection of assets that would exert the greatest settlement pressure. **557–562.**

Such deprivation could undermine the promised later hearing itself. A debtor who could not support the family or conduct ordinary affairs might have to settle even a meritorious defense. The court therefore concluded that a creditor’s private security interest, including concern about concealment, did not justify taking necessities before a hearing establishing actual validity of the claim. Post-attachment exemptions did not cure the problem: unfamiliarity with remedies, expense, and delay could leave the debtor without essentials in the meantime. The court required initial exemption of necessities rather than reliance solely on later release. **561–563.**

The discussion did not equate every property restraint with that level of hardship. Note 4 recognized that a real-estate attachment ordinarily imposed a lien without removing use and reserved the constitutionality of a provision confined to restraints that did not significantly impair use. **544–545 n.4.** The distinction matters when explaining why this broadly drafted statute failed.

## Holding, permissible alternatives, and judgment

The court held § 537(1) facially unconstitutional under the cited state and federal due-process provisions because it broadly allowed seizure without notice and hearing, lacked limits tied to extraordinary need, and permitted deprivation of necessities. It declined to undertake the wholesale statutory rewriting necessary to cure those defects. **557, 563.**

It nevertheless contemplated a valid legislative scheme protecting necessities, ordinarily permitting attachment after notice and a hearing on probable validity, and allowing carefully safeguarded exceptions where a creditor demonstrated an actual concealment or flight risk before a magistrate. In exceptional pre-hearing cases, prompt opportunity to challenge the creditor’s allegations and recover the property would remain necessary. Those observations describe possible constitutional alternatives; they are not judicial enactment of a replacement statute. **563 & n.29.**

The court ordered a **peremptory writ of mandamus directing the appellate department to order the trial court to dissolve the challenged attachment**. It did not decide whether the Randones owed the underlying legal-services debt. All seven Justices concurred; there were no separate opinions. The report records the September 23 modification after the mandate paragraph and concurrence listing. **564.**

## Reservations and assigned context

Note 21 addressed concerns about competing-creditor priorities in pending bankruptcies. The court did not see an inherent constitutional impediment to using an attachment date to determine priority, but left definitive adjudication of those issues to federal bankruptcy proceedings. Invalidity of this summary deprivation procedure was not a blanket resolution of every collateral bankruptcy consequence. **557 n.21.** Note 28 similarly declined a comprehensive assessment of existing exemption statutes, although the attached $176.20 apparently would not qualify on the present record even if it was the family’s only support. **563 n.28.**

The textbook’s short historical account correctly attributes invalidation of the summary statute to *Randone*. Its ensuing description of later attachment legislation and the separate *Lydig* offset dispute is editorial context, not part of this 1971 holding. The brief preserves the decision’s historical analysis and does not independently certify present California attachment law.
