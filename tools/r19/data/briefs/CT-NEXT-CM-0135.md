# Iron Trade Products Co. v. Wilkoff Co.
272 Pa. 172, 116 A. 150 (1922) | Contracts

## Rails contract and alleged interference with supply
In July 1919, Iron Trade contracted to buy 2,600 tons of section relaying rails from Wilkoff for $41 per ton, delivered in New York harbor at specified times. Wilkoff delivered none. Iron Trade alleged that it obtained replacement rails elsewhere: 2,000 tons at $49.20 and 600 tons at $49, below an approximately $50 market price. It claimed the excess replacement expenditure over the contract price. 272 Pa. at 174.

Wilkoff’s affidavit of defense alleged a limited available supply, obtainable in sufficient quantities only from sources in Georgia and West Virginia. While Wilkoff negotiated with them, Iron Trade announced an urgent desire to buy similar rails, bought 887 tons, and agreed to purchase substantially more from the same sources. Wilkoff asserted that these purchases depleted availability, raised prices exorbitantly, and made its performance impossible. The trial court found the affidavit and supplement insufficient and entered judgment for Iron Trade. Wilkoff appealed that procedural judgment, rather than an adverse verdict after trial of these allegations. Id. at 173–75.

## Greater expense was not wrongful prevention
The affidavit did not allege that Iron Trade knew supply was limited or intended to impede Wilkoff. No agreement restricted Iron Trade’s other purchases or required Wilkoff to obtain rails from a particular source. Nor was there an allegation that the other purchases exhausted the supply. Competition could increase difficulty and expense without legally preventing delivery; mere difficulty did not excuse breach. Id. at 174–75.

Wilkoff invoked United States v. Peck, in which the government’s conduct prevented the other party’s performance. The court distinguished that transaction: the parties understood that specified government-owned meadows were the sole source of the contracted hay, and the government had others cut all of it. Here neither an agreed exclusive source nor exhaustion was alleged. The prevention principle remained valid, but Wilkoff’s allegations did not place this bargain within it. Id. at 175.

## The affidavit admitted a market despite its general denial
Wilkoff also denied any market or current market price. Read as a whole, however, the affidavit described trade centers, rails available in the market, negotiations at ascertainable prices, and actual purchases at higher prices. Iron Trade had in fact obtained the full quantity. Those admissions defeated the general denial and did not sufficiently answer the complaint’s specific market-price allegation. A contradictory or equivocal affidavit could not prevent judgment merely by adding a conclusory denial. Id. at 175–77.

## Replacement cost and a collateral resale
The supplement alleged that Iron Trade had resold the rails for $42.25 per ton with Wilkoff’s knowledge and that the downstream buyer released Iron Trade from damage liability. Wilkoff argued that recovery should therefore be limited to the expected resale profit. The court rejected that restriction. Under section 67(3) of the 1915 Sales Act, ordinary nondelivery damages were the market/contract difference at the time and place of delivery, absent special circumstances supporting greater proximate loss. Where the buyer actually obtained substitute goods below market, recovery was confined to the actual excess expenditure over the contract price. Id. at 177.

Iron Trade sought that smaller replacement differential. Its intended resale did not define damages because the downstream buyer and resale were not made part of this contract, and other equivalent rails could fulfill the downstream obligation. The cases using resale price in the absence of an available market did not control. Knowledge of a resale did not automatically reduce the buyer’s benefit of its own bargain or inject its collateral settlement into this dispute. Id. at 177–78.

## Disposition and reading scope
The Supreme Court overruled the assignments and affirmed judgment for Iron Trade. Walling wrote; Moschzisker, Frazer, Walling, Kephart, Sadler, and Schaefer are listed before the case; no separate writing or numerical vote is reported. Id. at 173, 178.
