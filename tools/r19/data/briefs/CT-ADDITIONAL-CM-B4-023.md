# Kenford Company, Inc. v. County of Erie — 1986 lost-profits decision
67 N.Y.2d 257, 493 N.E.2d 234 (1986) | Contracts

## Court and precise result
The New York Court of Appeals decided this appeal on May 6, 1986, in a per curiam opinion. Chief Judge Wachtler and Judges Meyer, Alexander, Titone, and Kane concurred; Kane sat by constitutional designation. Judges Simons, Kaye, and Hancock, Jr., took no part. The court affirmed, with costs, the portion of the Appellate Division’s order dismissing Dome Stadium, Inc.’s claim for twenty years of prospective operating profits. Its review concerned that particular damages claim, not every other item in the litigation or a new decision on the county’s already-established liability. This is the 1986 judgment, distinct from the separately briefed 1989 Kenford stage. (257, 260–261, 263 & designation star.)

## The stadium agreement and procedural history
On August 8, 1969, Erie County contracted with Kenford and Dome Stadium, Inc. for a domed stadium near Buffalo. County construction was to begin within twelve months. The parties would negotiate a mutually acceptable forty-year operating lease within three months after the county received preliminary plans, drawings, and cost estimates. If no acceptable lease emerged, an appended management agreement would provide DSI with twenty years of operation after completion and availability of the stadium. (260.)

Extensive negotiations produced neither a lease nor construction, within the first year or afterward. Kenford and DSI sued in June 1971. Roughly a decade of preliminary proceedings culminated in an affirmed summary judgment establishing county liability and directing a damages trial. That trial lasted about nine months and produced a multimillion-dollar plaintiffs’ verdict. The Appellate Division reversed lost-profit and certain out-of-pocket awards and directed a new trial on other issues. Only dismissal of DSI’s prospective profits under the appended twenty-year management contract was before the Court of Appeals in this appeal. The assigned account identifies that operating-profit award as $25.6 million; the actual opinion describes its scope without printing that particular figure. (260–261; assigned 1033.)

## Parties’ positions and appellate issue
DSI maintained that recognized experts, comparative operating data, and a comprehensive local market study supplied a rational and legally sufficient basis for forecasting profits. It argued that the Appellate Division had misread the evidence and displaced the jury’s role. The county sought affirmance of the lost-profit dismissal. Its reported counsel points also attacked the earlier liability determination, but the Court of Appeals confined its decision to the damages portion actually under review. Counsel’s reported arguments must therefore be distinguished from holdings in the per curiam opinion. (258–261.)

The Appellate Division had considered the projections to depend on too many variables to produce a rational calculation. The Court of Appeals agreed that recovery failed, but explained its own two grounds: insufficient proof that the parties contemplated county liability for the claimed twenty-year profits and insufficient reasonable certainty in this particular projection. (261–262.)

## Lost-profit requirements
New York allowed prospective profits when the claimant proved that breach caused the loss, that the loss was reasonably certain and directly traceable rather than speculative or attributable to intervening causes, and that the particular damages were fairly within the parties’ contemplation when contracting. A new enterprise faced stricter scrutiny because it lacked an operating history from which to estimate profits. The court did not announce an absolute prohibition on new-business profits or require mathematical certainty. (261–262.)

The opinion acknowledged the considerable sophistication of DSI’s proof. Its experts used contemporary economic methods, data from other domed stadiums and related facilities, and a detailed Buffalo-area market study. The court accepted that such methods had been recognized in New York and elsewhere and found it difficult to identify additional relevant proof DSI could have supplied. That favorable assessment of the method did not establish that these inputs could reliably prove this loss. (261–262.)

## Why both contemplation and certainty failed
The contract’s default remedies did not suggest the heavy responsibility of guaranteeing the operator’s profits for the entire twenty-year term. In the absence of a provision addressing such liability, the court asked what the parties would reasonably have concluded had they considered the issue. The evidence did not establish contemplation of that liability at the relevant times. The opinion’s application refers both to execution of the basic agreement and to breach, while its general rule locates contemplation at contracting. The decision does not say that identifying a twenty-year management term, standing alone, necessarily establishes consent to every projected profit throughout that term. (261–262.)

The forecasts also assumed successful completion, availability, and operation for twenty years, hosting professional sports, entertainment, meetings, and conventions. At breach, only the Houston Astrodome offered a comparable facility in the country. The many assumptions about long-term success, economic conditions, popular demand, and public support for professional athletics undermined the model’s foundations. Sophisticated procedures could not eliminate the speculative nature of those assumptions. The objection was to the certainty of this twenty-year entertainment venture, not the mere existence of any adjustable projection. (262–263.)

The court adhered to its stricter reasonable-certainty authorities and expressly rejected the “rational basis” test stated in the federal Perma Research decision and adopted below. It consequently affirmed the dismissal of this damages cause rather than ordering another jury to estimate the same operating profits. Other issues affected by the Appellate Division’s order remained outside this limited affirmance. (263.)
