# Oblix, Inc. v. Winiecki
374 F.3d 488 (7th Cir. 2004) | Contracts

## Court and procedural stage
The Seventh Circuit decided this appeal on June 30, 2004. Judge Easterbrook wrote the opinion for the panel of Easterbrook, Diane P. Wood, and Williams; the complete report contains no dissent, concurrence, or judicial footnotes. The court reversed the refusal to compel arbitration and remanded with instructions to refer the parties to arbitration and dismiss Winiecki's counterclaim. It decided the forum for her employment claims, rather than the merits of those claims. (488–489, 492.)

Felicia Winiecki began working for Oblix in September 2000 and signed an employment agreement requiring arbitration. After her April 2002 termination, she asserted unpaid compensation and claimed that her discharge and other conduct violated Title VII. Oblix brought a declaratory action to require arbitration; she counterclaimed for substantive relief. Her counterclaim made it unnecessary to decide whether the employer's original declaratory suit had been premature. Oblix had its principal place of business in California, and the agreement selected California law. The federal district court in Illinois refused to compel arbitration because it believed further discovery and litigation were needed to determine unconscionability. (489.)

## Why the interlocutory appeal was timely
The district court initially denied arbitration on April 23, 2003. It invited reconsideration to address the Supreme Court's recently issued PacifiCare decision. Oblix moved on May 19, the district court again refused arbitration on July 1, and Oblix appealed on July 3. Winiecki argued that the thirty-day appeal period ran from April 23 and that the motion for reconsideration did not qualify as a tolling Rule 59 motion. The Seventh Circuit agreed with the latter point: there was no judgment to alter, and the motion also fell outside the then-applicable ten-day period. The appeal nevertheless was timely because the July 1 refusal independently qualified for interlocutory appeal under the FAA. The court did not hold that any late reconsideration request restarts the appeal clock. (489–490.)

Existing circuit precedent also treated refusal to direct arbitration while conducting arbitrability discovery as an immediately appealable denial of arbitration. Neither party asked the panel to reconsider that precedent. Accordingly, the district court's suggestion that more discovery was required did not prevent appellate review. (489.)

## Agreement scope and the distinction from delegation
Winiecki could defend the favorable order with properly preserved arguments without filing a cross-appeal. She argued that her compensation and discrimination claims fell outside the agreement, but the actual clauses contradicted that position. One specifically covered disputes over salary, severance, and similar amounts; another required binding AAA arbitration of disputes relating to interpretation, construction, performance, or breach of the employment agreement. The former included her compensation demand, and the latter encompassed her discriminatory-discharge contention. (490.)

The court expressly distinguished broad substantive coverage from delegation of the threshold arbitrability question. The wording was broad enough to cover these employment disputes, but not broad enough to compel the conclusion that arbitrability itself had been committed to an arbitrator. The panel consequently evaluated scope and the asserted unconscionability objection rather than presuming a delegation. (490.)

## Consideration, adhesion, and asymmetry
The agreement was offered on a take-it-or-leave-it basis and did not require Oblix to arbitrate every potential claim it might have against the employee. For example, the employer could seek a judicial forum for an alleged taking of trade secrets. The district court regarded these circumstances as possible unconscionability. The panel rejected the proposition that salary supplied consideration for work but not for an accompanying arbitration promise. Winiecki did not dispute that her salary supported the arbitration obligation: the employment exchange purchased several promises together, including this means of resolving disputes. A matching employer promise to arbitrate every claim was not necessary to provide consideration for her promise. (490–491.)

The court compared the arbitration term to a nonnegotiable salary offer, explaining that unwillingness to negotiate did not itself entitle the employee to substitute more favorable terms after accepting the package. Its reasoning was directed to the asserted objection to a standard-form bargain and one-sided arbitration coverage, rather than an adjudication that every conceivable employment term is valid whenever salary is paid. (491.)

The panel also invoked Prima Paint's distinction between an attack on the whole agreement and an attack directed to the arbitration provision. A generalized dislike of standard-form contracting attacked the entire bargain and could itself be sent to arbitration. Nevertheless, the panel addressed and rejected the unconscionability argument rather than stopping at that route. It cited commercial and labor arbitration practices and empirical work on employee arbitration outcomes as additional support. Those observations were the court's stated rationale; they did not resolve the truth of Winiecki's discrimination or compensation allegations. (491.)

## FAA equal treatment and the California objection
The panel applied section 2 of the FAA to prevent states from imposing special burdens on arbitration clauses that they did not impose on other provisions in form contracts. It noted California's enforcement of other standard terms, such as releases and indemnity provisions. Winiecki read Armendariz to require additional protections for employment arbitration, and the district judge had relied on that decision in ordering further inquiry. The Seventh Circuit observed that another federal appellate court read Armendariz differently, but treated the competing readings as ultimately immaterial to the federal prohibition on special disfavor of arbitration. Under ordinary contract rules, it held this agreement enforceable. The arbitral forum could entertain her remaining arguments. (491–492.)

The reported opinion therefore addresses consideration, a general adhesion objection, and the employee's asserted arbitration-specific requirements. It does not establish that ordinary state contract defenses disappear whenever the FAA applies, nor does it finally dispose of the underlying employee claims. Its precise command was arbitration and dismissal of her federal counterclaim. (492.)
