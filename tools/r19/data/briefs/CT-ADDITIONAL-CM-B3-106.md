# Chatlos Systems, Inc. v. National Cash Register Corp.
670 F.2d 1304 (3d Cir. 1982) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural setting
The Third Circuit affirmed a $201,826.50 warranty-damages award plus prejudgment interest on January 15, 1982. The per curiam majority consisted of Judges Aldisert and Weis; Judge Rosenn dissented. Rehearing was denied February 11, with Adams, Hunter, and Garth favoring rehearing and joining Adams’s written dissent from its denial. The report also records that certiorari was dismissed June 9, rather than denied. The issue on this appeal was valuation and interest after remand, not a new determination of warranty liability. (1304–1305, 1307, 1315–1316.)

## Transaction, performance, and earlier proceedings
Chatlos acquired an NCR 399/656 disc computer system intended to perform specified business functions. As described in Rosenn’s factual account, the July 1974 transaction priced the hardware at $40,165 and six programs at $5,855, totaling $46,020 before tax. Those programs covered accounts receivable, payroll, order entry, inventory deletion, state income tax, and cash receipts, with installation included. The computer was delivered in December 1974, and payroll became operational in March 1975. NCR remained unsuccessful with order entry and inventory deletion by March 1976. Chatlos demanded termination and removal on September 1, 1976. Footnote 3 explains the financing: NCR sold the system to a bank for $46,020 in July 1975, and Chatlos leased it from the bank under an arrangement involving finance charges. August 1975 was treated as the acceptance date. (1307–1308 & dissent nn. 2–5.)

Chatlos filed in New Jersey state court; NCR removed the diversity case to federal court. After a bench trial, the district judge found breach of warranty and initially awarded $57,152.76 for breach and $63,558.16 in consequential damages. On the 1980 appeal, the Third Circuit affirmed liability, set aside consequential damages, and remanded for recalculation of warranty damages. Those consequential damages were therefore outside the present appeal. On remand, the court valued the warranted system at $207,826.50 and the delivered goods at $6,000, producing $201,826.50 in damages. It added prejudgment interest; Rosenn’s account specifies eight percent from August 1975. (1305 & n. 2; 1308 & dissent nn. 4–5.)

## Parties’ valuation evidence and arguments
The applicable New Jersey enactment of UCC § 2-714(2) measured the difference, at acceptance, between the accepted goods’ value and the value they would have had as warranted, subject to special circumstances. NCR argued that the $46,020 contract price was the proper evidence of warranted value and that Chatlos’s expert had valued fundamentally more expensive equipment. It chose not to introduce additional valuation evidence on remand and offered no expert valuation of a system capable of performing everything NCR had warranted. (1305–1306 & nn. 1–3.)

Chatlos’s expert, Dick Brandon, estimated the value of a system performing the promised functions without limiting his analysis to the delivered NCR model or a single manufacturer. He acknowledged that the comparable functional systems were outside the delivered system’s price range. NCR characterized that approach as replacing a Ford with a Rolls-Royce. The district judge nevertheless credited Brandon, and testimony from NCR’s own witnesses provided some corroboration. Programmer Frank Hicks had advised that current software could not deliver the required program without rewriting or a different system. He recommended an NCR 8200, but was told it would not be supplied. Gerald Greenstein acknowledged that the 8200 was two levels above the 399 in sophistication and price, supporting Brandon’s $100,000–$150,000 hardware range. (1306–1307.)

## Majority’s rule and application
The majority treated the bargain as a computer system with specified capabilities, rather than merely a named model irrespective of performance. Contract price was relevant evidence of market value but not controlling. Section 2-714(2) could preserve a particularly favorable bargain even without an intervening market-price increase. Valuing equipment able to perform the warranted functions therefore did not necessarily confer an improper luxury upgrade. The majority preferred an analogy to equipment promised to lift one thousand pounds but able to lift only five hundred: the cost of equipment capable of lifting the promised load could illuminate warranted value. (1306 & n. 4.)

The parties’ conflicting valuation positions required the district judge to assess credibility and weight. The appellate court could have reached a different valuation as factfinder but could not replace the trial court’s findings merely on that basis. Brandon was well qualified, and the majority regarded the dissent’s objections primarily as disagreements about his estimates. Considering the evidence and corroboration, it found no clear error. It also held, briefly and by reference to the prior appeal, that awarding prejudgment interest lay within the district court’s discretion. The judgment was affirmed without reducing damages or excluding that interest. (1307 & n. 5.)

## Rosenn’s dissent
Rosenn viewed the problem as a legal misapplication of the statute as well as inadequate proof. In his reading, the focus had to remain the goods actually accepted in their warranted condition, not hypothetical goods capable of equivalent functions but possessing superior quality or technological power. He would review the legal question independently; even under clear-error review he considered the valuation unsupported. He distinguished the measure for accepted goods from cover damages, noting that Chatlos had not undertaken a proved cover transaction and that its IBM purchase was not intended at purchase to replace the NCR system. (1308–1310 & dissent nn. 6–9.)

He challenged Brandon’s estimates as speculative: they assembled unspecified packages from multiple vendors, worked backward from later figures through inflation adjustments, and did not value an operable NCR 399 itself. He also pointed to inconsistent testimony about including training time, the efficiencies of an integrated system, the hierarchy of computer equipment, and evidence of similar-size computers in the original price range. In his view, a higher-tier machine’s ability to perform the promised functions did not establish the warranted value of this particular lower-tier bargain. His authorities and analogies permitted recovery of proved market value exceeding price, but not a better product’s hypothetical price without adequate comparability. (1310–1314 & dissent nn. 9–12.)

Rosenn further would credit $1,000 for the usable payroll program, in addition to the retained hardware’s $6,000 value. With $46,020 as warranted value, his proposed award was $39,020. He also opposed prejudgment interest on these unliquidated, substantially disputed damages absent a showing that justice and fair dealing demanded it, emphasizing findings that NCR had not acted fraudulently or in bad faith. He would reverse and direct that smaller judgment with interest from entry of the initial judgment. Those proposed adjustments were not the court’s disposition. (1314–1315.)

## Rehearing and assigned significance
The rehearing order records that no judge concurring in the panel decision requested rehearing and that a majority of active judges did not vote for rehearing in banc. Judge Gibbons did not participate. Adams, joined by Hunter and Garth, considered full-court review warranted because the UCC valuation question extended beyond ordinary state-law error, could be harder for a single state court to correct nationally, and exposed commercial transactions to substantial obligations. Rehearing was nonetheless denied, leaving the panel’s affirmance intact. (1315–1316 & rehearing star note.)
