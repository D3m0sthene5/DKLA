# Rizzolo v. Poysher
89 N.J.L. 618, 99 A. 390 (1916) | Contracts

## Court, issues, and result

On November 20, 1916, New Jersey's Court of Errors and Appeals reversed the Supreme Court's intermediate judgment and affirmed the Circuit Court judgment for contractor Saverio Rizzolo. Justice Parker delivered the opinion; eleven members voted for reversal and none for affirmance. The report's heading and voting line identify the deciding tribunal, despite database metadata that describes it as the Supreme Court. Its issues included sufficiency of the mechanics' lien particulars, pleading amendments, and wrongful refusal of the architect's certificate required for final payment. (89 N.J.L. at 618, 620–626.)

## Contract, lien, and intermediate reversal

Rizzolo asserted performance of an October 29, 1912 building contract and separately agreed extras. His claim included the $1,500 final payment and specified additional work and materials, with a stated aggregate claim of $2,056. He sued Poysher as builder and Alfred Stahl as owner. The Circuit Court entered a general judgment against the builder and a special judgment against the owner. On intermediate appeal, the Supreme Court sustained the builder judgment but reversed the special property judgment; the former was not appealed to the Court of Errors and Appeals. The report reproduces the Supreme Court's per curiam explanation before Parker's opinion. (Id. at 618–620, 623.)

At the close of the plaintiff's case, the owner had moved to nonsuit for inadequate lien particulars. The trial judge permitted amendments to six extra-work items stating that they arose by agreement in February or March 1913. Other items, including the final payment, were not amended. The intermediate court reasoned that a deficient bill of particulars could not support a lien as to only part, so the owner was entitled to nonsuit. That conclusion was the principal appellate issue. (Id. at 618–620.)

## Valid items could sustain the lien

Section 16 of the Mechanics' Lien Act required description of the property, identification of owner and builder, and particulars of labor, materials, price, times, credits and balance. For contract work, a general statement of the work and agreed price could suffice. The claim had to be timely, properly filed and sworn, and a willful or fraudulent misstatement could defeat it. Parker found evidence of timely filing in the required form. (Id. at 620–621.)

Neither the statute nor the precedents established that one insufficiently stated item necessarily invalidated all others. Older strict-construction decisions involved bills with no supportable items; another decision had allowed a claim to prove valid work despite an improper item. More recent cases favored amendments and effectuation of the statute's purposes without injustice. A claim containing good and bad items could stand for the good ones unless they were inseparably blended. No willful or fraudulent misstatement was at issue here. (Id. at 621–622.)

Reading the amended claim as a whole, the court found sufficiently specified contracted extras to the main building contract. It treated the original and incidental extra work as a single complete undertaking, claimed completed April 29, 1913, with filing June 26 and suit July 1 within the relevant period. At least some items therefore belonged before the jury, defeating the motion to nonsuit the whole claim. Because the other items had only been challenged through that motion, whether each was independently recoverable was immaterial to this appeal. (Id. at 622–624.)

## Pleading and party amendments

The defendants also had asserted failure to reply to their defense that no architect's certificate was obtained. A preliminary nonsuit motion was denied without exception. At trial the plaintiff sought to add allegations of architect fraud and waiver of the written-authority requirement, although no reply then existed to amend. The motion's practical purpose was clear: to put the record in shape for the fraud issue actually tried. The defendants neither excepted to the allowed amendment nor demanded perfection of the record before proceeding. The Supreme Court later permitted the reply to be filed. No properly presented injurious error remained. (Id. at 624.)

Another amendment corrected the original description of both defendants as both builders and owners, limiting Poysher to builder and Stahl to owner. It reduced rather than enlarged their claimed liabilities and injured no outside party. The court expressly did not decide whether an amendment could enlarge liability after the filing period expired. An objection to the title of the amendment order likewise concerned form, and collateral attack required lack of jurisdiction. (Id. at 624–625.)

## Wrongful refusal of the certificate

The fraud question went to the jury under instructions to which no exception was taken. The architect's own testimony permitted an inference that he was ready to certify, but the defendants wanted several hundred dollars withheld on a counterclaim he largely would not support. He told the contractor to pursue the money, yet refused certification when asked the day before suit because he did not want to appear to issue a certificate for litigation. The court regarded that reason as insufficient and his refusal, if his testimony was true, as fraudulent in the sense relevant to excusing the certificate condition. (Id. at 625.)

Participation by the owner in that wrongful refusal was unnecessary. Otherwise a corrupt architect could use withholding of an earned certificate to extract money from the contractor. This was not a jury finding of a separate extortion payment; it was the court's explanation of why the proposed owner-participation requirement was unjust. The holding prevented the certificate condition from defeating recovery where the architect wrongfully withheld it on the evidence presented. (Id.)

## Remaining objections and final judgment

The extra-work writing objection failed under the rule of Headley v. Cavileer on the evidence; the court did not reproduce an extensive new modification analysis. Other arguments about building regulations and completion dates identified no trial ruling, and the timeliness objection involved factual evidence that completion occurred as late as April 26. The court found no injurious evidentiary error and could not review the claim that the verdict contradicted the charge through this appeal. (Id. at 625–626.)

The time-of-the-essence argument had not adequately been presented by the general directed-verdict motion. Even assuming preservation, the defendant had accepted the building and could claim at most delay damages, which apparently had not been pleaded. The court therefore reversed the intermediate Supreme Court judgment and affirmed the Circuit Court judgment, restoring the challenged owner's judgment; it did not order a new trial. (Id. at 626.)
