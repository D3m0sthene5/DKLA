# Rodriguez v. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation

**140 S. Ct. 713 (2020), subsequently reported at 589 U.S. 132. Supreme Court of the United States, February 25, 2020. Civil Procedure.** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, physical PDF page 288, printed page 540, Note 5. Judicial pinpoints here use the court-issued opinion’s six numbered opinion pages; the two preceding syllabus pages are separately numbered.

## The disputed refund

United Western Bank encountered financial trouble and entered receivership, with the FDIC acting as receiver. Its parent, United Western Bancorp, then entered bankruptcy, with Simon E. Rodriguez acting as chapter 7 trustee. When the IRS issued a $4 million refund for the affiliated corporate group, the bank’s receiver and the parent’s trustee each claimed the money. The parties could not resolve ownership, and the dispute proceeded through bankruptcy and district courts to the Tenth Circuit. That court ruled for the FDIC. The Supreme Court’s question was which legal framework could be used to resolve the dispute, rather than which claimant ultimately owned the refund. (Slip opinion 1–2.)

Federal law allows affiliated corporations to file a consolidated tax return. The regulations address their tax liability, designate an agent to receive a refund, and discharge the government’s refund obligation when it pays that agent. They say considerably less about subsequent distribution among members of the group. Corporate groups often address that problem through tax-allocation agreements. When agreements are absent or contested, state law normally supplies tools of contract interpretation, equitable trusts, and unjust enrichment. (2.)

Some federal courts instead developed the Bob Richards rule, named for a 1973 Ninth Circuit decision. Initially, the rule gave a refund to the member whose losses generated it when the parties lacked an allocation agreement. In some circuits it later became a stronger default: even when an agreement existed, the loss-generating member received the refund unless the agreement unambiguously required a different result. (3.)

The parties here had an agreement. At the FDIC’s urging, and following its circuit precedent, the Tenth Circuit applied that more expansive federal default. It asked whether the agreement unambiguously departed from Bob Richards and concluded that the subsidiary bank, through the FDIC, owned the money. Other circuits had rejected the premise that federal common law could legitimately supply such a rule, prompting Supreme Court review. (3–4.)

## The holding and its reasoning

Justice Gorsuch wrote for a unanimous Court that **Bob Richards was not a legitimate exercise of federal common lawmaking**. The Court **vacated and remanded** the Tenth Circuit’s judgment. It did not award the $4 million to Rodriguez, invalidate every possible allocation in the FDIC’s favor, or resolve interpretation of the parties’ agreement. (4–6.)

The reasoning began with institutional limits. The Constitution assigns federal legislative power to Congress and reserves most other regulatory authority to the States. Erie’s rejection of federal general common law therefore remains the baseline. Federal judges can make rules in limited recognized areas, such as admiralty or certain interstate disputes, but cannot create a new enclave merely because a dispute is in federal court. Absent congressional authorization, protecting uniquely federal interests is a threshold requirement. (4.)

There were federal interests in receiving the appropriate taxes and delivering a refund without exposing the Treasury to further claims from other group members. But once the refund was paid to the designated agent, no uniquely federal interest had been identified in deciding how private corporate group members should distribute it. Courts applying Bob Richards had bypassed or failed to justify that threshold. The FDIC had advocated the rule below, but before the Supreme Court, represented by the Solicitor General, expressly conceded that federal common law should not put a thumb on the ownership scale. (4–5.)

State law was well equipped to determine corporate property rights. The federal bankruptcy and tax setting did not change that conclusion: Congress generally leaves property interests in bankruptcy assets to state law, and the Internal Revenue Code ordinarily creates no property rights of its own. The Court did not deny that exceptional circumstances might sometimes justify a different approach. It held that no adequate explanation for such an exception existed for allocation of this consolidated refund. (5.)

## What remained open

The FDIC urged affirmance despite the defective framework, arguing that the Tenth Circuit had also consulted state law and that both state law and federal regulations concerning the group agent supported the same result. Rodriguez disagreed. The Supreme Court declined to decide those alternatives. State law could produce the same allocation as Bob Richards, or a different one; the court of appeals could address that on remand. The Court likewise did not decide how the IRS regulations interacted with state law. Its narrow ruling removed an unjustified federal decisional rule and required reconsideration without that rule. (5–6.)

This distinction is essential: rejecting the source of a rule does not establish the substantive ownership conclusion that would follow under the proper source. A decision may vacate a judgment because the method is wrong while leaving open whether the ultimate result is correct.

## Vote and assigned context

Gorsuch delivered the opinion for a **unanimous Court, 9–0**. The complete source contains one Court opinion, no separate writings, no judicial footnotes, and no appendix. The mandate is vacatur of the court of appeals’ judgment and remand for proceedings consistent with the opinion, not reversal coupled with an ownership award. (Syllabus 2; opinion 6.)

The assigned Note 5 accurately emphasizes the modest judicial role and the distinction between federal interests in tax receipt and private allocation after payment. Its position after the environmental-displacement discussion and before a note on procedural common law invites attention to different reasons federal common law may fail: this case concerns the lack of justification for creating the rule in the first place. It does not hold that all federal common law is forbidden or that every federal bankruptcy or tax dispute is exclusively controlled by state law. This brief concerns the historical 2020 decision and does not claim a current-law or citator update.
