# Totem Marine Tug & Barge, Inc. v. Alyeska Pipeline Service Co.
584 P.2d 15 (Alaska 1978) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

The Alaska Supreme Court decided this appeal on August 25, 1978. Justice Burke wrote for Chief Justice Boochever and Justices Rabinowitz, Connor, Burke, and Matthews; the reported decision contains no dissent or concurrence. The court reversed summary judgment enforcing a settlement release and remanded for trial. It held that Totem's evidence raised genuine factual issues concerning economic duress. It did not find duress proved, rescind the release itself, determine that Alyeska had wrongfully terminated the shipping contract, or award the unpaid contract balance. (15, 17, 19, 24–25 & nn. 6–7.)

## Voyage, payment crisis, and release

Totem was a closely held Alaska corporation that began operating in March 1975. In June it agreed to transport pipeline construction materials for Alyeska from Houston to a designated port in southern Alaska, possibly stopping to collect more cargo. This was Totem's first contract. It chartered the Marine Flasher barge and Kirt Chouest tug with loans and guarantees from Richard Stair and Pacific, Inc., a corporation controlled by Stair. The contract contemplated completion around August 15. The court's chronology came from summary-judgment materials and included disputed assertions; it was not a final set of trial findings. (17–18.)

According to Totem, Alyeska represented that Houston would supply approximately 1,800–2,100 tons of ordinary uncoated pipe, with another 6,000–7,000 tons potentially collected later. Instead, Totem found approximately 6,700–7,200 tons of coated pipe, steel beams, and valves improperly piled in Houston. Barge modifications, extra cranes, and stevedores were required, and loading took thirty days rather than the three Totem anticipated for 2,000 tons. Delayed assurances about payment, weather, and administrative problems also contributed. The heavier load slowed the voyage. (18.)

With Alyeska's verbal consent, Totem chartered the N. Joseph Guidry as a second tug on August 13. Because the written contract amendment had not arrived when that tug reached the Panama Canal, Stair directed it not to pass through. Alyeska executed the amendment on August 21 after further disputes. The second tug then lost its preferred passage and waited another two or three days. Once joined, the vessels encountered the tail of a hurricane that impeded progress for eight or nine days. (18.)

Near San Pedro, Totem planned to refuel and change crews, but Alyeska directed the vessels to Long Beach. Totem asserted that Alyeska began unloading without its consent or the necessary surveys, voiding Totem's insurance. After disputes and concessions, the cargo was unloaded. Alyeska terminated the contract about September 14 and reaffirmed that decision a few days later without explaining the reason. These events were potentially relevant to coercion; the Supreme Court expressly withheld any determination that the termination was wrongful. (18, 24 & n. 6.)

Totem submitted termination invoices totaling approximately $260,000–$300,000. An Alyeska official reportedly said review might result in payment in a day or in six to eight months. Totem's obligations had short, ten-to-thirty-day payment schedules; its creditors demanded payment, and Stair said immediate cash was needed to avoid bankruptcy. Totem instructed attorney Roy Bell to tell Alyeska about these difficulties. Bell negotiated in Seattle, and Alyeska offered $97,500. On November 6, Totem, through Stair, signed a release of all claims in exchange for that payment. The opinion initially identifies Stair as vice-president and later describes him as president at signing; the relevant fact is that he signed for Totem, not a judicial resolution of his changing office. (17–19.)

## Claims and the deposition issue

Totem, Stair, and Pacific sued on March 26, 1976. Their amended complaint sought rescission of the settlement for economic duress, the allegedly unpaid contract balance, and additional compensatory and punitive damages arising from the claimed wrongful termination. Before answering, Alyeska moved for summary judgment on the release. Totem asserted that Alyeska knowingly withheld an acknowledged debt while aware of its impending bankruptcy, leaving no alternative to accepting inadequate immediate payment. Alyeska disputed those assertions. The superior court granted summary judgment on November 30, 1976. (19.)

A preliminary issue concerned Stair's deposition. Both sides had relied heavily on it below, but it had not been formally opened and presented. After the appeal began, the superior court refused Totem's motion to publish it, treating it as outside the record. The Supreme Court held that this was error. The court below was required at least to consider the portions brought to its attention through the parties' memoranda and arguments. The deposition was properly part of the appellate record; rather than send the matter back merely for renewed consideration of the motion, the Supreme Court examined the pertinent portions itself to avoid delay. It also denied Alyeska's motion to strike references from the reply brief. A separate Cooke deposition was not considered because neither side relied on its contents on appeal. (19–20 & nn. 1–2.)

## Economic duress: wrongfulness and practical alternatives

The court had not previously decided an economic-duress case. It explained the development from narrow threats to life, limb, or imprisonment toward economic coercion, and from an ordinary person's firmness toward the effect on the particular induced person. At the same time, it recognized competing policies: freedom of contract and settlement finality, alongside refusal to enforce coercive bargains exploiting disproportionate power. The opinion did not make financial disadvantage alone a ground for undoing agreements. (21 & n. 3; 23 n. 4.)

Its discussion described economic duress through wrongful conduct that causes involuntary assent with no reasonable alternative. Mere reluctance or financial embarrassment is insufficient: the asserted coercion must result from the other party's wrongful and oppressive conduct, rather than the claimant's necessities alone. Wrongfulness is context-dependent and can extend beyond independently criminal or tortious acts. A threat to breach a contract or withhold an admitted debt can qualify, with bad faith implicit in that application. The source's printed cross-reference to Grimshaw contains an inconsistent reporter citation; the brief relies on Totem's own reasoning rather than reproducing that mismatch as verified. (21–22.)

An injured party must also lack a reasonable alternative or adequate remedy. A contract action, substitute goods or services, or other funds may provide one, depending on the circumstances. Adequacy is practical: a remedy that would arrive only after immediate and irreparable business loss may not be adequate. Austin Instrument and Gallagher supplied examples of contractors unable to obtain materials in time to avoid grave consequences under government contracts. The discussion of Capps addressed a claim that a small settlement had been accepted to avert imminent foreclosure and repossession. These authorities illustrated how delay can make a formally available lawsuit insufficient; Totem did not hold that every financially distressed creditor lacks a reasonable alternative. (22–23.)

## Applying the rule without deciding the disputed facts

If proved, Totem's allegations could establish duress: Alyeska deliberately withheld acknowledged payment while knowing that bankruptcy and pressing debts left Totem no practical alternative to a much smaller immediate settlement. Footnote 5 clarified that acknowledgment need not concern precisely every dollar invoiced, but must concern approximately the claimed amount. Proof that termination itself was wrongful was not indispensable, although the earlier events could illuminate wrongful pressure or withholding. (23–24 & nn. 5–6.)

Alyeska initially met its summary-judgment burden by showing an executed release, representation by counsel in negotiating it, and Stair's understanding of its consequences. A valid release would bar later contract claims. Totem therefore needed competent evidence of specific material facts, not merely its pleading. But it did not need to prove that it would ultimately win. The trial court mistakenly required a showing that Totem could sustain its trial burden, emphasizing that Stair had not personally negotiated and that Bell supplied no testimony. The Supreme Court found enough contrary evidence, with reasonable inferences favoring Totem, to require trial. It acknowledged the showing's weakness and that Bell's evidence would probably be needed to prevail, while distinguishing that eventual evidentiary burden from the threshold for opposing summary judgment. (24–25.)

The unresolved issues included whether Alyeska acknowledged any particular debt, whether bankruptcy was imminent, whether Alyeska knew Totem's financial condition, and whether it threatened by words or conduct to withhold payment unless Totem settled. Thus representation by counsel and knowing execution were relevant evidence, but did not conclusively establish voluntary assent against the claimed practical compulsion. (25 & n. 7.)

Stair and Pacific separately argued that the release could not bind them because they had not signed it. The court rejected that route around the release: neither was a party to the original contract or amendment, and neither claimed third-party-beneficiary status. They therefore had no independent contractual claim to release. Their fate depended on Totem's success or failure, rather than on an independent recovery created by nonsignature. The final disposition was reversed and remanded for trial under the stated principles. (25.)

## Assigned account and reading scope

It uses a $300,000 bill and a proposed $100,000 settlement to ask whether counsel could confidently predict enforcement. Those are the instructional problem's rounded or hypothetical numbers, not the actual executed $97,500 release and approximately $260,000–$300,000 invoices in the opinion.
