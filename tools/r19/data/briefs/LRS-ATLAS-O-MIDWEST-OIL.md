# United States v. Midwest Oil Company
236 U.S. 459 (1915) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and procedural posture

On February 23, 1915, the Supreme Court sustained President Taft's September 27, 1909 withdrawal of unappropriated public petroleum lands from private acquisition pending proposed legislation. Justice Lamar delivered the Court's opinion. Justice Day dissented, joined by Justices McKenna and Van Devanter; Justice McReynolds did not participate, making the decision 5–3. The Court remanded to the federal District Court for Wyoming with directions that its decree dismissing the United States' bill be reversed. It did not award a final accounting or adjudicate the quantity of oil extracted. (236 U.S. 459, 466–483, 484–512.)

The procedural route matters. The District Court had sustained the defendants' demurrer and dismissed the government's equity suit. The Eighth Circuit rendered no decision on the merits; it certified questions to the Supreme Court, which called for the entire record. After argument and reargument, the Court decided the controlling question of the withdrawal's validity. This was therefore reversal of the District Court's dismissal, not reversal of a substantive Eighth Circuit decision. (Id. 467–469, 483.)

The holding rested on longstanding executive practice known to and acquiesced in by Congress, understood as implied congressional consent to public-land administration. The Court expressly reserved whether, as an original question before any practice existed, a President could withdraw land Congress had opened to occupation and purchase. It also denied that executive conduct alone could manufacture a power. Those qualifications prevent treating the decision as a general presidential authority to suspend statutes whenever a different policy seems desirable. (Id. 469, 472–475.)

## The land regime, conservation problem, and withdrawal

Congress's February 11, 1897 statute allowed qualified persons to acquire land containing petroleum or mineral oils under the placer-mining laws. Revised Statutes §§ 2319 and 2329 supplied the broader system opening valuable mineral deposits to exploration and purchase. The economic conditions described by the Court encouraged rapid extraction: oil beneath adjoining parcels could be drained by competing wells, and public reserves were disappearing through private acquisition. Officials feared that the government would lose inexpensive access to petroleum needed for naval fuel and later buy back oil it once owned. (Id. 466–467.)

On September 17, 1909, the Geological Survey's director warned the Secretary of the Interior that the remaining public petroleum lands might soon pass from government control. He recommended suspension of new claims pending legislation. The Secretary approved the recommendation. On September 27, acting under the President's direction, the department issued Temporary Petroleum Withdrawal No. 5. It temporarily barred location, settlement, selection, filing, entry, or disposal under mineral and nonmineral public-land laws within the listed areas, in aid of proposed legislation concerning petroleum deposits. Existing valid claims could proceed after field investigation and examination. (Id. 466–467, 484–487.)

The lists covered areas totaling 3,041,000 acres in California and Wyoming; the Court cautioned that the order operated only on public lands within those areas and that their actual acreage was not shown. The dissent developed the administrative correspondence more fully: earlier California restrictions had allowed mining locations, and the new directive closed that remaining route. It emphasized that the Wyoming lands at issue were not identified as a prospective naval supply; their withdrawal sought a different congressional system for disposing of oil lands. That distinction informed the dissent's objection to justifying the entire order through the Navy's needs. (Id. 467, 485–488.)

## The post-withdrawal claim and competing positions

On March 27, 1910, William T. Henshaw and others entered a quarter section of the withdrawn Wyoming land. They explored, drilled a well, discovered oil, and assigned their interests to Midwest Oil and the other appellees, who took possession and extracted petroleum. A location certificate was filed on May 4. All these steps followed the September 1909 withdrawal. The United States sued to recover the land and obtain an accounting for 50,000 barrels alleged to have been illegally extracted. Dismissal on demurrer put the withdrawal's legal effect at the center of the litigation. (Id. 467–468.)

The government argued that the President's responsibilities for the public domain, the Navy's fuel supply, executive power, and Congress's tacit consent authorized the order. Appellees answered that Congress had affirmatively opened the land to acquisition and that the President possessed no dispensing power to suspend that law. An order seeking future legislation, they maintained, did not execute existing congressional policy but substituted the Executive's preferred policy for it. Counsel also relied on President Taft's subsequent request for express authority and ratification, and contended that the June 1910 statute supplied prospective authority without validating the earlier order. (Id. 461–465, 468–469.)

## Long practice and implied congressional consent

Lamar began with a history extending roughly eighty years. Excluding orders based on special congressional authorization, Presidents had repeatedly withdrawn mineral and nonmineral public lands from acquisition, affecting millions of acres. The Court counted 99 orders establishing or enlarging Indian reservations, 109 military reservations or associated facilities, and 44 bird reserves before 1910. It understood these 252 orders as useful public reservations lacking specific statutory authorization and found that Congress had repeatedly accepted, rather than repudiated, their use. Its documentary sources appear in the extended footnote at 470. (Id. 469–472 & n.1.)

Before a person initiated a legally protected claim, the public-land statute did not give that person an enforceable private interest in a particular government parcel. Reserving unappropriated land consequently preserved public property without defeating an existing private right. Congress could disaffirm the orders; the President's knowledge of conditions made temporary retention useful while Congress considered what to do. Grisar v. McDowell and opinions of government law officers had recognized the reservation practice. These circumstances supplied more than the bare assertion that a President had acted before. (Id. 470–472.)

The Court regarded settled governmental usage as relevant evidence in construing a statute or identifying a power, even when the usage's validity was itself disputed. Stuart v. Laird and other practical-construction decisions supported giving weight to longstanding arrangements around which public officials and citizens had organized their conduct. But practice did not itself create authority. Practice known to Congress and left undisturbed supported a presumption of congressional consent or a recognized administrative power to manage the public domain. The distinction was between an implied grant inferred from the principal's informed acquiescence and unilateral executive creation of power. (Id. 472–474.)

Congress's proprietary role strengthened that inference. Congress could deal with federal land as an owner as well as legislate under Article IV, § 3. General disposal rules might encounter changed conditions or emergencies requiring the managing agent to retain property temporarily. An owner's authorization to do that need not always be written. The Court compared implied licenses for grazing and mining on public lands: if congressional acquiescence could support private privileges, it could also support an agent's power to preserve the owner's interest. The opinion nevertheless distinguished cases in which an officer improperly withdrew land to enlarge a railroad's private statutory grant. (Id. 473–475.)

## Temporary withdrawals in aid of legislation

Appellees sought to distinguish reserving land for a public use from withholding it while seeking future legislation. The Court rejected a categorical distinction: if the President could make a permanent public reservation, the lesser step of temporary withdrawal had the same protective character and effect on would-be claimants. It also found direct precedent for withdrawals pending legislation in the Des Moines River cases. Land had been withheld while officials and Congress considered the disputed extent of Iowa's grant, and later settlers acquired no rights inconsistent with the operative withdrawal. The Court treated those withdrawals as valid from their inception, rather than as initially void actions retroactively cured to the prejudice of intervening settlers. (Id. 475–477.)

Other practice independently supported temporary withdrawals. The opinion reviewed islands and isolated small tracts, proposed forest reservations, phosphate, oil, coal, and park lands withheld pending legislation. Sometimes Congress later enacted a measure concerning them; frequently it did not. The absence of later legislation did not, in the Court's view, invalidate a temporary protective order. Congress's opportunity to know and object, the substantial tracts involved, and the duration of the orders supported the inference of consent. (Id. 477–480.)

The 1902 inquiry was particularly significant. The Senate specifically asked what land had been withdrawn administratively and what legal authority supported the withdrawals. The Interior Department responded with a detailed list and asserted a long-recognized power to prevent fraud, assist administration, and support pending legislation when exigencies demanded. The majority described 92 orders made for useful but nonstatutory reasons. Congress received the asserted basis and did not repudiate it then or afterward. That informed silence, the Court reasoned, permitted continuation of the practice until Congress revoked the power. (Id. 480–481.)

## The June 25, 1910 statute

Congress later expressly authorized temporary withdrawals. The legislation postdated both the challenged order and appellees' attempted location. It directed that it should not be construed as recognizing, abridging, or enlarging claims initiated on oil or gas lands after earlier withdrawals. The majority read this savings language as leaving the parties' rights to the law existing when the September 1909 order issued. It neither took away a valid intervening claim nor validated a location made ineffective by a lawful withdrawal. (Id. 481–483.)

The legislative history did not change that conclusion. Taft had acknowledged doubts and requested express authorization and ratification. A House bill included ratification, but Congress ultimately chose language avoiding resolution of contested public and private rights. Some senators believed the President already had a broader withdrawal power. The majority treated the statute as prospective clarification and restriction, without disaffirming prior valid orders. Since longstanding practice and congressional consent already authorized this withdrawal, ratification was unnecessary, and appellees acquired no rights through a later entry while it remained operative. (Id. 482–483.)

## Day's dissent, joined by McKenna and Van Devanter

The dissent agreed that express or sufficiently clear implied congressional authority could support an executive withdrawal. It also recognized legitimate withdrawals to carry out public uses Congress had selected or to preserve land while resolving ambiguity between congressional grants. Its disagreement concerned the breadth and relevance of the history. Article IV vested control over federal property in Congress; the President's executive and Take Care responsibilities did not include suspending its laws to await a preferable disposal policy. (Id. 488–492, 504–507.)

Day painstakingly reconsidered the precedents. Wilcox v. Jackson involved military, trading-house, and lighthouse uses authorized by Congress, together with statutory reservation exceptions. Grisar involved reservations recognized through legislation and appropriations. The Des Moines litigation concerned preserving an existing grant amid serious disagreement over its scope; withholding the disputed land served Congress's existing policy rather than replacing it. Even the later continuation protected settlers claiming under Iowa while Congress considered adjustment. The dissent's extended documentary footnote also challenged whether every government example lacked antecedent authority. It therefore rejected the inference that these practices authorized shutting land Congress had unambiguously opened because administrators wanted a new scheme. (Id. 489–504 & n.1.)

Prior decisions invalidating withdrawals in aid of railroad grants showed the limit: an executive order could not defeat Congress's existing policy in favor of lawful settlers. Day accepted the rule that withdrawal authority could be express or implied, but required an implication equivalent to congressional authorization, rather than an undifferentiated history of executive action. The present Wyoming withdrawal anticipated legislative change; its conservation purpose did not supply the missing legal authority. (Id. 504–507.)

The 1910 legislation reinforced this conclusion for the dissent. Taft had specifically requested ratification; Congress omitted it and conferred a restricted future power while preserving disputed claims. The Act protected existing settlements and diligent oil exploration under stated conditions and was not the unrestricted order the Executive had issued. Day regarded withheld ratification as defeating an implied-consent theory, and concluded that the President's July 2, 1910 reaffirmation could not cure the original defect. Courts should preserve the constitutional division of powers rather than enlarge executive authority because a different land policy appeared beneficial. The dissent would leave the dismissal in force. (Id. 507–512.)

The book's opening description calls the challenged withdrawal a 1910 order; the actual judicial report identifies September 27, 1909 as the controlling order, with a separate July 2, 1910 reaffirmation discussed by the dissent. This brief preserves those stages.

The editors' land-agent explanation captures the majority's proprietary and acquiescence reasoning, but its suggestion that the agent could contradict the principal's instructions needs the opinion's qualifications: no vested private right had arisen before withdrawal; the relevant history was known to Congress; Congress could disaffirm the practice; and executive action alone could not create power. The dissent contested both the historical analogy and the inference from the 1910 legislation. The complete official fifty-four-page source, including the majority's documentary footnote and the entire joint dissent with its extended footnote, was read before drafting. The next case beginning on the last physical page was excluded. The verified scope is this historical judgment and statutory setting, without a claim about the doctrine's current application to unrelated presidential action.
