# Securities and Exchange Commission v. Koenig

**Civil Procedure · CP-117**  
557 F.3d 736 (7th Cir. 2009) · February 26, 2009 · Chief Judge Easterbrook

## Fraud case and trial

James Koenig, Waste Management's former chief financial officer, used accounting methods a jury found fraudulent. One method offset ordinary operating expenses with unusual gains, making recurring profits appear stronger. Another transferred remaining depreciation from prematurely closed landfills to other assets, postponing losses. Waste Management announced in October 1997 that its financial statements were unreliable; its share value fell sharply. A February 1998 restatement took an approximately $1.1 billion charge for 1992–1996. Koenig blamed new management for an “earnings bath” designed to make its predecessors look worse, but the jury rejected his position. Slip opinion 1–4.

After a twelve-week trial, the district court ordered disgorgement of $831,500 in bonuses from 1992, 1994, and 1995, plus more than $1.2 million in prejudgment interest. It imposed a civil penalty of approximately $2.1 million and barred Koenig from serving as a director or top manager of a public company. His appeal did not contest evidentiary sufficiency for the fraud verdict. It challenged limitations, trial practices, expert evidence, and remedies. The assigned extract focuses on juror participation; the complete opinion resolves the additional issues too.

## Juror questions: issue and holding

The judge allowed jurors to submit written questions for witnesses, asking some, reformulating others, and withholding inappropriate or repetitive ones. Koenig argued that the practice risked turning jurors into advocates or causing premature commitments. He sought reversal or, alternatively, a restriction to narrowly “clarifying” questions.

The Seventh Circuit held that allowing juror questions was a matter of the trial judge's **sound discretion**, and that the discretion was properly exercised here. No statute or procedural rule prohibited the practice. Earlier judicial cautions about risks did not amount to a categorical ban. Studies and the ABA and Seventh Circuit jury projects supported benefits to attention and comprehension, especially in a long technical trial. Those recommendations informed the court's reasoning; they did not themselves operate as binding procedural law. Slip opinion 10–12.

## Filtering, impartiality, and practical safeguards

The judge should filter irrelevant or argumentative questions rather than let jurors examine witnesses unchecked. The court declined the proposed rigid distinction between “clarifying” and other questions, because information already clear to a specialist might be confusing to a lay juror. The judge used the appropriate filtering process in this trial.

Some questions were inadvertently omitted, and one witness was not recalled for additional inquiries. Jurors were instructed not to infer anything from the judge's decision not to ask a question. These ordinary management glitches supplied no basis for reversal on the record. Nor did it concern the court that three jurors submitted about two-thirds of the 127 questions: the total was approximately two per trial day, and differences in jurors' willingness to speak did not establish takeover of the proceedings. Slip opinion 12–13.

Pointed questions, especially those the judge withheld, did not prove disqualifying prejudgment. Jurors inevitably react to unfolding evidence; silence would conceal those reactions rather than prevent them. Questions could alert lawyers to confusion or missing proof and permit more useful presentations. The court preserved the requirement of impartiality while rejecting an inference of impermissible advocacy from these questions alone. Slip opinion 13–14.

## Other holdings in the full opinion

**Limitations.** The SEC filed in March 2002, after Koenig left office in January 1997. Section 2462 allowed five years from accrual for penalties. The court declined to decide accrual generally and relied on the historical fraud-concealment doctrine, treating the relevant period as running from discovery or when reasonable diligence would have discovered the fraud. Public disclosure occurred in October 1997, and Koenig did not contend a diligent SEC should have discovered it earlier. The court held the penalty claim timely. This describes the court's **2009 limitations ruling**, without asserting that its treatment supplies current limitations law. Slip opinion 4–7.

**Motive evidence and hearsay.** The district judge should have excluded the irrelevant “earnings bath” theme. Intentional material deception, rather than management's motive, was the issue. But Koenig pressed that theme despite warning that the SEC could respond with evidence of what managers knew. Much of the responsive evidence explained listeners' decisions rather than proving the statements' truth; to the extent genuine hearsay entered, the appellate court treated the problem as invited. The Rule 403 balance was not an abuse of discretion given Koenig's chosen defense. The court's criticism of trial length did not establish a fixed twenty-day legal ceiling. Slip opinion 7–10.

**Use of Koenig's expert.** Koenig retained economist Frederick Dunbar, disclosed his report, and participated in his deposition, but did not present him during the liability phase. The SEC used that deposition because Dunbar's estimate of stock-price effects supported materiality. Rule 26(a)(2)(A)'s identity-disclosure function had already been satisfied, and the SEC identified him in the pretrial witness list. Even assuming a further discovery-stage disclosure obligation, its omission was harmless under Rule 37(c)(1). After disclosing the report and conducting the deposition, Koenig could not restore trial-preparation confidentiality merely by withdrawing the expert. Slip opinion 14–16.

**Disgorgement and penalty.** Prejudgment interest measured the economic benefit of possessing the bonuses over time, so it could be included in pecuniary gain when calculating the penalty ceiling. The court also approved the remedial expert's qualifications and methods. But the district judge had not adequately explained the 1992 bonus: correcting both 1991 and 1992 earnings still showed growth, potentially entitling Koenig to some legitimate bonus. Comparing inflated prior-year earnings with corrected later-year earnings was inconsistent. The court required review of all relevant bonus calculations using consistently corrected accounts, with consequential interest and penalty changes if necessary. Slip opinion 17–20.

## Judgment and sources

The court **affirmed except as to calculation of the bonuses under proper accounting and remanded for further proceedings on that calculation and any resulting remedial adjustments**. It sustained the fraud judgment and juror-questioning practice; the remand did not direct a new liability trial. Easterbrook wrote for the panel of Easterbrook, Manion, and Wood. No separate writing or judicial footnotes appear in the complete twenty-page opinion.
