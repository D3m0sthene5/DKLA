Parties
Terry and twenty-six other truckdrivers, all members of Local 391, sued their employer, McLean Trucking
Company, and their union. They alleged that McLean violated the collective bargaining agreement and that the
union breached its duty of fair representation in handling their grievances. By the stage relevant to Supreme
Court review, the employer and requests for injunctive relief had been dismissed. The union was the petitioner
seeking to strike the employees’ jury demand; the employees were respondents seeking a jury on their
remaining damages claims. 494 U.S. at 561–564.
Material Facts
In 1982, McLean reorganized its operations and transferred the employees to its Winston-Salem terminal. It
agreed to give them special seniority rights relative to inactive drivers who had previously been laid off. After
approximately six weeks, the transferred drivers began experiencing repeated layoffs and recalls. They
challenged McLean’s treatment of their seniority and its practice of stripping the special rights from a
transferred driver upon layoff. Id. at 561–562.
The first grievance proceeding produced an order directing McLean to recall the affected transferred drivers,
lay off certain recalled inactive drivers, and preserve the transferred drivers’ special rights until the inactive
drivers were properly recalled. McLean initially complied, but then recalled inactive drivers in a manner
restoring their superior seniority. In subsequent layoffs, the transferred drivers again lost their jobs first. They
filed a second grievance alleging circumvention of the first decision. The union representative presented the
competing positions of both groups, and the committee concluded that McLean had not violated its earlier
decision. Id. at 561–562.
The employees filed a third grievance as layoffs and recalls continued. The union declined to refer it to a
committee, stating that the relevant questions had already been decided. The employees claimed that this
representation wrongfully deprived them of wages and health benefits. Their Supreme Court victory did not
establish that the union acted improperly or that McLean breached the agreement; those were issues to be tried.
Id. at 562–564, 573–574.

Procedural History
The employees sued in July 1983, alleging a contractual violation under § 301 of the Labor Management
Relations Act and a breach of the union’s fair-representation duty. They sought restoration of proper seniority,
an injunction against further unlawful conduct, and compensatory relief including lost wages and health
benefits. McLean filed for bankruptcy in 1986. The employees then voluntarily dismissed the employer and all
injunctive claims, leaving monetary relief sought from the union. Id. at 562–563.
The union moved to strike the jury demand. The district court denied that motion, and the Fourth Circuit
affirmed on interlocutory appeal. The Supreme Court granted review to resolve a disagreement among circuits
and affirmed. Thus, the judgment under review concerned entitlement to a jury before a merits trial, rather than
review of a damages verdict or a final finding of unfair representation. Id. at 563, 573–574.
Issue
Does the Seventh Amendment entitle employees to a jury when they seek damages from their union for lost
wages and benefits allegedly caused by breach of the duty of fair representation? How should the court classify
that claim when its historical analogies include an equitable trust action and a legal malpractice or contract
action, and when the requested payment is described as backpay? Id. at 564–573.
Rule and Holding
The Seventh Amendment inquiry examines the nature of the action or issues and the nature of the remedy,
giving greater importance to the remedy. The Court held that the employees’ requested compensation was legal
relief: it sought wages and benefits they would have received from their employer, rather than money the union
wrongfully held, and it was not intertwined with a remaining request for an injunction. The employees were
entitled to a jury on all issues presented in their suit. Id. at 564–565, 570–574.
The opinion’s division matters. Six Justices joined the remedial analysis and judgment. Marshall’s particular
historical comparison in Part III-A attracted four votes; Brennan and Stevens reached the same result through
different approaches. A brief should not describe that four-Justice comparison as an undivided majority
holding that the claim is governed by trust law. Id. at 565–570, 574–584.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the Fourth Circuit, leaving the district court’s denial of the motion to strike the
jury demand in place. The employees retained the right to jury determination of the remaining suit against the
union. They did not receive an award of backpay from the Supreme Court, and the Court did not determine that
either underlying breach had occurred. Id. at 563, 573–574.
Reasoning
The union’s duty arises from its exclusive authority to represent the bargaining unit. It must exercise that
authority honestly, in good faith, and without hostility, discrimination, or arbitrary conduct. Because grievance
procedures ordinarily carry finality, the employees’ damages case required proof of two connected matters:
McLean’s breach of the collective bargaining agreement and the union’s breach of its representation duty.
Those requirements remained even after McLean left the case. Id. at 563–564.
Marshall’s historical discussion first rejected the union’s analogy to an equitable proceeding setting aside an
arbitration award. The grievance committee had addressed the employer’s conduct, not the distinct claim that
the union mishandled its representation. The union’s alleged breach therefore had never been adjudicated in the

grievance process. Treating the suit against the union as necessarily undoing an award on that issue
misconceived what the committee had decided. Id. at 565–567 (plurality portion).
The trust analogy was more persuasive to the four Justices joining Part III-A. A trustee and a union both
exercise discretion for beneficiaries or represented employees whose individual preferences do not directly
control the representative’s decisions. An individual employee generally cannot fire the union and choose
another representative. Those features distinguished the relationship from an ordinary client’s control over a
lawyer. The parallel also extended to the representative’s authority to pursue or settle a claim against a third
party. Id. at 567–569 (plurality portion).
Marshall nevertheless found the historical inquiry inconclusive when the entire hybrid dispute was considered.
The employees also had to prove the employer’s contractual breach, an issue analogous to a legal contract
claim. He reasoned that the employees should not lose the significance of that issue merely because the
employer’s bankruptcy prevented continued litigation against both defendants. The claim combined distinct
representation and contract issues, rather than becoming wholly equitable through the trust analogy. Id. at
569–570 & nn.6–7 (plurality portion).
The six-Justice remedial analysis then examined what the union would have to pay if the employees prevailed.
Lost wages and benefits measured compensation for the injury allegedly caused by deficient representation.
The union was not holding those wages for the employees; the employer would have paid them if the
grievances had been handled successfully. The requested award therefore lacked the restitutionary feature
invoked to characterize some monetary remedies as equitable. Id. at 570–571.
Nor did the remaining award accompany an injunction. All injunctive claims had been dismissed, and the
employees sought only damages from the union. The Court consequently had no basis to treat this payment as
an incidental part of continuing equitable relief. It also rejected the argument that any monetary award
analogous to a trust remedy must automatically be equitable. That approach would let classification of the
historical cause of action predetermine the remedy and collapse the two inquiries, despite the greater weight
given to the remedy. Id. at 571 & n.8.
The term backpay did not settle the question. The union invoked the equitable treatment of Title VII backpay
and the connection between that statute and National Labor Relations Act remedies. The Court distinguished
statutory language, the party from whom the money was sought, and the purpose of the remedy. Congress had
expressly described Title VII relief in equitable terms, while the fair-representation duty lacked a comparable
remedial designation. Moreover, compensation from this union addressed the individual employee’s loss, and
did not simply reproduce a public enforcement scheme against an employer. Id. at 571–573.
The Court assumed without deciding that a Title VII plaintiff seeking backpay had no jury right in that context.
It did not decide that separate question here. The narrower conclusion was that the union could not carry the
classification of a different remedy into this case merely because both awards might be called backpay. The
legal nature of the damages actually sought supported a jury on the employees’ claims. Id. at 572–574.
Separate Opinions
Justice Brennan would have simplified the historical test by eliminating the search for a comparable
eighteenth-century cause of action. He would classify the relief using historical law-and-equity distinctions,
subject to questions concerning permissible adjudication in a specialized forum. Because the Court already
gave the remedy greater weight, he regarded the additional effort to fit modern statutory rights into ancient
writs as burdensome and unreliable. He joined the remedial analysis and result but not Part III-A. Id. at
574–581 (Brennan, J., concurring).

Brennan did not propose abandoning history altogether. He argued that remedial categories were historically
meaningful and more manageable than analogies between modern collective representation and institutions
developed in another legal system. If an unusual case left the remedial classification genuinely balanced, he
favored resolving the uncertainty in favor of a jury. His proposal remained a separate opinion, not an adopted
replacement of the Court’s two-part approach. Id. at 577–581 & nn.7–8.
Justice Stevens also declined to join Part III-A, but preferred the malpractice analogy. A union represents
workers in dealings with an adverse employer, and the members collectively can direct or replace their
representatives. Stevens saw these features as more like legal representation than the paternalistic management
of a trust. He further noted the development of fair-representation law through jury trials and the ordinary
employment, contract, and representation issues that juries could assess. He did not require an exact
eighteenth-century counterpart before protecting the jury right. Id. at 581–584 (Stevens, J., concurring).
Justice Kennedy, joined by O’Connor and Scalia, would have denied a jury. He accepted the trust analogy and
regarded it as controlling when rights and remedies were considered together. Both trustees and unions must
exercise independent discretion and reconcile beneficiaries’ competing interests. A lawyer’s ordinary duty of
loyalty, by contrast, generally prevents representation of opposing interests without consent. Monetary relief
making the beneficiary whole had historically been available in equity, so the demand for compensation did not
turn the relationship into a legal action. Id. at 584–588 (Kennedy, J., dissenting).
Kennedy also rejected extracting the employer-breach element from the overall claim against the union. Earlier
cases separated self-standing legal claims from procedures formerly available only in equity; in his view, they
did not require a jury whenever an equitable claim included a predicate question resembling contract law. A
beneficiary suing a trustee likewise might have to prove the merit of an underlying contract claim without
obtaining a jury. Kennedy insisted that the Amendment’s preservation language required a historically
grounded classification and reserved questions about a suit in which the employer remained a defendant. Id. at
588–595.
Notes
The hybrid structure matters even though only the union remained a party. Proof of the employer’s breach was
still necessary to the employees’ recovery. Removing the employer therefore changed the available defendant
and relief without eliminating the contract question from the elements of the case. Id. at 563–564, 569–570 &
n.6.
Use the remedial analysis concretely: who allegedly caused the loss, who holds the money, what injury the
payment compensates, and whether equitable relief remains. The word backpay alone did not answer those
questions. Equally, the Court did not announce that every payment is legal or that Brennan’s remedy-only
proposal replaced the existing analysis. Id. at 570–574, 574–581.
