# Brackenbury v. Hodgkin

116 Me. 399, 102 A. 106 (Me. Oct. 27, 1917) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B1-073

The Maine Supreme Judicial Court, sitting as the Law Court, dismissed the defendants' appeal and affirmed an equitable decree protecting the Brackenburys' interest in their mother's farm, with costs against her son Walter. The mother's written offer was accepted by the couple's move and entry upon the promised care, followed by continued performance to the extent she permitted it. The contract created an enforceable equitable interest, and the trial finding that the couple had not forfeited it through misconduct was supported. Chief Justice Cornish wrote for the listed court of Cornish, Spear, King, Bird, Hanson, and Madigan; no separate writing appears. (116 Me. at 400–402.)

## A definite written proposal induced a move across the country

Sarah Hodgkin was a widow living alone on her home farm outside Lewiston, Maine. She had six adult children: five sons, including Walter, and a daughter who, with her husband Joseph Brackenbury, lived in Independence, Missouri. After several letters about the couple returning to care for Sarah, she sent them the February 8, 1915 letter on which the suit rested. If they moved to Lewiston, paid their moving expenses, and maintained and cared for her on the farm during her life, they would have the property's use and income and the use of household goods, subject to specified exceptions. Sarah would retain the rooms she needed. The postscript promised them the place at her death. (Id. at 400.)

The offer had not been withdrawn or modified when the couple moved to Maine in late April 1915. They entered the farm and began the specified performance. Relations became unpleasant within weeks. Sarah brought two suits against her son-in-law on matters the opinion characterizes as trifling and ordered the couple to leave. They refused. On November 7, 1916 she deeded the premises to Walter, reserving a life estate for herself. Walter knew of the agreement and took the deed for the sole purpose of evicting the Brackenburys; he was not a bona fide purchaser for value without notice. He served a notice to quit that day and began forcible-entry-and-detainer proceedings on November 13. (Id. at 400–401.)

## The suit sought protection of the contractual land interest

The couple filed a bill in equity seeking reconveyance from Walter to Sarah, an injunction against his eviction action, and recognition that Sarah's legal title was impressed with a trust in their favor according to the contract. After detailed findings, the sitting justice sustained the bill, granted the requested relief, and imposed costs on Walter. The defendants appealed that decree. The Law Court addressed contract formation, the resulting equitable interest, alleged failure of performance, and the objection that an adequate legal remedy displaced equity. (Id. at 401.)

This posture matters: the dispute was not merely a damages action for the expense of relocating, and the mother had not died. The decree protected the interest arising under the continuing bargain while addressing a knowing transfer designed to defeat it. It did not order immediate outright ownership in the couple regardless of the mother's reserved interests and their care obligations. (Id. at 400–402.)

## Acceptance did not require a return promise

The court found a legal and binding contract clearly proven. Sarah's offer was written and definite. The court classified it as unilateral, seeking acts in return for her promise, rather than a bilateral exchange requiring the couple's reciprocal promise. Consequently, acceptance in words was unnecessary. (Id. at 401.)

The Brackenburys accepted by moving from Missouri to the farm and entering upon the specified acts; they thereafter continued performing as far as Sarah allowed. On those facts, the court declared the contract completed and valid. It did not require them to await Sarah's death and finish every lifetime-care act before acquiring any contractual protection. Equally, it did not say they could cease caring for her at will while retaining the property entitlement. The ongoing performance and the mother's interference were part of the actual reasoning. (Id.)

The opinion's formulation concerns acceptance through the move and entry upon performance in this particular arrangement. It does not expressly construct a separate option contract under a later Restatement section or decide the rights of a person who merely makes preparations but never arrives or begins the requested care. Describing the case as protection after performance begins should preserve its actual acts and continuing-performance finding. (Id. at 400–401.)

## The signed letter supported an equitable interest in the farm

The contract, with performance entered upon, created an equitable interest in the land. The governing Maine provision required a signed writing for a trust concerning land, except trusts arising or resulting by implication of law. Sarah's signed letter met that requirement. No particular formal document was necessary; a letter or memorandum could suffice if its terms and the parties' relationships were reasonably certain. The couple's interest was therefore entitled to protection. (Id. at 401–402.)

Walter's knowledge of the bargain and purpose in accepting the deed supported relief against the transfer. The opinion specifically excluded bona fide purchaser status; it did not decide what protection would have applied against a purchaser for value without notice. Reconveyance and restraint of the eviction suit protected the bargain without treating the intervening deed as a way to escape it. (Id. at 400–401.)

## The misconduct and adequate-remedy objections failed

The defendants asserted that the couple had treated Sarah improperly and unkindly, forfeiting any equitable relief. That was a disputed factual contention, not an established breach. The sitting justice resolved it for the couple, and the appellate court found the decision fully supported. Testimony and Sarah's own conduct and testimony showed her to be the principal provoking cause of the family's difficulties. The finding therefore did not warrant forfeiture of the Brackenburys' interest. (Id. at 402.)

Finally, the defendants invoked the ordinary objection that a plain and adequate legal remedy precludes relief in equity. The court explained that this restriction did not control where full equity jurisdiction or special statutory jurisdiction covered the case. Maine had expressly conferred equitable jurisdiction over trusts. The case fell within that exception, so the availability of a legal remedy did not defeat this trust relief. This was a conclusion about the cited statutory jurisdiction, not a general abolition of the adequate-remedy limitation. (Id.)

The final entry dismissed the appeal and affirmed the sitting justice's decree with costs against Walter Hodgkin. There was no remand for a new formation hearing, no money award fixed by the appellate court, and no ruling on a death during the couple's journey. (Id.)
