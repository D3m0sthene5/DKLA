# Tuckwiller v. Tuckwiller
413 S.W.2d 274 (Mo. 1967) | Contracts

## Court and posture

The Missouri Supreme Court, Division One, affirmed specific performance of a written promise to devise real estate in exchange for lifetime care. Commissioner Welborn’s opinion was adopted per curiam; Commissioners Houser and Higgins concurred, and all judges concurred. The report dates the initial decision March 13, 1967, and records denial of rehearing or transfer to the full court, with modification on the court’s own motion, on April 10. The reproduced opinion is the modified report, not an independently recovered premodification draft. The executor and Davidson College, the residuary beneficiary under Morrison’s will, appealed. Their sole appellate contention concerned equitable fairness and adequacy of monetary compensation. 413 S.W.2d at 275, 277–278, 281.

## Family arrangements and the care agreement

At death, Flora Metta Morrison owned a 160-acre Saline County farm valued in the estate inventory at $34,400. Her sister owned two other nearby tracts, including the family home place. John and Ruby Tuckwiller had occupied that home since 1958 and rented and operated the three farms. Rooms were reserved for the sisters’ visits. John was Morrison’s nephew. Morrison, who had taught abroad and later worked in New York, became increasingly mobile between relatives after losing her New York residence. By early 1963 her Parkinson’s disease had worsened. The actual report identifies the promised property as the whole 160-acre tract she owned; the shortened assigned narrative’s acreage description should not substitute for that fuller account. Id. at 275–276.

Ruby worked in food service at the State School for $206 monthly. Morrison appreciated her care and wanted her to resign and provide personal care for the remainder of Morrison’s life, rather than leave Morrison dependent on a nursing home as Parkinson’s progressed. Ruby initially used accrued vacation but hesitated to surrender steady employment for potentially several years of demanding care. Morrison suffered an apparent stroke in April, with some hospital confusion, but after discharge her physician and a longtime friend found her mentally clear on May 2 and 3. Id. at 276.

On May 3, in John’s presence, they agreed in writing. Ruby undertook lifetime care, including meals, a bed, possible nursing, and pleasures; Morrison agreed to devise her farm to Ruby while retaining its income during life and paying medication expense. Ruby wrote the terms and Morrison signed. Ruby resigned her job on May 6. That morning Morrison made a May 7 appointment with the lawyer who had drafted her will to change it. Later she fell again and required an ambulance. Before leaving, she asked for their agreement to be witnessed and dated so it would follow the will. One attendant recalled her insistence on completing business; the other did not recall her statements and said he signed at Ruby’s request. The court did not hide that evidentiary difference. Id. at 276–277.

Hospitalization prevented the lawyer’s appointment. Morrison briefly returned home, deteriorated, and was readmitted until her June 14 death at seventy-three. Special nurses cared for her during the final illness; Ruby visited frequently and assisted as possible, as did her daughters. Ruby did not pay the hospital expenses. The 1961 will remained unchanged: it directed sale of the farm and residuary property for a Davidson student loan fund, while giving John an option to buy the farm at appraised value. Ruby sought enforcement of the separate contract against the estate and beneficiary. Id. at 275, 277.

## The trial finding and the estate’s argument

Ruby presented life-expectancy evidence of roughly 8.69–9.35 additional years for a seventy-three-year-old. The defense offered hospital records, the second attendant’s testimony, and evidence that nursing-home care cost $200 monthly. The trial court found that, when the agreement was made, the kind and duration of care were too uncertain to measure by a monetary standard, the contract was then equitable, and Ruby had performed. It decreed specific performance. Id. at 277.

On appeal the executor and college focused on the care actually provided during the short interval before death. They characterized the value as readily ascertainable, suggested $133 for seven days’ services, and argued that transferring a $34,400 farm would unjustly enrich Ruby and injure the college. Their proposed remedy was the services’ reasonable monetary value, not enforcement of the devise promise. The court’s answer depended on evaluating the undertaking when made rather than reconstructing the price after its unexpected short duration. Id. at 277–279.

## Written contracts, equitable discretion, and timing

The court accepted that specific performance can be withheld from either written or oral contracts that are inequitable, unconscionable, supported by shockingly inadequate consideration, or adequately remedied at law. A writing does not automatically sanitize unfairness. But many defense precedents concerned oral promises to devise land, where equity must first decide whether avoiding fraud justifies an exception to the statute of frauds. In Selle, for example, a short period of services following an alleged oral promise resulted in compensation rather than transfer of land. This written agreement did not present that threshold problem. Id. at 278.

Fairness and adequacy must be examined prospectively. Ruby surrendered employment she liked and undertook care of uncertain duration that, given Parkinson’s course, could become increasingly onerous. The evidence did not show that she knew Morrison would die soon or accepted the deal expecting a trivial period of service. By the contracting period Morrison had apparently recovered from the acute hospitalization, remained optimistic, and possessed normal life expectancy measured in years. A warning about her earlier serious illness did not establish that Ruby anticipated early death at contracting. Id. at 278–279.

From Morrison’s perspective, personal attention from a compatible trusted caregiver had value beyond routine institutional care. Earlier care did not supply the new bargain’s contractual consideration, but the relationship and those services properly informed whether the new undertaking was fair. Morrison retained farm income and medication responsibility and had no immediate family dependent on this property as the natural object of her bounty. Her effort to change her will and demand for witnessed terms supported satisfaction with the arrangement. Id. at 279.

The defense’s hints of exploitation based on confusion during hospitalization did not overcome the consistent evidence of mental alertness during the interval in which the agreement was reached. The court rejected taking advantage of weakness as a basis for refusing relief. This was an assessment of the evidence at the relevant time, rather than a rule that hospitalization or age can never matter to contract fairness. Id.

## Why service-value damages were inadequate here

Once the agreement was found fair and adequately supported, its subject was real estate. The court treated specific performance of an unobjectionable written contract to convey or devise land as the ordinary remedy; the ability to price services already rendered did not replace the promised land with their much smaller retrospective value. Its statement addresses the adequate legal remedy for breach of the land promise, not whether nursing services can ever be valued in money. Id. at 279–280.

The oral-contract cases involved different difficulties: failure to establish the promise, failure to perform, enforcement attempted during the promisor’s life, or the statute-of-frauds exception. Buxton’s reference to written contracts was dictum in an oral-agreement case involving payment of money, rather than performance of care services. Monetary reimbursement can often avoid the particular fraud justifying relief from the statute in an oral case; when a valid writing supplies the realty promise, that rationale does not generally displace specific performance. The court acknowledged that no prior Missouri decision had enforced a devise agreement after so brief a service period, but held that the case must stand on its own merits rather than treating that historical absence as a categorical bar. Id. at 280–281.

## Exact result and source limits

The decree requiring specific performance was affirmed. The shortened course of actual services did not make the fair prospective bargain unconscionable or entitle the estate to substitute a reasonable-value payment. Id. at 281.
