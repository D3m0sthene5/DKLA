# Air Courier Conference of America v. American Postal Workers Union, AFL-CIO

**498 U.S. 517 (1991) — Supreme Court of the United States — February 26, 1991.** Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote for six Justices. Justice Stevens concurred in the judgment, joined by Justices Marshall and Blackmun. All nine agreed with the result; there was no dissent. Postal unions challenged a rule permitting private couriers to carry letters for deposit in foreign postal systems. The Court held that the unions' employment interests fell outside the zone of interests of the postal-monopoly statutes they invoked. That ruling prevented their APA challenge without deciding whether the rule actually served the public interest. The three concurring Justices would instead have held that the Postal Service was exempt from APA judicial review.

## Facts and administrative history

The Private Express Statutes, codified in parts of Title 18 and Title 39, protected the Postal Service's monopoly over carrying letters. The monopoly helped finance uniform-rate service to communities whose mail was expensive to deliver. Without it, private couriers could take the profitable routes while leaving the Postal Service with the costly ones. Congress also allowed suspension of the statutory restrictions on a mail route when the public interest required it. The case concerned the scope of that suspension authority, not a claim that Congress had prohibited every form of private carriage. **519–520, 525 n.4.**

In 1979, the Service suspended restrictions for extremely urgent letters, allowing private overnight delivery. Couriers subsequently used that permission for international remailing: they bypassed the Postal Service and deposited letters with foreign postal services for delivery to foreign addressees. The Service initially regarded that practice as a misuse of the urgent-letter exception and proposed a clarification in 1985 that would exclude it. Numerous adverse comments emphasized international remailing's cost, speed, reliability, and benefits to international business. The Service reconsidered, conducted a further rulemaking and public meeting, and adopted a regulation permitting the practice. **519–521.**

The American Postal Workers Union and the National Association of Letter Carriers represented employees who might lose work when private couriers carried this mail. They challenged the regulation under APA §702, arguing that the administrative record did not justify a finding that suspension of the monopoly was in the public interest under 39 U.S.C. §601(b). Air Courier Conference of America intervened to defend the regulation. **520–522.**

## Procedure and the questions before the Court

The District Court granted summary judgment to the Postal Service and the courier association. The D.C. Circuit vacated that judgment. It held that the unions satisfied the zone-of-interests requirement because the Postal Reorganization Act of 1970 included labor protections, and the postal monopoly supplied revenue essential to the postal enterprise and its employees. On the merits, the circuit concluded that the agency had conceived the public interest too narrowly by focusing on users of international mail. The courier association sought Supreme Court review. **521–522.**

The District Court had found that competition could adversely affect postal employment, and that injury-in-fact ruling was not appealed. The Supreme Court therefore addressed the separate statutory zone-of-interests question. Actual economic harm did not automatically entitle the unions to enforce every statute bearing on the business that employed them. **523–524.**

A threshold complication concerned 39 U.S.C. §410(a), which exempts the Postal Service from specified federal laws, including the APA's judicial-review provisions. The Service, although a nominal respondent, supported the courier association and argued for this exemption. The Court declined to decide that issue: it had not been argued or decided below, the courier association had not raised it in its certiorari petition, and it fell outside the questions on which review was granted. Footnote 3 explained why this was not an unavoidable jurisdictional defect: APA review concerns a cause of action, and the exemption could operate as a waivable defense. The Court could assume the availability of the action while resolving the zone-of-interests issue. **522–523 & nn.1–3.**

## Holding and reasoning

The relevant zone was that of the statute whose alleged violation supplied the substantive claim—the Private Express Statutes. Their language and history protected postal revenue and service to the public, rather than employment opportunities for postal workers. Their exception for private carriage of properly stamped letters was especially revealing: the government could receive its revenue even when its employees did not carry the mail. The statutory concern was payment for a functioning nationwide postal system, not retention of the work itself. **524–527 & n.4.**

The Court also examined the monopoly's historical development. Early postal operations relied substantially on contracted carriage, and the later struggle with private competition concerned deficits, uniform rates, and dependable national communication. That history did not establish a congressional design to preserve a particular workforce. The unions were not themselves competitors of the Postal Service or the private remailers; the competitive injury recognized in other zone-of-interests cases did not resolve their employment claim. **526–528 & n.5.**

The unions tried to link the monopoly provisions to the labor protections enacted in the Postal Reorganization Act. The Court rejected that connection without declaring that related statutory provisions can never inform the inquiry. In *Clarke*, two banking provisions had an integral relationship: one established a restriction and the other supplied an exception. Here, the postal monopoly and labor-management rules shared a broad codification, but no comparable operational relationship. Treating an entire title containing many different postal provisions as the relevant statute would erase the limits of the zone-of-interests test. **528–530.**

Congress had reenacted the monopoly provisions without substantive changes and directed the Service to study them. The legislative history did not connect them to the Act's labor protections. Even if the monopoly was the Postal Service's financial linchpin, that fact did not permit beneficiaries of a different statutory program to enforce the monopoly's public-interest restriction. The employees' economic concern was understandable, but it was not the interest protected by the provisions on which their challenge depended. **529–530.**

## Judgment and separate reasoning

The Court **reversed the D.C. Circuit's judgment**. It held that the unions lacked standing for this APA challenge and expressly did not reach whether international remailing served the public interest. The reported mandate says “Reversed”; it does not contain an additional express Supreme Court remand instruction. The ruling neither affirmed the regulation's substantive legality nor denied that employees might suffer economic injury. **530–531.**

Justice Stevens, joined by Justices Marshall and Blackmun, accepted the result on a different basis. He found §410(a)'s APA exemption unambiguous. Because APA review was unavailable, the Court's standing discussion answered a hypothetical question unnecessarily. He regarded the exemption as an issue the Court could notice on its own motion and did not find it necessary to decide waiver. His starred footnote also observed that the government had raised the exemption in opposing certiorari and that the circuit had acknowledged it before proceeding to review. This was an argument about the availability of judicial review, **not a more restrictive alternative zone-of-interests analysis**. **531–532 & starred note at 531.**
