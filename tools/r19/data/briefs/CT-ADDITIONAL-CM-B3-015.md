# Matter of People v. Quality King Distributors, Inc.
209 A.D.3d 62, 173 N.Y.S.3d 221 (1st Dep’t 2022); 2022 NY Slip Op 05010 | Contracts

## Decision and setting

On August 23, 2022, the First Department reversed the dismissal of New York’s COVID-era price-gouging proceeding against Quality King and reinstated the petition and answer for further proceedings. Higgitt wrote the opinion, with Manzanet-Daniels, Shulman, and Rodriguez concurring. The court rejected the wholesaler’s vagueness challenge and found a prima facie showing of several excessive-price transactions, but left cost justification, the full extent of violations, and appropriate relief for the trial court. This is the 2022 appellate judgment, not the 2020 order it reversed. (64–65, 80–84.)

## Facts, pleadings, and lower ruling

Quality King supplied grocery and discount stores, from national chains to local retailers. The relevant product was Lysol disinfectant in 19-ounce spray cans. Consumer complaints in February and March 2020 prompted the Attorney General to investigate and obtain the wholesaler’s sales and purchase records. Although the petition mentioned other Lysol products, the submitted evidence identified specific transactions involving only these cans. (65–66; n. 2.)

In May 2020, the Attorney General petitioned against Quality King and its CEO under General Business Law § 396-r and Executive Law § 63(12). She alleged that median monthly prices for twelve-packs rose from $51 before the crisis despite relatively stable acquisition costs, and that March’s gross profit margin was about 75 percent above November’s. The petition identified at least 432 sales between February 1 and April 7, involving 3,835 twelve-packs or 46,020 cans. It sought an injunction, accounting, restitution, disgorgement, and civil penalties. These totals and profit assertions were the Attorney General’s allegations, not the appellate court’s final liability findings. (66–67.)

Quality King denied gouging, alleged increased procurement and distribution costs, and asserted that the statute was unconstitutionally vague. After answering, it moved to dismiss under CPLR 3211(a)(1) and (7). The trial court’s September 23, 2020 order denied the petition, dismissed it against the CEO, and effectively dismissed the whole proceeding. It compared prices around the Governor’s March 7 emergency declaration, considered pricing across different Lysol products, and emphasized the absence of uniform increases. No formal judgment terminating the proceeding had been entered. Both sides appealed the order and also a so-ordered oral-argument transcript. (67–68; nn. 3–4.)

## Governing law and competing positions

The parties agreed that the pre-June 2020 statutory version controlled. The Attorney General had to establish an abnormal market disruption, goods vital and necessary for consumers’ health, safety, and welfare, and an unconscionably excessive sale or offer price. Excessiveness could rest on an extreme excess, unfair leverage or unconscionable means, or a combination. A prima facie case could compare the challenged price with the seller’s usual pre-disruption price or with prices readily available in the trade area. The seller could rebut it by proving additional costs outside its control. Here the Attorney General relied on the seller’s own historical prices, not the trade-area alternative. (72–74; nn. 1, 9.)

The Attorney General argued that a uniform scheme was unnecessary and that the trial court started the disruption too late. She proposed January 31, 2020, when federal health officials declared a public-health emergency. Quality King disputed when a disruption began, criticized the use of median prices rather than transaction-specific comparisons, challenged whether Lysol was necessary, and maintained that higher costs justified its prices. It also disputed the availability and calculation of remedies. As a special proceeding, the case required summary determination only insofar as no triable factual issues existed; disputed issues warranted a trial under CPLR 410. (69–71, 74.)

## The market disruption and necessity findings

The statute listed alternative causes of disruption. A Governor’s declaration was one alternative, not a prerequisite for every case. Demand for disinfectants had sharply increased and strained supply; Quality King itself acknowledged that the manufacturer had sold out and could not keep pace by February, if not earlier. The appellate court selected February 26, 2020, when CDC warnings made imminent community spread and the need for national preparation explicit. By then a national emergency had changed this product’s market. (75–77.)

The court rejected both proposed endpoints. The January 31 federal declaration enabled administrative responses but did not yet establish a national emergency in the statute’s ordinary sense. Waiting until March 7 would leave earlier emergency pricing outside the statute despite its remedial purpose. Footnote 8 recognized that this emergency developed gradually; the February 26 selection balanced the parties’ rights, the statute’s purpose, and the identified milestones. (77–78; nn. 7–8.)

Lysol was vital and necessary from the perspective of consumers confronting the emerging pandemic. Later disagreement about surface transmission or the disinfectant’s efficacy did not undo the reasonable early belief that disinfecting surfaces addressed a grave danger. The exceptional demand also created a temporary imbalance in bargaining power. (78–79.)

## Price comparisons, defenses, and constitutional ruling

Detailed transaction records permitted customer-specific comparisons instead of monthly medians. Prices ordinarily varied with customer type, order size and frequency, and delivery location. Generally, each post-February 26 purchase should therefore be compared with the same customer’s price immediately before the disruption. For a new customer without a historical purchase, footnote 10 directed comparison with a similar type of customer. (79–80; n. 10.)

For example, Jack’s American Outlet paid $60 to $66.09 per case on February 24 and $88.42 on March 16. CVS paid $62.40 on February 26 and later $84.01, $91.56, and $95.96. Clinton Variety’s February prices of $56.09 and $61.96 rose to $108.99 on April 1. These transactions established prima facie gross disparities on several occasions. They did not resolve which additional transactions violated the law or whether increased costs justified particular increases. Section 396-r could reach an individual violation without a uniform practice; repeated or persistent illegality remained relevant to Executive Law § 63(12). The trial court could order an accounting, an evidentiary hearing, or both. (80–81.)

Quality King was not aggrieved by an order dismissing the proceeding, so its separate appeal was dismissed. Its constitutional argument was nevertheless considered as an alternative basis to affirm. The statute regulated economic conduct through civil remedies and therefore received the relaxed vagueness review applicable to economic regulation. Read as a whole, its contextual standards gave ordinary persons adequate notice and constrained arbitrary enforcement. The absence of a fixed numerical pricing threshold was not fatal. Quality King supplied no precedent supporting a different state-constitutional result. (81–83.)

## Disposition and limits

The court reversed on the law, without costs, reinstated the petition and answer, and remanded. It unanimously dismissed Quality King’s separate appeal and both transcript appeals without costs. The transcript appeals were redundant because the written order supplied the same material findings and rationale. The court did not set a restitution amount, decide the disputed disgorgement arguments, or impose a particular penalty. Footnote 11 expressly reserved the parties’ remedy contentions as premature, while observing the statute’s mandatory civil-penalty language upon a violation. (83–84; nn. 4, 11.)
