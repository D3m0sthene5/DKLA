# In re National Football League Players Concussion Injury Litigation

**307 F.R.D. 351 (E.D. Pa. 2015) — April 22, 2015.** Judge Anita B. Brody. **Civil Procedure.** This is the district court’s final certification and settlement-approval decision, with its appended final order and judgment. The January 2014 refusal of preliminary approval and the Third Circuit’s 2016 affirmance are distinct stages. The assigned shared editorial account appears in CivPro PDF 446–448, printed 866–869.

## Facts and the path to the revised settlement

Retired professional football players alleged that the NFL knew about the long-term neurological dangers of repeated head impacts but concealed information and failed to protect players. The lawsuits asserted negligence and fraud, among other state-law theories. Players reported dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, ALS, and other symptoms; survivors also invoked postmortem diagnoses of chronic traumatic encephalopathy, or CTE. These were allegations and settlement evidence, not a judgment establishing the NFL’s tort liability. The memorandum identifies a July 19, 2011 California suit by seventy-three players as the first concussion case, followed by additional cases including Easterling. That chronology is more precise than the textbook’s description of Easterling as the first suit. By the settlement period, the multidistrict litigation encompassed more than 300 lawsuits and approximately 5,000 players. Claims against helmet manufacturer Riddell remained outside this NFL settlement. 307 F.R.D. at 361–363.

The NFL argued that collective-bargaining agreements preempted the claims under §301 of the Labor Management Relations Act. The court stayed discovery, received extensive briefing, and heard argument in April 2013. It did not resolve the motion before approving settlement. Preemption was therefore a serious litigation risk, not an established merits holding. The court ordered mediation in July 2013 under retired Judge Layn Phillips. Lawyers used medical experts, legal analysis, and a database of impaired plaintiffs while negotiating a proposed $765 million arrangement. In January 2014, Kevin Turner and Shawn Wooden filed the settlement class complaint, but the court refused preliminary approval because the capped monetary fund might run out during its sixty-five-year life and because of concerns about the proposed releases. A financial special master had been appointed in December 2013, before that refusal. Id. at 363–365 & n.8.

After further negotiations, the parties removed the monetary-award cap and narrowed the releases. The court granted preliminary approval in July 2014, disseminated notice, and held a November fairness hearing. It then required additional improvements, incorporated in February 2015: credit for NFL Europe play, protection of baseline examinations despite the assessment fund’s cap, extension of the CTE-death deadline to final approval, and improvements to claims procedures. The textbook’s five-month account concerns revision after the January refusal; final approval occurred in April 2015, after notice, objections, a hearing, and these further amendments. The memorandum reports 208 remaining opt-outs at this stage and 205 objectors joining eighty-three written objections. The later appeal’s count of 202 opt-outs should not replace the district court’s count. Id. at 364–365, 369–370 & n.22.

## What the settlement provided

The class included retired NFL players, authorized representatives of deceased or incapacitated players, and derivative claimants. It was divided into independently represented subclasses: those without a qualifying diagnosis before preliminary approval and those already diagnosed, including specified deceased players. The division addressed different interests in immediate compensation and future protection. The monetary-award fund was uncapped, adjusted for inflation, and available for sixty-five years. Players with a qualifying diagnosis did not have to prove that NFL football caused the condition or establish individual tort damages. Maximum awards ranged from $1.5 million for Level 1.5 neurocognitive impairment to $5 million for ALS; the intervening maxima were $3 million for Level 2 impairment, $3.5 million for Parkinson’s disease, $3.5 million for Alzheimer’s disease, and $4 million for death with CTE before final approval. These were maxima, subject to the settlement’s schedules and offsets. Id. at 365–368; award table at 366.

Awards varied with age at diagnosis and credited seasons. The agreement also provided offsets for specified severe traumatic brain injury, stroke, and failure to participate in baseline assessment, with exceptions. A player whose condition worsened could seek a supplemental award. The separate $75 million, ten-year baseline-assessment program provided examinations and some treatment benefits for lesser impairment; it did not itself diagnose Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, or ALS. A $10 million education fund supported education and prevention. Qualifying diagnoses made before the settlement’s effective date by appropriately credentialed professionals were honored under the specified rules; diagnoses after that date required the settlement’s approved physicians or providers and diagnostic criteria. Id. at 365–369 & nn.19–21.

Registration ordinarily had to occur within 180 days of notice of effectiveness, with good-cause extensions. Claims generally had to be submitted within two years of diagnosis or supplemental notice, whichever was later, with additional hardship protection. Claims required medical and employment documentation, but the NFL had an obligation to supply missing employment records. The process allowed representatives, assistance of counsel, correction of deficient packages, appeals, monthly audits, and judicial supervision. The $1,000 appeal fee was refundable on success and subject to hardship waiver. Releases concerned head, brain, and cognitive injuries against the NFL parties; they did not eliminate claims against the NCAA, amateur or youth football organizations, or Riddell, or eliminate preserved collective-bargaining and benefit rights. Id. at 367–369, 414–418.

## Questions and holdings

Could this proposed settlement class independently satisfy Rule 23(a) and Rule 23(b)(3), despite different diseases, career histories, and present versus future injuries? Was the amended settlement fair, reasonable, and adequate under Rule 23(e), with adequate notice and an opportunity to opt out?

The court answered both questions yes. Settlement did not dilute Rule 23’s certification requirements. It certified the class and subclasses under Rules 23(a) and 23(b)(3), approved the whole amended agreement under Rule 23(e), and entered final judgment. Approval did not adjudicate liability or award the requested attorneys’ fees. Id. at 370–382, 386–396, 423–426.

## Why certification was proper

The class of approximately 20,000 retired players readily satisfied numerosity. Commonality rested on the alleged shared NFL conduct: withholding information and failing to protect players against head injuries. Different seasons, playing conditions, symptoms, and potential award amounts did not eliminate that common question. The court distinguished the decentralized employment decisions in Wal-Mart. Turner, who had ALS, and Wooden, who lacked a qualifying diagnosis, presented claims typical of their respective subclasses because the alleged conduct and corresponding legal theories were common. Id. at 370–373.

Adequacy required attention both to competent representation and to possible conflicts within the class. The court found experienced counsel, sustained adversarial negotiation, capable representatives, and genuinely separate subclass representation. Named players need not personally negotiate every term. The fee arrangement deserved examination, but negotiation of fees followed agreement on substantive terms, fees would be paid separately, and the court retained authority to refuse an unreasonable fee. The NFL’s promise not to oppose fees up to $112.5 million was not itself an award. Id. at 373–375.

The decisive conflict analysis concerned whether differently situated players were sacrificing one another’s interests. Unlike a fixed fund that present claimants could exhaust, an uncapped fund with inflation protection and supplemental awards protected later claimants. Different awards and offsets reflected the severity, value, and litigation risks of different claims, rather than inherently antagonistic groups. Independent subclasses, the mediator, the financial special master, and continuing supervision reinforced those protections. Wooden had reason to seek broad protection because he did not know what illness he might develop. The court rejected an additional subclass for every possible symptom or disease because such fragmentation was unnecessary here and could defeat a workable settlement. This was a case-specific finding of no fundamental conflict, not permission to disregard established conflicts. Id. at 375–379.

Common questions also predominated: shared allegations about the NFL’s knowledge, conduct, and scientific relationship to injury bound a defined group engaged in a common activity. Individual damage calculations did not overwhelm that common nucleus. The court distinguished Amchem’s much more heterogeneous asbestos population. Class resolution was superior to decades of repetitive and uncertain litigation; trial-management difficulties need not be assessed for a class created exclusively for settlement. Id. at 379–382 & n.37.

## Notice and the court’s settlement review

The court examined both the content and distribution of notice. Summary and long-form notices described the lawsuit, class, relief, releases, hearing, objection rights, and opt-out consequences. Individual mailing used more than 30,000 addresses from thirty-three datasets, supplemented by publication, a website, and telephone assistance. The notice expert estimated that the program reached ninety percent of the class; due process demanded reasonable methods, not proof that every person actually received notice. The original notice described the preliminary-approval CTE deadline because the later extension had not yet been negotiated. The amendments improved class benefits without new concessions to the NFL, so they did not require a second notice and opt-out period for the whole class. Id. at 382–386 & nn.40–43.

Under the then-applicable Rule 23(e), the judge acted as a fiduciary for absent members. Experienced counsel and an agreement reached at arm’s length supported an initial presumption of fairness, but did not replace independent examination. Applying Girsh and relevant Prudential factors, the court considered complexity, cost and delay, class reaction, information available to counsel, risks of proving liability and damages, risks to maintaining a class, capacity to pay, and the settlement’s relation to reasonable possible recoveries. Medical and historical evidence would be difficult to develop, causation and preemption remained uncertain, and impaired players needed timely help. Informal investigation, expert analysis, and the database supplied meaningful information despite the absence of formal discovery. The NFL’s ability to pay was neutral because it had not claimed inability to pay more. An uncapped compensation program was reasonable in light of litigation risks, even though it was not the largest imaginable recovery. Id. at 386–396.

The court postponed the fee decision, leaving objectors an opportunity to contest a later petition and receive a Rule 23(h) hearing. The clear-sailing provision did not compel approval of $112.5 million or any other amount. This preserved scrutiny rather than approving counsel’s compensation indirectly through the settlement. Id. at 375, 395–396.

## Specific objections and the limits of the reasoning

CTE was the most contested issue. The judge evaluated the scientific record available in 2015, including limited, selected postmortem samples and disputed inferences about symptoms and causation. These are historical descriptions of that record, not a present medical assessment. She concluded that many cognitive symptoms associated with CTE would qualify for dementia-based awards without requiring proof of CTE itself. The absence of compensation solely for mood or behavioral symptoms reflected difficult causation questions and competing risk factors. The court’s discussion of an expert’s estimate that at least eighty-nine percent of subjects in the examined studies would qualify did not establish an eighty-nine-percent rate for all retired players. Id. at 396–401 & nn.50–55.

The special death-with-CTE benefit was limited to death before final approval. The court discussed both avoiding an incentive to suicide and, more importantly, compensating families of deceased players who had lacked sufficient notice to obtain qualifying diagnoses. Living players could seek diagnoses and claim benefits for compensable symptoms. Future scientific developments would be discussed at ten-year intervals in good faith, but modification required agreement and court oversight; this was not a guarantee that every later-recognized condition would automatically be covered. Id. at 401–403 & nn.56–59.

The judge also examined diagnostic thresholds, age and season adjustments, and the seventy-five-percent severe-injury and stroke offsets. The latter could be overcome by clear and convincing evidence that the other injury did not cause the qualifying condition. She distinguished the severe injury triggering the offset from the repetitive mild injuries alleged in the litigation. Standardized baseline examinations, stopping rules for impaired examinees, qualified providers, representation and cure procedures, good-cause extensions, and independent administration addressed objections that the system was too burdensome. Reasonable registration was an administrative prerequisite to benefits, not an impermissible replacement of the opt-out class with an opt-in lawsuit. Id. at 403–418 & nn.71–85.

The education fund was a distinct negotiated benefit rather than leftover money distributed cy pres; alternatively, it was suitably related to football injury prevention. Representatives of players who died before 2006 still had to establish timeliness under applicable state law. Footnote 90 explains that federal American Pipe tolling did not itself govern the state claims; relevant state analogues remained for examination. Security provisions, reserves, future trust arrangements, continuing NFL obligations, judicial remedies for material default, and lien administration supported payment without guaranteeing against every future risk. Paid experts were not presumptively biased, and objectors had neither an absolute right to discovery nor a right to force public disclosure of all NFL information as a condition of private settlement. Id. at 418–423 & nn.90–95.

## Judgment and significance

The appended final order certified the class and subclasses, confirmed representatives and counsel, approved the agreement, incorporated its documents, and required performance. It appointed administrators and joint special masters, preserved insurers’ policy obligations, and expressly disclaimed any admission of liability. It dismissed the class complaint and related pending lawsuits with prejudice, reserved fee motions, retained continuing exclusive enforcement jurisdiction, and excluded opt-outs from the binding settlement. Later changes required court approval and written consent of the parties’ counsel. Id. at 423–426, final-order paragraphs 1–18 & n.96. No separate judicial opinion accompanies Brody’s decision.

The decision illustrates how subclass structure, an uncapped fund, informed negotiation, effective notice, and active judicial supervision can address the concerns raised by Amchem and Ortiz. Its lesson is conditional: settlement convenience does not excuse Rule 23 defects, and approval of a compensation compromise does not establish the defendant’s underlying liability. The assigned account compresses the several procedural stages; this brief preserves the April 2015 district decision and its own factual counts and findings. The full memorandum, all ninety-five memorandum notes, and the appended order with its additional numbered note were read; the settlement’s separately incorporated contractual exhibits were not reproduced in the report and are not represented here as separately read.
