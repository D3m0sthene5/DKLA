# Memphis Light, Gas and Water Division v. Craft
436 U.S. 1 (1978) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and judicial posture

On May 1, 1978, the Supreme Court affirmed the Sixth Circuit’s judgment that the process afforded Willie S. and Mary Craft before municipal utility cutoffs was constitutionally inadequate. Tennessee law permitted termination only for cause and recognized protection for a customer’s bona fide billing dispute, giving the Crafts a protected property interest in continued service. Notice had to explain an available means of disputing the bill, and the customers had to receive a timely opportunity to present their complaint to personnel authorized to correct errors. An informal conference could satisfy that requirement. Justice Powell wrote for six Justices; Justice Stevens dissented with Chief Justice Burger and Justice Rehnquist. (3, 9–22.)

The Court adjudicated the federal procedural question, rather than finally awarding damages or ordering that utilities furnish free service. The damages demand kept the controversy alive after the billing problem had been clarified, but its validity remained for initial determination below. The Court also declined to adjudicate additional procedural safeguards the Crafts requested without a cross-petition. These reservations matter when identifying what the affirmance actually established. (7–9 & n.8; 12 n.12.)

## Meters, disputed bills, and repeated terminations

Memphis Light, Gas and Water Division was a municipal division directed by commissioners appointed by the City Council and ultimately controlled by the city. Its status exempted it from regulation by the state public service commission. When the Crafts moved into a former duplex in October 1972, the house had two gas meters, two electric meters, and one water meter. Relying on the seller’s information, they believed the second gas and electric meters were inactive. In 1973 they began receiving separate monthly bills, one identifying Willie S. Craft and another Willie C. Craft. Each bill included a city service fee. In October a meter reader explained that both sets were running. A plumber and electrical contractor attempted to consolidate them, but failed to do so correctly. The Crafts continued receiving two bills through January 1974 and suffered five service terminations during the period of disputed nonpayment. (3–5 & nn.1–3.)

Mrs. Craft repeatedly missed work to seek an explanation at the utility’s offices. The District Court found her efforts to understand apparent double billing were in good faith, but she did not obtain a satisfactory explanation or instructions for further recourse. The opportunity to discuss the problem with management had not adequately been explained to her. The litigation later disclosed that the accounts had not properly been combined in the utility’s computer billing and that gas charges and city service fees included overcharges. Those findings do not mean that every bill from the two physically separate meters was necessarily a duplicate charge; the dissent emphasized the distinction between split billing for separate consumption and genuinely duplicated fees. (4–5; 8 n.7; 9 n.8; 24 & n.5.)

The utility’s general procedures included a mailed bill, a later final notice, electric disconnection, and subsequent disconnection of remaining services if payment or other arrangements were not made. Employees could postpone a cutoff upon certain explanations, and a hardship program permitted payment of half an overdue bill with the remainder spread over the next three bills. Credit counselors and management personnel were nominally available to handle complaints, with a possible appeal to the commissioners. The constitutional issue concerned meaningful access to that process for these customers, not merely whether the utility could describe a general organization for handling complaints. (5–6 n.4; 13–17.)

## Proceedings, arguments, and continuing controversy

The Crafts and other customers sued in February 1974 under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, seeking declaratory and injunctive relief and damages from the utility and officials and employees. After trial, the District Court denied class certification and entered judgment for the defendants. It doubted that continued service during disputed nonpayment was protected property and also rejected the due-process claim. Nevertheless, it expressed hope that the Crafts would receive a $35 credit for unnecessary charges and expenses and recommended clearer termination notices. The credit was issued and notice procedures changed. The Sixth Circuit affirmed the denial of class certification but held that the process afforded the Crafts violated due process. (3–7 & nn.1–5.)

The utility sought Supreme Court review of state action, property, and procedural adequacy, but abandoned the state-action contention. The original dismissal of the municipal division itself under the then-applied understanding of section 1983 was unchallenged; the Court’s references to the utility also covered the individual petitioners sued personally and officially. The brief therefore does not retrospectively resolve a municipal-liability question that was outside the review presented. (3–4 n.1; 7 n.6.)

Because class certification remained denied, continuing jurisdiction depended on the Crafts’ own claims. They no longer sought a hearing on an unresolved bill or alleged a presently threatened cutoff. Their demand for actual and punitive damages nevertheless prevented mootness. The Court did not need to invoke the exception for disputes capable of repetition yet evading review. Issuance of the recommended $35 credit did not settle the damages claim: the recommendation was not an adjudicated damages award, and the claim was not plainly insubstantial or foreclosed. (7–9 & nn.7–8.)

## State-created entitlement and constitutional procedure

The substantive interest arose from Tennessee law; federal law determined whether that interest constituted protected property. Tennessee decisions distinguished just, undisputed bills from bona fide disputes over charges. The utility could cut off service for nonpayment of proper charges but did so at its peril when disputed charges were wrong. It owed nondiscriminatory service and could deny it only for good and sufficient cause. An entitlement subject to payment of properly due charges was still protected; property did not cease to be property because ownership or entitlement was disputed. (9–12 & nn.9–11.)

Existing state remedies demonstrated recognition of the interest, but did not themselves determine the federal adequacy of procedure. Nor did the state’s permission to collect just charges eliminate the exception for customers who paid the undisputed amount while contesting the rest. The Court distinguished a utility permitted to terminate only for cause from one operating entirely at will. (10–12.)

## Notice and a usable opportunity to contest the bill

The final notice explained the payment deadline and threatened cutoff. The majority accepted that it conveyed the threatened loss, but found that it did not adequately describe how to challenge the amount. One flyer, sent in areas covering about 40 percent of customers, offered credit assistance without mentioning disputes. Another referred to disputes, but there was no assurance that the Crafts received it. Mrs. Craft’s repeated unsuccessful inquiries illustrated the failure to inform her of access to authorized personnel. The requisite official did not have to be a corporate officer: the relevant authority was the power to review the bill and correct errors. (13–15 & nn.13–16.)

Notice and hearing were connected protections. For customers of varied education, experience, and resources, telling them merely to pay or suffer loss of an essential service could fail to make the opportunity to object effective. The majority explained that customers should know where, when, and before whom disputed bills could be considered. It did not universally require utilities to teach all available legal remedies. It also acknowledged that the utility’s revised notice, expressly describing credit counselors and review of disputed bills, might be adequate; that revised system was not the procedure adjudicated here. (14–16 & nn.15–16.)

The opportunity itself could be informal. A customer needed a chance to explain an overcharge or charge for service not provided to a designated employee empowered to resolve it. The Court treated the lower courts as having concurrently found that the necessary opportunity was not made available to Mrs. Craft and found no basis for overturning those findings. A later unexplained demand that she pay the other account, or her eventual discovery of the problem after repeated visits, did not establish a timely and accessible process before cutoff. (16–17 & nn.17–19.)

## Balancing and the insufficiency of litigation alone

Applying Mathews v. Eldridge, the majority balanced the private interest, risk of erroneous deprivation and value of added safeguards, and government burden. Electricity, gas, and water were necessities; even a short interruption could threaten health and safety. Computer-based billing presented a meaningful risk of error. An informal meeting well before the scheduled cutoff would serve the utility’s own interest in accurate bills and need not materially delay collection. The utility retained substantial discretion over scheduling and structuring that opportunity and could terminate after considering the dispute and finding the charges justly due. (17–19 & nn.20–22.)

The alternative remedies—a pretermination injunction, damages after cutoff, or payment followed by a refund action—did not cure the procedural defect. Restoration could not undo the loss of essential service during the interval, and ordinary litigation was poorly suited to factual billing disputes involving amounts too small to justify counsel and suit. Procedural constraints and delay made injunctions less effective than an accessible internal conference. Payment first could be impracticable for customers unable to meet unexpected charges, even under a plan requiring an immediate half-payment. (19–22 & nn.23–28.)

The Court distinguished circumstances where reliable decisionmaking and limited loss permitted action without additional advance safeguards, including school discipline in Ingraham and license revocation following convictions for which judicial hearings had already occurred. It did not announce that damages are always insufficient under due process. The holding depended on this deprivation, its error risk, and the modest administrative protection available. (19–21 & nn.24–26.)

## Stevens’s dissent and the limits of affirmance

Stevens agreed that Tennessee customers had an entitlement to continued service while paying undisputed charges, but disputed the majority’s reading of the record and the constitutional significance of its demands. The notices supplied deadlines and a conspicuous information-center telephone number; employees could delay cutoff and refer complaints to counselors. In his view Mrs. Craft had consulted officials with adequate authority but failed to persuade them that her disputed consumption charges were wrong. A deficient explanation of how to approach management did not establish that no responsible employee had heard her. (22–25 & dissent nn.1–7.)

The dissent considered notice of the threatened loss and time to respond sufficient in this setting. Requiring an explicit description of the internal complaint route was paternalistic and, even on the majority’s premises, involved distinctions better handled through administrative rulemaking. The utility already processed tens of thousands of complaints. Stevens also considered judicial remedies and the deterrent force of potential damages adequate protection. He criticized the majority for relying on hypothetical threats to health and safety rather than harm established in the Crafts’ record. His conclusion was that the Constitution did not require a procedure so simple that every person could act effectively without counsel. (25–30 & dissent nn.8–12.)

The majority affirmed, but expressly left unresolved whether other circumstances required an impartial decisionmaker, fuller opportunities to rebut records, or a written decision. Its footnote 12 distinguished the more extensive remedy in Palmer, noting that the findings warranting it were not present here. The Court did not award the Crafts damages or decide their remaining damages entitlement. (9 n.8; 12 n.12; 22.)
