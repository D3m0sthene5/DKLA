# Cheney v. United States District Court

**542 U.S. 367 (2004) · Supreme Court of the United States · June 24, 2004 · Civil Procedure**

**Assigned account:** CivPro, physical PDF page 624, printed page 1237, Note 3. Justice Kennedy wrote the Court's opinion. Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, and Breyer joined it entirely; Justices Scalia and Thomas joined Parts I–IV but opposed its remand disposition. Thus seven Justices joined those common discussions, while the judgment returning the matter to the court of appeals was **5–4**. Stevens also concurred separately; Thomas, joined by Scalia, concurred in part and dissented in part; Ginsburg, joined by Souter, dissented. 542 U.S. at 372, 392–405.

## Facts and the dispute over discovery

Shortly after taking office, President George W. Bush established the National Energy Policy Development Group to advise him about energy policy. Its appointed members were federal officials; Vice President Cheney chaired it and could invite other federal officers. Five months later it issued a final report, and the Government said it ceased operating. Judicial Watch and the Sierra Club brought separate suits, later consolidated, alleging that private individuals and lobbyists had participated as actual members despite their lack of formal appointments. That allegation mattered because the Federal Advisory Committee Act imposed public-meeting and disclosure requirements on covered advisory committees but excluded committees composed wholly of qualifying federal officers or employees. The plaintiffs sought access to materials they said the Act required to be public. 373–374.

Their theory depended on the D.C. Circuit's earlier *Association of American Physicians & Surgeons v. Clinton* decision, which recognized possible de facto committee membership. The plaintiffs wanted discovery to establish whether nongovernment participants had exercised membership functions. The Government disputed the statute's applicability and argued that discovery into presidential advisers' work would itself invade constitutionally protected executive deliberation. Private participation was an allegation to be investigated, not an established finding that the task force violated FACA. 374–377.

The District Court dismissed the nongovernment defendants because FACA did not itself supply a private cause of action and dismissed the task force because it no longer existed. It nevertheless permitted claims against executive officials through the federal mandamus statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1361, and claims against agency defendants under the Administrative Procedure Act. It postponed deciding whether a clear nondiscretionary duty supported mandamus and whether the agency-action requirements were met. It also postponed the constitutional objections. Discovery, the court reasoned, would develop the membership facts needed to decide the statutory and constitutional questions. 374–375.

Although the court described the contemplated discovery as tightly controlled, the approved requests swept broadly across task-force meetings, participants, communications, staffs, and subgroups. They demanded much more than particular evidence of private membership. The Court later reproduced six categories of requests to show how extensively they penetrated the advisers' activities. The Government sought protection from discovery and certification for interlocutory appeal under § 1292(b); the District Court denied certification. 375–377, 387–388.

## Procedure and competing positions

The Government filed both an appeal and a petition for mandamus under the All Writs Act, § 1651. It sought vacation of the discovery orders, resolution on an administrative record and any appropriate affidavits, and dismissal of the Vice President. A divided D.C. Circuit denied mandamus. Relying on *United States v. Nixon*, it reasoned that the Government first had to assert executive privilege with particularity. If the District Court sustained the privilege, ordinary proceedings could protect the Government; if it rejected the privilege, mandamus might then become appropriate. The court also dismissed the interlocutory appeal. The Supreme Court reviewed the denial of mandamus. 376–378.

The respondents defended discovery as necessary to determine whether FACA applied, and argued that the Government had not exhausted available protection below. They also argued that the petition came too late. The Government answered that requiring broad document identification and privilege assertions would itself impose serious executive burdens before the plaintiffs established that the statute covered the task force. These positions raised a procedural question distinct from ultimate FACA liability: could the court of appeals refuse to consider mandamus until executive privilege was formally invoked? 378–383.

## Rules and reasoning

The Court first rejected the timing objections. Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 4(a)'s deadline governs notices of appeal, not petitions under the All Writs Act. The Court left the availability and timeliness of collateral-order appellate review undecided. Nor did laches bar the petition: the Government had actively sought less drastic relief, rather than sleeping on its rights. A party should not be penalized for pursuing alternatives when mandamus ordinarily requires showing that adequate alternatives are unavailable. 378–380.

Mandamus remains extraordinary. The petitioner must have no other adequate means of obtaining the desired relief; must establish a clear and indisputable entitlement; and, even after satisfying those requirements, must persuade the issuing court that the writ is appropriate in the circumstances. These conditions constrain mandamus without making it impossible. Here they had to be evaluated with sensitivity to the separation of powers. The Vice President and senior officials advising the President were themselves the targets of discovery. Courts must consider whether their orders impair another branch's constitutional work. Presidential officials are subject to law, but the confidentiality and practical burden of presidential consultation differ from those of an ordinary private litigant. 380–382.

The central error was treating *Nixon* as requiring a particularized privilege claim before the Government could raise a separation-of-powers objection. *Nixon* concerned specifically identified evidence needed in an ongoing criminal prosecution, where the judiciary's constitutional responsibility and the defendant's fair-trial interests gave evidentiary production exceptional weight. This civil suit sought information to determine whether a statutory duty existed at all. Civil discovery lacked the same criminal-process filters and did not carry the same constitutional demand for identified evidence. The contrast concerned both the litigation's function and the orders' breadth; it was not a declaration that every civil request fails. 383–387.

The task-force requests would have compelled production of the materials the plaintiffs sought as final relief, and more, before deciding whether they were entitled to that relief. Requiring the Executive to examine and log the entire mass of material, then invoke privilege document by document, could itself create the institutional burden. Executive privilege is a consequential constitutional claim, not a routine step courts must always force before considering narrower ways to resolve a dispute. The lower courts could consider limiting discovery before bringing the branches into such a confrontation. 387–390.

The majority also disagreed with the appellate court's characterization of the Government's conduct. It identified objections to the scope and burdens of discovery, even though the Government's principal position was that discovery should not occur. In deciding whether mandamus was appropriate, the appellate court had to consider not just ordinary judicial usurpation or clear abuse of discretion but unwarranted impairment of another branch's constitutional duties. 388–391.

## Exact judgment and what remained open

The Court **vacated the D.C. Circuit's judgment and remanded for further proceedings**. It did **not** direct issuance of mandamus or itself dissolve the discovery orders. The appellate court had prematurely ended its inquiry on a mistaken reading of *Nixon*; it should exercise its own discretion after considering the separation-of-powers objections and other arguments. The Court left issues concerning the de facto membership theory and appropriate further proceedings to that court in the first instance. It also urged sensitivity to Government requests for interlocutory review of these questions. FACA coverage, the plaintiffs' ultimate entitlement to disclosure, and an absolute immunity from discovery were not adjudicated. 391–392.

## Separate writings

Stevens thought the discovery order should have been vacated, but joined the Court's remand because the D.C. Circuit had developed the de facto membership theory and should direct the next proceedings. Ordinary broad discovery was poorly fitted to a suit seeking mandamus for disclosure: discovery giving plaintiffs the very records demanded as ultimate relief effectively anticipated a merits victory. His starred note suggested limited interrogatories or depositions about substantive participation, such as voting on recommendations or drafting the report, rather than unrestricted exploration. This was Stevens's proposed approach, not a majority determination that particular outsiders were members. 392–393 and starred note at 393.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, would have required the court of appeals to issue the writ. The plaintiffs themselves sought mandamus under § 1361, yet needed sweeping discovery even to establish a right to relief. In his view, that defeated the clear-entitlement requirement and could improperly convert mandamus into a freestanding vehicle to enforce statutes lacking a private remedy. His position would terminate the challenged discovery more directly than the Court's discretionary remand. 393–395.

Ginsburg, joined by Souter, would have affirmed denial of mandamus. She agreed the requests were overly broad but read the procedural record differently: the Government sought protection from all discovery instead of meaningfully seeking a narrower plan that the District Court had invited. It therefore had an available avenue of relief below. Her nine notes address, among other things, the tentative character of the appellate order, prior FOIA production, the actual review burden, the possibility of narrow membership discovery, and the distinction between a clear legal duty and facts needed to prove its violation. She rejected the idea that the District Court clearly erred by awaiting a party's request rather than rewriting discovery itself. 396–405, particularly 399 n.5, 402 n.7, and 404–405 n.8.

## Assignment and source comparison

The assigned Note 3 accurately identifies the privilege-precondition error and gives all three mandamus requirements. Its description of the Court as having “reversed” should be read against the actual mandate: the Court **vacated and remanded**, leaving issuance of the writ undecided. The surrounding notes discuss other mandamus decisions; they are context and do not add findings about this task force. The full original report, including all three separate writings and their notes, supplies the distinctions omitted by the short assigned account.
