# Charter Township of Ypsilanti v. General Motors Corp.
201 Mich. App. 128, 506 N.W.2d 556 (1993) | Contracts

## Court and result
The Michigan Court of Appeals issued its per curiam decision on August 3, 1993. The original report identifies Presiding Judge Reilly and Judges Sawyer and P. J. Clulo; the designation note explains that Clulo was a circuit judge sitting by assignment. No separate writing or dissent is recorded. The court reversed the injunction preventing General Motors from moving Caprice and station-wagon production from Willow Run, and allowed GM to tax costs. The trial court had clearly erred in finding a definite promise of continued production; reliance would also have been unreasonable in the documented hearing context. The court expressly reserved the unresolved unjust-enrichment claim and its viability. (128, 130–134, 138–141; designation note at 130; n.1 at 132.)

## Tax abatements and consolidation
GM operated Hydra-Matic and Willow Run plants employing approximately 9,000 and more than 4,000 people, respectively. Ypsilanti created development districts in 1975 for Hydra-Matic and 1977 for Willow Run. It granted eleven abatements over time, eight involving Hydra-Matic and three Willow Run. The statute encouraged job creation or retention through exemptions for qualifying industrial facilities. The litigation concerned the 1984 and 1988 Willow Run abatements. (130–131.)

On July 17, 1984 the township approved a twelve-year, fifty-percent personal-property tax abatement for a $175 million investment introducing a new car; the State Tax Commission later issued the certificate. In April 1988 GM announced a new rear-wheel-drive Chevrolet Caprice project at Willow Run. Its October 7 application sought another twelve-year, fifty-percent personal-property abatement, this time for a $75 million investment. The township approved it after public hearings, and the commission issued the certificate. (131.)

On December 18, 1991 GM announced consolidation of Willow Run and Arlington, Texas production at Arlington. It attributed the change to record losses and declining Caprice demand: projected annual sales of 330,000 had run around 275,000 and fallen below 100,000 by late 1991. Those explanations were GM's claims, not a judicial finding that a contractual condition excused a proved promise. The appellate court instead concluded that the alleged production promise had not been made. (131–132, 138–141.)

## Litigation and the trial court's promise finding
The township sued on April 29, 1992. Washtenaw County joined voluntarily; Michigan initially appeared as amicus and was added as a plaintiff by the trial court. Claims included statutory and conduct-based contract theories, promissory estoppel, unjust enrichment, and misrepresentation. After a lengthy trial, the circuit court found that the abatement statute and application did not create a contract. It nevertheless required GM, through promissory estoppel, to retain Caprice production at Willow Run as long as GM made that model. Its February 9, 1993 injunction prevented transfer of the sedan and specified station-wagon production. (130–133.)

The trial court emphasized plant manager Harvey Williams's prepared statement that completion of the project and favorable market demand would allow Willow Run to continue production and maintain continuous employment. It interpreted the presentation and abatement history as a promise not to move that work elsewhere. The court described loss of thousands of jobs after substantial tax concessions as grossly unfair. The appellate court reviewed factual findings for clear error and concluded that neither equitable concern nor community expectations established the requisite actual promise. (132–134.)

The first note identifies claim limits. Misrepresentation was incorporated into the estoppel ruling rather than separately analyzed. Environmental nuisance had been voluntarily dismissed without prejudice. Unjust enrichment was neither discussed nor decided below; the appellate court expressed no opinion on the claim or whether it remained viable. Reversal therefore should not be described as an adjudication of every possible restitution claim. (132; n.1.)

## Expectations and statutory representations were not a clear promise
Promissory estoppel required an actual, clear, definite promise inducing reasonable action or forbearance, with enforcement necessary to avoid injustice and the remedy potentially limited as justice required. Curry did not establish liability simply from any favorable prediction: its clear promise distinguished it from this dispute, as the second note emphasizes. (133–134; n.2.)

The court gave several reasons for rejecting the trial court's inference. Seeking abatements with assurances about jobs was consistent with the legislation's purpose, not itself evidence of a commitment to operate indefinitely or for a specified term. The statute required certification that completion had a reasonable likelihood of creating or retaining employment or preventing its loss. That eligibility representation was different from a guarantee of continued production. Corporate hyperbole and promotion similarly did not necessarily create a binding undertaking. (134–135.)

The history of plant tours, lunches, presentations, and abatement applications showed efforts to obtain a statutory benefit. Much of that course of conduct concerned Hydra-Matic rather than Willow Run. The State Tax Commission's economic-development concerns were its own expectations, not GM's promises. Comptroller Russell Hughes's statement about roughly 5,000 employees since 1981–1982 described history, not an assurance about future employment. The township assessor's confidence that GM had kept its word was also the assessor's assessment rather than a new undertaking by GM. (135–137.)

## Williams's language and the hearing as a whole
Williams described the $75 million investment, changes needed for a new 1991-model car, and increased ability to produce front- or rear-wheel-drive vehicles. His statement said that project completion and favorable market demand would allow production and employment to continue. The parties disputed the meaning of favorable demand, but the court read the statement as a qualified expectation of what the investment would enable, not a commitment to continue these lines at Willow Run whenever GM produced them anywhere. Statements about cooperation and future investment added promotional language rather than a definite duration obligation. (137–139.)

Even assuming a promise, the record defeated reasonable reliance. County chairman Dillard Craiger explicitly asked for a duration commitment and complained that none existed. At the hearing, Hughes warned about job losses from declining market share. Craiger repeated that GM would not say whether or when it might move. Other speakers likewise said GM was promising nothing, that sales could not be predicted, and that the plant could not remain open if sales fell. Township officials did not contradict those concerns or require a response from GM. (139–141.)

Supervisor Prater corrected a speaker's misunderstanding of the tax category but did not challenge his statement that no promise existed. The township then unanimously approved the twelve-year abatement without conditioning it on operation for any particular period. That contemporaneous record contradicted reasonable reliance on a guaranteed duration. The court reversed the injunction and allowed GM costs. It did not order repayment of abatements or find that a proved guarantee had been breached. (140–141.)
