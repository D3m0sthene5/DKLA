# American Civil Liberties Union v. Department of Defense

**Citation:** 406 F. Supp. 2d 330 (S.D.N.Y. 2005). **Court and judge:** Southern District of New York, District Judge Alvin K. Hellerstein. **Stages:** December 19, 2005 order denying relief; the same report also contains the January 10, 2006 final-judgment clarification. **Course:** Civil Procedure — newly discovered evidence and Rule 60(b). **Assigned:** CivPro.pdf, physical pages 597–598, printed pages 1179–1180, Note 1 following Patrick.

## Facts and procedural posture

The plaintiffs sought records about detainee interrogation through FOIA. The disputed requests were Item 29, a Justice Department memorandum specifying interrogation methods the CIA could use against top Al-Qaeda members, and Item 61, a presidential directive concerning foreign detention facilities and/or interrogation methods. The CIA gave Glomar responses: it would neither confirm nor deny whether responsive documents existed. This was a response about existence, rather than an acknowledgment that particular documents existed followed by a refusal to produce them. (331.)

The September 29, 2005 partial-summary-judgment decision upheld these two responses. It treated Item 1, a memorandum interpreting the Convention Against Torture, differently: the CIA had to produce responsive material or establish an exemption. On November 2 the court denied the CIA's motion to reconsider Item 1. The parties subsequently agreed that plaintiffs would forgo appeal concerning Items 29 and 61 if the CIA forwent appeal concerning Item 1. These were different document requests and earlier procedural stages. (331.)

Later press reports described public acknowledgment of CIA agents' participation in interrogations and discussion of the requested documents. Plaintiffs characterized these reports as newly discovered evidence and sought partial relief under Rule 60(b)(2) and (6), asking that the CIA produce the Items 29 and 61 documents or establish an exemption. The court decided the reopening motion; it did not determine the legality of any interrogation practice. (331–332.)

## Issue, governing rule, and holding

**Did the new media material justify reopening the ruling sustaining the two Glomar responses? No.** The evidence would not change the earlier ruling and was cumulative of material already submitted. Nor could the same newly-discovered-evidence grounds be reassigned to Rule 60(b)(6). (332.)

The court described Rule 60(b) relief as extraordinary and requiring exceptional circumstances. Its four-part Rule 60(b)(2) test required evidence of facts existing at the time of the earlier decision; excusable ignorance despite due diligence; admissible evidence probably effective to change the result; and evidence that was not merely cumulative. Its Rule 60(b)(6) test required extraordinary circumstances and grounds outside clauses (1) through (5). These are the rules applied in this historical decision. (332.)

The motion failed on the third and fourth elements of the Rule 60(b)(2) test. Under the court's earlier Glomar reasoning, press disclosures did not suffice to overcome the response. To the extent sourced in the media, the new material also repeated evidence already presented; the requested documents had been extensively discussed in the press. The denial therefore did not depend on a finding that plaintiffs lacked diligence or that the underlying facts arose only after the prior decision. (332.)

Rule 60(b)(6) did not cure that failure. Plaintiffs' asserted ground was newly discovered evidence, already recognized in clause (2). The court consequently held clause (6) unavailable on these asserted grounds and denied relief under both provisions. It did not announce that clause (6) can never support relief in a FOIA case. (332.)

## Glomar reasoning and judicial reservations

The court explained that its earlier decision followed precedent affording broad protection to intelligence sources and methods. Even public press disclosures could leave the boundary between acknowledged and concealed information significant to foreign intelligence agencies. This reasoning supported the existing Glomar ruling; it was not a factual determination that every news report was false. (332–333.)

The judge nevertheless reiterated concern that loosely regulated Glomar responses could promote excessive classification, conceal information already public, or protect embarrassment rather than intelligence interests. He also recognized FOIA's independent judicial duty to assess exemptions de novo and, when necessary, examine documents in camera. Bound by the precedents he had applied, he thought the legal and policy questions warranted appellate review. That criticism did not grant the plaintiffs' reopening motion. (333.)

## Exact orders and separately reported clarification

**December 19:** the motion for relief from the September 29 order, as supplemented November 2, was denied. Both sides were allowed to appeal all or part of those orders and this denial notwithstanding their contrary agreement. The court's third numbered direction provided an additional thirty days from its date for noticing appeal, subject to existing appellate scheduling and stay orders. This brief records that direction as entered; it does not independently certify its validity under current appellate rules. (333.)

**January 10, 2006:** the separate final-judgment order clarified that judgment was final concerning claims adjudicated through the summary-judgment motions and that there was no just reason to delay their appeal under Rule 54(b). It described defendants' appeal concerning the Darby photographs and plaintiffs' appeal concerning Items 29 and 61, and discussed appealability of disclosure orders and denials. It did not reverse the December denial, order disclosure of Items 29 and 61, or award damages. (333–334.)

The report itself refers to a clarification letter dated January 4, **2005**, despite placing it after the December 2005 order; it also uses September **19**, 2005 in its concluding recital while repeatedly identifying September **29** elsewhere. These internal date inconsistencies are preserved as source discrepancies rather than silently corrected into new facts. (333–334.)

## Textbook comparison and source verification

The assigned note accurately states the four-part newly-discovered-evidence test, the cumulative/effect-on-result grounds for denial, and the inability to move these grounds into clause (6). Its shorthand that judgment favored the government should be confined to the disputed Items 29 and 61: the broader earlier decision granted and denied partial-summary-judgment motions and treated Item 1 differently. The note omits the appeal directions and subsequent final-judgment clarification. Patrick, Title, and Hazel-Atlas are surrounding, separate decisions. (Assigned 597–598; actual 331–334.)

The entire reported judicial text, both separately headed orders, all five original reporter pages, and both complete assigned pages were read and compared. No judicial footnote, dissent, concurrence, or appendix appears. Native reporter extraction scrambled column order; the original images supplied the controlling sequence and confirmed the two internal date inconsistencies.
