# Microsoft Corp. v. Baker

**137 S. Ct. 1702 (2017) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 12, 2017.** Justice Ginsburg's opinion was joined by Justices Kennedy, Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan. Justice Thomas concurred in the judgment, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justice Alito. Justice Gorsuch did not participate. Civil Procedure: appellate finality, voluntary dismissal, and Rule 23(f). Assigned accounts: CivPro physical PDF 438, printed 847, and physical 626, printed 1238–1239. Pinpoints below use the preserved official slip opinion; Thomas's concurrence has its own page sequence.

## Facts and the earlier litigation

Xbox 360 owners alleged that a design defect caused the console to scratch and destroy game discs during ordinary use. An earlier proposed class suit had sought relief for the same alleged problem. In that suit the district court denied certification because individual causation and damages issues predominated, the Ninth Circuit denied permission for an immediate Rule 23(f) appeal, and the plaintiffs eventually settled individually. Those proceedings supplied the background for a new suit; the Supreme Court did not find the product defective or resolve purchasers' damages claims. **Court slip 8.**

In 2011 the present plaintiffs, represented by some of the same counsel, proposed a nationwide Xbox-owner class in the same district court. They argued that an intervening Ninth Circuit decision had changed the governing certification law sufficiently that the court should not defer to the earlier denial. The district court disagreed: it concluded that the new precedent had not undermined the earlier causation analysis and that comity required adherence to the prior result. It struck the new complaint's class allegations. **8–9.**

An order striking class allegations has the same relevant effect as denying class certification. Plaintiffs therefore requested permission from the Ninth Circuit under Rule 23(f) to appeal immediately. They argued that their small individual claims made litigating to a final merits judgment economically irrational. The court of appeals denied permission. The district court could still revise its class ruling; plaintiffs could continue individually and later appeal, seek the district court's separate §1292(b) certification, or settle. Instead they selected a different route. **9–10 & n.7.**

## The attempted route to an appeal as of right

Plaintiffs voluntarily dismissed their individual claims with prejudice, intending to appeal only the earlier order striking class allegations. They asserted that, if successful on certification, their claims could return to life on remand. Microsoft agreed to dismissal while expressly maintaining that it did not create a right to appeal the class ruling. Plaintiffs did not challenge the dismissal they had requested. **10.**

The Ninth Circuit accepted appellate jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. §1291. It viewed the dismissal as sufficiently adverse and final because it did not embody a settlement. Reaching the class issue, it held that the district court had misread circuit precedent and improperly applied comity. It did not decide that a class must be certified; other certification issues were left for the district court. The Supreme Court reviewed whether this dismissal mechanism permitted an appeal under §1291 and Article III. The majority resolved the statutory question and expressly did not decide the constitutional one. **10–11 & n.8.**

## The governing structure: finality and discretionary class appeals

Section 1291 generally authorizes review of final district-court decisions. The final-judgment rule balances trial and appellate responsibilities and avoids repeated interruptions, expense, and delay. A certification decision is ordinarily interlocutory: it does not resolve the underlying claims and remains subject to revision. Once an individual claim reaches final judgment on its merits, its representative may obtain review of the denied class treatment. **1–4, 9–12.**

In *Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay*, the Court had rejected a “death-knell” approach that treated class denial as final when individual litigation was economically impractical. That approach invited successive appeals if certification was denied on different grounds, bypassed §1292(b)'s screening, and aided plaintiffs without offering equivalent review to defendants threatened by large class liability. The practical pressure of an interlocutory ruling was not enough to turn it into a final decision. The collateral-order doctrine likewise did not apply. **2–5 & nn.1–3.**

Congress later authorized rulemaking about finality and interlocutory appeals. Rule 23(f), adopted in 1998, provided a calibrated alternative: a court of appeals could permit review of a certification grant or denial in its discretion. This route did not require district-court certification under §1292(b), but it also did not make every certification decision immediately appealable as of right. The rulemakers examined competing proposals and deliberately chose discretion to provide protection against improvident class rulings without routine litigation delays. The rule also did not automatically stay trial proceedings. **5–8 & nn.4–6; 13 n.9.**

## Majority holding and reasoning

The five-Justice majority held that plaintiffs' engineered voluntary dismissal did not qualify as a final decision under §1291 permitting review of the class order. Finality does not depend solely on whether a document purports to close a case. It must be construed in light of the statute's function and the prescribed appellate structure. The majority identified three connected defects. **12–17.**

First, the device invited repeated interlocutory appeals. Under the rejected death-knell doctrine, appellate judges at least assessed whether individual litigation remained economically feasible. Under plaintiffs' theory, plaintiffs alone could force immediate review by dismissing. If successful and the district court again denied certification on a different ground, they could repeat the procedure. The actual Ninth Circuit judgment illustrated that prospect, because it resolved only comity and left other certification objections open. **12–14.**

Plaintiffs argued that dismissal promoted efficiency because losing on appeal would end their claims permanently. The majority answered that weak merits claims could make that risk attractive, particularly when obtaining class certification could induce a substantial settlement. It did not find these particular claims weak or decide that the alleged defect was nonexistent. It explained why a plaintiff's willingness to risk its own claims did not protect the overall appellate system from piecemeal litigation. **13–14 & nn.9–10.**

Second, mandatory review through dismissal would defeat Rule 23(f). Congress had designated rulemaking as the method for deciding which interlocutory decisions should be immediately appealable, and the rulemakers had already addressed class orders. The plaintiffs had requested and been denied discretionary review; their theory would let them bypass that denial—or avoid applying under Rule 23(f) altogether. Calling the dismissal a final judgment could not erase that structural conflict. Their appeal attacked only the earlier class order, while preserving the hope of reviving claims after success. **14–16.**

Third, the tactic was one-sided. Plaintiffs could use dismissal to obtain immediate review of a denial, whereas defendants could not similarly force review of an adverse certification grant. Defendants also faced economic pressure to settle when class liability was potentially enormous. Rule 23(f)'s evenhanded discretionary system should not be displaced by a device available only to plaintiffs. **17.**

## Thomas's different jurisdictional rationale

Justice Thomas, with Roberts and Alito, agreed that appellate jurisdiction was absent but disagreed with the majority's interpretation of statutory finality. The dismissal with prejudice ended all individual claims and left only execution of judgment. In his view that satisfied §1291, and concern about bypassing Rule 23(f) should not alter the statute's meaning. **Thomas slip 1–3.**

He instead found no Article III controversy. By voluntarily dismissing their claims, plaintiffs had consented to judgment and disclaimed relief from Microsoft. The class allegations were a procedural mechanism for litigating individual claims, rather than an independent substantive claim capable of preserving adversity after the underlying claims disappeared. A wish to revive them after a favorable class decision did not establish a presently existing controversy. Nor, in his view, could deciding certification itself reverse the merits consequences of their voluntary dismissal. That constitutional explanation belonged to the three-Justice concurrence; the majority expressly reserved it. **Thomas 3–4; Court 11.**

## Exact judgment and textbook distinctions

The Court **reversed the Ninth Circuit and remanded for further proceedings**. All eight participating Justices agreed with that outcome, with five using statutory finality and three using Article III. The Court did not decide whether Xbox purchasers ultimately satisfied Rule 23 or whether Microsoft was liable. **Court 17.**

The first assigned account compresses the Ninth Circuit's decision into a reversal of the certification denial; the full opinion makes clear that the appellate ruling concerned comity and left certification's remaining grounds open. The second account incorrectly says the district court denied permission to appeal under Rule 23(f). That permission was requested from and denied by the Ninth Circuit. Its account of plaintiffs' reserved right to revive claims accurately identifies the feature the majority criticized. The accompanying *Brown v. Cinemark* discussion describes another case and does not convert Microsoft into a holding about every negotiated settlement.
