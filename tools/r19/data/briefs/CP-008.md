# Diniero v. United States Lines Co.
288 F.2d 595 (2d Cir. 1961), cert. denied, 368 U.S. 831 (1961) | Civil Procedure

## Facts and disputed injury theory

Julio Diniero, a junior third assistant engineer aboard the Pioneer Land, claimed that repeatedly operating a defective blow-down valve between April and September 1954 injured his back, eventually requiring disc removal and spinal fusion. Ordinarily a reach rod operated the valve through a slot in a floor plate. Diniero testified that defects and the absence of a reach rod required him to remove the plate, crouch, and apply force with a wrench. The shipowner denied both the defect and the supposed need for this method. Medical evidence also disputed whether Diniero's condition resulted from that work or a degenerative disc disease. The eight-day trial presented factual questions about working conditions, how Diniero operated the valve, and causation. Full opinion, opening factual paragraph.

At the close of evidence, the judge gave a charge the appellate court considered unobjectionable and submitted eight questions under Rule 49(b). Two supplied the general-verdict alternatives; questions two through six concerned unseaworthiness, negligence, contributory negligence, and proximate cause. The troublesome first question combined whether Diniero injured himself with whether operating the valve required plate removal, crouching, and exerting force with a wrench rather than his hand. Full opinion, paragraphs introducing the questions.

## Deliberations and the judge's response

The jury began deliberating at 2:45 p.m. At 5:40 it asked the meaning of the word indicating that Diniero had to perform the described acts. The judge's explanation alternated between assuming the necessity of those acts and telling the jury it still could decide whether they were necessary. He also described the question as asking whether Diniero was injured aboard the ship in the manner he claimed. This attempted clarification left unclear whether the jury was deciding causation on assumed working conditions or independently resolving the necessity and occurrence of all those conditions. Full opinion, paragraph beginning with the start of jury deliberations and following quoted explanation.

At 6:40 the jury reported that it could not agree on question one and saw no prospect of doing so. The judge withdrew all the questions and directed the jury to consider a general verdict. That note was not read to counsel until after the verdict, but no objection to the delay in disclosure was made. Nor did withdrawal produce immediate agreement. At 9:22 the jury again reported an inability to agree; the judge quoted Allen and returned the jury to deliberations at 9:30. Counsel objected to withdrawing the questions, but not to this further deliberation instruction. At 10:30 the jury returned a $46,150 verdict for Diniero and was polled. Full opinion, paragraphs describing the 6:40 and 9:22 notes.

The judge separately allowed $9,012 on the reserved maintenance-and-cure claim in an opinion reported at 185 F. Supp. 818. The only issue raised on this appeal was whether withdrawal of the questions was error; the appellate court was not separately reviewing the maintenance-and-cure merits or an Allen-charge objection. Full opinion, procedural paragraph following the initial question.

## Arguments and governing principle

The shipowner argued first that Rule 49(b) authorizes submitting interrogatories but never withdrawing them once deliberations begin. Alternatively, it argued that the first question addressed facts necessary to recovery and that withdrawing it let a jury unable to agree on those facts reach a plaintiff's general verdict anyway. That could undermine the control and consistency checks supplied by written factual answers.

The Second Circuit rejected the categorical prohibition. A trial judge's express discretionary power to submit questions implies power to withdraw them in proper cases, subject to appellate review for abuse of discretion. The rule does not make the first formulation irrevocable when a confusing question impedes rather than assists the deliberations. Footnote 1 sets out Rule 49(b), including its requirements for handling answers inconsistent with each other or with a general verdict. This case involved withdrawal before any answers or verdict were returned, rather than disregarding completed inconsistent findings.

## Why withdrawal was permissible here

The panel found inherent ambiguity in question one, confirmed by the jury's request for clarification and the judge's inconsistent explanation. Withdrawal removed confusion caused by an improperly formulated question. Whether to clarify again, declare a mistrial, or withdraw the questions and ask for a general verdict was a matter of judgment. The jury's continued deliberations for nearly four hours after withdrawal further distinguished an abrupt general verdict replacing a required unresolved factual answer. Full opinion, paragraph discussing inherent ambiguity and judicial choices.

The state authorities addressed in the full opinion and footnotes 2–4 supported discretion in proper cases but also supplied limits. Receiving a general verdict while the jury remains unable to agree on proper, material factual questions can be error because the verdict lacks a necessary foundation. Likewise, withdrawing clear and necessary interrogatories over objection after a short delay, followed quickly by a plaintiff's verdict, can prejudice the defendant and constitute an abuse of discretion. The court agreed with that general warning. It held the warning inapplicable because the confusing first question could not fairly be treated as a properly framed material question necessary to the verdict. Full opinion, final comparative-authority paragraphs and nn.2–4; final discussion at 600.

## Judgment and assigned scope

The Second Circuit affirmed. Medina wrote for a panel with Friendly and Smith; no separate opinion appears. The result preserves Diniero's judgment without establishing an unrestricted judicial power to discard material questions whenever jurors disagree. The controlling distinction is between repairing confusion from an ambiguous question and abandoning a proper factual question that reveals failure to agree on an indispensable issue.
