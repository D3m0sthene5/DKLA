# Hubner v. Spring Valley Equestrian Center — Supreme Court
203 N.J. 184, 1 A.3d 618 (2010) | Contracts

## Court and judgment
Justice Hoens delivered the New Jersey Supreme Court’s opinion on July 28, 2010. Justices Long, LaVecchia, Albin, Wallace, and Rivera-Soto joined; Chief Justice Rabner did not participate. The six participating justices unanimously reversed the Appellate Division and reinstated the Law Division’s summary judgment for Spring Valley. The Equine Activities Liability Act barred this claim because the injury resulted from an inherent risk. The court expressly did not decide the alternative issue whether the contractual release was valid. There are no separate writings. (203 N.J. at 184, 188, 208.)

## Accident and proposed expert report
On September 3, 2005, Gloria Hubner visited Spring Valley with her husband, relatives, and friends for horseback riding. Michael was also a plaintiff, but the opinion focused on Gloria’s accident. She signed a release before entering the ring for training preceding a trail ride. Employee Kate Martin instructed her on steering and handling the horse, including pulling back if it reared its head. Wooden rails, or cavaletti, lay near the ring’s center for a planned exercise preparing riders for trail conditions. A portable mounting block was also nearby. Gloria declined to use it and mounted from the ground with Martin’s assistance. Martin then led horse and rider away from the mounting area, leaving them facing the rails and block while other riders mounted. (Id. at 189–190 & n. 1.)

The horse slowly turned about 180 degrees, putting the equipment behind it, then moved its head, whinnied, and backed up. Gloria screamed and pulled backward on the reins. Martin told her to stop; she complied, but the horse continued backward, accelerated, tripped over a rail, and fell. Gloria was thrown against the mounting block and injured. The court treated these material circumstances as undisputed, while giving her the favorable inferences applicable on summary judgment. (Id. at 188–190, 207.)

Andrew Elder’s proposed expert report attributed the accident to unsecured rails near the mounting area and behind the horses, and to the block left behind the horse. He reasoned that a horse unable to see behind itself could become frightened and fall upon stepping on an unsecured pole. Footnote 2 clarifies that Elder had never been formally offered as an expert and that the parties had not litigated his qualifications. The Supreme Court’s ultimate rejection of the factual assumption supporting his opinion should therefore not be mistaken for an adjudication that he had been qualified or a general ruling excluding every equine expert. (Id. at 190 & n. 2; 207–208.)

## Procedural history and parties’ arguments
The Law Division granted summary judgment because the horse’s unpredictable movement and collision with the rails were inherent risks covered by the Act. It found that the rails were not faulty equipment but part of the ring. As an alternative ground, it enforced the release. The Appellate Division reversed and remanded, reasoning that equipment placement could support a statutory exception and that the report created a factual issue; it also rejected enforcement of the exculpatory clause in those circumstances. Those were the lower courts’ rulings, not the Supreme Court’s ultimate conclusions. Certification followed. (Id. at 190–192 & nn. 3–4.)

Spring Valley argued that good-condition equipment was not faulty, that the horse’s turning and backward movement caused the accident, and that broad statutory protection should not be swallowed by expansive exceptions. Hubner defended the panel’s construction, invoking both knowingly supplied faulty equipment and negligent disregard for participant safety. The Farm Bureau and Horse Council supported protective construction based on equine activity and agriculture. The court separately observed that, although the Horse Council invoked insurance concerns, New Jersey’s expressed legislative intent did not explicitly include that rationale. (Id. at 192–193, 203 n. 8.)

## Reading the statutory scheme as a whole
The Act’s findings identify equine activity’s economic importance and preservation of open space, and allocate essentially impractical or impossible-to-eliminate risks to participants. Section 5:15-2’s nonexhaustive inherent-risk definition includes horses’ propensities and unpredictable reactions, collisions, and a participant’s failure to maintain control or act within ability. Section 5:15-3 deems those and other inherent conditions assumed; section 5:15-5 makes resulting claims completely barred notwithstanding comparative negligence. Section 5:15-9 supplies exceptions, including knowingly furnishing faulty equipment that contributes to injury and acts or omissions constituting negligent disregard for safety. (Id. at 195–197.)

Each provision looked plain in isolation, but broad exception language could eliminate the broad protection altogether. That latent ambiguity required harmonizing the whole enactment and examining legislative context. The court treated exceptions as strictly but reasonably construed in light of the statute’s purpose, rather than allowing general negligence terminology automatically to overcome the assumed-risk provisions. (Id. at 193–198.)

The legislative history of the Equine Act recited both assumed risks and exceptions without expressly resolving their interaction. The court therefore also examined the related Ski and Roller Skating Rink Acts and the distinction in Meistrich between primary assumption of risk—an inherent risk for which the operator owes no duty—and secondary assumption, involving exposure to a risk negligently created by an operator who does owe a duty. The recreational statutes preserve a line between assumed risks and breaches of specified operator responsibilities; comparative-negligence reform does not erase that line. This history supported broad protection for inherent equine risks and a correspondingly limited reading of exceptions where the provisions conflict. (Id. at 198–204 & nn. 5–8.)

## Meaning of the exceptions and application
The statutory protection does not excuse every act of the operator. Assigning a beginner to a known difficult horse, furnishing a saddle with a known broken strap, or intentionally injuring a participant could implicate an exception. But “faulty equipment” means equipment itself is faulty. Good-condition rails do not become faulty simply because an expert criticizes their unsecured placement. Improper placement instead must be evaluated under the separate negligent-disregard exception. (Id. at 204–205.)

That exception requires a breach of a recognized duty owed by the operator that caused the injury. A participant cannot avoid the statute merely by recasting an inherent-risk injury as an operator’s omission. The court illustrated the difference through a negligently maintained stall door falling on a participant, versus a frightened horse running into the door: the former could involve an operator’s duty, while the latter could remain an assumed equine risk. These were explanatory hypotheticals, not additional facts of Gloria’s accident. (Id. at 206–207.)

The proposed expert’s placement theory depended on a premise contradicted by the record. The employee did not initially position the equipment behind horse and rider. Both before and after mounting Gloria faced the rails, and they came to be behind her only because the horse turned. The rails were in good condition and served the planned training exercise. The later turning, head movement, backing, and collision were defined inherent dangers. The opinion identified no supported breach within a statutory exception causing this injury. Accordingly, it reinstated summary judgment without reaching release enforceability. (Id. at 188, 205, 207–208.)

## Assigned-stage correction and reading scope
C&M printed page 747 cites the 2009 appellate decision with the correct notation that it was reversed in 2010. Its subsequent prose, however, attributes an exception-based result and the quotation about unenforceable releases to the Supreme Court. The actual 2010 opinion records those propositions as the Appellate Division’s reasoning and reaches the opposite statutory result. In particular, the Supreme Court reserved the release issue, rather than declaring an exception to release enforceability. The separate B3-032 brief concerns that earlier appellate judgment; this B3-033 brief preserves the later reversal and reinstatement. References in the assigned account to a 2012 tentative law-revision report are editorial context, not a legislative amendment adjudicated by this 2010 court.
