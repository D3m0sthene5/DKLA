# Grouse v. Group Health Plan, Inc.

306 N.W.2d 114 (Minn. 1981) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B2-005

## Dispute and sequence of reliance

John Grouse, a 1974 pharmacy graduate, worked at Richter Drug in Minneapolis for about forty-one hours weekly at $7 an hour. He wanted a hospital or clinical position for its work environment, compensation, and benefits. After applying to Group Health in September 1975, he interviewed with its chief pharmacist, Cyrus Elliott, and later with general manager Donald Shoberg. Elliott asked him to wait while other candidates were interviewed. These preliminary discussions preceded the definite offer on which the claim rested. 306 N.W.2d at 115.

On December 4, Elliott offered Grouse a pharmacist position at Group Health's St. Louis Park Clinic. Grouse accepted, explaining that he needed to give Richter two weeks' notice. That afternoon he declined an offer from a Veterans Administration hospital in Virginia because he had accepted Group Health's position. Elliott subsequently called to confirm that Grouse had resigned. Thus Group Health knew of, and specifically checked, the concrete change of position following its offer. Id.

Only afterward did Elliott tell Shoberg that he had hired, or was considering hiring, Grouse. Shoberg required a favorable written reference, a background check, and general-manager approval. Two faculty members declined to provide references, while former work contacts reported insufficient experience with Grouse to evaluate him. Elliott did not contact Richter because Grouse had requested otherwise on his application. Unable to obtain a favorable reference, Shoberg hired someone else. On December 15, when Grouse reported that he was available to start, Elliott told him the position had been filled. A director apologized without further action. Grouse had difficulty recovering full-time employment and lost wages. The opinion calls these relevant facts essentially undisputed; it does not characterize the missing references as proof of professional unfitness. Id. at 115–116.

## Procedure and competing theories

Grouse sued for damages. The trial court entered judgment for Group Health on the ground that he had failed to state an actionable claim. The narrow appellate question was whether that conclusion was correct, rather than the amount of recoverable loss. Justice Otis wrote the Supreme Court of Minnesota's opinion. The reproduced report identifies no separate writing or numerical vote. Id. at 115–116.

The parties emphasized anticipatory repudiation of an at-will employment arrangement. Group Health argued that recovery would anomalously favor a person turned away before beginning work over an employee discharged after one day. The court instead located the remedy in promissory estoppel. Its historical formulation was that bilateral termination power left the parties' promises illusory and no contract existed in fact on these circumstances. Section 90 nevertheless permitted an obligation implied in law where foreseeable, induced action or forbearance made enforcement necessary to avoid injustice. Id. at 116.

## Holding and reasoning

Group Health should have expected its definite offer to cause Grouse to resign. Grouse actually did so, and Elliott verified the resignation. It would be unjust to refuse any relief after the employer induced that surrender and denied even an opportunity to perform. Grouse could reasonably assume he would receive a good-faith opportunity to do the job to the employer's satisfaction. That opportunity, rather than guaranteed permanent employment, explained the promise protected here. Id.

The court rejected the proposed before-work/after-work dividing line: under appropriate circumstances, section 90 could also apply after employment had begun. But it expressly cautioned that its conclusion did not impose liability whenever an employer discharged an at-will employee. The facts of foreseeable reliance and the denied opportunity mattered; at-will status did not itself settle the estoppel question in either direction. Id.

## Remedy and exact result

Section 90 relief could be limited as justice required and measured by reliance. Because Group Health's prospective employment could have ended at any time, the proper focus was less what Grouse would have earned there than what he lost by quitting Richter and declining at least one other offer. The opinion therefore supplied a basis and direction for recovery, not a calculation, fixed period of employment, or damages award. The court **reversed and remanded for a new trial on damages**. Id. at 116.
