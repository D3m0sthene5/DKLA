# Swift v. Tyson

**Civil Procedure · 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 1 (1842) · Supreme Court of the United States · January Term, 1842**

Justice Story delivered the Court’s opinion. The Court unanimously answered the certified question in the negative, protecting Swift against the defenses Tyson offered concerning the original transaction. Justice Catron separately agreed with that result but declined to endorse the opinion’s broader discussion of instruments pledged as collateral for an existing debt. The disposition was an answer certified to the circuit court, rather than an affirmance or reversal of a final judgment. This brief explains the historical decision; the neighboring assigned Erie excerpt expressly repudiates its general-law doctrine.

## Facts and procedural history

Nathaniel Norton and Jairus Keith drew a bill of exchange at Portland, Maine, dated May 1, 1836, payable six months later with the additional grace period described by Story. Tyson accepted it in New York. Norton then endorsed it to Swift in payment of an existing promissory-note debt owed by Norton and Keith. Swift took the accepted bill before maturity, without notice of a defect in the underlying land transaction and believing it justly payable. The bill was dishonored at maturity. **41 U.S. at 14–15.**

Swift sued Tyson as acceptor in the federal circuit court for Southern New York. Acceptance and endorsement were admitted. Tyson introduced Swift’s answer to a bill of discovery, establishing the circumstances in which Swift received the instrument. Tyson then offered to prove that his acceptance had been part payment for Maine lands supposedly owned by Norton and Keith; that their representations concerning ownership and value were fraudulent; and that they lacked title. Swift objected that defects between those original parties could not be asserted against him. The proposed proof should be understood as Tyson’s offered defense, not a final adjudication that Norton and Keith committed fraud. **Id. at 14–15.**

The circuit judges divided over whether Tyson could assert the same defense against Swift as against Norton or Norton and Keith, and whether the offered evidence was admissible against Swift. That question came to the Supreme Court through a certificate of division. The Court decided the legal effect of the admitted holder circumstances and directed a negative answer to be certified. **Id. at 14–15, 23–24.**

## Issues and holdings

The dispute presented two connected questions. Did the Rules of Decision Act require the federal court to follow New York’s decisional treatment of preexisting debt as consideration for negotiable paper? If the Court could decide under general commercial principles, did receiving the bill for an existing debt supply valuable consideration sufficient to protect an innocent holder from defenses between the original parties?

Story held that state judicial decisions concerning general commercial law were entitled to respect but were not conclusively binding under the Act as he construed it. Independently assessing commercial law, the Court held that an existing debt supplied valuable consideration. Swift’s timely, good-faith receipt without notice protected him from Tyson’s asserted antecedent equities. **Id. at 18–22.**

## The parties’ positions and the New York cases

Tyson argued that the acceptance made in New York should be governed by New York law, including its courts’ interpretation of the effect of an existing debt. His position treated the Rules of Decision Act’s reference to state laws as encompassing state judicial decisions. Swift argued that the commercial question could be decided independently and that accepting negotiable paper in payment of an existing obligation was valuable consideration. The report separately prints lengthy counsel arguments before Story’s opinion; those arguments are not additional holdings. **Id. at 3–14, 16.**

Story first examined the New York decisions themselves. He described earlier authority supporting existing debt as consideration, later cases pointing the other way, and recent decisions again undermining the restrictive view. He considered it doubtful whether New York’s rule had become finally settled, particularly because its Court of Errors had not definitively adopted the restrictive proposition. The decision nevertheless did not depend solely on that uncertainty: Story proceeded to assume the doctrine settled and ask whether it bound the federal court. **Id. at 16–18.**

## The historical reading of the Rules of Decision Act

Section 34 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 required state laws to serve as rules of decision where applicable, except when the Constitution, treaties, or federal statutes supplied otherwise. Story distinguished positive state enactments and fixed local usages from judicial opinions about general commercial principles. He regarded a judicial decision as evidence of law, rather than law itself, because courts could reconsider and alter their decisions. **Id. at 18.**

On this account, the Act bound federal courts to state statutes and state courts’ construction of them, as well as strictly local matters such as rights and titles to real property. General questions about ordinary commercial instruments were different: state and federal courts performed the same task of ascertaining principles through reasoning and analogy. State opinions deserved deliberate consideration, but did not conclusively determine the federal court’s judgment. Story associated the law of negotiable instruments with the broader commercial world rather than a single locality. **Id. at 18–19.**

The distinction explains why the presence of a Maine land sale did not itself resolve the dispute as a local-property question. The issue certified concerned the rights of a later holder of an accepted negotiable instrument. The Court was not deciding title to the land. Its independent-law holding concerned the commercial effect of transfer, notwithstanding the underlying transaction that generated Tyson’s defense.

## Existing debt as valuable consideration

The opinion began from the established protection of a good-faith holder who took negotiable paper for value before maturity without notice of defects between antecedent parties. It also described the holder’s prima facie entitlement: absent rebutting proof, the holder did not initially have to prove those favorable circumstances; the defendant bore the burden of overcoming them. Here, Swift’s good faith and lack of notice were established, leaving the legal sufficiency of the existing debt as the central dispute. **Id. at 15–16.**

Story considered payment of, and security for, existing debt consistent with ordinary commercial practice. Such transfers allowed a creditor to realize or secure an obligation and potentially extend credit or forbear enforcement. The debtor could employ negotiable securities without first selling them, perhaps at a disadvantageous discount, and then using the proceeds to pay the creditor. Excluding such transactions would impair circulation and the renewal or security arrangements commonly used by banks. **Id. at 19–20.**

The Court supported that reasoning with its own precedents, English decisions and commentary, and American state decisions. It acknowledged that American authority was not entirely uniform but concluded that an existing debt constituted valuable consideration for the bona-fide-holder protection. Swift therefore was not subject to the equities Tyson sought to assert between the original parties. **Id. at 20–22.**

## Catron’s separate writing

Catron agreed that extinguishing a debt through the transfer presented in the record protected the recipient against an instrument’s prior infirmities. But he would not endorse the additional proposition that merely taking an instrument as collateral for an existing debt produced the same protected position. That issue was outside the record and counsel’s arguments, and contrary state authority deserved consideration when the question actually arose. He stressed that he had not been prepared to decide that further issue. **Id. at 23.**

His concurrence therefore narrows what can fairly be called the unanimous decision. All agreed on the result for the payment transaction before the Court; Catron expressly withheld agreement on the principal opinion’s broader collateral-security discussion. There is no separate judicial dissent or judicial footnote in the complete report.

## Disposition and assigned significance

The Court ordered certification that Tyson was **not entitled to the same defense against Swift as against the original parties**, and that the offered defense evidence was **not admissible against Swift** on the stated facts. It did not separately enter a damages judgment or resolve the land-title allegations. **Id. at 23–24.**
