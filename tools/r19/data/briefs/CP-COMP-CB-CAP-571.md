# Montana v. United States

**440 U.S. 147 (1979) · Supreme Court of the United States · February 22, 1979**

Justice Marshall wrote for eight Justices. Justice Rehnquist also filed a brief concurrence; Justice White dissented. The Court reversed the three-judge federal district court on collateral-estoppel grounds without deciding the constitutionality of Montana’s tax. The assigned account appears on CivPro PDF page 677, printed 1344, before Martin v. Wilks.

## Tax and litigation chronology

Montana imposed a one-percent gross-receipts tax on contractors performing public construction, but not private construction. Public contractors could credit specified personal-property and income-tax payments against that liability. Remaining liability was customarily reflected in higher construction costs passed to the government financing the work. The statutory definition covered federal, state, and local public contracts exceeding $1,000 and corresponding subcontractors. 440 U.S. at 149–150 & nn.1–3.

In 1971, Peter Kiewit Sons’ Co., a contractor on a federal dam project, sued in state court. It alleged that the tax discriminated against the United States and those doing business with it. The United States directed and financed that litigation. Less than a month later, the government also sued in federal district court to challenge the tax. By stipulation, the federal action was continued pending the state litigation. The Montana Supreme Court unanimously upheld the tax in Kiewit I, rejecting Supremacy Clause and equal-protection objections. Kiewit filed a notice seeking Supreme Court review, but abandoned it at the Solicitor General’s direction. Id. at 151.

Kiewit then sued in state court concerning different tax payments. In Kiewit II, the Montana Supreme Court considered the new legal challenge materially identical and affirmed dismissal through collateral estoppel and res judicata. The federal action subsequently proceeded before a three-judge district court. A majority held that the United States was not bound by Kiewit I and that the tax violated the Supremacy Clause. It reasoned that taxing only public contractors discriminated against the federal government even though state contractors were also taxed: when contractors passed the charge to Montana, the State received offsetting tax revenue, but the United States did not. One district judge dissented on both preclusion and the merits. Id. at 151–152.

## Question and holding

Could the United States relitigate the constitutional issues despite not having been a named party in Kiewit I? No. A nonparty that controls litigation protecting its own direct financial or proprietary interest can be bound by the issues actually adjudicated. Here, the government’s control was undisputed, the same constitutional issue had been decided, the relevant legal and factual context had not materially changed, and no special circumstance justified renewed litigation. The Supreme Court reversed without reaching the tax’s constitutional merits. Id. at 153–155, 164.

The opinion distinguished claim preclusion from collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion. Claim preclusion bars further claims on the same cause of action after a final merits judgment. Issue preclusion makes an actually and necessarily determined issue conclusive in a later action even on a different cause of action. Because the government had litigated through another party and then asserted its own cause of action, the relevant doctrine was issue preclusion. The rationale was not simply that the government had an interest in Kiewit’s success. It had assumed control of litigation conducted for its own benefit. Id. at 153–155 & n.5.

## The evidence of control

The government stipulated that it required the Kiewit I suit to be filed, reviewed and approved the complaint, paid counsel’s fees and litigation costs, directed the appeal to the Montana Supreme Court, appeared there as amicus and submitted a brief, directed the notice of appeal to the United States Supreme Court, and caused that appeal to be abandoned on the Solicitor General’s advice. Taken together, those acts gave it the substantial “laboring oar” that justified treating the adjudicated issues as binding. Id. at 155.

This is a concrete control-based exception to ordinary nonparty freedom from judgments. The Court did not hold that any interested observer, funder, or amicus is automatically bound. Its reasoning rested on this combination of stipulated litigation decisions and direct governmental interests. Id. at 154–155.

## Same issue and alleged factual or legal changes

The Court examined three further questions: identity of issues, material changes in controlling facts or legal principles, and other exceptional circumstances. Comparing the complaints and state decision, it found the precise discrimination and federal-immunity challenge had been presented and rejected. The government’s federal complaint substantially repeated Kiewit’s objections concerning disparate taxation, procurement choices, and increased federal construction costs. Id. at 155–158.

The government argued that the facts had changed. Kiewit’s earlier federal contract forbade taking the available tax credits. The government later changed its policy to require contractors to seek those credits, yet evidence showed a net tax remained. According to the government, Kiewit I assumed that credits could completely eliminate liability, making the later revenue-raising effect a different issue. Id. at 158–159 & n.6.

The majority rejected that interpretation. The Montana court had discussed the possible “washout” while explaining the statute’s enforcement history, but its constitutional analysis rested on classification and equal treatment of federal and other public contractors. It did not condition the constitutional conclusion on a complete offset. Kiewit II also treated actual remaining liability as insufficient to justify relitigation. Additionally, the federal complaint sought relief for pre-1971 taxes too, weakening the assertion that all disputed contracts involved the changed policy. Id. at 159–161 & nn.7–8.

Sunnen recognized that a significant change in controlling legal principles can defeat issue preclusion, especially where repeated taxation would otherwise perpetuate unequal treatment. But the government alleged no intervening doctrinal change, and its renewed complaint repeated the old legal objections. Neither a material factual change nor a new legal climate was established here. Id. at 161–162 & n.9.

## Other exceptions and opportunity to litigate

The Court also distinguished Moser’s allowance for fresh legal argument in successive actions involving unrelated subject matter. Preclusion should not freeze all legal or constitutional doctrine for recurring litigants merely because the parties are the same. These demands, however, were closely aligned in time and subject matter, rather than unrelated disputes raising an abstract proposition of law. Id. at 162–163.

Nor had the government been compelled to accept state adjudication after properly invoking federal jurisdiction and reserving its claims under the England abstention framework. It freely submitted the constitutional objections for state decision. The Court acknowledged that the federal action had technically been continued by agreement rather than abstained from, and that the government had not been a named state party. Those distinctions did not defeat the applicable control-based issue preclusion; the Court invoked England to distinguish voluntary state submission from compelled adjudication with a reservation. Id. at 163–164 & n.10.

Finally, the government alleged no unfairness or inadequacy in the state procedures. The Court preserved the principle that doubts about the quality, extensiveness, or fairness of earlier proceedings can warrant reconsideration, but found no such reason here. It held the government estopped and reversed. Id. at 164 & n.11.

## Separate writings

Rehnquist joined the majority with a qualification about its references to scholarship and Restatement drafts: those citations did not bind the Court to propositions concerning issues outside this case’s facts. His concurrence adds no separate tax-merits holding. Id. at 164.

White dissented on both grounds. First, he considered the earlier court’s understanding of a tax-enforcement measure materially different from the later evidence of unavoidable positive taxation. He thought the majority improperly treated that understanding as irrelevant dictum. Kiewit II’s suggestion of a possible administrative remedy did not establish that the first decision had approved unmitigated revenue collection; his first note identified testimony that no such remedy existed. His second note distinguished the state court’s equal-protection reasoning from its Supremacy Clause analysis. Id. at 164–167 & nn.1–2.

Second, White would have upheld the federal district court’s merits judgment. He argued that taxing similarly situated public but not private contractors weakened the political protection against burdens on federal functions. Equal treatment of federal and state public contractors was insufficient because Montana could recoup taxes passed through its own contractors, while federal agencies could not. Potential state assistance to local bodies could further weaken their resistance to increases. He rejected asserted regulatory distinctions and the suggestion that private contracting was negligible; his remaining notes addressed those record and statutory points. These are dissenting views, not the Court’s constitutional resolution. Id. at 167–172 & nn.3–5.
