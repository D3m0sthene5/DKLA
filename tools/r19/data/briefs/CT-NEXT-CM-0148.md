# Peevyhouse v. Garland Coal & Mining Co.
1962 OK 267, 382 P.2d 109 (1962), modified and rehearing denied (1963) | Contracts

## Agreement, breach, and trial
Willie and Lucille Peevyhouse leased coal-bearing farm property to Garland in November 1954 for a five-year strip-mining operation. Their agreement permitted use of the surface necessary for mining and payment of royalties, but also included specific restoration obligations. Those included filling pits at property lines to permit fences and access, smoothing spoil-bank tops, leaving the creek crossing compatible with those crossings, and leaving no shale or dirt on the pits’ high wall. The parties stipulated that all contractual obligations had been performed except the remedial work; Garland conceded that omission. 382 P.2d at 110–111, 114–115.

Experts estimated that the omitted work, involving many thousands of cubic yards of dirt, would cost about $29,000. The owners sued for $25,000. Over their objection, Garland introduced testimony concerning the difference between the farm’s value without restoration and its value if restoration occurred. The trial judge required a verdict for the owners but allowed the jury to consider both performance cost and the other evidence in determining the amount. The jury awarded $5,000. Both sides appealed: the owners sought the performance-cost measure, while Garland sought to limit that measure to the value the restoration would add. Id. at 111.

## Issue and holding
The question was the proper damage measure when restoration cost greatly exceeded the increase in market value. Jackson’s majority stated that reasonable cost of performance ordinarily measures a lessor’s loss from omitted remedial work under a coal lease. It nevertheless adopted an exception when the breached provision is merely incidental to the agreement’s main purpose and the economic benefit of performance to the lessor is grossly disproportionate to its cost. In that combination of circumstances, recovery is limited to diminution in value caused by the omitted work. Id. at 112–114.

Both qualifications matter. The court did not announce that market-value damages necessarily replace performance cost for every construction or restoration promise. It expressly acknowledged that a principal contractual purpose could be an improvement that reduces the property’s resale value; in such a case performance cost would ordinarily govern. Id. at 114.

## Majority reasoning
The majority characterized the principal bargain as economical extraction and sale of coal for the parties’ profit, and the restoration provisions as incidental. It considered an ordinary owner’s hypothetical willingness to spend $29,000 for a $300 value increase unrealistic. It distinguished the Restatement’s concern about destroying a substantially completed structure from a broader inquiry into expense disproportionate to the end attained. No existing completed building needed destruction here, but the majority regarded relative economic benefit as an appropriate consideration nonetheless. Id. at 112–113.

The majority compared Groves with decisions applying diminution in value to similar grading arrangements and discussed the competing readings of construction-damages authorities. Its governing statutory grounds were Oklahoma’s provisions barring damages greater than the benefit of full performance and requiring reasonable damages rather than unconscionable, grossly oppressive amounts contrary to substantial justice. It analogized those limits to restrictions on contractual penalties. On its valuation premise, a performance-cost recovery would give the owners more economic benefit than completed restoration. Id. at 111–114.

The court found no evidence supporting more than $300 as the increase in value attributable to the restoration and noted that the owners did not argue for a higher figure under that measure. It modified the $5,000 judgment to $300 and affirmed it as modified. The result was a reduction of an existing award, not a finding that no contract was breached or a remand for a new damages trial. Id. at 114.

## Irwin’s dissent and actual vote
Irwin disputed the incidental-purpose characterization in light of the actual negotiations. Garland admitted that the owners insisted on restoration and would not lease without those terms. The company obtained royalties’ corresponding mineral rights and use of the land, while the owners bargained for royalties plus restoration. The work’s cost was reasonably predictable when the contract was made; nothing showed a subsequently unforeseeable barrier to performance. Irwin considered the breach willful and without good faith because the company could perform but did not attempt even substantial performance. These characterizations are the dissent’s account, not independent majority findings. Id. at 114–116.

For Irwin, reducing recovery to the market-value increment rewrote the bargain after Garland had received its benefits. If benefits mattered to the measure, those received by the breaching company also deserved consideration. He argued that lawful, unambiguous obligations not tainted by fraud, mistake, or accident should be enforced as written and favored Groves’ performance-cost approach. His reference to entitlement to specific performance supported that position; it was not the remedy ordered by the majority. Id. at 115–116.

Jackson’s opinion was joined by Welch, Davison, Halley, and Johnson. Williams, Blackbird, Irwin, and Berry dissented. Thus the reported original division was five to four. The casebook’s bracketed 4–3 disposition conflicts with the actual report and is not repeated as the vote here. The report includes Irwin’s substantive dissent, while listing the other dissenters without separate opinions. Id. at 114, 116.

## Rehearing: evidence, theory of trial, and constitutional argument
The owners argued on rehearing that exclusion of evidence concerning the total property deprived them of an opportunity to establish loss. Jackson’s supplemental opinion explained that their pleadings described only sixty acres, although their testimony referred to additional owned and leased land. They had also initially pleaded blasting damage to the home and well, but dismissed that separate claim without prejudice. They neither amended to add other lands nor preserved the particular exclusion objections now advanced. Id. at 116–118.

The owners had tried the remaining claim on the premise that only performance cost mattered. They objected to valuation testimony, did not cross-examine Garland’s valuation witnesses or introduce rebuttal, and did not contest the $300 restoration increment in the appellate briefing. The opinion distinguished the farm’s decline from the mining operation generally from the smaller increment attributable to the particular unperformed restoration. Rehearing did not permit changing the theory of the case to develop a valuation claim previously rejected as legally irrelevant. Presenting contrary value evidence would not itself have waived their objection to the admissibility of Garland’s evidence. Id. at 117–119.

Amici also raised the federal Contract Clause. The court explained that the cited Oklahoma damage statutes predated this 1954 agreement and that the decision neither overruled an Oklahoma holding relied upon by these parties nor gave effect to a later legislative impairment. It distinguished Sturges’ subsequent bankruptcy legislation and rejected the constitutional argument. Rehearing was denied with the same five-to-four grouping; the report records modification and denial on March 26, 1963, and denial of a second rehearing on May 28. Id. at 119–120; report heading at 109–110.

## Additional assigned lease and source discrepancies
The assigned Selections reproduces the original signed lease and a retyped version, rather than another judicial opinion. The original sets a five-year term and contains crossed-out printed provisions, a changed royalty rate, and typed clauses 7a–7h. Those clauses include a $2,000 advance royalty, the restoration terms, restrictions on assignment with a specified exception, and a premining boundary survey. Sections 8 and 9 separately address cancellation on thirty days’ notice for unprofitable operations, notice of a lessor’s claimed default, and removal of the lessee’s equipment after termination. These additional provisions supply the contract’s context; the appellate opinion did not decide a dispute about invoking each of them. Selections printed pages 692–700.

The retyped §1 says three years, whereas the original scan and the judicial opinion say five. The retyped page is therefore not used to silently alter the opinion’s chronology. The casebook labels the court year 1963, consistent with the rehearing stage, while the original judgment was issued December 11, 1962. The brief identifies both stages rather than treating them as different principal decisions.
