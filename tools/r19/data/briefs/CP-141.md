Citation

federal Circuit Court for the Northern District of New York. Justice Field delivered the opinion. Justice
Clifford dissented without a separately reproduced explanation. The supplied report does not state precise
argument and decision dates. Assigned excerpt: Civil Procedure casebook pp. 1293–1295. Full judicial
opinion: Full judicial opinion.
Parties
Russell was the patentee and complainant seeking equitable relief against Place and the other defendants for
alleged infringement. He had previously recovered a damages judgment against them in an action at law. In the
second proceeding, he invoked that judgment to prevent them from contesting infringement and patent validity.
The defendants acknowledged the earlier judgment but denied that it necessarily resolved the issues presented
by the later suit. 94 U.S. at 606–608.
Material Facts
The patent concerned an asserted improvement in preparing leather. It contained two claims: one concerning
use of fat liquor generally in treating leather, and the other concerning a particular compound and method for
treating bark-tanned lamb or sheepskin. A patent claim identifies a particular claimed invention or protected
feature; the two claims therefore required separate attention. Proof sufficient to sustain one did not
automatically establish the validity or infringement of the other. Id. at 609.
The original patent had been surrendered because its description was allegedly defective or insufficient, and a
reissued patent supplied an amended specification. Russell's earlier damages action alleged generally that the
defendants made and used the invention and caused others to do so. The verdict and record did not reveal
whether the jury had found infringement of the first claim, the second, or both. No outside evidence was
introduced in the second proceeding to resolve that uncertainty. Id. at 607, 609.
The later equity suit alleged further unlicensed manufacture, use, and sale. Russell sought an injunction and an
accounting of the defendants' gains and profits. The defendants challenged novelty, asserted public use more
than two years before the patent application, and contended that the reissue differed from the original
invention. Russell maintained that the prior judgment prevented those challenges because they either had been
or could have been defenses in the first action. Id. at 607–608.
Procedural History
In the earlier action at law, the defendants pleaded the general issue and gave the statutorily required notice of
their novelty and prior-use defenses. A jury returned a damages verdict for Russell, and judgment was entered
on it. The general character of the pleadings and verdict left the particular patent claim underlying the recovery
unidentified. Id. at 607–609.
Russell then brought the equity suit for subsequent infringement, relying on the earlier judgment as an
estoppel. The Circuit Court entered a decree adverse to the relief he sought, and he appealed. The Supreme
Court explained that the case otherwise resembled Russell v. Dodge, decided on substantially the same
testimony; the distinct question addressed in this opinion was the preclusive effect of the prior damages
judgment. The Court affirmed the decree. Id. at 606–607, 610.

Issue
Does a general judgment for damages for infringement of a patent containing two claims conclusively establish
validity and infringement in a later suit when neither the record nor extrinsic evidence identifies which claim
necessarily supported the first recovery? What must a party show to establish the particular issue determined
by an earlier judgment? Id. at 608–610.
Rule and Holding
A prior judgment is conclusive in a later action between the same parties on a question directly involved and
determined in the first action. The party relying on that effect must establish, from the record or consistent
extrinsic evidence, the precise question necessarily resolved. When several distinct matters could support the
judgment and the uncertainty is not removed, the judgment does not conclusively determine a particular one of
them. Id. at 608–609.
The earlier infringement verdict did not show which of the two patent claims supported recovery, and Russell
offered no extrinsic evidence resolving the point. A patent can be valid as to one claim and invalid as to
another. The judgment therefore did not prevent the defendants from contesting infringement or validity in this
later suit. Id. at 609–610.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the Circuit Court's decree. It rejected Russell's effort to use the earlier damages
judgment as conclusive proof of the disputed patent issues in the equity suit. It did not vacate that earlier
damages judgment or declare that the prior jury had decided nothing; its ruling concerned the absence of proof
identifying the specific issue to carry forward into the second action. Id. at 609–610.
Reasoning
The Court accepted the general principle that a question directly determined by a competent court cannot be
litigated again between the same parties. The obstacle was evidentiary: determining what the prior court had
actually resolved. A judgment's existence establishes a result, but it may not identify each factual or legal issue
essential to that result. An advocate must connect the particular proposition asserted in the second case to a
necessary determination in the first. Id. at 608.
The record sometimes makes that connection evident. When it does not, extrinsic evidence may explain the
matters submitted and necessarily decided. Such evidence must be consistent with the record and identify the
prior adjudication; it cannot enlarge that adjudication by showing that another issue could have been raised.
The opinion's discussion of the Sickles litigation illustrates the distinction: a general verdict on several counts
might rest on ordinary money claims rather than the special contract whose validity a party later sought to treat
as settled. Id. at 608–609.
Russell's record had the same defect. The declaration generally alleged infringement of the invention without
identifying whether the unlawful act was use of fat liquor itself or use of the particular patented process. The
jury could have found for Russell under either claim. Nothing in the resulting damages verdict required the
conclusion that both claims were valid and infringed. Id. at 609.
That ambiguity concerned more than the details of how infringement occurred. Because patent validity can
differ from claim to claim, a verdict supporting one claim did not necessarily reject every defense directed at
the other. The defendants' prior opportunity to contest the patent as a whole did not establish that the jury
actually resolved all possible issues adversely to them. The inference Russell needed was therefore not
compelled by the earlier judgment. Id. at 607–609.

No extrinsic evidence supplied the missing link. The Court could only conjecture whether the verdict rested on
the first claim, the second, or both, and conjecture was insufficient for estoppel. Its analogy to an action for
money had and received made the point concrete: a bare judgment for a sum does not necessarily reveal the
particular transaction from which the indebtedness arose. The fixed result remains binding, but its scope as
proof in another action must be demonstrated. Id. at 609–610.
Russell's broader argument also confused preclusion of a claim with preclusion of an issue. The second suit
addressed subsequent conduct and sought equitable relief. For this use of the first judgment, it was essential to
show the precise adjudicated question, rather than every defense that could have been offered earlier. The
Court consequently permitted the defendants to contest the patent issues in the second action while leaving the
original damages recovery intact. Id. at 607–610.
Separate Opinions
The report records that Justice Clifford dissented but supplies no separate reasoning. It would be speculative to
attribute to him a particular patent-law or preclusion rationale in this case. His written dissent in Cromwell,
another reading in this compilation, is not a substitute for an explanation absent from this report. Id. at 610.
Notes
The distinction between a patent claim and a litigation claim matters here. Russell's patent contained two
claimed inventions or aspects of invention; the lawsuit was the vehicle for asserting infringement. The
uncertainty concerned which patent claim had supported the first damages verdict. The opinion does not
establish a universal rule that alternative grounds are never preclusive; it addresses a record that did not
identify which ground was actually decided. Id. at 609.
Read alongside Cromwell, the case adds an identification requirement to issue preclusion. Cromwell explains
why only issues actually determined may carry across distinct demands. Russell requires proof of what those
issues were. A general verdict, ambiguous pleadings, or multiple possible paths to judgment may therefore
require examination of the trial record or other consistent evidence. 94 U.S. at 608–610; Cromwell, 94 U.S. at
353–356.
The complete Russell opinion incorporates the other patent questions through its reference to Russell v. Dodge
and does not re-explain those merits. This brief does not invent a new technical invalidity analysis for that
incorporated decision. It follows the assigned excerpt's focus on estoppel while preserving the actual
affirmance and the limited statement about Clifford's dissent. 94 U.S. at 606–607, 610.
