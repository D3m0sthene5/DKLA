# Mallory v. Norfolk Southern Railway Co.
143 S. Ct. 2028 (2023), No. 21-1168, decided June 27, 2023 | Civil Procedure

## Facts and statutory setting
Robert Mallory worked for Norfolk Southern for roughly twenty years in Ohio and Virginia. He alleged that repairing freight cars exposed him to asbestos and other carcinogens, including materials in pipes, paint, and railcar demolition work, and caused his cancer. After leaving the railroad, he lived in Pennsylvania for a period, then returned to Virginia. He was a Virginia resident when he filed this action in 2017. Norfolk Southern was incorporated and headquartered in Virginia at the relevant time. Its later move of headquarters to Georgia did not supply the jurisdictional premise of the case. Neither Mallory's alleged exposure nor his claim arose from Pennsylvania work. He sued under the Federal Employers' Liability Act in Pennsylvania state court. The opinion's opening illustration involving an Ohio derailment and personal service on an individual conductor is a hypothetical; it is not Mallory's accident or procedural history. (Gorsuch opinion, slip pp. 2–4 & n. 2.)

Norfolk Southern nevertheless had substantial Pennsylvania operations, including more than 2,000 miles of track, eleven rail yards, and three locomotive repair shops. Pennsylvania required foreign corporations regularly conducting extensive intrastate business to register. Norfolk Southern had registered in 1998 and maintained a registered office. Under 42 Pa. Cons. Stat. §5301(a)(2)(i), qualification as a foreign corporation supplied a sufficient basis for Pennsylvania courts to exercise general personal jurisdiction, and subsection (b) permitted any cause of action against a corporation covered by that provision. Pennsylvania also afforded registered foreign corporations the privileges of domestic corporations and subjected them to corresponding liabilities. Thus the asserted jurisdictional basis was registration under a statute assigning registration that consequence, rather than claim-related contacts or the proposition that every large local operation makes a corporation at home. (Gorsuch opinion, slip pp. 3–4, 10–12.)

## Procedure and competing positions
Norfolk Southern objected that hearing this suit would violate the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. Under the modern contacts cases, it argued, a corporation ordinarily faces general jurisdiction in its place of incorporation and principal place of business, with only exceptional additional forums. Pennsylvania was neither, and there was no connection between Pennsylvania and Mallory's alleged injury. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court agreed that the registration statute was unconstitutional as applied and affirmed dismissal. Mallory sought review on the theory that registration constituted consent to general jurisdiction, a distinct basis recognized in Pennsylvania Fire Insurance Co. v. Gold Issue Mining & Milling Co., 243 U.S. 93 (1917). (Gorsuch opinion, slip pp. 3–4.)

The Supreme Court granted review of the due process question. Norfolk Southern also advanced a dormant Commerce Clause objection, but the Pennsylvania Supreme Court had not addressed it and the Supreme Court did not grant review of it. That objection remained available on remand. This reservation is essential: the decision approves the asserted jurisdictional basis against this due process challenge, without finally sustaining Pennsylvania's statute against every constitutional objection. (Gorsuch opinion, slip p. 4 n. 3; Alito concurrence, slip pp. 1–2, 15.)

## Issue, holding, and judgment
The question was whether the Due Process Clause prohibited Pennsylvania from exercising jurisdiction over Norfolk Southern on an unrelated claim after the company registered to do business under a statute expressly making registration a basis for general jurisdiction. Five Justices answered no. Pennsylvania Fire controlled the due process issue, and International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), had not overruled it. The Court vacated the Pennsylvania Supreme Court's judgment and remanded for further proceedings. It did not decide whether Norfolk Southern negligently injured Mallory, award damages, or resolve the reserved Commerce Clause challenge. (Gorsuch opinion, Parts I and III-B, slip pp. 2–4, 10–12, 24.)

The voting structure matters. Justice Gorsuch announced the judgment. Justices Thomas, Sotomayor, and Jackson joined his entire opinion. Justice Alito joined only Parts I and III-B, producing a five-Justice Court opinion in those portions, and separately concurred in part and in the judgment. The remaining portions of Gorsuch's opinion—Parts II, III-A, and IV—were a four-Justice plurality. Justice Jackson also concurred separately. Justice Barrett dissented, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kagan and Kavanaugh. The judgment was 5–4; Gorsuch's entire reasoning was not a five-Justice majority opinion.

## Controlling reasoning: Pennsylvania Fire and consent
As Gorsuch explained in the plurality’s historical account, in Pennsylvania Fire, an Arizona mining company sued a Pennsylvania insurer in Missouri over insurance on a Colorado facility damaged by a Colorado fire. To obtain a Missouri business license, the insurer had appointed a state official to receive process. Missouri interpreted that appointment to reach suits unrelated to the insurer's Missouri business. Justice Holmes's unanimous opinion rejected the insurer's due process objection. Consent through the appointment supported jurisdiction even over that unrelated dispute. Pennsylvania Fire therefore addressed the question Norfolk Southern presented here: whether a foreign corporation can accept an identified jurisdictional consequence in exchange for permission to conduct local business. (Gorsuch opinion, Part III-A, slip pp. 8–10.)

The five-Justice Part III-B held that Pennsylvania Fire controlled Pennsylvania’s explicit statutory arrangement and Norfolk Southern’s registration under it. The Court declined to speculate about other statutory schemes and facts. A lower court must follow a directly controlling Supreme Court precedent even if it believes later cases undermine the precedent’s reasoning, leaving overruling to the Supreme Court. Pennsylvania’s high court therefore could not disregard Pennsylvania Fire on its conclusion that intervening cases had implicitly overruled it. (Gorsuch opinion, Part III-B, slip pp. 10–12.)

The conclusion that International Shoe had not overruled Pennsylvania Fire also commanded five votes, but the supporting explanations appear in Gorsuch’s four-Justice Part IV and Alito’s separate concurrence. Gorsuch treated International Shoe as supplying an additional contacts-based route for jurisdiction over a corporation that had not consented, while Alito separately agreed that consent remained a valid basis and that Pennsylvania Fire should not be overruled here. These writings support the five-vote result; Part IV itself is a plurality opinion. (Gorsuch opinion, Part IV-A, slip pp. 13–16; Alito concurrence, slip pp. 3–5; see also Gorsuch opinion, slip p. 23 n. 11.)

The consequence is narrower than a general rule that commercial activity creates unlimited jurisdiction. The at-home cases govern general jurisdiction based on corporate affiliations. This case involved a statutory registration carrying an express jurisdictional consequence, plus the corporation's decision to register and conduct extensive business. Nor does the decision establish that every state's registration law constitutes consent. The content and authoritative construction of the relevant state statute remain important.

## The plurality's broader explanation
Gorsuch's four-Justice portions situated corporate consent in a longer tradition of jurisdiction over transitory actions. An individual physically present in a state could be served there on an unrelated claim. States also developed ways for corporations, which lacked an individual's physical body, to accept service and jurisdiction while conducting local business. The plurality resisted making corporations able to enjoy sustained local operations while avoiding jurisdictional consequences individuals could incur through brief presence. It treated International Shoe as an additional path, not a comprehensive replacement of traditional grounds. Burnham v. Superior Court's preservation of jurisdiction based on personal service within the state supported that account. (Gorsuch opinion, Parts II and III-A, slip pp. 4–10, and Part IV-A, slip pp. 14–16.)

The plurality also rejected a fairness objection on these facts. Norfolk Southern had thousands of Pennsylvania employees, more than 2,400 miles of track according to its own public materials, a major locomotive shop, and a long-standing registered presence. It publicly described itself as part of the Pennsylvania community. A lawsuit in that state was not an unforeseeable imposition on a stranger. These facts helped the plurality answer the alleged unfairness of the consent arrangement; they did not themselves convert Pennsylvania into the railroad's principal place of business. (Gorsuch opinion, slip pp. 17–20, including the reproduced corporate factsheet.)

For the plurality, personal jurisdiction protects an individual right that can be waived. Parties routinely accept procedural consequences through contracts, registration papers, litigation conduct, or failure to object. The Constitution does not require that every effective acceptance use a special verbal formula. Although jurisdiction doctrine also reflects federalism, the plurality found no precedent invalidating genuinely consent-based jurisdiction under the Due Process Clause merely because it allowed one state's court to hear an out-of-state dispute. The dissent disputed both the characterization of registration as consent and this treatment of structural interests. (Gorsuch opinion, slip pp. 21–24.)

## Jackson's concurrence
Jackson emphasized Insurance Corp. of Ireland v. Compagnie des Bauxites de Guinée, which recognizes personal jurisdiction as a waivable individual right. Waiver may be express, arise from conduct accepting legal benefits, or follow failure to comply with litigation procedures. Norfolk Southern registered and obtained the benefits of doing extensive Pennsylvania business while Pennsylvania law clearly assigned registration a jurisdictional effect. Jackson therefore viewed the company as having voluntarily relinquished its due process objection. The relevant choice was its decision to conduct substantial local business under that arrangement, rather than a supposed requirement that every passing train consent. She analogized to other effective waivers of constitutional rights: the availability of a right does not prevent its informed relinquishment. Her opinion addressed the due process defense, without disposing of another constitutional limit on the state's arrangement. (Jackson concurrence, slip pp. 1–4.)

## Alito's concurrence: due process versus interstate commerce
Alito agreed that Pennsylvania Fire controlled and should not be overruled in this case. The statutory consequence was clear, and Norfolk Southern was a sophisticated corporation with extensive Pennsylvania operations. Due process posed no special unfairness in permitting this litigation in that forum. He joined the judgment and the controlling Pennsylvania Fire analysis while declining to join Gorsuch's full opinion. (Alito concurrence, slip pp. 1–5.)

His substantial reservation concerned a different constitutional doctrine. A state's decision to condition access to its market on accepting suits concerning out-of-state parties and conduct may burden interstate commerce. He invoked Davis v. Farmers Co-operative Equity Co., 262 U.S. 312 (1923), which invalidated an expansive jurisdictional requirement imposed on an interstate railroad. Dormant Commerce Clause doctrine can prohibit discrimination against interstate commerce and, under Pike, invalidate evenhanded regulations whose interstate burdens are clearly excessive in relation to local benefits. A personal due process right may be waived without eliminating these structural limits on state regulation. (Alito concurrence, slip pp. 5–12.)

Alito identified the potential burden of exposing a company to unrelated suits, different liability rules, litigation costs, and unpredictable claims wherever it seeks to do business. Smaller companies may avoid a state's market because of that exposure. Pennsylvania has a legitimate interest in disputes involving local residents or local injuries, but an unrelated dispute between out-of-state parties presents a much weaker local benefit. He considered a Commerce Clause challenge substantial and potentially successful, while expressly leaving its resolution to the courts on remand. Those observations are a concurrence's analysis, not a holding that Pennsylvania's law violated the Commerce Clause. (Alito concurrence, slip pp. 10–15.)

## Barrett's dissent
Barrett would have affirmed. General jurisdiction based on corporate operations normally requires an at-home forum; specific jurisdiction requires a link between the forum and the claim. Mallory satisfied neither route. BNSF Railway Co. v. Tyrrell had rejected general jurisdiction over comparable FELA claims against a railroad merely because it had substantial tracks and employees in the forum. For the dissent, Pennsylvania could not evade those limits by labeling compulsory registration as consent. (Barrett dissent, slip pp. 1–5.)

The dissent distinguished actual consent through a jurisdiction clause or express appointment from the papers Norfolk Southern submitted. Registration was mandatory for conducting the business, the papers did not themselves expressly accept all-purpose jurisdiction, and Pennsylvania's statute separately listed qualification and consent as jurisdictional grounds. Knowing that a state assigns an unconstitutional consequence to commercial activity would not make the consequence constitutional; otherwise every invalid long-arm statute could be converted into consent by giving advance notice. This reasoning would permit states to erase Daimler's limits through a registration condition. The dissent also invoked decisions rejecting state conditions requiring foreign corporations to surrender access to federal court, even though an individual party could voluntarily waive that procedural right. (Barrett dissent, slip pp. 5–10.)

Barrett further disputed the historical analogy. Burnham involved an established and continuing tradition of personal service on individuals. Modern broad registration jurisdiction was unusual, and many older corporate statutes or decisions had confined jurisdiction to locally connected claims. International Shoe rejected fictional corporate presence and implied consent as explanations for jurisdiction based on business activity; Shaffer v. Heitner required other traditional assertions of jurisdiction to satisfy modern due process principles. The dissent thus regarded Pennsylvania Fire as inconsistent with subsequent doctrine, or alternatively distinguishable because it involved an express appointment with the pertinent scope. Its objections show why a statute's label alone does not resolve the doctrinal disagreement. (Barrett dissent, slip pp. 10–18.)

## Significance and limits
For the assigned Civil Procedure topic, Mallory separates three possible bases: claim-linked contacts, at-home general jurisdiction, and consent. The five-Justice holding preserves Pennsylvania Fire against the argument that International Shoe automatically abolished registration-based consent, and prevents lower courts from anticipating the overruling of a directly controlling case. The decision is sensitive to the actual statutory arrangement and does not declare every corporation subject to every suit wherever it does business. Its remand also preserves a distinction between a waivable personal jurisdiction defense and an independently asserted structural limit on a state's regulation of interstate commerce.
