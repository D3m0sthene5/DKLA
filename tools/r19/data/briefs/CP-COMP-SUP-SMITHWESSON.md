# Smith & Wesson Brands, Inc. v. Estados Unidos Mexicanos

**Citation and court:** 605 U.S. 280 (2025), Supreme Court of the United States, No. 23–1141, decided June 5, 2025. Justice Kagan wrote for a unanimous Court. Justices Thomas and Jackson each filed a concurrence. All five Court judicial footnotes and both complete concurrences were read; neither concurrence contains a substantive footnote.

**Assigned reading:** Civil Procedure Supplement, printed pages 512–514, physical PDF pages 345–346, complete captioned account, verified against both scans. The full official preliminary report, pages 280–302, supplies the complete decision and separate writings. Justia separately corroborates the case identity, date, lineup, and reversal/remand. Pinpoints follow the official report.

**Material allegations.** Mexico sued seven American gun manufacturers in 2021, alleging that they failed to exercise reasonable care to prevent trafficking of their firearms into Mexico, where cartels used them for crimes. The suit also named one distributor, but the complaint said virtually nothing about its conduct; the Court collectively referred to defendants as manufacturers for convenience. The violence and trafficking descriptions were allegations and background, not a final adjudication of defendant culpability. 605 U.S. at 287–288 & n.1.

To invoke the Protection of Lawful Commerce in Arms Act's predicate exception, Mexico alleged aiding and abetting of dealers' unlawful sales. Its three principal theories were continued supply to supposedly known rogue dealers, failure to monitor or constrain downstream sales, and design/marketing intended to appeal to cartels. The industry used three tiers: manufacturers sold to distributors, distributors to dealers, and dealers to customers. The complaint did not explain the relationship between manufacturers and distributors or meaningfully differentiate the distributor defendant. Id. at 288–290 & nn.2–3. Note 2 defines straw purchasing; it does not establish a specific assisted illegal transaction by these defendants.

**Procedural history.** The district court dismissed under PLCAA. The First Circuit reversed, finding plausible allegations of aiding and abetting and proximate causation sufficient for the predicate exception. The Supreme Court reviewed whether the complaint plausibly alleged the manufacturers' aiding and abetting. Id. at 290–291.

**Statutory framework.** PLCAA generally bars covered civil actions against gun manufacturers and sellers for third parties' criminal or unlawful misuse of firearms. Its predicate exception permits an otherwise covered suit where the defendant knowingly violated a state or federal statute applicable to sale or marketing and the violation proximately caused the claimed harm. Aiding and abetting a federal gun offense can qualify, including through 18 U.S.C. § 2(a)'s treatment of an accomplice as a principal. The statute itself gives specified aiding-and-abetting examples. Id. at 285–287. The exception remains real; this decision does not bar every lawsuit against gun manufacturers.

**Issue and holding.** Did Mexico plausibly plead aiding and abetting sufficient to invoke the predicate exception? No. The allegations did not plausibly show conscious and culpable assistance of unlawful sales, so PLCAA barred this suit. Id. at 291–299. The Court expressly did **not** reach proximate cause because the alleged predicate wrongdoing was insufficient. Id. at 291.

**Reasoning.** Aiding and abetting requires an affirmative act furthering the offense and intent to facilitate it. Liability usually concerns specific wrongful acts. A claim covering a broad category of misconduct is possible, but requires correspondingly pervasive, systemic, culpable participation. Passive omissions ordinarily do not suffice absent an independent duty to act, and ordinary commerce does not become complicity merely because a merchant knows some customers misuse products. Id. at 291–293.

Direct Sales illustrates active assistance: enormous narcotics supplies to a named doctor, special discounts, high-pressure sales, and continued stimulation despite warnings supported deliberate participation. Twitter supplies the contrast: general services, awareness of misuse, and failure to remove bad actors did not themselves amount to aiding and abetting. Id. at 292–293.

Mexico's generalized accusation identified no specific criminal transaction assisted by a given manufacturer. That was not categorically fatal, but heightened the need for plausible allegations of systemic culpable assistance. The complaint alleged that rogue dealers received the same products on the same terms as lawful dealers, without analogous active stimulation. More fundamentally, it did not plausibly explain how manufacturers knew which individual dealers, two tiers down, engaged in unlawful sales. Id. at 294–296. Notes 3 and 4 preserve the inadequacy of the distributor-specific allegations. Note 5 explains why an article listing twelve dealers associated with recovered crime guns did not establish wrongdoing: volume or geography could explain that list. Id. at 289 n.3, 295 n.4, 296 n.5.

What was plausibly alleged was awareness that some unidentified dealers broke the law and failure to do more to identify or restrict them. That was indifference or nonfeasance, not the intentional assistance required. Proposed monitoring and restrictions on lawful bulk or home sales did not supply the missing affirmative participation. Id. at 296–297.

Design and marketing allegations likewise failed: the described guns were widely lawful and purchased by ordinary consumers, and Spanish-language names or historical imagery could appeal to lawful buyers. Cartels' preferences did not establish complicity. Nor did failure to adopt nondefaceable serial numbers, which federal law did not require. Id. at 297–298. The Court assessed these allegations, rather than making a general factual declaration that a manufacturer could never intentionally assist criminal sales.

That interpretation also fit PLCAA's purpose of preventing manufacturers from being held liable for downstream misuse while preserving defined exceptions. Mexico's theory would expand the predicate exception enough to swallow much of the general bar. Id. at 298–299.

**Thomas's concurrence.** Thomas agreed with pleading insufficiency but flagged an unresolved question: whether the statute's requirement of a “violation” demands an earlier adjudication of guilt or liability rather than allegations litigated for the first time in the civil suit. He suggested that concerns about criminal safeguards and constitutional avoidance warrant examination. The Court did not adopt such a prerequisite. Id. at 299–300.

**Jackson's concurrence.** Jackson viewed the central defect as absence of nonconclusory allegations of particular statutory violations. PLCAA's predicate exception links civil liability to duties imposed legislatively, rather than using litigation to create additional industry regulation. In her view, lawful industry practices connected only generally to unspecified downstream offenses did not satisfy that structure, regardless of whether such practices might support secondary liability in another statutory or common-law setting. Id. at 300–302. Her concurrence does not announce a universal rejection of those practices as evidence in other contexts.

**Exact disposition.** The Supreme Court **reversed the First Circuit's judgment and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion**. Id. at 299. It did not itself enter a new damages judgment or decide proximate causation. The insufficient pleading meant this complaint could not proceed under the asserted PLCAA exception.

**Course significance.** Plausibility depends on the substantive elements of the asserted claim. General knowledge, preventable harm, and omitted safeguards do not automatically support an inference of deliberate criminal assistance. Distinguish a broad claim requiring systemic participation from a categorical rule requiring every plaintiff to name one isolated transaction.
