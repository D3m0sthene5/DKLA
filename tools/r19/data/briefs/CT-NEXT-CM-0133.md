# Nelson v. Hazel
91 Idaho 850, 433 P.2d 120 (1967) | Contracts

## The second appeal after defective remodeling
Nelson initially sought foreclosure of a mechanic’s and materialman’s lien for remodeling the Hazels’ home. The trial court found defects but substantial performance and awarded him judgment. In the first appeal, reported at 89 Idaho 480, the Supreme Court reversed: the work was not workmanlike, substantial performance was absent, and the lien could not be enforced. It directed assessment of the homeowners’ breach damages. The assigned 1967 decision is the later appeal from proceedings on remand, rather than a reversal of that earlier finding of defective performance. 91 Idaho at 851.

On remand the parties submitted the existing record and exhibits without new evidence. The district court credited $5,090.86 in labor and materials, deducted the $1,997.51 already paid and $2,494.50 in repair damages, and entered a $598.85 judgment for Nelson with interest and costs. The homeowners appealed, arguing that the earlier lack of substantial performance barred any contractor recovery and that the unfinished remedial work meant they had received no benefit. Id. at 851–52.

## Contract failure did not require forfeiture of retained value
The court distinguished enforceability of the construction contract and lien from quasi-contractual payment for an excess benefit. The earlier mandate required compensation for the homeowners’ loss, which the remand court supplied through the repair-cost offset. Contract damages compensate injury; they do not necessarily authorize retaining all of a defaulting contractor’s valuable work without paying any net balance. Id. at 851.

Building contracts furnished an exception to the usual rule against a defaulting party’s contract recovery because improvements remain with the property owner. Even performance falling short of substantial performance can confer benefits greater than the injury. For a negligent contractor, avoiding forfeiture justified recovery of that excess. The court did not declare Nelson’s work substantially compliant after all; it identified a quasi-contractual duty to pay value beyond the homeowners’ compensable loss. Id. at 851–52.

Where defects were remediable, the market cost of completing or correcting the work normally measured the offset. Two experts estimated repairs to the roof, floors, and walls at $2,000 and $2,300. The district court used the higher figure and included $194.50 for restoration of the chimney, yielding the $2,494.50 deduction. No evidence established additional damage, particularly the correction cost of allegedly defective kitchen built-ins. The asserted large remaining expenditures were therefore already reflected in the credited estimates, rather than demonstrating an uncompensated loss exceeding all value. Id. at 852.

## Holding, judgment, and participation
The Supreme Court upheld the calculation as reasonable and consistent with the earlier remand. It affirmed the $598.85 judgment with costs to Nelson. Smith wrote; Taylor, McQuade, Spear, and District Judge Norris concurred, five participating judges in the reported unanimous disposition. Id. at 852.

The result depends on negligent, remediable defective work with benefits exceeding proven injury. It does not award the full contract price despite default, revive the mechanic’s lien, or excuse defects. The court accepted the remand’s labor-and-material figure as the starting value on this record and subtracted both prior payments and repair compensation; it did not establish that every contractor’s expenditure invariably equals enrichment.

## Complete-source and assigned-excerpt scope
The entire 1967 report, which contains no judicial footnotes or separate writings, and the assigned principal on PDF pages 227–228 were read. The bracketed casebook introduction compresses the first appeal; the complete later opinion supplies the lien posture, remittitur, and absence of additional evidence on remand.
