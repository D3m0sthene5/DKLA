# Department of Commerce v. New York
588 U.S. 752 (2019) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and posture

The Supreme Court upheld the Secretary of Commerce’s constitutional authority to ask about citizenship, rejected the district court’s ordinary arbitrary-and-capricious and Census Act objections, but required an agency remand because the sole stated Voting Rights Act justification did not match the decisionmaking record. A permissible policy choice still required a genuine explanation. The judgment was affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded; the Court did not categorically prohibit citizenship questions or decide that the Secretary acted with unconstitutional discriminatory intent. Chief Justice Roberts’s principal opinion ends at slip page 29; the citations below use the separately numbered pages of each archived writing.

The vote differed by issue. Parts I and II, including Article III standing, were unanimous. Roberts, Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh joined Parts III, IV–B, and IV–C, addressing constitutional authority, the policy’s ordinary reasonableness, and statutory compliance. Roberts, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, Kagan, and Kavanaugh joined Part IV–A permitting APA review. Roberts, Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan joined Part V requiring a genuine justification. Thomas, joined by Gorsuch and Kavanaugh, dissented from that last ruling. Breyer, joined by Ginsburg, Sotomayor, and Kagan, also considered the policy independently arbitrary. Alito would have dismissed the APA claims as committed to agency discretion, while agreeing alternatively with the merits conclusions in Parts IV–B and IV–C. Breyer’s joinder in Part V excluded its suggestion that the decision was otherwise reasonable. See principal opinion parts I–V; Thomas slip 2–3 & n.1; Breyer slip 1–2, 22; Alito slip 20 & n.15.

## Census practices and the Secretary’s decision

The census serves apportionment, districting, allocation of federal funds, and demographic purposes. Citizenship or birthplace had appeared in nearly every census between 1820 and 2000, but citizenship questions went to a sample on the long form from 1960 through 2000. In 2010, the annual American Community Survey replaced that long form; the ACS sampled approximately 2.6 percent of households. This history supported authority to collect citizenship information without establishing that a question on every household’s short form had the same consequences as earlier sampling. Federal statutes also protected individual census responses against disclosure and prescribed truthful answers. Principal slip 1–3 & n.1.

Secretary Wilbur Ross announced on March 26, 2018, that a citizenship question would appear on the 2020 census. His memorandum attributed the decision to a December 2017 Justice Department request for more precise citizenship data to enforce the Voting Rights Act. DOJ sought block-level citizen voting-age population figures rather than the ACS’s larger geographic groupings, estimates, and margins of error. Census Bureau staff examined continued ACS modeling, adding a question, relying on administrative records, and combining a question with records. Ross selected the combined approach. Administrative records covered approximately 90 percent of the population; a question would, in his account, reduce the number whose citizenship required imputation from approximately 35 million to 13.8 million. He acknowledged the Bureau’s contrary assessment that adding a question would reduce response, increase costs, and impair accuracy, but considered the projected response effect uncertain and the records-only alternative insufficiently demonstrated. Principal slip 3–5, 16–20.

The scientific disagreement mattered. The Bureau predicted a 5.1 percentage-point differential decline in response among households containing noncitizens, later updated to 5.8 points. These figures concerned response rates, not a mechanically identical final undercount after follow-up. Staff considered administrative records reliable and warned that citizenship answers often conflicted with them. Ross nevertheless preferred greater completeness through the combined approach and questioned the absence of a tested, adequate benchmark for the records-only imputation model. The principal opinion regarded the tradeoff as one Congress had assigned to the Secretary. Breyer regarded the Secretary’s treatment of the evidence as seriously deficient. Principal slip 16–20 & n.2; Breyer slip 4–18.

## Litigation, the record, and standing

States, the District of Columbia, counties, cities, and other governmental and nongovernmental plaintiffs challenged the decision. After dismissing the Enumeration Clause claim, the district court conducted a bench trial and found standing, APA violations, and Census Act violations. It also rejected the separate equal-protection claim for insufficient proof of discriminatory intent. It vacated the decision and enjoined use of the question unless the identified defects were cured. The government appealed to the Second Circuit and obtained Supreme Court review before appellate judgment because printing deadlines made prompt resolution important. The Supreme Court also directed briefing on the Enumeration Clause as an alternative ground supporting the judgment. Principal slip 5–8.

The initial administrative record presented the DOJ request as the impetus. A supplemental memorandum acknowledged that Ross had begun considering the question early in 2017 and had asked other agencies whether they wanted it. The parties stipulated to additional record material, while the district court authorized extra-record discovery. The Supreme Court stayed Ross’s deposition, which never occurred, but permitted other discovery to proceed. The principal opinion distinguished supplementing an incomplete administrative record from probing decisionmakers through extra-record evidence: the initial discovery order preceded an adequate showing, but the expanded administrative record eventually supplied a strong basis for the remaining inquiry. Principal slip 5–7, 24–26.

Standing did not require proof that every feared injury would occur. The district court’s supported prediction of a roughly two-percent noncitizen undercount sufficed to establish impending loss of federal funding to some state plaintiffs. Predictable third-party behavior could establish causation even if a failure to respond violated legal duties or rested on fears that officials would breach confidentiality safeguards. The Court rejected treating those predictable responses as independent acts that automatically broke the causal chain. It did not separately adjudicate every asserted injury of every plaintiff. Principal slip 8–11.

## Reviewability and substantive authority

Section 701(a)(2)’s exception for action committed to agency discretion is narrow. Broad power over the census’s form and content did not eliminate all legal standards. The Census Act contained provisions concerning accurate enumeration, sampling, administrative records, and reporting to Congress, and courts had reviewed census decisions before. The Court therefore permitted APA review. This was a holding about the challenged decision, not a conclusion that every administrative choice is reviewable. Principal slip 13–16.

The Enumeration Clause required counting the whole population, including noncitizens, but did not bar other demographic questions. Historical practice demonstrated that the constitutional census could encompass information beyond the total count. The Court declined to transfer a test governing enumeration methods mechanically to every individual question. It upheld the citizenship question against this constitutional objection without endorsing all possible questions or all methods of administering them. Principal slip 11–13.

Ordinary arbitrary-and-capricious review allowed Ross to choose among reasonable alternatives. The Court emphasized that an agency head need not accept staff preferences, and courts may not replace a reasonable weighing of risks and benefits with their own. Ross recognized the nonresponse risk and explained why he thought the combined method’s more complete information justified it. The uncertainty surrounding the new records-only model also mattered. This assessment resolved the substantive reasonableness question independently of whether Ross’s stated purpose accurately described the process. Principal slip 16–20.

The statutory challenges also failed. Assuming 13 U.S.C. §6(c)’s preference for records over direct inquiries applied to the citizenship information here, Ross had judged the combination more accurate than records alone; the Court did not finally resolve the provision’s disputed reach. Section 141(f) required advance congressional reporting about subjects and questions, but allowed modifications based on newly arising circumstances. The later question report could simultaneously modify the earlier subjects report; the DOJ request supplied the asserted new circumstance. The Court also considered any reporting defect harmless because Congress had been fully informed. It assumed rather than resolved the full scope of judicial enforcement of that reporting provision. Principal slip 20–23 & n.3.

## Why a reasonable choice nevertheless required remand

Judicial review ordinarily examines the contemporaneous administrative record and avoids inquiry into officials’ mental processes. The Court did not hold that an administrator must disclose every motive, refrain from political considerations, or approach a subject without an initial policy preference. Soliciting supporting information is not itself unlawful. A strong showing of bad faith or improper behavior, however, can justify inquiry beyond the ordinary record. Here the stipulated, expanded administrative record eventually justified examining the explanation more closely despite the premature initial discovery order. Principal slip 23–26.

The aggregate evidence contradicted the memorandum’s account of a routine DOJ request. Ross had sought the question shortly after entering office, well before DOJ asked for it. Commerce explored requests from other agencies, including immigration-related needs, and sought a suitable justification. DOJ’s request followed Ross’s contact with the Attorney General; Commerce helped produce it. DOJ declined discussion of alternative data sources. These circumstances, considered together, made the sole announced VRA rationale appear contrived rather than the reason reflected in the actual process. Principal slip 26–28.

The defect was therefore more specific than the existence of undisclosed motives. A genuine VRA justification accompanied by additional political preferences would present a different case. The Court found a disconnect between the record and the sole explanation offered, defeating the purpose of requiring reasons that courts and the public can scrutinize. It approved an agency remand for a proper explanation rather than declaring citizenship inquiries inherently forbidden. Principal slip 28–29.

## The separate opinions

Thomas disputed both the exceptional inquiry and the pretext finding. He argued that the record showed multiple possible motives, not that VRA enforcement played no role at all. The Secretary’s initial interest, pursuit of agency support, and refusal to revisit alternatives could coexist with an actual desire for better citizenship data. On his view, regularity should be presumed where the evidence supported competing innocent and improper explanations. The Court’s approach threatened intrusive discovery whenever opponents suspected undisclosed motives, with substantial separation-of-powers costs. Thomas distinguished any genuinely unconstitutional purpose from the APA explanation dispute before the Court. Thomas slip 1–15, including nn.1–5.

Breyer independently concluded that the choice lacked a reasoned evidentiary foundation. Three Bureau studies linked citizenship inquiries to reduced participation; the Secretary’s comparisons to longer forms and a paid Nielsen survey did not answer their controls or their different incentives. Uncertainty did not make the available evidence worthless. Follow-up and proxy responses could not reliably eliminate the resulting count and accuracy problems. Administrative records were generally more reliable than citizenship answers, and Ross did not adequately explain how to handle conflicting answers or why the additional question would improve imputation. Nor did the VRA rationale demonstrate a need that could not be met through records and existing data. Breyer would have sustained the ordinary arbitrary-and-capricious ruling as well as the remand for the inadequate explanation. Breyer slip 1–23.

Alito viewed selection of a demographic question as committed to agency discretion. He distinguished this choice from review of constitutional enumeration requirements or explicit statutory prohibitions. The statute’s broad form-and-content delegation, Congress’s oversight mechanisms, centuries of practice, and the absence of manageable standards weighed against APA review. General reason-giving requirements could not themselves supply reviewability without nullifying §701(a)(2). Census policy also involved numerous interacting judgments unsuitable for judicial supervision. He would have dismissed the APA portion of the complaint, while joining the alternative merits conclusions. Alito slip 1–20, including nn.1–15.
