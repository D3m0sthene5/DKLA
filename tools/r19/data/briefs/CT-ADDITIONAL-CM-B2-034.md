# Haumont v. Security State Bank
220 Neb. 809, 374 N.W.2d 2 (1985) | Contracts

## Decision and appellate review

The Nebraska Supreme Court affirmed equitable rescission of a guaranty, note, and mortgage the bank obtained from Lee and Letha Haumont to secure their adult son's business debts. Judge Hastings wrote for Chief Justice Krivosha and Justices Boslaugh, White, Caporale, Shanahan, and Grant; the September 20, 1985 report contains no separate writing. The bank unsuccessfully challenged proof of duress, notice of rescission, and restoration of its position. Although review was de novo on the record because the action was equitable, the court gave weight to the trial judge's opportunity to observe witnesses where their testimony irreconcilably conflicted. That rule was crucial to resolving the bank president's denial of threats. (809–810, 814–816.)

## The son's debts and progressively stronger demands

The Haumonts had farmed their own 1,500 acres near Broken Bow for more than forty years. Their son Glen operated M & G Implement beginning in 1977. When its difficulties emerged in fall 1981, the parents arranged an approximately $75,000 bank loan for the company; that was their first awareness of its financial trouble. In January 1982 the bank and Production Credit Association discovered six irregular installment contracts totaling about $324,000. Two involved resold machinery without paying liens, three involved repeated financing of the same machinery, and one involved a forged contract subsequently financed. Those problems were additional to $251,932.60 owed on the bank's line of credit. (810–811.)

On January 20 Glen told his father that he could be prosecuted and jailed, describing feared prison conditions without explaining the business dealings. At a February 8 meeting with the accountant, the parents refused further financial backing. The next day Glen nevertheless told bank president David Schweitz that they would support long-term refinancing. The bank sought a written commitment; a statement prepared through the lawyers contemplated pledging Lee's real estate to finance sufficient payment of the company's debts. (811.)

When asked to sign on February 12, Lee insisted on consulting his lawyer, who advised against the unconditional commitment. The lawyer added a right to withdraw until February 26, and Lee signed that revocable version, believing it would give his son time to resolve the business. The bank rejected the qualification. On February 15, after Lee again refused the original commitment, Glen and Schweitz returned to the home. According to Lee, both said his son would be prosecuted and jailed if he did not sign. Lee signed to avoid responsibility for that consequence. Schweitz instead testified that no threat occurred. (811–812.)

The following morning Lee called to say he wanted no more documents. Schweitz persuaded him to permit an explanatory visit; meanwhile, learning equipment had been shipped out of state, the bank prepared a guaranty and mortgage. According to the parents, at the February 16 visit Schweitz demanded signature by 3 p.m. or prosecution through Omaha attorneys would follow. Lee signed because concern about imprisonment displaced everything else, without recognizing that he encumbered debt-free land for $628,708.99. Letha said she did not understand the documents and twice wanted legal advice. Schweitz read them but said there was no time to wait for her lawyer, repeating the prosecution threat. She signed because she could not lose another son. The parents also testified that signatures were not then notarized and no property description was included. (812–813.)

Their daughter corroborated a statement that Schweitz needed to notify his lawyer by noon whether prosecution would occur. Another son testified that Schweitz said prosecution would gain nothing and that signing the mortgage was preferable. The bank president denied any discussion of criminal charges; its lawyer said prosecution was not planned and recalled another local bank's attempt to collect through such threats. The trial judge credited the parents, and the Supreme Court accepted that version. It found their risking a lifetime's savings for the deeply indebted business incredible absent the fear of imprisonment. (813–814.)

## Duress despite the son's involvement and earlier legal advice

Nebraska law treated pressure that overcame the person's will and destroyed the ability to reject unjust demands as duress. Its more specific rule permitted avoidance when a parent or relative executed an instrument under threats and fear of criminal punishment of a child or relative. The accepted evidence fit that rule. The decision did not make ordinary parental concern or a son's request alone sufficient; it rested on the bank president's participation in the threatened prosecution and the resulting constrained choice. (814.)

The earlier consultation did not establish freely informed consent to the later unconditional instruments. Lee had followed the lawyer's advice by agreeing only to a revocable statement. Three days later he again refused an unconditional promise before the threats induced signature; a similar pattern followed the next day for the guaranty and mortgage. Letha's attempt to obtain her own advice was likewise met with urgent prosecution pressure. These circumstances supported rescission rather than making the parents' signatures controlling. (814–815.)

## Notice, restitution, and final disposition

The bank relied on a case requiring notice when a party rescinds by its own act. The court distinguished that legal rescission from asking equity to cancel the instruments. In the latter situation filing the action supplies sufficient disaffirmance; prior notice is unnecessary. Notice had in fact reached the bank's lawyer on February 23 and the bank on March 5, 1982, but the legal distinction independently defeated the objection. (815.)

For the same reason, the parents did not need to accomplish restitution before bringing this equitable action. More decisively, the bank's approximately $89,000 subsequent expenditure conferred no benefit on them, and the court found the bank acted in bad faith. The bank's detriment could ordinarily supply contract consideration, but it did not preserve repayment liability on instruments obtained through the established duress. Cancellation rendered the guaranty, note, and mortgage void, extinguishing any obligation the parents otherwise would have had to repay funds expended under them. The district court's judgment was affirmed. The opinion does not award the parents damages or adjudicate whether the son should actually be criminally prosecuted. (815–816.)
