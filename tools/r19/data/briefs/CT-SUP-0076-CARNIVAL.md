# Carnival Cruise Lines, Inc. v. Shute
499 U.S. 585 (1991) | Supreme Court of the United States | Contracts

## Purchase, injury, and the ticket term

Eulala and Russel Shute bought a seven-day cruise on Carnival's Tropicale through a travel agent in Arlington, Washington. The agent forwarded their payment to Carnival's headquarters in Miami, and Carnival then prepared and sent the tickets to the Shutes in Washington. The sequence mattered to the dissent: the purchasers paid before receiving the detailed terms. The ticket face directed passengers to read three contract pages. Paragraph 3(a) treated acceptance of the ticket as agreement to its terms; paragraph 8 required disputes arising under, connected with, or incident to the passage contract to be litigated exclusively in a court in Florida. The term selected a state in which suit could be brought, rather than one particular state or federal court. 499 U.S. at 587–588.

The Shutes boarded in Los Angeles. The ship traveled to Puerto Vallarta, Mexico, and back. While the ship was in international waters off Mexico, Mrs. Shute slipped on a deck mat during a guided galley tour. The Shutes sued Carnival in the Western District of Washington, alleging negligence by the cruise line and its employees. The Supreme Court decision concerned where that claim could proceed; it did not determine whether Carnival negligently caused the injury or what damages the Shutes could recover. Id. at 588.

## Procedure and the question actually decided

Carnival moved for summary judgment on two grounds: the contractual Florida forum and constitutionally insufficient contacts with Washington. The District Court granted the motion on personal jurisdiction. The Ninth Circuit reversed, reasoning that Carnival's solicitation in Washington was a but-for cause of the cruise and injury and supplied sufficient contacts. It also refused to enforce the forum term because it had not been freely negotiated and because, in the appellate court's view, the record showed the Shutes could not physically and financially pursue suit in Florida. Id. at 588–589.

The Supreme Court's procedural footnote explains an intermediate step that the short account can obscure. The Ninth Circuit's earlier 1988 opinion was withdrawn while it certified a Washington long-arm statutory question to the Washington Supreme Court. That court answered affirmatively, and the Ninth Circuit refiled its opinion with modifications in 1990. The judgment reviewed here was that later Ninth Circuit judgment, reported at 897 F.2d 377. Id. at 588 n.*.

The Supreme Court held the forum term enforceable and reversed. Because that ground disposed of whether the Washington court should hear this action, the Court did not decide Carnival's constitutional personal-jurisdiction argument. Federal admiralty law governed contractual enforceability. The Court also expressly declined to decide whether the Shutes had received sufficient notice before entering the passage contract: they had essentially conceded reasonable communication of the term, and the Ninth Circuit had assumed knowledge despite expressing doubt. A holding about sufficient pre-purchase notice cannot be extracted from a case that reserved that question. Id. at 589–590, 597.

## Negotiation and reasonableness under The Bremen

The Shutes argued that The Bremen v. Zapata Off-Shore Co. required refusal of this unnegotiated term. The Bremen had enforced a forum clause in a carefully negotiated international towage agreement between corporations. Carnival invoked the same decision's favorable treatment of forum clauses. Blackmun explained that neither side could simply transplant its broad language without accounting for the different transactions. Id. at 590–593.

A sophisticated company arranging the international tow of expensive equipment could be expected to negotiate the forum. A passenger buying an ordinary cruise ticket ordinarily could not negotiate its standardized terms and did not have bargaining parity with the cruise line. The majority acknowledged that disparity; it rejected the inference that lack of negotiation by itself made every such term unenforceable. The analysis instead had to assess the reasonableness of a form passage contract in its commercial setting. Id. at 592–593.

The Court gave three reasons a reasonable forum term could be useful. A ship carries passengers from many places, so an accident could expose its owner to suits in numerous forums. Selecting a forum in advance reduces uncertainty and litigation over venue, saving the parties' expense and judicial resources. Passengers might also benefit from lower fares reflecting the carrier's litigation savings. This last point was the majority's economic inference, not a finding that Carnival had proved a particular reduction in the Shutes' ticket price. Id. at 593–594.

Those reasons supported allowing a reasonable clause; they did not remove judicial scrutiny for fundamental fairness. The majority found no indication Carnival selected Florida to discourage legitimate claims. Carnival's principal place of business was there, and many of its cruises began and ended at Florida ports. No evidence showed fraud or overreaching in obtaining the Shutes' acceptance. Given the notice concession, the majority presumed they could reject the contract without adverse consequence. The dissent challenged that last premise by examining the actual ticket and purchase sequence. Id. at 595, 597–598.

## Inconvenience and the statutory objection

The majority rejected the Ninth Circuit's independent conclusion that inability to litigate in Florida required refusal of the term. The District Court had made no findings on the Shutes' physical or financial obstacles; the appellate court's conclusory reference to the record did not provide a basis to validate its finding. The Bremen's concern about serious inconvenience had arisen in discussion of a hypothetical essentially local dispute sent to a remote foreign forum. Florida was not a foreign forum, and an accident off Mexico was not inherently a Washington local dispute. In this setting, and with notice conceded, the Shutes had not carried the heavy burden required to set the clause aside for inconvenience. The Court did not hold that inconvenience could never defeat a form forum clause. Id. at 594–595.

The Shutes separately relied on former 46 U.S.C. App. § 183c. It prohibited passenger-contract provisions relieving a shipowner of negligence liability or weakening the claimant's right to a trial in a competent court on liability and damages. The majority read the ticket as preserving judicial resolution in a competent Florida court, without limiting Carnival's negligence liability. The legislative history addressed liability limits and provisions removing liability determinations from courts through arbitration; it did not prohibit forum selection. The Court therefore rejected the statutory challenge. Id. at 595–597.

This reasoning distinguishes the forum term from an exculpatory clause. Carnival prevailed on the location of litigation, while the passenger's underlying negligence cause of action remained available in the selected forum. The opinion did not approve every other term printed on the ticket. The appended specimen included broad liability provisions, but their presence did not turn the Court's ruling about paragraph 8 into a holding validating the entire ticket.

## Stevens's dissent, joined by Marshall

Stevens disputed both the majority's practical picture of assent and its governing legal approach. He appended the ticket's actual small-print facsimile to show paragraph 8 among 25 terms. Passengers often received those terms only after purchasing. Paragraph 16(a) disclaimed refunds for wholly or partly unused tickets. Without deciding whether that no-refund provision itself was enforceable, Stevens reasoned that an ordinary purchaser would accept the risk of Florida litigation rather than risk losing a paid vacation. Prominent pre-purchase notice would not, in his view, cure the broader statutory and admiralty objections. Id. at 597–598 and appendix following 605.

He connected the case to traditional scrutiny of adhesion contracts and the rule against contractual restrictions on the place of suit. Unequal bargaining power, weak actual assent, and deterrence of negligence had long supported refusal of passenger-ticket exculpation. A forum burden could weaken practical recovery even without formally extinguishing the claim. The Bremen involved equal corporate parties and international negotiation, so Stevens would not extend its reasoning to this consumer passage ticket. Id. at 598–602.

On the statute, Stevens emphasized the prohibition against weakening access to a competent court and the legislative condemnation of similar liability-restricting practices. He regarded the cost of transporting a west-coast injury dispute and its witnesses to Florida as a practical impairment of the individuals' claim. Decisions under analogous cargo legislation had invalidated foreign forum clauses; the burden on individuals crossing the country could be proportionately comparable to the burden on a corporation litigating overseas. He would invalidate the term under the statute and, independently, under the traditional admiralty rule. Id. at 602–605.

His footnotes reinforce those distinctions. Short notice and limitations periods and unfavorable choice-of-law terms can also impair recovery. Lower fares cannot alone justify every restriction, because the same savings argument could support a complete liability waiver prohibited by established law. He also noted that the majority did not clarify whether its result would extend to a clause choosing Panama, Carnival's place of incorporation. These were dissenting criticisms and an identified limit, rather than holdings that Florida and Panama clauses were equally enforceable. Id. at 599–600 nn.1–4, 602 n.5, 604 n.6.

## Judgment, alignment, and assigned coverage

The Court reversed the Ninth Circuit's judgment. Blackmun wrote for seven Justices: Rehnquist, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and himself. Stevens dissented with Marshall. There was no concurrence. The operative conclusion enforced this Florida clause; the Court did not resolve constitutional personal jurisdiction or the negligence merits, award Carnival damages, or adjudicate all ticket provisions. Id. at 586–587, 597.
