# Nez Perce Tribe of Indians v. United States
176 Ct. Cl. 815 (1966) | Contracts

## Court, decision, and posture

The United States Court of Claims decided this appeal on July 15, 1966. Judge Laramore delivered the court’s opinion. The Nez Perce Tribe challenged the Indian Claims Commission’s rejection of additional compensation for reservation lands ceded to the United States. The court agreed that the Tribe had not established fraud, duress, or unfair and dishonorable dealings, but rejected the Commission’s treatment of unconscionable consideration. It reversed and remanded for further proceedings while rejecting the Tribe’s asserted right to interest on the additional recovery. The preserved judicial reproduction contains one court opinion and seven notes, without an appellate concurrence or dissent; it does not identify a numerical voting lineup. (Opinion ¶¶ 1–3, 22–24; 819, 829–830.)

## The cession and the claim

The disputed transaction was the agreement of May 1, 1893, ratified on August 15, 1894. Immediately before this agreement, the Tribe’s northern Idaho reservation contained approximately 762,000 acres. The agreement provided for individual allotments and tribal trust lands, with the remaining 549,559 acres ceded to the United States for a price the opinion describes as $2.97 per acre. The agreement specified total consideration of $1,626,222: $626,222 was to be paid per capita as soon as practicable after ratification, and the remaining $1 million was to be held in trust at five percent interest. These express payment terms later controlled the distinct claim for interest. (¶¶ 3, 19, 22; 819, 827–828, 829–830.)

The Tribe sought relief under section 2, clauses (3) and (5), of the Indian Claims Commission Act. Clause (3) authorized claims that would result from revising treaties, contracts, or agreements on grounds including fraud, duress, or unconscionable consideration. Clause (5) addressed fair and honorable dealings not recognized by existing rules of law or equity. The case therefore arose under a specific statutory remedy for tribal claims against the United States, rather than an ordinary private purchaser’s suit to rescind a land contract. (¶ 2 & n. 2.)

August 15, 1894 was the agreed valuation date. Before the Commission, the Tribe maintained that the ceded lands were worth $12.63 per acre; the government argued for $1.29. The Commission said the evidence could support $4 as a maximum, but considered the difference between that figure and the price paid insufficiently large to be unconscionable. It also found no proven fraud, duress, or unfair and dishonorable dealing. Commissioner Scott separately regarded the price discrepancy as unconscionable when considered in its aggregate amount, but believed precedent requiring a very gross disparity constrained that conclusion. Scott’s writing belonged to the Commission stage and is not a Court of Claims concurrence. (¶ 3 & nn. 1, 4–5; 819.)

## The land and the valuation evidence

The ceded area lay east of Lewiston, principally on a high, gently undulating plateau divided by canyons. Slightly more than half was best suited for agriculture, especially cereal crops; somewhat less than a quarter was timberland, with the balance suited for grazing. Climate and soils favored cultivation, and Lewiston and nearby counties were developing. Navigable portions of the Clearwater River, nearby rail transportation, and wagon trails provided some access, but accessibility varied considerably across the tract. The allotted lands tended to include favorable agricultural classifications and areas closer to markets and transportation, leaving the ceded area less attractive than a simple comparison with the surrounding region might suggest. (¶¶ 4–7; 819–820.)

When the ceded lands opened to settlement in November 1895, the best lands were taken rapidly: 507 homestead filings occurred in thirteen days. The agreement and opening proclamation set purchase prices of $3.75 for agricultural acres and $5 for stone, timber, and mineral acres. The opinion also reports that much of the land was subsequently acquired free under the homestead laws. Thus the later disposal history did not establish that the government sold every acre for the statutory price, but purchases at those prices helped the court assess whether its original bargain had been unusually favorable. The court also considered the large undeveloped acreage, necessary improvement expenditures, limited access, and the depressed economic conditions surrounding the 1894 valuation date. (¶¶ 8–9, 16; 821–822, 825–826.)

Both sides relied chiefly on appraisals using comparable sales, supplemented by maps, photographs, charts, and evidence about soils and transportation. The Tribe’s appraiser, William C. Brown, reached $11.89 per acre; that expert figure should not be conflated with the Tribe’s pleaded $12.63 valuation. The government’s appraiser, Homer Hoyt, reached $1.29. Brown included transactions in developed areas with superior land or transportation; Hoyt used fewer transactions and included distant, inadequately comparable lands. The Commission found Brown too optimistic and Hoyt too pessimistic. The court agreed with those criticisms, while giving greater weight to Brown’s basic approach. (¶¶ 10–11, 16; 822–823, 825–826.)

## Why $4 was a minimum, rather than a ceiling

The court examined several possible adjustments to Brown’s comparable-sales analysis. Brown used a twenty percent discount for improvements. The court thought a figure around twenty-five percent might better reflect not merely existing improvements on comparable properties, but the actual cost of bringing the ceded land to a similarly developed state. It also considered an additional twenty-to-twenty-five percent allowance for remoteness and limited transportation access, and a similar allowance for the tract’s immense size. A purchaser of half a million acres would incur subdivision, resale, and holding costs that purchasers of small improved parcels would not. The court acknowledged the Tribe’s constrained position in the cession, while explaining why bulk-sale valuation could still account for genuine development and disposition costs. (¶¶ 12–14; 823–825.)

These percentages were exploratory valuation tools, not mandatory discounts imposed on remand. The court expressly said it did not know whether the Commission had used them and did not direct it to do so. Its rough illustration started around $20 per acre and produced $5–$7 after discounts of sixty-five to seventy-five percent. Appraisal was an inexact undertaking, especially seventy years after the valuation date. The point was that even substantial allowances supported $4 as a floor; they did not justify treating it as the highest possible value. The court held that substantial evidence supported the $4 figure and that the overwhelming weight of the evidence required treating it as a minimum. (¶¶ 15–16; 825–826.)

## Unconscionable consideration

The governing precedent required a very gross inequality before disparity in price alone could establish unconscionable consideration. It supplied no mechanical percentage formula; each case required attention to its facts and circumstances. Earlier decisions involving disparities exceeding one hundred percent did not establish that smaller discrepancies were automatically exempt. The Commission had relied too heavily on the percentage gap. Its Miami comparison also required care: as note 6 explains, the prior appeal corrected the consideration figure to thirty-eight percent of actual value, and therefore had not decided whether payment of sixty-three percent would itself be adequate. (¶¶ 17–18 & n. 6; 826–827.)

Three features made this transaction significant. First, the $1.03 difference between the reported purchase price and the minimum $4 value, multiplied by 549,559 acres, amounted to $566,045.77—a substantial sum in 1894. Second, the court considered the likelihood that additional consideration, if paid at the time, would have earned interest in a trust comparable to the agreement’s existing trust. Its very rough estimate put that hypothetical interest near $200,000. Third, and most importantly, $4 was the bare minimum a reasonable willing seller would have accepted, not merely one defensible valuation among several overlapping with the price paid. The legitimate range of disagreement began at $4 and extended upward. These considerations supported relief for unconscionable consideration without abandoning the requirement of a very gross disparity. (¶¶ 19–21; 827–829.)

The court did not require the Commission to find a particular higher value, nor did it award a final damages sum. Its reasoning allowed recognition of a higher, unstated value when assessing unconscionability, while remanding for further proceedings. The $566,045.77 differential and hypothetical interest were analytical measures, not a money judgment entered by the court. (¶¶ 19–24; 827–830.)

## Why interest was nevertheless unavailable

The Tribe separately argued that additional compensation should bear five percent interest because a fair payment in 1894 would have entered the existing trust. Note 7 records that interest was not mentioned in the Commission petition; the Tribe said it raised the issue at the beginning of trial and that all parties thereafter knew of it. The court addressed the asserted entitlement rather than rejecting it simply for omission from the petition. (¶ 22 & n. 7; 829–830.)

Interest on claims against the United States ordinarily required an express statutory or contractual authorization, apart from the identified constitutional taking exception. Article III prescribed an exact total and made only its specified $1 million remainder interest-bearing. It did not create an open-ended trust encompassing any later judicial determination of fair value. Unlike Pawnee, where an expressly promised sum had not been paid, this claim concerned money beyond the amount the agreement specified. A probability sufficient to inform the unconscionability analysis could not rewrite the agreement to establish legal entitlement to interest on the differential. The court therefore rejected that interest claim. (¶ 22; 829–830.)

## Result and the assigned problem

The reported judgment reverses the Commission’s decision and remands for proceedings conforming to the opinion. It leaves intact the conclusion that fraud, duress, and unfair and dishonorable dealings were not proved, but corrects the Commission’s minimum-versus-maximum valuation and its approach to unconscionable consideration. No separate appellate writing appears in the preserved complete reproduction. (¶¶ 3, 23–24; 819, 830.)

The assigned historical-price problem begins at the bottom of C&M printed 698 and ends near the top of 699, physical PDF 41–42. It compresses $4 into the Commission’s valuation and describes the purchase price as placed in trust. The complete opinion distinguishes the Commission’s maximum from the court’s minimum and specifies that $626,222 was payable per capita, with only the $1 million remainder placed in the contractual interest-bearing trust. The full opinion also explains why the trust’s economic significance supported unconscionability without authorizing interest on the additional recovery.
