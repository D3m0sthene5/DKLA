# Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.

**465 U.S. 770 (1984) · Supreme Court of the United States · March 20, 1984**

## A national libel claim in the one remaining timely forum

Kathy Keeton, a New York resident, claimed that Hustler Magazine had libeled her in five issues published between September 1975 and May 1976. Hustler was an Ohio corporation with its principal place of business in California. It sold approximately 10,000 to 15,000 magazines in New Hampshire each month. Keeton’s only connection with New Hampshire was that a different magazine she helped produce circulated there and credited her work in fine print. Her claim was not about that other magazine; it concerned Hustler’s allegedly defamatory publications. 465 U.S. at 772.

Keeton first sued for libel and invasion of privacy in Ohio. That action failed under the applicable limitations rules: Ohio’s limitation barred the libel claim, and the Ohio court applied New York’s limitation to the privacy claim. She then filed this diversity action in the federal district court in New Hampshire in October 1980. New Hampshire’s six-year period made it the only state where the libel action was not time-barred when filed. Id. at 772–773 & n.1.

The district court dismissed for lack of personal jurisdiction under the Fourteenth Amendment. The First Circuit affirmed. It thought New Hampshire had too little interest in redressing a nonresident’s libel injuries, most of which occurred elsewhere. It also relied on the single publication rule, under which one action ordinarily covers damages from the publication across jurisdictions, and on New Hampshire’s unusually long limitation period. Combining nationwide damages with this small share of circulation and this largely unconnected plaintiff seemed unfair to the court of appeals. The Supreme Court reviewed that jurisdictional dismissal, not a finding that Hustler’s articles were actually libelous. Id. at 772–774.

## Holding: regular forum circulation supported this publication-based claim

The Court held that Hustler’s deliberate, regular circulation in New Hampshire supplied sufficient contacts for a libel action based on the magazine’s contents. Monthly sales of thousands of copies were purposeful market activity, rather than random or isolated encounters. New Hampshire’s long-arm statute reached torts committed in whole or part there and had been interpreted to extend to constitutional limits. The claim arose from the very publication activity Hustler conducted in the state. Id. at 774–775 & n.4.

The holding did not depend on treating those sales as enough for every unrelated lawsuit. The Court expressly distinguished the more substantial corporate activities that supported unrelated-claim jurisdiction in *Perkins*. It was Hustler’s purposeful market exploitation together with the publication-based claim that mattered here. Id. at 779–780 & n.11.

## Why nationwide damages did not make the forum unfair

The Court accepted that it had to assess fairness in light of the actual suit for multistate damages, not imagine a smaller action limited to New Hampshire injuries. But New Hampshire had legitimate interests in the dispute. Defamation harms readers as well as the person defamed; the state could protect its residents from false statements circulated there. A nonresident could also suffer reputational harm within New Hampshire even without a previously established local reputation, because defamatory publication could create an unfavorable reputation where the person had previously been unknown. New Hampshire’s criminal defamation law and its removal of a resident-victim restriction from the long-arm statute confirmed that its protection was not limited to local plaintiffs. Id. at 775–777 & nn.5–7.

The single publication rule strengthened the justification for a unitary proceeding. It generally permits one damages action covering a publication across jurisdictions and makes the resulting merits judgment preclusive between the same parties. That approach conserves judicial resources and protects the publisher against repeated litigation over one publication. A state with a genuine connection to the publication could cooperate with other states by providing that forum, rather than confining the action to the fraction of harm within its borders. Id. at 773 n.2, 777–778 & n.8.

This jurisdictional holding did not itself award nationwide damages or decide that every substantive condition for such an award had been satisfied. Footnote 9 expressly reserved the actual applicability of the single publication rule to the case’s circumstances as a substantive-law issue. The Court held that the district court could entertain the multistate suit. Id. at 778 n.9.

## Limitations and the plaintiff’s connections were separate issues

The Court rejected the idea that the expiration of other states’ limitation periods defeated New Hampshire jurisdiction. Those periods did not change the relationship among Hustler, New Hampshire, and the alleged libel. Keeton’s search for an advantageous forum resembled ordinary litigation choices, while Hustler had deliberately entered the market and could be expected to know the state’s laws. Id. at 778–779.

That did not establish that New Hampshire’s limitation period necessarily governed every out-of-state injury. The applicability and possible constitutional unfairness of that choice were issues to address after jurisdiction existed. The Court separated choice of law from the power to adjudicate and declined to resolve whether applying the local limitation would itself violate due process. Id. at 778 & n.10.

Nor did due process require Keeton herself to have minimum contacts with New Hampshire. The jurisdictional protection concerns the nonresident defendant’s relationship with the forum. A plaintiff’s residence may enhance that relationship when the defendant’s conduct focuses on the plaintiff there, but it is not an independent prerequisite. Keeton’s limited local connection did not defeat jurisdiction supported by Hustler’s own conduct. Id. at 779–781.

## Brennan’s concurrence and the decision’s limits

Justice Rehnquist wrote for eight Justices. Justice Brennan concurred in the judgment, making the result unanimous. Brennan agreed that Hustler’s regular circulation was sufficiently important and related to the libel claim to satisfy due process. He preferred that direct explanation without independently invoking New Hampshire’s substantive-law or limitation interests. State interests mattered, in his view, only insofar as they bore on the defendant’s protected liberty, rather than as separate claims of state sovereign power. Id. at 782.

The Court reversed the First Circuit and remanded. It did not decide that Larry Flynt or Hustler’s holding company, also named as defendants, was subject to jurisdiction merely because Hustler was. Each defendant’s contacts had to be assessed separately, and their jurisdictional status remained open on remand. Employment did not supply automatic immunity, but corporate jurisdiction did not automatically establish employee or affiliate jurisdiction either. Id. at 781 & n.13.

The Court also rejected a special jurisdictional shield derived from the First Amendment; the relevant speech protections operated through the substantive law rather than defeating otherwise proper personal jurisdiction. Id. at 780 n.12. The assigned casebook account at printed pages 124–125 emphasizes market contacts, state interests, the nonresident plaintiff, and limitations.
