# Carranza v. Mercedes-Benz USA LLC

**Contracts — 2021 WL 4805198 (C.D. Cal. 2021), as cited in the assignment. Assigned: C&M Textbook 1051–1313, PDF 172, printed 1178, Problem (3). Source-limited assigned problem; the complete cited judicial decision remains unrecovered.**

The textbook presents a customer who buys an automobile from a dealer. The dealer bought it from the manufacturer. The customer–dealer sales agreement includes a mandatory arbitration clause; after problems with the car, the customer sues the manufacturer. The problem asks whether the manufacturer can claim intended-beneficiary status under the customer–dealer contract and compel arbitration. Carranza is cited as the case to consult. These facts are the editors' formulated problem, not certified findings or a verbatim account of the unrecovered opinion. (Assigned 1178.)

The distinction between the two commercial relationships makes the question intelligible. Being the manufacturer connects the defendant to the product, but the arbitration promise described in the problem appears in the customer's separate agreement with the dealer. The intended-beneficiary argument attempts to explain why a party outside that agreement might nevertheless enforce one of its promises. The problem therefore calls for attention to the actual agreement's language, whom it benefits, and the legal requirements for enforcement by a nonsignatory. It supplies no answer to those inquiries and no contractual wording beyond the presence of mandatory arbitration.

The location of the problem reinforces that focus: it follows discussion of third-party beneficiaries and precedes a different principal decision. It should not be turned into a finding that the manufacturer necessarily was—or necessarily was not—an intended beneficiary. Nor does the assignment resolve alternative arbitration theories or establish that an order compelling arbitration was entered.
