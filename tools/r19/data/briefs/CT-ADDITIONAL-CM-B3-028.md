# Hilton Hotels Corp. v. Butch Lewis Productions, Inc.
107 Nev. 226, 808 P.2d 919 (1991) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

Justice Springer wrote the Nevada Supreme Court’s April 3, 1991 opinion; Chief Justice Mowbray, Justices Steffen and Young, and Senior Justice Zenoff concurred. Zenoff sat in place of Justice Rose, as the final judicial note records. Rehearing was denied August 28, 1991. The court reversed the defendants’ judgment and remanded for a new trial on Hilton’s claim for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing because critical rebuttal evidence had been excluded. It rejected Hilton’s objections to jury tampering and the contract instructions. The remand did not establish that the promoters had deliberately caused a title forfeiture or award Hilton damages. (107 Nev. at 226–228, 229–232, 237 & n. 8.)

## Bargain, disappointment, and litigation

Hilton contracted with Dynamic Duo, Inc., a corporation formed by boxing promoters Butch Lewis and Don King, to present the final four events in a heavyweight Unification Series at the Las Vegas Hilton. The series was intended to select a single champion from the titleholders recognized by three boxing organizations. Michael Spinks then held the International Boxing Federation championship. Hilton contended that the bargain contemplated his participation throughout the four events. The written instrument did not expressly impose that continuing obligation. Its recital did identify Spinks for the first September 1986 event, but its provision requiring Dynamic Duo to supply boxers for each event did not name particular fighters. That limited reference matters: the contract was not wholly silent about Spinks, but it did not expressly require him in all four bouts. (Id. at 228–229 & n. 1.)

Before the final Ultimate Unification Bout, Spinks forfeited his IBF championship and became ineligible for the series. Hilton alleged that Dynamic Duo and its agents intentionally brought about the forfeiture to pursue more profitable fights outside the series, defeating Hilton’s expected benefits. It sued on breach-of-contract, conspiracy, and intentional-interference theories. The jury found for defendants on all claims; judgment followed, and the district court denied Hilton’s new-trial motion. Hilton appealed the judgment and denial order, challenging jury misconduct, the contract instructions, and exclusion of testimony either as rebuttal or upon reopening its case. (Id. at 228–229.)

## Contract interpretation and the rejected instruction challenge

Dynamic Duo argued that Hilton should have put a continuing Spinks requirement into the instrument if it wanted one. Hilton proposed an instruction explaining that breach could concern an express or implied term recognized by the parties despite its absence from the written text. The trial court declined that wording but instructed the jury to determine the parties’ intention from the contract’s words and surrounding circumstances, including negotiations, precontract statements, the transaction’s object, and its subject matter. Those instructions permitted Hilton’s interpretation; they did not confine the jury to a rigid literal reading. (Id. at 230–232 & nn. 2–3.)

The Supreme Court favored that contextual approach but concluded the jury had been given a fair opportunity to find a mutually understood obligation to produce Spinks throughout the series. It declined to infer that obligation itself after the jury rejected it. There was consequently no instructional error warranting a new trial on ordinary contract breach. Separately, the court accepted the trial judge’s handling of the alleged jury tampering and misconduct as within the bounds of discretion and found no substantial prejudice requiring reversal on that ground. These rejected grounds did not preclude reversal for a different evidentiary error affecting the good-faith claim. (Id. at 229–232.)

## The implied covenant and its distinct function

Compliance with the contract’s literal obligations did not dispose of Hilton’s separate contention that the promoters deliberately defeated its purpose. Relying on A.C. Shaw Construction, the court explained that contractual damages could be recovered for performance contravening the bargain’s intention and spirit even where express requirements had been satisfied. It distinguished this contractual claim from the narrower tort remedy, which required special reliance or a fiduciary element in settings such as insurance or certain wrongful-discharge cases. This case did not generally convert every disappointed commercial expectation into a tort claim. (Id. at 232–233 & n. 4.)

Although Dynamic Duo had no established contractual obligation to supply Spinks for all four events, it had a duty not deliberately to interfere with his ability to participate and thereby destroy Hilton’s contractual benefits for its own advantage. Whether Lewis intentionally caused Spinks to lose his title in order to secure more lucrative outside fights remained a factual question. The court treated the duty to perform fairly and refrain from such manipulation as distinct from an affirmative promise guaranteeing a particular fighter’s continued championship. (Id. at 233–235.)

Hilton had not specifically pleaded the covenant claim, although its complaint broadly alleged breach of obligations under the agreement. The court concluded that the theory apparently had been tried by consent: an unobjected-to instruction told the jury that parties could not destroy or injure one another’s right to receive contractual benefits or prevent performance. Its discussion of a percentage-rent tenant diverting business to reduce rental obligations was an illustrative analogy, not a fact about Hilton or a damages calculation. (Id. at 233–234 & nn. 5–6.)

## Why the excluded evidence required a new trial

Hilton’s trial evidence of Lewis’s intentions had consisted of newspaper accounts. Several quoted him taking credit after the fact for higher purses outside the series. Those retrospective claims did not clearly establish a prior design deliberately to cause Spinks’s forfeiture. An earlier article could instead suggest an intention to remain within the series. The opinion’s lengthy seventh note identifies the articles and their differing temporal contexts. Their reported millions in fight purses were evidence discussed in the opinion, not an award of contract damages. (Id. at 235–236 & n. 7.)

In rebuttal, Hilton offered testimony from Duane Ford, then chairman of the Nevada Athletic Commission, that Lewis had said in September 1986—before the Spinks-Cooney arrangement—that he planned to withdraw Spinks from the series to make more money elsewhere. The trial court excluded it as material that should have been in Hilton’s case-in-chief and as more prejudicial than probative. The Supreme Court disagreed. The defense maintained that Lewis and Dynamic Duo had done nothing to interrupt Spinks’s title; Ford’s proffer directly answered that position and supplied powerful evidence of earlier intention. Defendants could cross-examine him, offer contradictory material, or request time, and Lewis remained present despite the contention that other witnesses had been released. The record did not show a critical defense witness unavailable to respond. (Id. at 236.)

If Hilton discovered the statement only after its initial presentation, reopening might also have been appropriate, but the Supreme Court was reluctant to declare abuse of discretion on that ground. Its actual holding was that exclusion as rebuttal was erroneous and prejudicial. It therefore reversed and remanded specifically for a new trial on the good-faith covenant claim, leaving intent and liability for determination on a record permitting the testimony. (Id. at 236–237.)
