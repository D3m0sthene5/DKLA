# Link v. Wabash Railroad Co.

**370 U.S. 626 (1962) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 25, 1962.** Justice Harlan wrote the majority opinion. Justice Black dissented, joined by Chief Justice Warren; Justice Douglas separately noted his dissent without a written opinion. Justices Frankfurter and White did not participate in the decision. The judgment divided 4–3. Civil Procedure: inherent dismissal authority, failure to prosecute, notice, and counsel’s conduct. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical pages 529–530, the developed Link account at printed 1039–1040 and its attached editorial discussion through 1041.

## Facts and litigation history

William Link filed a diversity negligence action in August 1954 seeking compensation for injuries from a collision between his vehicle and a Wabash train. The district court originally entered judgment on the pleadings for the railroad; the Seventh Circuit reversed in 1956, and the Supreme Court denied the railroad’s petition in 1957. The district court thereafter set a July 1957 trial, continued it at Link’s request without the railroad’s objection, and later issued a notice that the case would be dismissed unless retained. Link answered outstanding interrogatories the day before the show-cause hearing, more than 19 months after they had been served. The judge retained the case and set another trial, but continued that setting on the railroad’s motion with Link’s agreement. Additional interrogatories were answered in 1960 after an extension. These details matter because the majority relied on the accumulated history, while the dissent disputed its implications. (627–629, n.2; 634–635, n.11.)

On September 29, 1960, the court notified both sides’ counsel of a pretrial conference in Hammond, Indiana, for October 12 at 1 p.m. Link’s lawyer called opposing counsel the preceding morning and said he expected to attend. At about 10:45 a.m. on conference day, he called the judge’s secretary from Indianapolis, roughly 160 miles away. He said he was preparing papers for the Indiana Supreme Court, was not actually engaged in an argument, and could attend the next afternoon or on October 14 if the conference were reset. He did not appear at the scheduled time. (627–628.)

Two hours after the scheduled conference, the district court found that counsel had supplied no reasonable explanation and dismissed the case on its own initiative, invoking inherent authority and failure to prosecute. The Seventh Circuit affirmed over a dissent. The Supreme Court reviewed both the court’s power to dismiss and whether exercising that power here exceeded its discretion. It did not decide the underlying collision’s negligence merits. (629.)

## Holding and the source of power

The Supreme Court **affirmed**. A federal trial court has inherent power to dismiss with prejudice for failure to prosecute, and Rule 41(b)’s authorization for a defendant to move for dismissal does not eliminate the court’s ability to act on its own. Here, the majority found no abuse of discretion when the missed conference was considered with the litigation’s earlier delays. (629–636.)

Link argued that Rule 41(b)’s reference to a defendant’s motion implied that such a motion was necessary. The Court rejected that negative implication. The rule’s permissive language did not clearly withdraw a longstanding judicial power to manage proceedings and prevent undue delay. The opinion traced historical nonsuit and non-prosecution practice, identified cases recognizing the inherent authority, and surveyed local practices for clearing stale cases. Note 7’s extensive list of local rules supplies historical support, not a determination that a particular listed local rule governed Link’s dismissal. (629–632, nn.3–7.)

Note 8 separately rejects the argument that no local rule covered this situation. The Court pointed to Rule 83’s permission to regulate matters not provided for by rule in ways consistent with the rules and found no inconsistency. The decision therefore did not rest on a newly discovered local rule expressly authorizing this exact sanction. (633, n.8.)

## Notice and the opportunity to seek correction

Absence of advance notice of dismissal or a preliminary adversary hearing did not automatically invalidate the order. Due process requires a meaningful opportunity to be heard, but the adequacy of procedures depends partly on what the circumstances tell a party about the consequences of its conduct. The majority concluded that this record permitted dismissal without an additional warning and hearing. This is a contextual ruling, not a declaration that notice is unnecessary whenever a judge labels a sanction “inherent.” (632–633.)

The availability of Rule 60(b) relief reduced the significance of omitted advance procedures in the majority’s analysis. Link never asked the district court to reopen the judgment with a better explanation for counsel’s absence. The Court expressly did **not** decide whether denial of such a supported motion would have abused discretion; no such application was before it. It also distinguished inability to comply beyond a party’s control: counsel had notice of the conference and the record did not demonstrate an unavoidable failure. (632, 635–636.)

## The narrow discretionary ruling

The majority considered counsel’s telephone explanation inadequate and thought his absence, together with the earlier course of the litigation, reasonably supported an inference of deliberate delay. The case was the oldest civil matter on the district court’s docket, according to note 9. Note 11 emphasizes Link’s initial post-remand continuance, the long-unanswered interrogatories, his agreement to the railroad’s later continuance, and the lack of an affirmative effort to bring the matter to trial. These were the majority’s reasons for rejecting the contention that the delay was solely the railroad’s or the court’s fault. (633–635, nn.9, 11.)

Crucially, the Court **reserved whether unexplained absence from a pretrial conference alone would justify dismissal with prejudice when no other dilatoriness appeared**. It sustained this order because the district court relied on the broader circumstances. Note 12 explains that even if the appellate opinion could be read more broadly, the Supreme Court could affirm on the narrower, supported district-court ground. The holding is consequently not an automatic one-absence rule. (634–635, n.12.)

## Responsibility for counsel

The majority rejected the argument that holding the client responsible unfairly punished someone other than the lawyer. Link had voluntarily selected counsel, whose acts and omissions were attributed to him under representative litigation. Note 10 discusses related authority and identifies malpractice litigation as a remedy where counsel’s performance fell substantially below reasonable conduct. Continuing the railroad action solely to spare Link the consequences of counsel’s omissions would, in the majority’s view, shift their burden to the defendant. The Court did not find malpractice liability or award compensation against the attorney. (633–634, n.10.)

## Black’s dissent, joined by Warren

Black disputed both the record and the fairness of extinguishing the claim. He read the dismissal and appellate reasoning as punishment for the lawyer’s missed conference rather than a substantiated determination of general non-prosecution. Much of the case’s age arose from the railroad’s successful but erroneous pleading motion and the ensuing review; other delays were agreed continuances or litigation activities. The court had previously retained the case after a show-cause hearing. Even counsel’s request to reset the conference indicated continued prosecution, not abandonment. (636–643, dissent nn.1–10.)

The dissent did not deny that courts could discipline lawyers or that clients sometimes bear consequences of counsel’s decisions. It rejected mechanically extending those propositions to destruction of an innocent client’s claim without warning. Black treated the cause of action as valuable property, argued that due process required notice to the client before such a loss, and explained why an ordinary layperson could reasonably rely on a licensed lawyer rather than supervise every procedural task. His long note 11 distinguishes the majority’s criminal-waiver authorities from the situation here; note 13 rejects Rule 60(b)’s availability as an answer to the client’s lack of notice. (637, 644–648, dissent nn.11–13.)

Black also challenged the docket-management justification. Dismissing potentially meritorious cases without reaching the merits might generate years of appellate litigation rather than save resources; he considered this case an example. He would not endorse dismissal merely because the lawyer failed to attend under the circumstances presented. Warren joined that written dissent; Douglas’s separately reported dissent adds no written rationale to summarize. (648–649; notation 636.)

## Judgment and assigned comparison

The Court **affirmed the Seventh Circuit’s judgment sustaining dismissal**. The action ended without a negligence trial. The majority’s record-specific ruling, its reserved single-absence question, and the unpresented Rule 60(b) issue should all remain distinct. (635–636.)

The assigned introduction says that petitioner and counsel failed to attend; the actual judicial narrative concerns counsel’s nonappearance and the attribution of its consequences to Link. It also calls the event a trial date, whereas the particular October event was a pretrial conference after two prior trial settings had been continued. The assigned quotations correctly retain the majority’s express reservation about absence alone. Its dissent summary emphasizes sanctions directed at counsel rather than dismissal harming the client; the full dissent develops the additional procedural, historical, and constitutional objections above. (Assigned printed 1039–1040; actual 627–629, 634–636.)
