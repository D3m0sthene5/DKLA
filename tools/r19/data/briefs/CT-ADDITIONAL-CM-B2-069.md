# Fina Supply, Inc. v. Abilene National Bank
726 S.W.2d 537 (Tex. 1987) | Contracts

## Holding and appellate stages
The Texas Supreme Court affirmed a take-nothing judgment against the beneficiary of a letter of credit. A bank officer's representations about the legal effect of amendments were nonactionable opinions in this transaction between equally sophisticated businesses with access to counsel. Reformation also failed, and the beneficiary's documents did not strictly comply with the unreformed credit. Wallace wrote the March 18, 1987 opinion; rehearing was denied April 22. No separate writing or divided vote is reported. (537–542.)

The final judgment was not a reversal of the Court of Appeals. That court had already reversed the trial court's fraud award and reformation order and rendered that Fina take nothing. The Supreme Court affirmed that result, but disapproved the intermediate court's election-of-remedies rationale. The assigned note's abbreviated description of reversal refers to the displacement of Fina's trial victory; this brief preserves the cited Supreme Court stage separately. (538, 541–542; assigned printed 519.)

## Oil exchange and documentary credit
Fina and Brio Petroleum acquired oil for their affiliated refineries. They agreed to exchange stocks of oil to reduce transportation costs, intending equivalent quantities and payments to settle imbalances. At Brio's request, Abilene National Bank issued irrevocable letter of credit No. 1342 in Fina's favor on September 28, 1981. The opinion describes the oil-exchange agreement as made in October; those reported dates are retained rather than silently reordered. (538–539.)

The credit required Fina's draft with a commercial invoice for approximately 124,000 barrels of Giddings-type crude at $36.25 per barrel delivered through October 1981, a pipeline receipt or statement showing delivery to Brio through the specified Exxon Pipeline station, and an authorized certification that the amount represented balances Brio owed. Partial drawings were prohibited and presentment could not precede November 23. These requirements made the credit's documentary scope significant independently of the underlying oil debt. (539.)

Fina and Brio negotiated an extension to cover November and December imbalances. Fina representative McEwen contacted Kathy Kiser, the bank vice president responsible for letters of credit. The first amendment expressly expanded coverage to those months and extended expiration to January 31, 1982. Fina's lawyers reviewed that amendment to ensure the desired coverage. (539–540.)

Three later amendments extended expiration to February 26, March 3, and May 31, 1982, without expressly extending the shipment months. Fina representatives Reese and Holmes sought coverage for additional months; Fina maintained that Kiser assured them extending expiration would also cover later exchange imbalances. Reese specifically questioned why the second amendment differed from the first. Those alleged assurances supplied the fraud theory, rather than a claim that the written amendments themselves explicitly expanded shipment coverage. (539.)

## Trial judgment and legal effect of the amendments
The bank dishonored Fina's draft and sought a declaration that its documents did not strictly comply. Fina sued for breach, fraud, and reformation; the actions were consolidated. Following a jury verdict, the trial court awarded $4.5 million actual and $6.5 million exemplary damages on fraud and reformed the credit to cover imbalances through April 1982. The Court of Appeals reversed and rendered a take-nothing judgment. Those amounts describe the displaced trial award, not Fina's final recovery. (538.)

The Supreme Court applied its earlier Westwind decision: extending a credit's expiration while leaving other terms unchanged lengthened the time to make proper presentment, not the months of covered shipments. Kiser's contrary assurance therefore concerned the legal effect of an instrument. Such a representation ordinarily counted as opinion rather than actionable factual fraud. (539–540.)

The rule had exceptions. Superior knowledge coupled with studied deception of another's legal ignorance could support relief; a fiduciary or confidential relationship could justify reliance; and a legal representation intended and understood as factual could sometimes be treated as such. The court did not hold that every statement about a document's meaning was immune from fraud liability. (540.)

Here both parties were sophisticated businesses dealing at arm's length, with no confidential or fiduciary relationship and equal access to legal advice. Fina had extensive experience with letters of credit and had actually obtained legal review of the first amendment, but did not do so for the later ones. Nothing prevented its inspection or independent legal evaluation. In this equal-bargaining setting, the court declined to apply the exception for legal propositions intended and understood as facts. Kiser's statements concerned the amendments' effect, not an asserted existing factual state, and Fina's choice to accept her interpretation did not excuse ordinary business care. (540–541.)

## Election of remedies did not bar reformation
Fina successfully challenged the Court of Appeals' election-of-remedies analysis. Election required pursuit of one among inconsistent available remedies to the exclusion of others; the doctrine prevented obtaining inconsistent relief for the same wrong and was not favored. Mistaken pursuit of a remedy unavailable as a matter of law did not create an election. Because Fina had no actionable fraud claim, that unsuccessful pursuit did not bar its alternative reformation request. The Supreme Court expressly disapproved this part of the intermediate opinion. (541.)

That correction did not entitle Fina to reformation. Reformation required mutual mistake or another sufficient ground. The trial court had relied on unilateral mistake coupled with fraudulent or inequitable conduct, alternatively mutual mistake. The Supreme Court had rejected actionable fraud and found no inequitable conduct preventing Fina's examination. Its mistake arose from misplaced reliance on an opinion about legal effect. (541.)

There was a further problem specific to the multiparty credit transaction. Reformation had to rest on sufficient findings concerning each affected participant, including Brio. Expanding the bank's liability would also alter Brio's reimbursement obligations. The governing statutory consent requirement for modifying an irrevocable credit and the cited documentary-credit customs reflected that interdependence and the need to predict liability. Without a finding that Fina, the bank, and Brio agreed to coverage through April 1982, the reformation order could not stand. A finding involving only beneficiary and issuer was insufficient. (541–542.)

## Strict compliance and exact disposition
Without reformation, the amended credit covered October through December 1981, not imbalances through April 1982. Fina's attempted draw reached the later period. Its documents also supplied provisional rather than commercial invoices, failed to evidence Giddings-type crude, and sought payment for oil delivered to an unlisted delivery point. These defects independently established failure to comply strictly with the credit's conditions. Fina was not entitled to payment under it. (542.)

The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals' take-nothing judgment despite correcting its election-of-remedies reasoning. It did not reinstate the $11 million trial damages, order a retrial, or authorize the proposed reformation. (538, 541–542.)
