# Kegerise v. Susquehanna Township School District

**Civil Procedure · CP-072 · 321 F.R.D. 121 (M.D. Pa. 2016), memorandum of February 3, 2016, No. 1:CV-14-0747, ECF 81.** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, PDF pp. 359–361, printed pp. 684–688; judicial notes 4–5 are relocated to PDF p. 375. Judge William W. Caldwell issued this district-court ruling.

## Why the former superintendent sought judgment from an answer

Dr. Susan Kegerise, formerly superintendent of Susquehanna Township School District, sued the district and school-board members Carol Karl, Jesse Rawls, Sr., and Mark Sussman. She mainly challenged her alleged discharge as violating federal and state law. In this particular memorandum, the court did not decide whether her employment rights had been violated. It examined the defendants' responses to her third amended complaint. (Official memorandum p. 1.)

Kegerise moved under Rule 12(c) for judgment on the pleadings on Counts II, III, IV, VIII, IX, and X. Her strategy was to identify insufficient responses under Rule 8(b), have the corresponding allegations deemed admitted, and use those admissions to establish entitlement to judgment. Defendants resisted, including by arguing that allegations stating legal conclusions required no response. Caldwell agreed that many responses were inadequate but chose a different consequence: permit an amended answer curing the deficiencies under Rule 15(a)(2), rather than grant judgment on the claimed admissions. (Pp. 1–2, 5.)

## The governing response rules

Rule 8(b) requires a party to admit or deny allegations asserted against it. A general denial is appropriate only if the party in good faith intends to deny everything. Otherwise, it must identify what it denies or deny all allegations except those specifically admitted. When only part of an allegation is disputed, the answer must admit the true part and deny the rest. A genuine lack of sufficient knowledge or information has the effect of a denial. Finally, when a responsive pleading is required, an allegation other than the amount of damages is admitted if not denied; when no responsive pleading is required, it is considered denied or avoided. These alternatives explain why an answer cannot simply leave material portions unanswered. (P. 2.)

## Three defects and the court's reasoning

First, partial responses must address the whole allegation. Paragraph 48 alleged a teacher's transfer, Rawls's intervention against the administration's decision, and his approach to a union president urging a grievance. The answer admitted the transfer and denied attempted intervention but did not respond to the allegation concerning the union president. A denial of one portion did not silently deny everything else. The court found similar deficiencies in other challenged responses and required each portion to be admitted or denied. It did not instruct defendants to deny all previously unanswered matter: any true portion still had to be admitted. (Pp. 3–4.)

The complete opinion supplies two qualifications omitted from the excerpt. Footnote 1 excluded paragraph 184 because it belonged to a previously dismissed count. Footnote 2 found paragraph 93 adequately answered despite its inclusion in Kegerise's list. The ruling therefore did not establish that every response she challenged under this theory was defective.

Second, an answer cannot evade a response by declaring an allegation a legal conclusion. Defendants invoked the portion of Rule 8(b)(6) treating allegations as denied or avoided when no responsive pleading is required. But an answer to this third amended complaint was required. That provision distinguishes pleadings requiring responses from those not requiring them; it did not exempt selected allegations within a required answer simply because they involved law. If anything, failing to deny such allegations would engage the admission rule. (Pp. 4–6.)

Caldwell accepted that most challenged allegations were conclusions applying law to facts. That characterization did not justify refusing to respond. He also extended the rule to allegations consisting purely of law, including an abstract statement of the elements of procedural due process. Rule 8(b)'s recognized responses remained admission, denial, or a proper statement of insufficient knowledge. This ruling addresses the adequacy of an answer; it does not hold that every legal proposition asserted by a plaintiff is substantively correct. (Pp. 5–7; nn.4–5.)

Again, the full memorandum is more qualified than a general summary. Footnote 3 noted that responses to paragraphs 102 and 199 supplied additional denial grounds. The court specifically found the extra ground for paragraph 102 adequate and excepted it from the required amended responses. It did not give paragraph 199 the same express exemption. (Pp. 5, 7; n.3.)

Third, professed lack of knowledge must satisfy honesty and reasonable inquiry. Defendants used that response for allegations that Rawls approached a union official, visited schools without advance notice, and prompted an investigation into purported nepotism. Rawls's personal knowledge should permit answers about his own actions. Information concerning the investigation could be obtained with reasonable effort from district records. A party cannot use Rule 8(b)(5) to avoid information it possesses or could reasonably obtain. The court allowed a response explaining vagueness if paragraph 30 was too vague; it did not require fabrication of knowledge. (Pp. 7–9.)

The memorandum reproduced paragraph 50 for context but expressly noted that Kegerise was not challenging that response. Thus the court's discussion was not a finding that the underlying nepotism accusation was false or that an investigation actually cleared her; those were quoted pleading allegations. (P. 8 n.6.)

## The precise disposition

**The court would issue an order requiring an amended answer complying with Rule 8(b). It did not deem the challenged allegations admitted and award Kegerise judgment on her requested counts.** This distinction is the central practical result: defective denials required correction, but the court allowed the parties to litigate the merits through properly framed pleadings. (Pp. 2, 9.)

Footnote 7 reserved another issue. Kegerise generally criticized responses saying a document speaks for itself but identified no particular response for adjudication on that ground. The court therefore declined to decide their adequacy, noting defendants' assertion that they also denied her characterization of documents. The broader discussion of pure legal assertions did not resolve that separate, insufficiently specified objection.
