# New York v. United States Nuclear Regulatory Commission
589 F.3d 551 (2d Cir. 2009) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and regulatory setting

Massachusetts in 2006 and California in 2007 petitioned the NRC to revise its treatment of spent nuclear fuel stored in pools. The NRC's 1996 Generic Environmental Impact Statement treated that storage as a Category I issue with a small environmental impact. Category I issues were common to nuclear plants, could receive a uniform significance classification, and did not require plant-specific kinds of mitigation. Category II issues required individual evaluation. A generic statement combined with the site-specific statement supplied the environmental analysis for a plant's license renewal, which NRC regulations treated as a major federal action requiring an EIS. (553.)

The states argued that newer information showed a greater fire risk and potentially devastating consequences, requiring a higher impact assessment and plant-by-plant consideration. New York and Connecticut supported the petitions. The NRC consolidated and denied them in August 2008. New York, Connecticut, and Massachusetts then sought appellate review, invoking the danger that accidents at nearby plants posed to their citizens. California's role as an original rulemaking petitioner should not be confused with membership in that three-state judicial petitioner group. (551–553.)

The court had jurisdiction over final NRC orders under 28 U.S.C. § 2342(4). Chief Judge Jacobs, Circuit Judge Kearse, and District Judge Gardephe, sitting by designation, issued a unanimous per curiam opinion on December 21, 2009. The case concerned denial of these particular rulemaking requests, not review of a particular plant's individual license renewal. (551–553.)

## Deferential but available review

The court expressly recognized that denial of a rulemaking petition was reviewable. Under Massachusetts v. EPA and circuit authorities, however, review was extremely limited and highly deferential, at the upper end of the ordinary arbitrary-and-capricious range. Reversal was reserved for rare, compelling circumstances, typically including plain legal errors about delegated authority. The comparison to nonreviewability described the demanding standard; it did not deprive the court of power to evaluate whether the decision was reasoned and considered relevant factors. (554.)

The states offered two principal objections: the studies showed a risk too significant to classify as small, and reliance on mitigation varying by plant could not sustain a generic Category I classification. The court considered both rather than dismissing them merely because the agency declined to commence rulemaking. (554–555.)

## Scientific evidence and generic mitigation

The NRC had studied fuel-pool risks over decades, including fire from terrorist attacks and post-September 2001 information. Most materials submitted with these petitions had already been analyzed; the states disagreed with the NRC's interpretation. The Commission also examined the one previously unconsidered study and found it less accurate than the studies it relied upon. Because these were technical scientific disputes within agency expertise, the court would not substitute its own appraisal where the agency's conclusion had a substantial factual basis. The studies supplied such a basis for the low-risk conclusion. (554–555.)

The mitigation objection also failed. Although the Commission relied in part on measures such as coolant sprays and replacement-water systems in case pools drained, it required those strategies at every operating plant through license amendments and safety evaluations. It likewise required heightened security. Thus the court did not understand the low-impact conclusion to depend on optional precautions adopted at only some plants. An agency could account for mitigation when judging environmental significance if substantial evidence supported its effectiveness; the NRC's studies met that requirement here. The court therefore accepted the generic treatment on this record. (555.)

The sole judicial note preserved an important issue. Circuits disagreed whether the NRC had to consider terrorism when preparing an EIS for license renewal. The court did not decide that legal question because the NRC had sufficiently considered terrorist-fire risks in this case. It neither declared terrorism categorically outside NEPA nor adopted the opposite circuit's general rule. (554 n. 1.)

## Judgment and assigned scope

The court **denied the states' petition for review**. It found the denial reasoned, supported by relevant studies and factors, and within the agency's broad discretion; it rejected the remaining arguments without elaborating new holdings. No separate writing or appendix accompanied the opinion. This did not judicially guarantee that fuel-pool fires were impossible or that every future rulemaking refusal would survive review. (553, 555.)
