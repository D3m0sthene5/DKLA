# Koch v. Consolidated Edison Company of New York, Inc.
62 N.Y.2d 548, 468 N.E.2d 1, 479 N.Y.S.2d 163 (1984) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

The New York Court of Appeals decided the case on June 14, 1984. Judge Jones wrote for all seven judges; Chief Judge Cooke and Judges Jasen, Wachtler, Meyer, Simons, and Kaye concurred. The appeal concerned competing motions for partial summary judgment following the 1977 New York City blackout. The court upheld issue preclusion against Con Edison and the plaintiffs’ status as intended third-party beneficiaries, but modified the order to dismiss claims for additional governmental operating expenditures and lost revenues or similar economic damage. It affirmed the order as modified, without costs, dismissed the plaintiffs’ cross appeal without costs, and answered the certified question in the negative. This was a ruling defining liability issues and recoverable categories, not a final damages assessment. (548, 553–554, 562.)

## Facts and claims

Electrical service failed at about 9:36 p.m. on July 13, 1977, throughout New York City except an area of Queens supplied by another utility. Complete restoration did not occur until about 10:40 p.m. the following day. The city and fourteen public benefit corporations sued on September 7, 1978, alleging damage from Con Edison’s gross negligence and reckless and willful conduct. Their demands included physical injuries and property losses, looting and vandalism, additional police, fire, sanitation, and hospital personnel costs, lost public revenues, and employee absenteeism or lost productivity. These allegations and requested damages must be distinguished from the findings actually established on the motions. (553–554, 560–561.)

The plaintiffs obtained their electricity from the Power Authority of the State of New York, or PASNY, rather than buying it directly from Con Edison. Legislation enacted in 1974 authorized emergency measures to preserve reliable metropolitan electric service. PASNY acquired Con Edison’s partially completed Astoria 6 and Indian Point 3 generating units in December 1974 and December 1975. The acquisitions contemplated continued transmission and delivery through Con Edison’s facilities. A service agreement expressly provided that Con Edison would assist PASNY in serving the designated Astoria–Indian Point customers, deliver the same quality of service supplied to its own customers under its regular tariffs, and operate and maintain the facilities necessary to deliver their power according to good utility practice. A simultaneous power-sale contract required sufficient energy to meet those customers’ requirements. The plaintiffs belonged to that designated customer group. (558–559.)

## Procedural history and arguments

In separate litigation, Food Pageant obtained a jury verdict finding that Con Edison’s gross negligence caused the blackout and awarding $40,500 for a grocery chain’s spoiled food and lost business. That judgment was upheld on appeal. The present plaintiffs sought partial summary judgment treating the gross-negligence determination as conclusive. Con Edison sought partial summary judgment denying third-party-beneficiary status, excluding losses caused by criminal activity or other asserted intervening causes, and excluding governmental expenditures. Special Term granted the plaintiffs’ motion and denied the relevant portions of Con Edison’s motion; the Appellate Division affirmed without opinion and granted leave to appeal. (553–554 & n. 1.)

Con Edison opposed preclusion by pointing to other blackout decisions, investigative reports including the Clapp Report, the absence of mutuality, possible jury compromise, the small Food Pageant verdict relative to more than $200 million in aggregate blackout claims, comparative negligence, and due process. It also argued that PASNY customers were outside its contractual obligations and that the requested damages exceeded legally recoverable losses. The plaintiffs relied on the earlier gross-negligence finding, the express service obligations for PASNY’s customers, and factual issues concerning the claimed harm. (554–561.)

## Issue preclusion

The court applied third-party issue preclusion: an issue actually litigated, necessarily determined, and established by a valid final judgment may bind the losing party in litigation with a different opponent, subject to a full and fair opportunity to litigate and circumstances justifying renewed litigation. Notes 2 through 4 distinguish ordinary same-party preclusion from the broader exceptions considered when a new opponent invokes the judgment. Con Edison bore the burden of showing a reason to relitigate and supplied insufficient admissible evidence to require a factual trial or disturb the lower courts’ judgment. (554–555 & nn. 2–4.)

The inconsistent decisions were small-claims judgments, whose simplified procedure and governing statutes expressly prevented treating their factual determinations as adjudications in other actions. The reports had already been available, offered, and excluded in Food Pageant; Con Edison did not establish a new basis for their admissibility. Restoring mutuality would undo an established rule without sufficient justification. The jury-compromise argument remained speculative. Despite the relatively small verdict, Con Edison had the same forum, procedures, and burden of persuasion and had defended vigorously with express awareness of possible future preclusion. No claim that the present plaintiffs caused the blackout created a comparative-negligence issue, although mitigation might affect damages. A full and fair prior opportunity also defeated the asserted due-process objection. Thus gross negligence causing the blackout was established, while the plaintiffs’ particular losses and recoverable damages still required appropriate proof. (555–558 & nn. 5–7.)

## Intended beneficiaries and the government-contract distinction

The contracts and legislation identified the plaintiffs as the very consumers they were designed to serve. Direct purchase from Con Edison was unnecessary: the promise ran to PASNY, but its explicit performance concerned the designated end users. They therefore qualified as intended third-party beneficiaries. The court contrasted Moch’s public fire-hydrant arrangement and Kornblut’s Thruway repair-service agreement with an express undertaking to supply these customers directly. The decisive distinction was the service obligation’s stated beneficiaries and purpose, rather than the mere existence of a public contract or a general benefit to society. (558–559.)

Koch’s retrospective description of Kornblut should be read with the earlier opinions themselves: Hopkins’s adopted opinion discussed intended benefits under particular service clauses but denied these consequential personal-injury damages, whereas Martuscello separately would have denied an enforceable promise to the motorist. Koch’s own holding concerns the expressly designated PASNY electricity customers; it does not eliminate that distinction between beneficiary status and the scope of an available remedy. (559; separately briefed Kornblut stages.)

## Damages, remaining proof, and disposition

The action sounded in contract and tort. Partial summary judgment could eliminate distinct damage categories without resolving every claim. Physical injury directly resulting from the interruption could be compensated upon supporting proof. Looting and vandalism were not automatically superseding causes: sufficient facts required a trial on whether rioters’ intervention was contemplated by the parties or reasonably foreseeable to Con Edison. If proved, a properly instructed jury could include those losses. The court did not conclusively find every looting loss foreseeable or award such damages. (559–560.)

Additional public-service costs were barred by the general public-policy rule against recovery of expenditures made in performing governmental functions. The closer contractual relationship did not create an exception. The court identified statutory reimbursement rights in other settings, but no comparable statute was offered for this blackout. Lost sales and transaction taxes, transit fares, tolls, off-track-betting receipts, and productivity losses were also excluded: the tendered proof depended speculatively on collateral transactions and public policy opposed shifting losses from general economic disruption to the utility. Notes 8 and 9 distinguish nongovernmental claims; the opinion does not impose the same prohibition on all private lost-profit claims. (560–562 & nn. 8–9.)

The order was accordingly modified to dismiss the added-governmental-services and lost-revenue or similar economic claims and otherwise affirmed. The plaintiffs’ cross appeal failed because they had taken no appeal to the Appellate Division. The gross-negligence determination and beneficiary status survived, together with physical-damage claims subject to proof and the unresolved looting foreseeability question. (562 & n. 10.)
