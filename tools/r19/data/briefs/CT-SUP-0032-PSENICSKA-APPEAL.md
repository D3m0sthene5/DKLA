# Psenicska v. Twentieth Century Fox Film Corp. — appeal
409 F. App'x 368 (2d Cir. Nov. 17, 2009) | Contracts

## An appeal from three related dismissals
People who appeared in Borat sued the filmmakers for claims including fraud, unjust enrichment and infliction of emotional distress. They alleged that the production had been represented as a documentary about a foreign visitor's experiences in the United States, intended for Belarus television. Psenicska's particular account described a documentary about the integration of foreign people into American life. The plaintiffs had signed Standard Consent Agreements expressly disclaiming reliance on statements about the film's nature or the identities of its participants and creators. The district court dismissed their complaints under Rule 12(b)(6). The appellate panel affirmed the September 24, 2008 judgment dismissing all three actions. 409 F. App'x at 369–370 & n.1.

This is a separate judicial decision from Judge Preska's September 3 memorandum and order. The Second Circuit's summary disposition was issued by Judges Roger J. Miner, John M. Walker Jr. and Reena Raggi, with no separate writing. It reviewed the dismissals de novo, accepting the allegations as true and drawing reasonable inferences for the plaintiffs. It did not conduct a trial or determine that the alleged misrepresentations never occurred. Id. at 369–370.

The underlying participation arrangements involved a driving lesson, an etiquette dinner and etiquette instruction. The district court's separate brief supplies the detailed chronology and differences between the releases. This appellate decision assumes familiarity with that record and focuses on contract ambiguity and the plaintiffs' asserted reliance. The form's Paragraph 5 specifically addressed statements about the film and the people involved, while release provisions waived claims arising from participation. The plaintiffs needed an effective challenge to those writings to revive their suits. Id. at 370–371.

## Why documentary-style was sufficient
The plaintiffs argued that an agreement limited to a documentary-style film did not unambiguously authorize use of their images in Borat. Contract ambiguity was a legal question subject to de novo construction. The panel agreed that no reasonable trier of fact could find that Borat fell outside the stated style. Id. at 370–371.

Borat's central character was fictional, but the film unmistakably told his story in the style of a traditional factual documentary. The resemblance was itself one of the film's comedic devices: real interviews and events made the fictional visitor's behavior more conspicuous. The plaintiffs' objection treated fiction as incompatible with documentary style. The panel instead distinguished the story's truth from the production's manner of presentation. A fictional journey can display documentary characteristics without becoming a factual account of an actual visitor. Id. at 371.

Accordingly, uncertainty about the genre's outer boundaries did not make this application uncertain. Whatever harder productions might exist, Borat fell comfortably within the contractual description. The panel rejected ambiguity as a reason to withhold enforcement of the releases at the pleading stage. Id.

## Specific disclaimers and the peculiar-knowledge exception
Reasonable reliance is an essential element of fraudulent inducement under New York law. Under Danann and related authority, a party that expressly disclaims reliance on representations concerning the very subject of the alleged fraud ordinarily cannot base an inducement claim on those representations. The panel applied that principle to Paragraph 5's targeted reference to the film's nature and participants. Id. at 371.

The plaintiffs invoked an important exception: a specific disclaimer may not defeat reliance when the relevant facts lie peculiarly within the alleged wrongdoer's knowledge. The panel explained why the exception did not apply here. It protects a party facing high costs in determining whether an oral representation is true; it does not ordinarily protect someone who could insist that a deal-breaking assurance be included in the written bargain. Id. at 371–372.

The plaintiffs apparently took no steps to confirm the representations, including inexpensive steps such as requesting the reporter's name or asking to meet him. More fundamentally, they could not claim collateral harm from walking away if the filmmakers refused written terms that satisfied their conditions for participation. They therefore had an available opportunity to make the asserted condition part of the transaction or decline the offer. The exception did not permit them to sign a writing expressly disclaiming the relevant representations and then enforce those oral representations through a fraud claim. Id. at 371–372.

This reasoning should not be enlarged into a finding that the filmmakers acted honestly. The panel expressly declined to credit the alleged conduct if true. Its conclusion was contractual and doctrinal: these allegations did not fit the exception to the rule holding parties to written releases. Nor did it eliminate the peculiar-knowledge exception for genuinely costly or inaccessible information. It found the plaintiffs' circumstances insufficient to invoke it. Id.

## Concealment required the same missing reliance
The plaintiffs also argued that the defendants owed a duty to disclose more information. But reasonable reliance was necessary for fraudulent concealment or a disclosure-based theory too. Nothing in the agreements affirmatively represented that this was a documentary about a foreign traveler for Belarus television. That understanding could come only from the alleged oral statements, reliance on which the plaintiffs specifically disclaimed. Calling the same theory an omission claim did not avoid the defect. Id. at 372.

The panel thus endorsed the district court's conclusion that the plaintiffs were restyling allegations of misrepresentation as allegations of omission. The short and specific disclaimer remained controlling. This was not a holding that every omission is interchangeable with an affirmative lie; it was a conclusion about the same alleged misunderstanding, the absence of a supporting written assurance and the particular nonreliance provision. Id.

## Judgment, significance and checked sources
After considering the remaining arguments and finding them meritless, the panel affirmed the judgment dismissing all three complaints. It ordered no damages, new factual hearing or renegotiation. The result left the releases enforceable against these claims. The decision illustrates how a subject-specific nonreliance clause can defeat a fraud-based challenge to a release, and why the ability to demand written assurances matters when applying the peculiar-knowledge exception. Id. at 372.
