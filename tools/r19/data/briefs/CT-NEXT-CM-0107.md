# Northwest, Inc. v. Ginsberg
572 U.S. 273 (2014) | Contracts

## Loyalty program and pleaded termination theory

Ginsberg joined Northwest’s WorldPerks frequent-flyer program in 1999 and reached its highest, Platinum Elite, status in 2005 through extensive travel. Members earned miles on Northwest and partner flights and redeemed them for tickets and upgrades. In 2008, Northwest terminated his membership, apparently invoking a clause allowing cancellation for program abuse, including improper conduct determined in its sole judgment. Its letter reported twenty-four complaints since December 2007, including nine about baggage reaching the carousel late, and listed $1,925 in travel vouchers, 78,500 bonus miles, a voucher extension, and $491 in reimbursements. The company said it would stop granting compensation with every contact. 572 U.S. at 277–278.

Ginsberg alleged that termination instead served cost cutting connected to Northwest’s merger with Delta. His proposed class action asserted breach of contract, breach of the implied covenant of good faith, and negligent and intentional misrepresentation. He sought more than $5 million and restoration of membership. The claimed ulterior motive was an allegation, not a judicial finding about the airline’s actual intent. Minnesota law controlled under an unchallenged choice-of-law determination. Id. at 278 & n.1.

## The issue that reached the Supreme Court

The district court dismissed the covenant and misrepresentation claims as preempted by the Airline Deregulation Act. It dismissed the express-contract claim without prejudice for failure to identify a material breach, reasoning that the agreement reserved sole discretion over abuse. Ginsberg appealed only the implied-covenant dismissal. The Ninth Circuit reinstated that claim as insufficiently connected to rates or services to trigger preemption. Thus the Supreme Court reviewed the covenant theory, rather than adjudicating the merits of the abandoned express-contract claim. Id. at 279.

## Preemption framework

The ADA sought to allow competitive markets to set airline prices, routes, and services and expressly barred state enactment or enforcement of provisions having the force of law related to them. Morales gave “related to” a broad connection-or-reference meaning. Wolens distinguished state consumer regulation from enforcing the airline’s own voluntarily accepted promises: privately ordered contractual obligations could be enforced without imposing additional state standards. That distinction, rather than the mere contractual label on a pleading, controlled this case. Id. at 279–281.

Common-law rules qualified as provisions having the force of law. The statutory language, its earlier references to rules and standards, the nonsubstantive 1994 recodification, and the deregulatory purpose all rejected the notion that only statutes and administrative regulations could be preempted. A differently worded boat-safety statute considered in Sprietsma did not compel another answer. Nor could the older general aviation saving clause override the later specific preemption rule. Id. at 281–284.

This covenant claim also related to airline prices and services. Reinstatement would restore miles redeemable for cheaper tickets and access to flights and upgrades. Describing the dispute as termination rather than access to those benefits did not remove the connection. The Court left open whether changes in programs that earned or redeemed miles outside aviation might matter in another case; Ginsberg alleged only flight-earned miles and ticket or upgrade redemptions. Id. at 284–285.

## Why Minnesota’s covenant added state obligations

The Court rejected both categorical positions that all good-faith claims must be preempted and that all must survive. Different states used the covenant differently: some implemented implicit contractual intentions, while others imposed community standards independent of assent. Minnesota did not permit parties to disclaim its covenant, making it a state-imposed obligation. Its general application except to employment contracts for policy reasons independently showed that state policy, rather than only the parties’ understanding, determined its reach. Footnote 2 surveys jurisdictions allowing and forbidding contractual avoidance; that survey explains the Court’s 2014 reasoning and is not a current fifty-state certification. Id. at 285–288 & n.2.

Northwest argued that dependence on state law would produce a regulatory patchwork. The Court answered that airlines could expressly contract around the covenant where the relevant state allowed that step, weighing transaction costs and customer appeal. It also identified market reputation and federal DOT enforcement as protections for passengers. Most importantly for contract doctrine, an express-contract claim might have tested whether discretion to determine abuse actually authorized termination for an ulterior cost-cutting purpose. Ginsberg chose not to pursue that dismissal, so the Court did not decide whether the agreement permitted such conduct. Id. at 288–289 & n.3.

## Judgment and source scope

Justice Alito wrote for a unanimous Court on April 2, 2014. Because Ginsberg’s Minnesota implied-covenant claim enlarged the airline’s voluntarily adopted obligations, the ADA preempted it. The Court reversed the Ninth Circuit and remanded for proceedings consistent with that holding. It did not announce that every airline contract or every implied-covenant claim was unenforceable. Id. at 289–290.
