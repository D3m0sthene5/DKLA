Citation
Richardson v. Perales, 402 U.S. 389 (1971). Supreme Court of the United States, decided May 3, 1971. Justice
Blackmun delivered the Court's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justices Harlan, Stewart, White,
and Marshall. Justice Douglas dissented, joined by Justices Black and Brennan. Full opinion: Full judicial
opinion . Assigned casebook: printed pages 401–410, October second scan, PDF pages 8–17.
Parties
Pedro Perales sought Social Security disability insurance benefits for an asserted disabling back condition. The
Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare defended the administrative denial and the use of examining
physicians' written reports. The physicians whose reports opposed the claim were different from Dr. Leavitt,
the nonexamining medical adviser who testified at the hearing, and from Dr. Morales, the treating general
practitioner who supported Perales's claim. Keeping these roles distinct is essential to understanding both the
evidence and the competing opinions. 402 U.S. at 390–398.
Material Facts
Perales, a San Antonio truck driver, claimed disability beginning with a September 1965 lifting injury at work.
His neurosurgeon, Dr. Munslow, initially tried conservative treatment and then operated in November 1965.
Surgery revealed no protruded disc or other definitive pathology. Perales continued to report pain; Munslow
and a consulting neurologist could not find an objective neurological explanation. Munslow advised a return to
work. These were medical observations and opinions in the record, not an independent Supreme Court
diagnosis. 402 U.S. at 390–392 & nn.2–3.
Dr. Morales subsequently treated and hospitalized Perales. Morales concluded that the injury was genuine,
permanent, and totally disabling, and stated that Perales was not malingering. Other specialists reached
materially different conclusions. The state agency obtained orthopedic, psychiatric, and electromyographic
examinations. Their reports described limited objective findings, suggested poor effort or functional
components in the reported weakness, and did not support disability of the extent Morales described. The
majority stressed their independent examinations, medical detail, and consistency. Id. at 392–395, 402–404.
After the claim and reconsideration were denied, Perales received notice of a hearing. He and his attorney
could inspect the documentary file beforehand, submit additional evidence, and bring medical and other
witnesses. Hearings occurred on January 12 and March 31, 1967. Counsel objected to the adverse reports on
hearsay, cross-examination, and other grounds. The examiner admitted them, as well as the reports Perales
offered. Perales, Morales, a former coworker, a vocational expert, and Dr. Leavitt testified. Id. at 395–396.
Leavitt was an independent medical adviser called by the examiner. He had not examined Perales, but
reviewed the medical materials, explained technical matters, and gave an opinion consistent with the reports
that the impairment was mild. Counsel cross-examined Leavitt. The statute and regulations allowed requests
for subpoenas; although represented by counsel and afforded a supplemental hearing, Perales never requested
subpoenas for the reporting physicians. The distinction between actual cross-examination of Leavitt and the
unused means of obtaining the other doctors' attendance materially limits the holding. Id. at 396–397, 404–405,
408.

The examiner found a mild low-back impairment, an emotional component insufficient to produce statutory
disability, and capacity for certain less strenuous work. The Appeals Council sustained the denial after
receiving additional materials, including a workers' compensation judgment and another medical report.
Neither the existence of some impairment nor that compensation judgment automatically established disability
under the federal statute's substantial-gainful-activity standard. Id. at 396–398.
Procedural History
Perales sought judicial review under Social Security Act § 205(g), which made supported administrative
factual findings conclusive. The District Court rejected the evidentiary foundation of the denial and remanded
for a hearing before a different examiner. It was troubled by unsworn reports and by a nonexamining adviser's
reliance on them. 402 U.S. at 397–398.
The Fifth Circuit agreed that the reports and Leavitt's testimony were admissible and that Perales had not used
the available subpoena procedure. Nevertheless, it concluded that the objected-to reports could not constitute
substantial evidence when directly contradicted by the claimant's and his physician's live testimony. On
rehearing, it clarified that it was not adopting a universal rule against all uncorroborated hearsay, but a rule for
this configuration of conflicting evidence. Id. at 398–399.
The Supreme Court granted review and rejected that categorical exclusion from the substantial-evidence
calculation. It reversed and remanded without deciding whether the administrative denial was supported by the
complete admissible record. That ultimate review remained for the District Court. Id. at 399, 410.
Issue
May reports by licensed physicians who personally examined a disability claimant constitute substantial
evidence supporting an adverse finding, despite their hearsay form, the absence of cross-examination of their
authors, and contrary live testimony, when the represented claimant did not request available subpoenas?
Related questions concerned the fairness of using a nonexamining medical adviser, the examiner's combined
evidence-development and decisionmaking responsibilities, and whether the procedure conflicted with the
APA's cross-examination provisions. 402 U.S. at 390, 399–402, 408–410.
Rule and Holding
An examining physician's report, stating medical findings within the physician's competence, may be received
and may constitute substantial evidence despite those objections when the claimant has not exercised the
available right to request the physician's attendance for cross-examination. The Court relied on the reports'
demonstrated reliability and probative value together with the procedural opportunity to test them. It did not
equate all admissible hearsay with substantial evidence or hold that every such report necessarily outweighs
live testimony. 402 U.S. at 401–406.
Substantial evidence means more than a scintilla: evidence a reasonable mind could accept as adequate to
support the conclusion. The Social Security Act permits receipt of evidence inadmissible under courtroom
rules, while preserving fundamentally fair procedures. The challenged use of the medical adviser and examiner
did not violate that requirement on this record. The Court found the relevant Social Security procedures
consistent with APA § 556(d), and expressly declined to decide the APA's general applicability to disability
claims. Id. at 400–402, 408–410.

Judgment
The Court reversed and remanded for further proceedings. It expressly intimated no view on the merits of
Perales's entitlement. The District Court was to determine whether the Secretary's findings were supported by
substantial evidence after considering all material proffered and admissible evidence. The Court did not itself
award benefits, make a final finding of nondisability, or require a new hearing before another examiner.
Douglas, Black, and Brennan would have affirmed the judgment under review. 402 U.S. at 410, 414.
Reasoning
1. Separate admissibility from sufficiency. Congress gave the Secretary procedural authority and expressly
permitted evidence that ordinary judicial evidentiary rules would exclude. That established the reports'
potential admissibility, but § 205(g)'s substantial-evidence standard still governed whether the resulting
findings could stand. The Court rejected the Fifth Circuit's rule that contradiction by live witnesses
automatically disqualified these reports from supplying substantial evidence. It left the actual whole-record
sufficiency determination to the reviewing court. 402 U.S. at 398–402, 410.
2. Informality served the statutory design, subject to fairness. Disability proceedings process claims on a large
scale and were meant to be accessible to lay claimants. The statute and regulations assigned the examiner
responsibility to inquire fully and receive relevant, material evidence, without importing all the formalities of
courtroom litigation. The Court considered that informality legitimate only within a fundamentally fair hearing.
Administrative volume supported the design; it did not replace the examination of this claimant's opportunities
and this evidence's reliability. Id. at 399–401, 406, 410.
3. These reports had concrete indicia of reliability. The five reporting physicians had actually examined
Perales, used ordinary professional methods, and described observations, tests, diagnoses, and
recommendations within their specialties. The neurosurgeon reported what surgery showed; other specialists
supplied neurological, orthopedic, psychiatric, and electromyographic findings. The Court found the reports
mutually consistent. Their independent sources and medical detail gave them rational probative force beyond
unsupported rumor. The fact that their conclusions were adverse did not itself establish bias. Id. at 402–404.
4. Payment and agency involvement did not establish adversarial unreliability on this record. Some physicians
were retained through the state agency and paid for their work. The majority treated those payments as
compensation for professional services and declined to infer an interest in defeating the claim. It described the
statutory agency process as adjudicative rather than adversarial. This assessment is the majority's reasoning
about the record, not proof that government-selected experts can never be biased. Douglas sharply disputed its
confidence in the arrangement. Id. at 402–404; id. at 413–414 (Douglas, J., dissenting).
5. The unused subpoena procedure was central. The reports were available for inspection, their authors were
identified, and the regulations permitted the claimant to seek subpoenas. Perales had counsel and two hearing
opportunities separated by more than two months. Because he made no request, the Court rejected his claim
that the process had denied him confrontation and cross-examination. The decision therefore does not resolve a
case in which a claimant timely requests a materially necessary subpoena and the agency refuses it. Id. at 397,
404–405, 410.
6. Contrary live testimony did not create an automatic priority rule. The claimant and Morales sincerely
asserted serious disability, while other examinations supplied different medical conclusions. Resolving such a
conflict ordinarily belonged to the administrative factfinder. The Court interpreted Consolidated Edison's
warning about uncorroborated hearsay in light of its concern with evidence lacking rational probative force. It
declined to turn that warning into a categorical bar against reliable medical reports. Id. at 399, 407–408.

7. The cited welfare-termination decision involved different procedural circumstances. In discussing Goldberg,
the majority emphasized that Perales sought an initial disability award, had notice, could inspect the reports,
and could request the doctors' attendance. It characterized this record as a professional disagreement over
medical conclusions rather than a credibility contest requiring resolution through unprovided confrontation.
Those distinctions supported the holding; a general proposition that all initial benefit applications fall outside
due process would contradict the Court's own acceptance that procedural due process applied here. Id. at
401–402, 406–407.
8. The medical adviser did not purport to replace the examining doctors. Leavitt helped the lay examiner
understand technical evidence, particularly electromyography. He did not vouch from personal knowledge for
the underlying facts, and his opinion was consistent with the reports. His lack of a personal examination did not
render his explanatory role unconstitutional or improper. The majority likewise rejected the assertion that
developing the record necessarily made the examiner an advocate. It evaluated the functions and available
safeguards, rather than presuming disqualification from the combination alone. Id. at 408–410.
9. The APA question could be resolved without deciding wholesale applicability. Section 556(d) allows
documentary evidence and requires cross-examination when necessary for full and true disclosure; it also
contemplates written procedures in benefits cases under specified conditions. The Court found the procedure
used here consistent with those requirements because material reports remained subject to subpoena and
cross-examination. It therefore reserved whether the APA generally governed all such proceedings. Id. at
409–410.
Separate Opinions
Justice Douglas dissented, joined by Justices Black and Brennan. He agreed that documentary hearsay could be
admitted but denied that these untested reports could, by themselves, supply substantial evidence against the
live testimony of the claimant and his examining physician. His objection accordingly went beyond an ordinary
courtroom hearsay rule: he read the substantial-evidence and fair-hearing provisions to require testing of the
adverse medical material in this situation. 402 U.S. at 411–413 (Douglas, J., dissenting).
Douglas regarded cross-examination as essential to disclose the strengths and weaknesses of competing
medical opinions. He objected especially to reliance on a medical adviser who never examined the claimant
and to the majority's treatment of retained experts as reliably neutral. In his account, the administrative
arrangement risked giving the Government the benefit of adverse opinions without requiring the authors to
defend them. He invoked the APA's legislative history recognizing that necessary cross-examination can
extend to written evidence. Id. at 411–414 & nn.1–2.
The dissent would have affirmed the judgment requiring further agency proceedings. It did not adopt the
majority's view that the unused subpoena procedure and the reports' consistency sufficiently answered the
fairness objection. Its criticisms of agency-selected doctors and the adviser are separately attributed here
because the majority expressly rejected that characterization. No other separate opinion is reported. Id. at
408–410, 413–414.
Notes
Three distinctions prevent overreading. First, admission of hearsay, its capacity to supply substantial evidence,
and the sufficiency of the complete record are different questions. Second, the five examining report authors
were different from the nonexamining adviser who appeared and was cross-examined. Third, the Court's
reliance on a requestable subpoena procedure does not establish an unconditional entitlement to every
subpoena a claimant might seek. 402 U.S. at 395–405, 408–410.
