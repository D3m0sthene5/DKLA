# SVAP III Poway Crossings, LLC v. Fitness International, LLC
87 Cal. App. 5th 882, 303 Cal. Rptr. 3d 863 (2023) | Contracts

## Appeal, undisputed record, and result
California's Fourth District Court of Appeal, Division One, filed its published opinion January 20, 2023. Justice Buchanan wrote, with Acting Presiding Justice Huffman and Justice O'Rourke concurring. The court affirmed judgment for the commercial landlord on its rent claim and the tenant's cross-complaint, awarding SVAP costs on appeal. Fitness asserted no disputed material facts; the appellate issues were legal and received de novo review. The undisputed unpaid rent was $520,361.29 as of October 2021. (Slip pp. 1, 6, 8–9, 20.)

Fitness's January 2022 notice attached only the summary-judgment minute order. After the appellate court questioned appealability and offered to construe the notice as covering a final judgment, the trial court entered a final judgment April 13, 2022. Note 2 explains why the court did not separately adjudicate Fitness's own denied summary-judgment motion: its briefing did not distinguish the motions or explain entitlement to its own judgment. (Slip pp. 5 & n. 2, 8.)

## Lease, closures, claims, and defenses
The parties' June 12, 2002 retail lease initially ran fifteen years with three five-year renewal options; amendments extended the initial term through October 31, 2025. Fitness occupied space in the Poway Shopping Center for its health club. Pandemic orders made operation of gyms temporarily illegal, and it could not operate during intermittent periods from March 2020 through March 2021. An executive eviction restriction expressly preserved rent obligations and the landlord's right to recover rent. (Slip pp. 2–3.)

SVAP sued in May 2020 after April and May rent went unpaid. It alleged continuing possession and performance of its landlord obligations. Fitness raised thirty-seven defenses and cross-claimed for contract breach, breach of the good-faith covenant, declarations, specific performance, and promissory estoppel. It argued that health-club operation was the essential exchange and that closure excused rent or required abatement. SVAP sought summary judgment on its claim and the cross-complaint. The trial court found possession provided, intermittent rent withheld, and no applicable excuse. (Slip pp. 3–8.)

## The landlord promised possession, not immunity from government regulation
Fitness asserted that SVAP breached a guarantee of continuous health-club use, which excused Fitness's performance. Reading the entire lease, the court rejected that guarantee. The Initial Uses provision described a health club, but the opening obligation required that use for only one day. The landlord's legality warranty was explicitly measured as of the June 12, 2002 effective date. Treating another provision as guaranteeing legal operation throughout the term would render that dated warranty meaningless. (Slip pp. 9–11.)

Fitness also had a right to use the premises for permitted purposes throughout the term, including changing to another lawful retail use within specified restrictions. That language bound the landlord not to restrict permitted uses; it did not insure against later governmental prohibitions. Fitness admitted continued possession and identified government interference, not restrictions imposed by SVAP. The landlord therefore had not materially breached. Because the asserted guarantee did not exist, the court did not reach the claim that it was a condition precedent. Note 4 states that Fitness had not explicitly challenged summary judgment on its contract cross-claim, and that the same merits analysis would defeat that claim in any event. (Slip pp. 9 & n. 3, 10–12 & n. 4.)

## Force majeure, impossibility, and statutory excuses
The force majeure clause covered restrictive laws and similar or dissimilar causes beyond reasonable control, but excluded financial inability and failures curable by paying money. The closure orders were restrictive laws, yet they did not delay or prevent the performance Fitness owed: paying rent. Fitness offered no evidence or argument that the orders hindered payment, and the clause's financial exclusions would independently defeat that rationale. SVAP continued performing its possession obligation. (Slip pp. 12–13.)

Impossibility or impracticability also focused on Fitness's own promised performance. Laws can make performance illegal, but these laws prohibited operating a gym rather than paying rent. Fitness's argument that the contractual object had become impossible confused that defense with frustration. Nothing in this record made rent payment impossible or impracticable. (Slip pp. 13–14.)

Civil Code § 1511(1) excuses performance prevented or delayed by law; it failed because payment remained lawful. Section 1511(2) addresses an irresistible, superhuman cause unless parties expressly agree otherwise. Again there was no prevention of payment, and the force majeure clause expressly preserved failures curable by money. The opinion thus relied on both the actual obligation and contractual allocation, rather than simply denying that a pandemic could qualify as an extraordinary event. (Slip pp. 15–16.)

## Frustration, lease value, and the proposed temporary defense
Frustration required a mutually contemplated purpose, an unallocated and not reasonably foreseeable risk, and total or nearly total destruction of counter-performance's value. Increased difficulty or unprofitability alone was insufficient. For this defense, the court assumed without deciding that operating a fitness facility was the lease's primary purpose and that government-closure risk had not been allocated exclusively to Fitness. It did not adopt the landlord's competing arguments on those first two elements. (Slip pp. 16–17.)

Even under those assumptions, temporary closure for months did not destroy the value of a lease already used for more than nineteen years and spanning more than twenty-three years overall. Possession remained valuable. Note 5 further questioned a sole gym purpose because the contract required gym operation for only one day and allowed other uses, but that doubt was not necessary to the court's value-based holding. (Slip pp. 17–18 & n. 5.)

The court additionally rejected the proposed temporary frustration defense under the California cases it applied. Commercial frustration terminated a contract rather than suspending it for a temporary regulatory interval. Fitness remained in possession, did not seek rescission, and continued to receive the premises. Its temporary-impossibility precedent did not govern: California treated impossibility and frustration as distinct doctrines. This California analysis should not be silently replaced by another jurisdiction's treatment of temporary frustration under Restatement § 269. (Slip pp. 18–19.)

The court denied judicial notice of other Fitness cases as irrelevant or unnecessary and affirmed, awarding SVAP appellate costs. (Slip pp. 19 & n. 6, 20.)
