# Advanced Tactical Ordnance Systems, LLC v. Real Action Paintball, Inc.

**751 F.3d 796 (7th Cir. 2014), decided May 9, 2014; corrected May 12, 2014.** Civil Procedure — specific personal jurisdiction, claim-related contacts, and Internet communications. Chief Judge Wood wrote for Chief Judge Wood and Judges Posner and Kanne; no separate opinion was filed. Assigned: CivPro PDF page 127, printed page 204, Note 5. Pinpoints use the thirteen-page May 9 slip opinion, together with the one-page correction order.

## The commercial dispute

Advanced Tactical manufactured PepperBall products: projectiles filled with an irritant resembling pepper spray, sold primarily to police departments, private security firms, and similar customers. It acquired the PepperBall trademarks and other property in a 2012 foreclosure sale from California-based PepperBall Technologies. Advanced Tactical alleged that its own headquarters was in Indiana, although the court noted uncertainty about that assertion and an apparent California office. This was not an appellate factual finding that every aspect of its business was located in Indiana. Slip pp. 1–2.

Before foreclosure, PepperBall Technologies had obtained projectiles from Perfect Circle, a half owner of Advanced Tactical, and APON, a Mexican company. After the acquisition, APON stopped manufacturing or assembling PepperBall projectiles. APON's California-resident chief operating officer approached Real Action Paintball, a California company, about supplying it with irritant projectiles. They reached a deal in August 2012. Real Action then posted a website notice and emailed its subscribers announcing that it had acquired the machinery, recipes, and materials formerly used by PepperBall Technologies. Advanced Tactical objected that this announcement misleadingly suggested Real Action was now the sole maker of PepperBall projectiles. Slip pp. 2–3.

After receiving a cease-and-desist letter, Real Action added a disclaimer denying affiliation with PepperBall Technologies or its brands and stating that its projectiles were not made by the current company operating under that name. Advanced Tactical remained dissatisfied and sued Real Action and its president, K.T. Tran, in the Northern District of Indiana. The theories included federal trademark infringement, common-law infringement and unfair competition, trade-dress infringement, and misappropriation of trade secrets. For this appeal, the court treated the company and Tran together because there was no material difference between them. That convention did not establish trademark liability. Slip pp. 2–3.

## Preliminary injunction and the threshold jurisdictional question

Advanced Tactical alleged several Indiana connections: an interactive website accepting orders, tortious conduct outside Indiana with foreseeable harm inside it, sales generating Indiana revenue, and an alleged conspiracy to harm an Indiana business. It emphasized email blasts reaching recipients in Indiana and at least one Indiana sale. Real Action preserved its objection to personal jurisdiction. Following a December 7, 2012 evidentiary hearing, the district court found jurisdiction and granted a preliminary injunction. Real Action appealed both the injunction and the jurisdictional ruling under § 1292(a)(1). Slip pp. 3–4.

Because the district court held an evidentiary hearing, Advanced Tactical had to establish personal jurisdiction by a preponderance of the evidence. This was not merely the lower prima facie showing that may suffice when a court decides jurisdiction on written submissions without a hearing. Slip pp. 4–5.

Advanced Tactical argued that personal jurisdiction was outside the interlocutory appeal; Real Action invoked pendent appellate jurisdiction. The court rejected both characterizations. A preserved objection to personal jurisdiction presented an essential threshold question: the district court could not validly enjoin a defendant over whom it lacked jurisdiction. The injunction appeal therefore required considering that underlying limitation on the district court's authority. Although personal jurisdiction can be waived, no waiver had occurred here. Slip pp. 5–6.

The Lanham Act did not supply a special federal personal-jurisdiction rule. Rule 4(k)(1)(A) led to Indiana's long-arm provision, which Indiana precedent treated as extending to federal due-process limits. Advanced Tactical conceded that it could not establish general jurisdiction, leaving specific jurisdiction based on the defendant's suit-related forum conduct. Subject-matter jurisdiction over the federal and supplemental claims did not itself establish power over these defendants. Slip pp. 6–7.

## Why sales and foreseeable Indiana injury were insufficient

The court applied ordinary minimum-contacts principles, including the Supreme Court's recent Walden decision. The relevant connection must arise from the defendant's conduct linking it to the forum and the litigation. The plaintiff's location, or the foreseeable effect on a plaintiff located there, cannot serve as the sole link. This limitation applies to intentional torts as well as other claims. Slip pp. 7–10.

The district court relied in part on several Indiana orders fulfilled after Real Action's allegedly misleading announcement. But Advanced Tactical did not connect those orders to the misconduct in suit. There was no evidence that the purchasers had seen the announcement before ordering or had been misled about the identity of the PepperBall manufacturer. The allegation that Real Action had made more than six hundred Indiana sales during the preceding two years did not cure that failure. Specific jurisdiction requires litigation-related conduct, not an undifferentiated inventory of every transaction involving the state. Slip p. 8.

The court also expressed concern that allowing unrelated or inadequately linked shipments to suffice would expose a seller to suit in every state receiving an item. Its analysis should not be reduced to a categorical prohibition on jurisdiction arising from a small number of sales: the central defect was the absence of evidence connecting relevant Indiana transactions to the alleged wrong. Advanced Tactical bore the evidentiary burden and did not meet it. Slip pp. 8–9.

Nor did Real Action's knowledge of Advanced Tactical's asserted Indiana location establish jurisdiction. The district court had reasoned that Real Action could foresee local injury from misleading statements and sales. Under Walden, however, affecting a plaintiff connected to the forum does not establish a sufficient defendant–forum connection. The court acknowledged tension in its own precedents concerning injury to a forum plaintiff, but explained that any reading making the plaintiff the only link to the state could no longer be authoritative after Walden. Slip pp. 9–10.

## Email lists, interactivity, and geographic targeting

The email blasts went to a subscriber list containing Indiana recipients. Subscribers' physical locations did not establish that Real Action deliberately cultivated Indiana as a market for the alleged misleading communication. Email can be sent and opened wherever its users happen to be, and an ordinary list subscription does not by itself establish a substantial connection with every state appearing in subscribers' postal addresses. The district court's reliance on the economic benefit of adding customers to the list did not identify the required deliberate Indiana connection. Slip pp. 10–11.

The court gave geographically restricted online advertising as a possible example of different evidence. Such advertising could demonstrate the defendant's deliberate targeting, rather than merely users' decisions to subscribe. It did not hold that email could never support jurisdiction or that online advertising invariably would. Advanced Tactical introduced no evidence of comparable targeting and made no such argument on appeal. Slip pp. 11–12.

Website interactivity likewise was a poor substitute for a forum-specific relationship. Ordinary due-process analysis remained workable for Internet conduct; a special categorical Internet test was unnecessary. Moreover, even if interactivity were relevant evidence, the allegedly infringing announcement was itself a posted notice. The site's other interactive features did not explain how that notice created a claim-related Indiana connection. Treating an accessible interactive website as sufficient would leave no meaningful geographic limit. Slip pp. 10–13.

The court recognized that Walden itself reserved questions about virtual contacts. It therefore applied existing principles through the circuit's Internet precedents rather than claiming that the Supreme Court had already resolved every online-jurisdiction question. That qualification matters when using this decision historically. Slip p. 10.

## Judgment, correction, and the assignment

The court concluded that Real Action lacked minimum contacts supporting specific jurisdiction in Indiana. It **remanded with instructions to vacate the preliminary injunction and dismiss the complaint for lack of personal jurisdiction**. The merits of trademark infringement and the substantive requirements for injunctive relief were not adjudicated. Slip p. 13, read with the May 12 correction order.

The original final sentence referred to vacating the “judgment.” The May 12 order expressly directed replacing that word with “preliminary injunction.” The order's subsequently quoted versions inconsistently retained “preliminary judgment” and “judgment”; those are source inconsistencies, not a reason to invent a final merits judgment. The express replacement instruction and the opinion's entire interlocutory posture identify the preliminary injunction as the relief to be vacated.

The textbook accurately focuses on foreseeability, email location, and deliberate exploitation of the forum market. The complete opinion adds the evidentiary-hearing burden, the reason jurisdiction was reviewable on the injunction appeal, and the missing relationship between Indiana sales and the alleged misconduct. These points explain why this particular record failed without turning the decision into a rule that all Internet sales or all targeted communications are jurisdictionally irrelevant.
