# Volkswagenwerk Aktiengesellschaft v. Schlunk

**486 U.S. 694 (1988) — June 15, 1988. Civil Procedure.** Justice O’Connor wrote for Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices White, Stevens, Scalia, and Kennedy. Justice Brennan, joined by Justices Marshall and Blackmun, concurred in the judgment. The outcome was unanimous, with a six-Justice majority rationale and a three-Justice alternative rationale. Assigned account: CivPro, physical page 158, printed page 267, the “Second” paragraph of Note 3.

## Facts and the domestic service method

Herwig Schlunk’s parents died in an automobile accident in 1983. As administrator of their estates, he sued in Illinois state court, alleging that defects in the car caused or contributed to their deaths. He initially named Volkswagen of America, Inc. (VWoA), alleging that it designed and sold the car, and also sued the other driver for negligence. The other driver defaulted and was no longer a party to this litigation. When VWoA answered that it had not designed or assembled the car, Schlunk amended the complaint to add Volkswagen Aktiengesellschaft (VWAG), the German parent company. The Supreme Court did not decide whether the car was defective or who caused the accident. [696–697.]

Instead of transmitting process to Germany, Schlunk served VWoA as VWAG’s domestic agent. VWAG appeared specially to challenge service, asserting that the Hague Service Convention supplied the exclusive permissible route and that Schlunk had not used it. The Circuit Court of Cook County found VWoA an involuntary service agent under Illinois law. It relied on complete ownership, overlapping directors, and the subsidiary’s contractual role as exclusive importer and distributor of the parent’s products in the United States. The parent’s refusal or failure formally to appoint the subsidiary did not defeat the agency the Illinois court recognized. VWoA itself was registered to do business in Illinois and had a registered service agent there. [697.]

On certified questions, the Illinois Appellate Court affirmed the agency determination and concluded that the domestic service did not violate the Convention. The Illinois Supreme Court denied leave to appeal. The United States Supreme Court granted review of the treaty question; VWAG did not seek review of the holding that service was proper as a matter of Illinois law. That limitation is essential: the decision does not independently create a general rule that every domestic subsidiary is necessarily its foreign parent’s service agent. [697–698, 706.]

## Issue and Convention framework

The issue was whether the Hague Service Convention applied when state law permitted service on a foreign corporation through its domestic subsidiary without requiring documents to be sent abroad as part of completing service. Article 1 made the Convention applicable when a judicial or extrajudicial document had to be transmitted for service abroad. Where the Convention applied, its requirements were mandatory and displaced inconsistent state service methods through the Supremacy Clause. The dispute concerned that threshold, rather than whether a covered litigant could disregard the treaty. [698–700.]

The Convention established central authorities to receive service requests, arrange service by an appropriate method, and provide certificates. It also permitted certain alternative channels, subject to the relevant conditions, and addressed default judgments and relief from them in Articles 15 and 16. Its purposes included facilitating service abroad, improving mutual judicial assistance, and assuring foreign defendants timely notice. The report’s numbers of participating countries describe 1988, not a verified present-day membership total. [698–699.]

## Majority: the forum’s law identifies whether service requires transmission abroad

O’Connor treated service as a legal act: a formal delivery sufficient to charge the defendant with notice of the suit. Because the treaty did not supply a complete standard for deciding when a delivery was legally sufficient, the forum’s internal law supplied that standard. If the applicable method required sending documents abroad to complete service, the Convention applied. If valid service was already complete domestically, a later private communication from the recipient to a foreign defendant was not itself the treaty-triggering act. [700–702, 707.]

The majority examined drafting history alongside the text. Negotiators refined the language to distinguish service from broader transmission or delivery and rejected language that might cover sending documents abroad without service there. The history also addressed whether the requesting state’s law would determine when foreign service was necessary. The majority interpreted the failure to add an express reference to that law as rejection of a redundant clarification, rather than rejection of the underlying principle. The official explanatory materials acknowledged that leaving the threshold to the requesting state reduced the Convention’s compulsory reach but offered clarity. [700–702.]

VWAG argued that this approach permitted states to evade the treaty by labeling procedures domestic and undermined its notice-protection objective. It pointed to notification au parquet, a criticized procedure in which documents were deposited with a local official for a foreign defendant and service could be regarded as complete before effective foreign notice. The majority acknowledged the negotiators’ wish to eliminate that practice, but expressly left unresolved whether the Convention applied to it. It found no comparable negotiating evidence barring domestic subsidiary service of the kind Illinois recognized here. [703–704.]

The majority further distinguished the Convention’s enabling and notice functions. Domestic-agent service did not prevent establishment and use of central authorities for foreign service. It conceded that its reading did not necessarily advance every notice-protection objective, but expected states not to deliberately evade appropriate foreign service. American due process independently required a method reasonably calculated to inform the foreign defendant and provide an opportunity to object. Parties also remained free to follow Convention procedures voluntarily, sometimes gaining greater certainty about service and foreign enforcement of a later judgment. These practical observations supported the interpretation; they did not make compliance optional once the Convention actually applied. [704–706.]

## Application and important reservation

VWAG argued that VWoA would necessarily send the complaint to Germany and that due process required such notification. Therefore, it reasoned, the case inevitably involved transmission abroad for service. The Court rejected that equivalence. Once domestic-agent service was valid and complete under both state law and due process, the inquiry ended. Internal communications between the domestic agent and foreign principal were outside the treaty issue; only a transmission required as a necessary part of service triggered Article 1. Due process did not invariably require an official transmission abroad whenever the defendant was foreign. On the established Illinois-law premise, the Convention did not apply. [706–708.]

The majority’s starred judicial footnote supplies a related limit. A subsidiary’s activities do not automatically establish jurisdiction over the parent, and determining the constitutional adequacy of substituted service may require a fact-specific inquiry. The Court did not decide whether the particular parent–subsidiary relationship itself made substituted service constitutionally sufficient. The case concerned the Convention on the unchallenged state-law service premise, not a newly announced universal agency or jurisdiction rule. [705–706 n.*.]

## Brennan’s concurrence

Brennan agreed that serving this closely controlled, wholly owned domestic subsidiary was consistent with the Convention, but rejected leaving its reach entirely to each forum’s own labels. He feared that local law could revive inadequate foreign-notice practices by simply declaring service complete domestically. Because American constitutional protections did not bind other nations, reliance on domestic due process did not protect American defendants sued abroad. [708–711, 716.]

His reading of the negotiating history emphasized the final explanatory report, the concern over notification au parquet, and the United States delegation’s understanding that the Convention imposed minimum protections. His six footnotes support that history: note 1 explains the criticized practice and default consequences; note 2 reproduces the commission’s understanding that it remained governed; notes 3–5 record supporting executive explanations; note 6 documents assurances that ordinary American procedures would require little change. His standard asked whether the domestic method was designed to bring documents to the foreign addressee in sufficient time, rather than simply accepting the forum’s declaration that service was complete. [709–715 nn.1–6.]

This case passed Brennan’s substantive standard because service on the wholly owned, closely controlled subsidiary was reasonably calculated to reach the parent promptly. The negotiating history did not suggest abolition of that established method. He therefore concurred in affirmance while preserving a treaty-based limitation against inadequate purportedly domestic procedures. The majority’s response in its starred note challenged the textual basis, uncertain contours, and ease of applying his proposed standard. Neither position should be presented as the other’s rationale. [705–706 n.*, 714–716.]

## Judgment and assigned account

The Court **affirmed the Illinois Appellate Court’s judgment**. The service objection based on the Convention failed, and the wrongful-death litigation could proceed; no liability or damages judgment was rendered here. [708.]

The textbook’s statement that the Convention does not govern service effected in the United States is a concise account of this result. The complete decision supplies the qualifications: valid domestic service must actually be complete without a required foreign transmission; the state agency determination was not under review; constitutional agency sufficiency was reserved; and three Justices supported the outcome on a different treaty standard. The entire 23-page official report, including the majority’s continued starred note and Brennan’s six notes, and the complete original assigned page were read. The accompanying review register preserves source scope and separate verification. This brief describes the historical decision without certifying later law.
