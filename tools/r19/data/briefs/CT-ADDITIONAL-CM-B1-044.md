# Simmons v. United States
308 F.2d 160 (4th Cir. 1962) | Contracts

## Court, actual dispute, and result
Chief Judge Sobeloff wrote for a panel including Haynsworth and Boreman. On August 28, 1962, the Fourth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for the United States in William and Viola Simmons’s tax-refund action. The actual defendant was the federal government, not the brewery that offered and paid a fishing prize. The court held that the $25,000 payment was taxable, did not qualify for the invoked statutory exclusions, and could constitutionally be taxed without apportionment. Its reward-contract discussion was part of the analysis rejecting treatment of that payment as a gift. No separate writing appears. (160–162, 168.)

## The contest, performance, and tax proceedings
American Brewery placed a tagged rockfish called Diamond Jim III in Chesapeake Bay on June 19, 1958, in cooperation with Maryland game officials. Its widely publicized Third Annual American Beer Fishing Derby promised $25,000 to whoever caught that fish and presented it with the tag and an affidavit that it had been caught on hook and line. Other tagged fish carried smaller prizes. (161–162.)

William Simmons caught Diamond Jim III on August 6. He initially paid little attention to the tag, but recognized the prize fish when he looked again about half an hour later. He then complied with the remaining conditions and received the money during a television appearance arranged by the brewery. He had known about the contest before fishing, but did not have the remote prospect of catching Diamond Jim in mind when he set out that morning. Knowledge of the outstanding offer and the reason he went fishing were therefore separate matters. (162.)

The Internal Revenue Service assessed a $5,230 deficiency on the ground that the payment was income under Internal Revenue Code sections 61(a) and 74(a). Simmons paid, sought a refund, and received a small refund based on deductions. He sued for the balance, arguing that the payment was not includable income, alternatively that it was an excluded achievement award under section 74(b) or a gift under section 102, and finally that taxing it would violate the Constitution. The district court entered summary judgment for the government. (162 & nn.1–4.)

## Achievement award: recipient’s activity controls
The then-operative section 74(b) exclusion concerned awards made primarily in recognition of specified religious, charitable, scientific, educational, artistic, literary, or civic achievements, subject to further conditions about entry and future services. Simmons invoked civic achievement, arguing that the contest promoted Maryland’s recreation and resort facilities and rewarded his skill. The court found that the advertising plainly aimed to sell beer, but made the broader point that the donor’s purpose was not the controlling issue under this particular exclusion. The character of the recipient’s achievement determined whether it belonged to a protected category. A commercial donor could reward a qualifying civic achievement; a public promotional purpose could not turn an otherwise ordinary activity into one. (162–163 & nn.4–7.)

Civic achievement required positive, exemplary, unselfish action broadly beneficial to the community, understood alongside the other listed achievements. Legislative history contrasted genuinely meritorious awards with ordinary giveaway and contest prizes. Catching this fish was principally fortunate, not a civic accomplishment, and the facts could not reasonably support the requested jury finding even viewed favorably to Simmons. The court’s hypothetical of capturing a dangerous whale illustrated the distinction; it was not a factual feature of this contest. Because the necessary qualifying achievement was absent, the court did not need to establish that every other statutory exclusion requirement independently failed. (163–164.)

## Gift exclusion and the unilateral-contract analysis
Gift status turned on the donor’s motivation under Duberstein. Although motivation ordinarily calls for factual inference, summary judgment was proper if the record viewed favorably to the taxpayer could not reasonably support detached generosity, affection, respect, charity, or similar impulses. There was no personal relationship between Simmons and the brewery and no need-based charitable payment. The prize went to whoever caught the fish regardless of wealth, and Simmons’s successful catch and publicized award supplied the publicity the brewery sought. (164 & nn.8–10.)

Contract principles reinforced the conclusion. In the absence of pertinent Maryland decisions, the court reasoned that an announced prize for a specified act constitutes an offer for a unilateral contract. Performance with knowledge of the offer can accept it. The performer need not act principally to obtain the reward: Simmons knew about the Derby, and his unrelated reason for going fishing did not prevent acceptance when he caught the fish and satisfied the other conditions. A payment discharging the resulting obligation was not a gift simply because the catch was fortuitous. (164–165 & nn.11–13.)

Judicial note 14 carefully qualified enforceability. Because payment depended on catching a particular fish by chance, the arrangement might be attacked as a wagering contract; Maryland law did not clearly resolve that issue. The court did not adjudicate such a defense against the brewery. Even if the promise were unenforceable on that ground, the tax result would remain: extensive advertising created a strong moral duty to pay, a payment proceeding principally from moral or legal constraint was not a gift, and gains from unenforceable wagers could still be income. Thus the opinion’s conclusion about taxation did not require an unqualified holding that the brewery’s promise defeated every possible Maryland contract defense. (165 & n.14.)

## Constitutional taxing power and the alternative income holding
The court separated Congress’s general Article I taxing power from the restrictions applicable to particular kinds of taxes. The pertinent question was apportionment, which applies to direct taxes unless the Sixteenth Amendment removes that requirement for income. The taxpayer therefore needed to establish both that the tax was direct and that the payment was outside income covered by the amendment. Merely disputing the government’s chosen label for the levy would not establish constitutional invalidity. (165–166 & nn.15–21.)

The court first characterized this as an indirect tax on receipt of money, rather than a direct tax solely on ownership of property. It relied on precedents concerning transfers, gifts, inheritances, and other incidents of ownership to distinguish those events from taxes on property merely because it is owned. In the alternative, even assuming a direct tax, Simmons’s receipt was income within the Sixteenth Amendment. Glenshaw Glass and James treated realized accessions to wealth under the recipient’s dominion as income; Simmons had complete control and a permanent legal benefit, without a duty to restore the money. The court also explained why the constitutional and statutory income inquiries supported one another here and why the narrower language in Eisner v. Macomber did not govern all later income questions. It affirmed. (166–168 & nn.19–28.)

## Assigned account and reading scope
“The One That Didn’t Get Away” at printed 208 accurately contrasts knowledge of the contest with the fisherman’s immediate motive and asks why Simmons, rather than the brewery, called the prize a gift. The complete opinion explains that he sought favorable tax treatment after the brewery had already paid.
