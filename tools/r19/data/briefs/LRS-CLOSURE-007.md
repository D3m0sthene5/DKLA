# Chemical Waste Management, Inc. v. EPA
873 F.2d 1477 (D.C. Cir. May 5, 1989) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## The regulatory dispute and review posture

The Resource Conservation and Recovery Act required hazardous-waste treatment, storage, and disposal facilities to obtain permits, while certain existing facilities could operate on interim status during the permitting process. Section 3008(a) authorized EPA enforcement orders for regulatory violations, including civil penalties and permit suspension or revocation. Section 3008(b) required a public hearing upon a timely request and authorized subpoenas and discretionary discovery rules. EPA’s Part 22 regulations used APA formal adjudication for those enforcement hearings, including an administrative law judge and witness examination. Its explanation tied that choice to sanctions for past conduct and disputed historical facts, rather than declaring that every statutory public hearing necessarily required formal procedures. (1478–1479.)

The 1984 amendments added § 3008(h), allowing corrective-action orders when hazardous waste had been released from an interim facility, and extended the public-hearing right to those orders. An order could require investigation or corrective measures and could suspend or revoke interim authorization; noncompliance could trigger penalties of up to $25,000 per day. EPA’s challenged Part 24 rules retained formal Part 22 procedures for orders suspending or revoking interim status or assessing noncompliance penalties. For other investigative and corrective orders, they provided informal procedures, differentiated according to cost and technical complexity. The petition therefore did not challenge elimination of formal hearings for every kind of corrective-action enforcement. (1479.)

Under those informal procedures, facility operators could submit written evidence and argument, make oral presentations, and receive assistance from legal and technical advisers. Direct and cross-examination were barred, but the presiding officer could question either party; in specified proceedings a respondent could request permission to submit up to twenty-five written questions on material facts. The officer had to be a Regional Judicial Officer or another agency attorney without prior connection to the case, including investigative or prosecutorial work. EPA disclosed the relevant materials considered in developing the order, excluding privileged internal communications. The officer recommended a decision; the Regional Administrator received party comments and decided, with APA judicial review available. (1479–1480, 1482–1483.)

Chemical Waste Management and Waste Management of North America petitioned directly for review of these procedural regulations. They asserted that the statute required formal adjudication, that EPA had inadequately justified its contrary interpretation, and that the rules facially violated due process. This was review of the rules themselves, not a factual challenge to an individual corrective-action order. Judge D.H. Ginsburg wrote for Chief Judge Wald and Judge Starr; there was no dissent or concurrence. The court denied the petition. (1477–1478, 1480, 1485.)

## Statutory ambiguity and departure from the hearing presumption

Applying the historical Chevron framework, the court first asked whether Congress had unambiguously settled the hearing’s form. The public-hearing language did not specify formal rather than informal proceedings. Discretionary authority to issue discovery rules did not require their use: Congress distinguished things EPA “shall” do from those it “may” do. The court therefore rejected a mandatory-discovery inference without deciding whether discovery would otherwise imply formal procedures. (1480.)

EPA’s 1978 explanation did not lock the agency into a universal formal-hearing interpretation. It had selected formal process because of the particular issues arising under subsection (a). Even a prior broader interpretation could have been changed if legally permissible and adequately explained. Nor did the 1984 extension of subsection (b) incorporate Part 22 into the statute. Senator Chafee’s statement that the subsection’s procedures applied to new corrective orders referred to the statutory subsection, not expressly to the regulatory hearing rules. Ambiguous legislative history did not establish congressional adoption of those regulations. (1480–1481.)

Union of Concerned Scientists had stated a presumption that a hearing requirement in adjudication meant an on-the-record hearing. Chemical Waste treated that statement as dictum: the earlier case concerned bypassing a hearing altogether, expressly withheld a categorical ruling on formal procedure, and relied on particular statutory history and long-standing NRC practice. More fundamentally, the court reasoned that such a presumption prematurely converted ambiguous language into an unambiguous limit and displaced the agency’s interpretive role under Chevron. It announced neither a presumption favoring formal hearings nor one favoring informal hearings. The agency would initially resolve the ambiguity, subject to reasonableness review using the usual interpretive tools. The sole judicial footnote states that rejection of the prior presumption had been circulated to and approved by the entire court; this was a unanimous panel judgment with that additional full-court approval, not a newly rendered en banc merits judgment. (1481–1482 & n.*.)

## Reasonableness: an accepted ground and a rejected ground

EPA expected corrective-action cases to raise fewer historical factual disputes than sanction cases, with issues largely involving technical data or policy rather than demeanor and witness credibility. Written submissions, oral statements, and officer-directed questions could resolve those matters, with discretion to tailor proceedings to an individual case. That explanation reasonably supported the informal procedures for the typical covered corrective order. Speculation that some issues might admit competing interpretations or benefit from more formal fact-finding did not invalidate the rules. (1482–1483.)

The court nevertheless rejected EPA’s additional reliance on a congressional desire for speed. The 1984 amendments permitted action against interim facilities without waiting years for completed permits. Senator Chafee’s concern addressed that prior regulatory obstacle, not slow adjudication after corrective orders issued. EPA therefore could not justify informal hearings on that legislative-history ground. Its independent explanation concerning the number and nature of factual issues remained sufficient. The result should not be described as acceptance of both asserted rationales. (1483.)

## Facial due process and reserved individual challenges

Under Mathews v. Eldridge, the court weighed private interests, error risk and the value of additional safeguards, and governmental interests and costs. Corrective measures could have multimillion-dollar consequences, but costs varied. The possibility of unusually expensive individual orders did not make the procedural scheme facially unconstitutional when designed around typical cases without status suspension or penalties. EPA also identified existing corrective-action regulations, agency guidance, and CERCLA remedial regulations as sources of substantive standards pending new standards; petitioners did not challenge their utility in reply. (1483–1484.)

Petitioners did not show that trial-type hearings would substantially improve decisions ordinarily turning on technical evidence and policy instead of credibility. Nor did due process categorically require an ALJ. The agency’s rules prohibited an officer’s prior investigative or prosecutorial involvement in the case. Under Withrow, even combined functions were not automatically unconstitutional, and an individual challenger had to overcome the presumption of adjudicative honesty with circumstances demonstrating intolerable unfairness or probable actual bias. A facial challenge supplied no such particular circumstances. Petitioners also failed to explain why an agency attorney would be less capable of evaluating technical matters than their preferred ALJ. (1484–1485.)

EPA estimated that informal hearings would save roughly half its participation costs. Petitioners’ theories about later remands or improved reports under cross-examination did not compare those potential costs with the substantial, undisputed savings in other cases. Given the limited demonstrated error reduction, formal procedures did not justify their added governmental expense. The court held Part 24 consistent with due process on its face, while expressly contemplating possible trouble in a particular case combining high stakes with issues benefiting substantially from trial-type procedures or an actually biased officer. EPA could avoid those problems by attending to individual needs. (1485.)

## Exact judgment and assigned scope

The court denied the petition for review because Part 24 reasonably interpreted an ambiguous statutory hearing mandate and survived the facial constitutional challenge. It did not affirm a specific hazardous-waste cleanup order, preclude all as-applied challenges, or hold that the APA never required formal adjudication without particular words. The complete judicial opinion and its sole asterisk footnote have been read. (1485.)
