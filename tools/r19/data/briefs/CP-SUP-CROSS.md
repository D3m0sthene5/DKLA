# Cross v. United States
336 F.2d 431 (2d Cir. 1964) | Civil Procedure | Summary judgment and credibility

## The dispute and the travel that produced it

Ephraim Cross, an assistant professor at City College in New York, taught French, Spanish, and romance linguistics. He and his wife claimed a $1,300 deduction on their joint 1954 income-tax return for expenses associated with his summer trip to Europe and the Mediterranean. The legal question underlying the refund suit was whether his travel expenses were ordinary and necessary expenses of his teaching business or personal vacation expenses. The appellate decision addressed the narrower procedural question whether the taxpayers had established their entitlement to a refund without a trial.

Cross, his wife, and their dog left New York on June 30, 1954, aboard a French freighter. Over twenty-one days, the vessel stopped briefly in Portugal, Morocco, Tangiers, Oran, Algiers, Naples, and Genoa before reaching Marseilles. The couple then separated: Mrs. Cross continued touring with a friend, while the professor and the dog traveled to Paris. He returned on a French passenger liner and rejoined his wife in the United States on September 23.

Cross neither enrolled in a formal course nor conducted research. He instead visited schools, courts, churches, publishers, theaters, movies, restaurants, cafés, and other public places; read newspapers; listened to the radio; spoke with students and teachers; and attended political meetings. He maintained that contact with contemporary language and foreign culture preserved and improved his linguistic skills and made his teaching more effective. Those activities were real features of the itinerary, but whether professional improvement was their primary purpose remained contested. Assigned PDF pp. 1–3.

## Proceedings and the parties' competing positions

The district court granted the taxpayers' motion for summary judgment. Its record included Cross's discovery deposition and affidavits from professors explaining why travel abroad could benefit a language teacher. The court considered the essential facts undisputed and reasoned that the government had failed to present facts refuting Cross's account of his purpose. It also regarded the government's opportunity to question him at his deposition as sufficient. The lower decision is reported at 222 F. Supp. 157 (S.D.N.Y. 1963).

The government opposed summary judgment and appealed. It wanted to cross-examine Cross and the affiant professors about the expenses and the educational benefits allegedly sought. It disputed whether the trip was entirely professional, whether some or all of it was a vacation, and whether the claimed amount included expenses for Mrs. Cross. The appellate opinion states that Cross's deposition was taken for discovery and did not include cross-examination. That description matters: the court did not treat an untested explanation of subjective purpose as a conclusive finding merely because it appeared in a deposition. Assigned PDF pp. 1–3, 5.

The taxpayers argued that foreign-language immersion was a legitimate method of improving professional skill even without classroom instruction. The government did not have to disprove the potential value of immersion to raise the distinct issue whether this particular trip, or particular components of it, qualified for the claimed deduction.

## Governing law and the issue on appeal

Section 162(a) of the Internal Revenue Code permitted deduction of ordinary and necessary expenses incurred in carrying on a trade or business. The regulation quoted in the opinion allowed educational expenses undertaken primarily to maintain or improve skills required in the taxpayer's employment. It required a factual assessment and recognized customary professional education as evidence of an appropriate purpose. But the regulation also treated travel as a form of education as generally personal and nondeductible. Assigned PDF pp. 2–3.

Under the then-applicable Rule 56(c), summary judgment required the absence of a genuine issue of material fact. Here, the material facts included the trip's primary purpose, the relationship between particular activities and Cross's teaching duties, and the allocation of the claimed expenses. The question was whether the undisputed basic facts—that Cross taught languages and traveled abroad—also established those disputed ultimate facts as a matter of law.

## Why the Second Circuit required a trial

Judge Moore's opinion concluded that summary judgment had been improvidently granted. Much of the important information lay within Cross's own knowledge, and the competing inferences concerned motive, intent, and subjective reactions. Accepting his account as dispositive would require a credibility assessment. The government was entitled to test that account through cross-examination before the trier of fact. Assigned PDF pp. 3–4.

The court acknowledged its recent rule that unsupported allegations in pleadings ordinarily would not defeat a properly supported summary-judgment motion. It nevertheless distinguished that principle from this record, where disputed factual questions turned on the credibility of the moving parties' witnesses. Its point was not that any opponent may manufacture a trial by asserting disbelief. The professional-versus-personal character and allocation of an actual mixed itinerary were live issues, and the government's inability to produce a competing account of Cross's internal purpose did not establish the truth of his own account.

The court also refused to equate educational usefulness with complete deductibility. It endorsed the importance of exposure to current language and culture and discussed Revenue Ruling 64-176, under which a French teacher's travel could qualify if the itinerary and major activities primarily served improvement of teaching skills. Informal visits, movies, plays, and conversations could satisfy that standard. Thus, the absence of a formal course did not itself defeat Cross's claim. Assigned PDF p. 4.

But the same ruling cautioned that teachers' educational travel commonly resembled other travelers' recreation. Deduction depended on the travel's direct connection to teaching duties and extended only as far as that connection justified. A lawyer might learn from observing British trials, or a surgeon from watching a European operation, without making all summer vacation expenses deductible. A potentially professional benefit therefore did not resolve the trip's primary purpose or the amount properly allocated to it. Assigned PDF pp. 4–5.

The twenty-one-day freighter journey before Marseilles, Mrs. Cross's expenses, and charges attributable to the dog illustrated the unanswered allocation questions. The opinion's joke about a French poodle did not create a holding about pet expenses; it highlighted that the aggregate $1,300 claim required factual examination.

Finally, a discovery deposition did not replace the right to call and examine an adverse party at trial. Relying on Arnstein v. Porter, the court emphasized examination and cross-examination before the factfinder. A trial would also give Cross a chance to explain more specifically why immersion was a superior method of maintaining linguistic skill. The procedural ruling opened a path for both sides to develop the evidence rather than choosing the government's preferred account. Assigned PDF p. 5.

## Judgment, participation, and limits

The Second Circuit reversed and remanded for trial. It did not deny the deduction, declare the whole trip a vacation, or establish what fraction of the $1,300 was deductible. Those matters remained for factual determination. Judge Moore wrote for a panel consisting of Judges Moore, Kaufman, and Marshall; the reproduced decision contains no concurrence or dissent.

The decision illustrates the distinction between undisputed events and disputed inferences about those events. It also shows why a court must identify the actual material factual dispute rather than assume that helpful professional activity establishes the required primary purpose. Its strongly worded discussion of summary judgment is presented on its 1964 terms; this brief does not claim to resolve how later Rule 56 decisions would analyze a different record.
