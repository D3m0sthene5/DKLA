Pyeatte v. Pyeatte
135 Ariz. 346, 661 P.2d 196 (Ct. App. 1982)
Court, author, and source scope
The Court of Appeals of Arizona, Division One, decided this appeal on October 28, 1982.
Judge Corcoran wrote the opinion; Acting Presiding Judge Froeb and Judge Hathaway
concurred. The assigned excerpt, printed at pages 155–160, presents contract, statutory
property, and restitution theories but omits substantial discussion of restitution between
spouses and part of the remedy analysis. The complete opinion was read through the
judgment and participation note, including the limit on recovery based on the anticipated
reciprocal benefit.
Parties and material facts
Margrethe May Pyeatte and H. Charles Pyeatte married in December 1972. Both had
bachelor's degrees. In early 1974 they agreed that Margrethe would support the couple while
Charles completed three years of law school, without his having to work, and he would
afterward support her while she obtained a master's degree without working. The husband
acknowledged an agreement, although he disputed its qualifications. The trial court found an
undertaking with no express or implied exception for divorce. 135 Ariz. at 349.
Margrethe supported them from her salary during Charles's first two years of law school.
When she lost her job during the last year, they used savings to which both had contributed,
with a significant amount supplied by her. Charles completed law school and was admitted to
the bar. After they moved to Prescott, his beginning law-firm salary could not simultaneously
support the household and finance her studies. She agreed to postpone her education for a
year or two while he established his practice and obtained part-time teaching work. Id.
In April 1978, Charles told Margrethe that he no longer wanted to remain married. She filed
for dissolution in June. When the case was tried in March 1979, the community had little
property. There was no dispute about dividing the available property, and she neither
requested nor received spousal maintenance. Thus, the couple separated before her
educational turn and before a substantial marital estate had accumulated from his
professional earnings. Id.
Procedural history
The trial court treated the education arrangement as an enforceable contract. It found that
Margrethe performed and Charles did not. Based on expert evidence of the cost of her
proposed education, it awarded $23,000 as contract damages. The judgment called for
quarterly payments of at least ten percent of his net income, required efforts to generate
income and maintenance of records, permitted review of those records, and retained
supervisory jurisdiction. Id. at 349–50.
Charles appealed. Margrethe defended the award on three grounds: an enforceable contract,
equitable reimbursement through the property-division statute, and restitution for unjust
enrichment. The appellate court rejected the first two grounds and recognized a narrower
restitutionary basis. It reversed the $23,000 award and remanded for calculation under that

Issues
Was the reciprocal education agreement sufficiently definite to establish a contract? Could
the husband's education, degree, or earning capacity be treated as property supporting
reimbursement under A.R.S. § 25-318? If both theories failed, did the wife's support
nevertheless confer a benefit whose retention would be unjust, and what measure and form
of relief could follow? Id. at 350–52, 357–58.
Holdings
The agreement was too indefinite to enforce as a contract. Critical matters concerning the
wife's educational program—including when it would begin and end, where she would study,
and the cost and associated relocation obligations—were unresolved. Her performance could
not authorize the court to supply an entire arrangement that the parties had not adequately
defined. Id. at 350–51.
The husband's education and professional qualification were not community property subject
to valuation and division under the cited statute. That prevented the proposed statutory
reimbursement theory, but did not foreclose a personal restitutionary obligation arising from
unjust enrichment. Id. at 351–54.
In the particular circumstances, restitution was appropriate. The agreement evidenced an
expectation of reciprocal compensation and an extraordinary, substantially unilateral
contribution that had benefited the husband without the anticipated return to the wife before
dissolution. Recovery should reflect her financial contribution to his living and direct
educational expenses, subject to a cap if the benefit she expected from the bargain was
smaller. Equitable relief could be payable in installments tailored to the circumstances. Id. at
353–58.
Actual judgment
The portion of the judgment awarding $23,000 was reversed, and the matter was remanded
for proceedings consistent with the opinion. The court did not affirm $23,000 as the price of
the husband's degree or order specific performance of a promise to fund the wife's master's
program. It required a restitution calculation rather than the original expectation-based
measure. Id. at 357–58.
The court approved the permissibility of periodic payments and related record review as
equitable mechanisms; it did not prescribe a uniform installment formula for all
educational-support cases. The amount had to be recalculated, and the form of relief
remained responsive to the parties' circumstances. Id. at 357–58.

Reasoning
Indefiniteness concerned the scope of the promised performance
The court accepted that the parties had reached an understanding. It did not treat a spousal
agreement as unenforceable merely because the parties were married. The problem was
whether the wife's reciprocal entitlement was defined sufficiently for the court to determine
the husband's obligation. Id. at 350–51.
Unlike the specified three years of law school, her course of study had no settled duration.
There was no adequate agreement on a starting date, school, location, relocation, or
expenditure level. The difference between an inexpensive local program and an expensive
out-of-state program could materially change the obligation. The husband’s contention
concerning the agreement's consequences upon divorce was not necessary to decide,
because the other deficiencies were sufficient. Id.
The court acknowledged the preference for interpretations preserving agreements and the
significance of one party's performance. It nevertheless distinguished interpretation from
making a contract. A desire to give a sympathetic claimant relief could not supply essential
commitments whose boundaries the parties themselves had never adequately fixed. Id. at
351.
No divisible property interest in the degree
Margrethe argued that community resources had been exhausted to create a valuable asset
retained by Charles. The court followed Wisner in rejecting the characterization of education,
professional licensing, or enhanced earning capacity as divisible community property. The
property statute therefore did not supply the proposed lien or reimbursement. Id. at 351–52.
The opinion later refers descriptively to his education as the marriage's valuable asset. That
language expresses economic benefit; it does not reverse the doctrinal holding that the
degree is not property divisible under the statute. A useful brief preserves that distinction
between economic value and the legal category needed for a particular statutory remedy. Id.
at 352, 356–57.
The failed agreement remained evidence relevant to restitution
The absence of an enforceable contract did not make the education arrangement irrelevant.
It showed that Margrethe's support was furnished with an expectation of reciprocal
educational assistance rather than solely as a gratuitous contribution. Charles received a
concrete benefit: relief from living and educational expenses allowed him to devote time to
professional training instead of working, delaying study, or borrowing more. Id. at 352–53.
The court distinguished implied-in-fact contracts from quasi-contractual duties. The former
would still require the agreement and definiteness already found lacking. Restitution instead
prevents unjust retention of a benefit in circumstances warranting compensation. The wife's
claim thus could not succeed merely by relabeling the same indefinite bargain; it needed an
independent explanation of injustice. The sequence of extraordinary support, deferred
reciprocity, and early dissolution supplied that explanation. Id. at 353–54.

Restitution did not authorize a financial accounting of every marriage
The court addressed its earlier statement in Wisner that restitution is generally inappropriate
within an ongoing marital relationship. Ordinary reciprocal household activities are
consumed in the shared relationship and do not become invoices upon divorce. Wisner
involved a long marriage, substantial benefits from the husband's earnings, and property and
maintenance awarded to the wife. Pyeatte presented materially different circumstances. Id.
at 353–55.
Here one spouse's concentrated contribution financed the other's education, little marital
property existed, and dissolution closely followed completion before the working spouse
could receive the planned reciprocal benefit. The court reviewed decisions allowing relief in
comparable circumstances and distinguished cases where the supporting spouse had already
enjoyed substantial returns through the marriage and property division. Id. at 354–57.
Its holding remained limited. The court expressly declined to decide the standards applicable
absent an agreement, and rejected treating marriage as an enterprise in which every
contribution must be valued and reimbursed at dissolution. The agreement and the
extraordinary, unrecompensed nature of the effort were essential limiting facts. Id. at 357.
Restitution measured the conferred benefit, with an additional ceiling
The original $23,000 represented the projected cost of Margrethe's own education. That was
a measure of the promised return under an unenforceable contract, not the value that should
automatically be restored in restitution. The court directed consideration of her financial
contributions to Charles's living and direct educational expenses. It required findings on
unjust enrichment, the benefit's value, and the amount due. Id.
The full opinion adds that recovery cannot exceed the benefit she anticipated under the
arrangement if that benefit was less than what she contributed. In other words, the court did
not permit restitution to give her a better financial return than the reciprocal benefit she had
expected. This cap is absent from the shortened remedy passage in the assigned excerpt and
must be restored from the complete opinion. Id.
The court also upheld the equitable authority to structure payment over time, with interest
and appropriate access to income records. Charles could pay a lump sum; periodic payment
was not a reason to invalidate relief designed to accommodate his financial circumstances.
That approval concerned remedial flexibility, not automatic reinstatement of the original
amount. Id. at 357–58.
Separate opinions
Froeb and Hathaway concurred in Corcoran's opinion. No separate concurrence or dissent is
reported. The discussions of dissenting opinions in other jurisdictions, including the Colorado
education case, are authorities examined by the court and not dissents in Pyeatte itself.

Study analysis and limits
The case has three independent routes whose outcomes must be separated. The contractual
route fails for indefiniteness; the property route fails because a degree is not divisible
property under the governing law; the restitution route succeeds in principle because the
particular benefit would otherwise be retained unjustly. Failure of one route does not
establish the success or failure of the others.
The remedy also resists a simple label. It focuses on financial contributions to the husband's
support and education, yet imposes a ceiling tied to the expected reciprocal benefit. It
permits equitable installments without validating the indefinite educational promise as a
contract. An answer that says only “the wife recovered $23,000 in restitution” misses the
reversal and the need for a new calculation.
This brief describes the 1982 Arizona decision. It does not adopt the casebook's historical
comparative statements about other states as present law, and it does not offer a current
marital-property survey. Those questions require separate research rather than
generalization from this opinion.
