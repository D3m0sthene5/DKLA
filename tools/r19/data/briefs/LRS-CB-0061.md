Parties
The corporate defendants, A. L. A. Schechter Poultry Corporation and Schechter Live Poultry Market,
operated slaughterhouse markets in Brooklyn. The individual defendants were the Schechter brothers involved
in those businesses. They challenged criminal convictions for violating the Live Poultry Code and conspiring
to violate it. The United States prosecuted the alleged violations, defended Congress’s authorization of the
Code, and separately sought restoration of the convictions that the court of appeals had overturned. 295 U.S. at
519–521, 527–528.
The industry associations that proposed the Code were not the prosecuting government or the defendants’
legislature. Their role in developing rules mattered because the Government defended reliance on their
practical expertise, while the Court examined whether presidential approval could give their proposals binding
legal force under the statute. Id. at 521–523, 537–539.
Material Facts
Section 3 of the National Industrial Recovery Act authorized the President to approve codes of fair competition
proposed by representative trade or industrial groups. He had to find that the proposing groups imposed no
inequitable membership restrictions, that the code would not promote monopolies or oppress small enterprises,
and that it would tend to advance the Act’s policy. He could add conditions, exceptions, and exemptions, or
prescribe a code when none had been approved. Violation of an approved code in a transaction in or affecting
interstate or foreign commerce was a misdemeanor punishable by a fine of up to $500; each additional day
could constitute another offense. Congress thus attached criminal consequences to rules whose content would
be supplied through the code-making process. Id. at 521–523 & n.4.
The statutory policy included industrial rehabilitation, increased purchasing power, reduced unemployment,
improved labor standards, fuller use of productive capacity, and elimination of unfair competitive practices.
These objectives encompassed much more than correcting a previously defined category of unlawful business
conduct. The President approved the metropolitan New York Live Poultry Code on April 13, 1934. It regulated
hours, wages, labor relations, staffing, trade practices, administration, records, and reports. Generally, it limited
work to forty hours per week and required fifty cents per hour, with stated exceptions. It also barred
employment of children under sixteen. Id. at 523–526, 534–536 & n.9.
The defendants bought poultry that had largely arrived from other states, transported it to their Brooklyn
premises, and slaughtered or sold it to local dealers and butchers supplying consumers. Once purchased for that
local disposition, the birds were not destined to resume interstate travel. One challenged trade rule required
purchases on a coop or half-coop basis rather than selection of particular birds. Ten counts concerned allowing
such individual selection. Other convictions concerned wages and hours, an unfit chicken, inspection and
licensing requirements, and reports. The constitutional questions therefore arose from concrete criminal
enforcement against local business practices. Id. at 520–521, 527–528, 542–543, 550.

Procedural History
A federal indictment contained sixty counts. The trial court dismissed twenty-seven, and the defendants were
acquitted on fourteen. They were convicted on eighteen counts charging Code violations and one conspiracy
count. The Second Circuit sustained the conspiracy conviction and sixteen substantive counts, but reversed the
two convictions concerning wages and hours. Both the defendants and the Government obtained Supreme
Court review of the adverse portions of that decision. Id. at 519–520 & n.2.
The defendants argued that Congress had unconstitutionally delegated legislative power, that the regulated
conduct exceeded federal commerce authority, and that certain provisions violated the Fifth Amendment. The
Court resolved the delegation and commerce grounds and found it unnecessary to reach the remaining
due-process objections. Its review concerned the validity of the legal rules supporting the convictions, rather
than an independent reassessment of whether the defendants’ practices were economically desirable. Id. at 519,
528–530, 550–551.
Issue
Did § 3 supply adequate legislative standards for presidential approval or prescription of enforceable industrial
codes, or did its broad recovery objectives and limited conditions transfer Congress’s essential lawmaking
function? Separately, could the Code’s wage, hour, and sales provisions constitutionally regulate these local
slaughterhouse transactions because the poultry had come from other states and the businesses’ costs and prices
could affect interstate trade? Id. at 529–531, 542–546.
Rule and Holding
Congress may establish policies and standards while entrusting subordinate rules and factual determinations to
administrative officers. It may not transfer the essential legislative function by giving the President
substantially unrestricted authority to create binding rules across trades and industries. Section 3’s general
recovery objectives, representative-industry requirement, and anti-monopoly restrictions did not adequately
define the permissible subjects and content of those codes. The resulting code-making authorization was an
unconstitutional delegation. Id. at 529–542.
The Code provisions also failed under the Court’s then-applied distinction between direct and indirect effects
on interstate commerce. The defendants’ purchases had ended the birds’ interstate movement, and the local
wages, hours, and sales practices affected interstate commerce only indirectly in the Court’s analysis. The
challenged provisions were invalid on both grounds. This states the 1935 holding, not a certification that its
commerce analysis supplies the governing test for a present-day dispute. Id. at 542–551.
Judgment
The convictions could not stand. The Court reversed the portion of the Second Circuit’s judgment sustaining
the convictions and left intact the reversal of the wage-and-hour convictions challenged by the Government. It
did not merely require the President to explain the existing Code more fully or send the defendants back for a
new determination under that Code. The legal authorization supporting the challenged criminal rules was
invalid. Id. at 519–520, 551.
Reasoning
The Court acknowledged the emergency that prompted the Act but held that economic distress could not
enlarge constitutionally assigned powers. Legislative flexibility remained necessary: Congress could not itself
resolve every detail of complicated administration. The relevant inquiry was whether Congress had performed
its own function by determining the governing policy and standards before leaving implementation to others.
The existence of administrative discretion was therefore the beginning of the analysis, not a sufficient reason to
invalidate a statute. Id. at 528–530.

The term fair competition did not confine the delegation to a sufficiently identified body of existing law.
Common-law unfair competition primarily concerned deceptive appropriation of another trader’s business or
goods, with extensions to related misconduct. The recovery codes reached a much wider field, including
wages, hours, and affirmative industrial planning. The Government itself did not contend that only practices
already unlawful could be prohibited. Reading the phrase as a broad label for industrial betterment left the
central legislative choices to the code makers. Id. at 531–536.
The Federal Trade Commission comparison did not save the statute. Under the FTC Act, an expert agency
addressed particular allegedly unfair methods through complaints, evidence, findings, and judicial review.
Those arrangements linked administrative judgment to a legal standard and concrete controversies. The
recovery scheme both dispensed with an analogous constrained process and authorized a substantially broader
kind of rulemaking. The Court’s comparison examined the combination of substantive scope and institutional
safeguards; it did not announce that adjudicatory procedure was invariably necessary for every valid
delegation. Id. at 532–534.
Industrial expertise also supplied no independent constitutional authority. Trade associations could offer
assistance, but their familiarity with business conditions did not entitle them to enact whatever rules would
benefit their industries. The Code’s penal force ultimately depended on presidential action, so the Court
examined the constraints on that action. A requirement that an association be representative concerned who
proposed a code, rather than what the code could command. Limits on monopolies and discrimination against
small firms left an enormous range of wage, production, marketing, and other policy choices untouched. Id. at
537–538.
The required presidential finding that a code would advance statutory policy provided little additional
confinement. The declared aims were broad enough that the finding amounted to a judgment that an entire
regulatory program would promote recovery. The President could modify proposed codes, supply exceptions,
or prescribe a code himself. Reports from assisting officials did not bind him. The statute accordingly lacked a
workable legislative standard for distinguishing an authorized policy choice from one outside the delegation
across the many industries it covered. Id. at 538–542.
Earlier administrative cases involved more bounded authority. Congress had established the framework for
common-carrier regulation; agencies applied it to evidence concerning transportation. Radio licensing involved
a limited resource and standards related to the service to be supplied. In Hampton, Congress supplied a
tariff-adjustment plan linked to differences in production costs. The Court preserved those decisions and
explained that their defined subjects and standards did not support the much broader recovery-code authority.
Id. at 539–541.
The commerce ground independently examined what the defendants did with these particular birds. Their prior
interstate shipment did not keep all subsequent transactions permanently interstate. At the Brooklyn
slaughterhouses the poultry was held for local sale and consumption, with no intended onward interstate
movement. Cases involving temporary stops within a continuing interstate stream therefore did not describe
this record. Id. at 542–544.
Nor did ordinary effects of local costs on prices establish the necessary direct relationship under the Court’s
approach. The Government argued that lower wages and longer hours lowered operating costs, depressed
poultry prices, and damaged the interstate price structure. The Court reasoned that this chain would also allow
control of rent, advertising, staffing, and nearly every cost of local business. It distinguished intrastate conduct
that directly obstructed interstate transportation or deliberately restrained interstate trade. These defendants had
been prosecuted for Code violations, not for such a separate interstate restraint. Id. at 544–550.

The same analysis covered selective chicken purchases, the unfit bird, and the local reporting, licensing, and
inspection requirements. The Court did not determine that these practices were harmless or that states could not
regulate them. It concluded that their indirect relationship to interstate trade did not sustain the particular
federal Code. Because either principal constitutional ground defeated the convictions, it left the Fifth
Amendment objections unresolved. Id. at 550–551.
Separate Opinions
Justice Cardozo, joined by Justice Stone, agreed with the result and distinguished this statute from the narrower
delegation he had defended in his Panama Refining dissent. In that earlier setting, the Executive’s discretion
concerned the occasion for taking an identified kind of action. Here the authority extended to finding industrial
problems and choosing a broad range of regulatory means to solve them. Cardozo considered that breadth
incompatible with the absence of a definite or even approximate standard. Id. at 551–553 (Cardozo, J.,
concurring).
He nevertheless accepted delegation to identify and prohibit unfair business practices under established
standards, including consultation with industry participants. The constitutional difficulty arose when a code
could prescribe whatever affirmative improvements its authors thought desirable for industrial prosperity.
Selective buying illustrated the difference: the Code prohibited an established practice without tying the
prohibition to a preexisting ethical or commercial standard. Even a narrowing construction of the statute would
leave the Code pervasively beyond that narrower authorization; its permissible and impermissible parts could
not be separated without destroying the arrangement. Id. at 552–554.
Cardozo also agreed on the commerce issue. Remote economic repercussions did not make these local
transactions national in character, and attention to degree remained necessary. Because wages and hours were
integral to the statutory code plan, removing those provisions would destroy the regulatory structure rather than
preserve a workable remainder. His concurrence therefore supplied separate discussion of both the scope of
delegated choice and the inseparability of the Code’s components. No Justice dissented. Id. at 554–555.
