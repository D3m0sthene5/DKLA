# Fibrosa Spolka Akcyjna v. Fairbairn Lawson Combe Barbour, Ltd.
[1943] A.C. 32 (H.L., decided June 15, 1942) | Contracts

## Decision and judicial lineup
The House of Lords unanimously allowed a Polish buyer's appeal to recover a £1,000 advance on machinery that an English seller could no longer lawfully deliver after war began and Germany occupied the destination port. The contract was frustrated, but that discharge did not prevent restitution where the buyer had received none of the performance for which the advance formed part of the price. The rule in Chandler v. Webster preventing recovery merely because payment preceded frustration could not govern this total failure of consideration. This was restitution, rather than an award of damages for the seller's breach. Lord Simon L.C. and Lords Atkin, Russell of Killowen, Macmillan, Wright, Roche, and Porter each delivered a speech supporting the result; Macmillan read Russell's speech. (39–40, 50–57, 61, 73, 76, 84.)

## Contract, interruption, and litigation
On July 12, 1939, Fairbairn agreed to supply Fibrosa with flax-hackling machinery for a lump price of £4,800, c.i.f. Gdynia. One third, £1,600, was payable with the order and the £3,200 balance against shipping documents. Delivery was contemplated within three to four months after final details, and the price included a skilled installer. The machinery was intended for Fibrosa's factory at Vilna, although the written contract did not specify that erection location. Fibrosa paid £1,000 on July 18, rather than the entire first installment. Clause 7 allowed a reasonable extension for delay beyond the seller's control, including war; other terms dealt with dispatch and storage. (33–36, 40–41.)

Germany invaded Poland on September 1; Britain entered the war on September 3; and Gdynia was occupied on September 23. Fibrosa's agents requested repayment on September 7. The seller refused the next day, pointing to work already performed and suggesting reconsideration after the war. The May 1, 1940 writ asserted contractual relief, including damages or specific performance, and alternatively repayment of the advance with interest. Tucker J. dismissed the action on March 7, 1941, and the Court of Appeal affirmed on May 15. The lower courts treated Chandler as preventing restitution once a valid contract was subsequently frustrated. (32–39.)

By the hearing in the House, Vilna itself was enemy territory. A Board of Trade license obtained on April 17, 1942 permitted the appeal to proceed. The House expressly preserved whatever statutory restrictions applied to payment to an enemy; its determination of the buyer's legal entitlement did not itself displace those restrictions. (35 n. 2, 39–40.)

## Why the machinery contract was discharged
The buyer first sought to avoid frustration by relying on the express war-delay clause. That clause addressed a reasonable extension, however, rather than indefinite suspension of performance throughout a war. Occupation of Gdynia made the contemplated British shipment to that destination unlawful. Manufacturing the machinery at Leeds did not satisfy an agreement for shipment c.i.f. Gdynia, and the court would not rewrite its destination to preserve performance. The supervening event therefore discharged further performance despite the limited delay provision. The speeches distinguished this determination from treating the contract as void when made. (Simon, 40–41; Atkin, 50; Roche, 73; Porter, 83.)

## Restitution after a valid contract has been frustrated
The seller argued that the advance was paid pursuant to a valid promise, that work and expense had already been incurred, and that the settled frustration rule left earlier payments where they stood. The central answer was that money paid for future performance can become repayable when the whole relevant performance fails. The buyer's inability to claim contractual damages for an excused failure did not extinguish the distinct claim for money had and received. Neither fault in the recipient nor invalidity of the original contract was essential. (Simon, 41–49; Atkin, 50–55; Russell, 55–57; Wright, 61–68; Porter, 81–84.)

Simon distinguished the promise that supplies consideration for forming a contract from the performance whose absence supports repayment. Fibrosa received neither machines nor shipping documents. The advance was part of the price for that performance, rather than an independently final payment for receiving the seller's promise. Work undertaken in the seller's own factory did not supply the buyer with the promised benefit. Atkin reached the same result while questioning whether two different meanings of consideration were necessary: the promised delivery had failed, even though the promise originally supported a valid exchange. (48, 53–55.)

The whole relevant consideration had to fail. The speeches did not authorize a general proportional refund whenever a disappointed buyer received less than expected. A wholly failed, severable part could support repayment of the sum attributable to that part, but partial performance of an entire exchange ordinarily defeated this particular common-law restitution claim. Separately earned installments or payment for completed work could remain due. Atkin's illustration involving payment for an animal and earlier weekly upkeep explained why accrued obligations must be examined individually rather than erased wholesale. (Simon, 42–43; Atkin, 50–55; Russell, 56–57; Wright, 65–68; Porter, 77–78.)

Contractual allocation also mattered. A payment made finally for a chance or promise, or an advance governed by a contrary established custom, need not be refundable. Prepaid freight was the prominent exception. Roche emphasized final versus provisional payment and reserved the possibility that particular coronation-case contracts actually stipulated final payments. Porter likewise asked what this agreement made the £1,000 payment buy. Here it bought part of the lump machinery price; goods remained at the seller's risk until the shipping documents were presented and accepted. No such performance had occurred. The included installer service did not change Porter's conclusion that shipment and documents would have substantially performed the sale, subject to damages if the installer were not supplied. (Roche, 74–76; Porter, 79–83.)

## The speeches and the limits of loss sharing
Russell framed a narrow exception for total failure of consideration rather than a comprehensive redistribution of losses following frustration. Macmillan considered the historical and comparative law of restitution but rested the English result on its own established money-recovery doctrine. Wright explained restitution as a legal obligation distinct from contract and tort: it did not depend on inventing a real promise to repay, and the disappearance of old pleading forms did not eliminate the substantive obligation. His discussion of unjust enrichment remained tied to recognized conditions for restitution, rather than granting an unrestricted power to rearrange every unfortunate bargain. (55–68.)

All these routes confronted the seller's reliance expense. Repayment could leave a manufacturer bearing substantial sunk costs, even when it had done nothing wrong and had almost completed goods useful only to this buyer. The common law then furnished no general deduction of such costs from the buyer's recovery. Simon and Wright pointed to legislation as the route to broader adjustment; Porter rejected judicial creation of an unspecified equitable fraction. Roche added that the seller here said its partly completed machines could be realized without loss, while recognizing that other cases might be harsher. The result therefore should not be recast as a judicial finding that every seller in every frustrated contract deserves no expense allowance. (49–50, 54–55, 72, 76, 77–79.)

## Exact result and the assigned problem
The report ends with the appeal allowed. The £1,000 advance was recoverable on total failure of consideration, subject to the preserved enemy-payment restrictions. It does not print a separate quantified interest or costs order after that disposition. The contractual breach theory failed with the rejection of the contention that performance remained enforceable. (39–41, 84.)

The assigned account at printed page 1152 uses this case to contrast full restitution with protection of the seller's reliance interest. Its £800 expense figure is expressly an assumption for classroom discussion, not a judicial finding. The reprise at 1157 asks why parties require prepayments and compares proposals for sharing unavoidable loss. Those questions illuminate the speeches' distinction between an advance on future performance and a genuinely final payment. The assignment also discusses the later Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943 and its discretionary expense provision. That statutory treatment is subsequent to this 1942 judgment and is not a rule the House applied in this appeal.
