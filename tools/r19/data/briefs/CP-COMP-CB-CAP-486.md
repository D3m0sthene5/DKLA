# Grimes v. Miller

**429 F. Supp. 1350 (M.D.N.C. 1977)** — Middle District of North Carolina, Salisbury Division, three-judge court; April 12, 1977, heard February 24; C-76-220-S. Circuit Judge Craven wrote for a court also comprising Chief District Judge Gordon and District Judge Ward. Assigned: CivPro physical PDF page 612, printed 1209, Note 1.

## Constitutional challenge and result

After forty-nine days of civil imprisonment and release under an insolvent's oath procedure, Grimes brought a section 1983 action challenging North Carolina body execution. The court **upheld postjudgment body execution only with additional protective construction**, invalidated the twenty-day notice requirement insofar as it barred preliminary release, and required notice implementing a statutory counsel right. It dismissed the prejudgment challenge for lack of standing, denied class certification, injunctive relief, damages against Miller, and section 1988 fees, and directed each side to bear its costs. This was a federal constitutional action, not an appeal reversing the underlying conversion judgment. (1352–1359.)

## Underlying tort judgment and imprisonment

Professional stockcar racer Patricia Miller delivered her 1972 Pinto to Grimes in 1973 for rebuilding, engine replacement, and racing improvements. He instead stripped its parts, leaving the frame and body shell when she recovered it in May 1974. Her state complaint alleged intentional fraud and malicious conversion, plus a separate allegation that he transferred an $18,000 Petty-built dragster to his wife to defeat creditors. (1352–1353.)

The Grimes defendants were served and failed to answer. After default, Judge McConnell found intentional, willful, malicious, fraudulent conversion; the car's value had fallen from $6,500 to $150. He awarded **$6,350 loss and $5,000 punitive damages** and authorized execution against Grimes's person if property execution failed. He made **no findings about the alleged fraudulent asset transfer**. The wife's case was dismissed without prejudice. (1353.)

Property execution returned unsatisfied; body-execution writs issued January 15 and May 23, 1975. Grimes temporarily fled to Florida, either to avoid bad-check arrest or civil arrest—the court did not resolve which. Arrest followed July 30. His wife initially failed to find counsel; the federal court could not determine whether inability to pay caused that delay. Counsel eventually accepted the matter without a fee, unsuccessfully sought habeas relief, then petitioned under the insolvent's oath provisions. After twenty days' creditor notice and examination, the clerk found no assets beyond $50 and released Grimes September 17. He had spent forty-nine days in jail. (1353.)

He then sued Miller, the clerk Hugh Shepherd, and Sheriff Paul McCrary under section 1983, requesting personal and class relief and $100 from Miller. The opinion did not award that sum. (1352–1353, 1359.)

## Standing, mootness, and classes

Grimes lacked standing to challenge prejudgment arrest under Article 34. His own arrest occurred after judgment, and he identified no threatened prejudgment proceeding against him. The court refused an advisory ruling. He did have standing to challenge postjudgment section 1-311: other outstanding debts and a reasonable risk of recurrence made release insufficient to moot the claim. Forty-nine-day confinement could end before federal adjudication, so the court treated the injury as capable of repetition yet evading review. It distinguished Juidice's circumstances. (1354.)

It declined plaintiff and defendant classes. He could not represent prejudgment detainees and, with his own postjudgment claim still live, no class was necessary to preserve adjudication. Note 2 records rejection of abstention: no pending state proceeding or state-law ambiguity warranted deferral. Note 1 explains that section 1-311 incorporates Article 34's case categories for postjudgment execution. (1353–1355 & nn. 1–2.)

## Substantive challenges rejected within the saved scheme

The court characterized the legislative objective as compelling disclosure and preventing concealment of assets by debtors adjudged guilty of fraud or similar wrongs, rather than incarcerating ordinary indigent debtors for inability to pay. It relied on Carter v. Lynch and found no substantive due-process or equal-protection violation under its construction. Rich debtors concealing assets could also remain jailed. It likewise rejected the asserted Eighth Amendment violation. These conclusions must be read together with the procedural safeguards that follow, not as unrestricted approval of imprisonment upon any unpaid tort judgment. (1355–1356.)

## Initial execution requires additional probable-cause findings

Findings of the original tort alone did not provide a constitutionally sufficient basis for civil detention. Otherwise confinement would punish the wrong without criminal procedural protections. Construing section 1-311 to preserve its civil purpose, the court required facts establishing probable cause that the debtor **was about to flee to avoid creditors, had concealed or diverted assets fraudulently, or would do so unless immediately detained**. Findings concerning the original wrong remained necessary; further findings connected detention to preserving recovery. (1355–1356, 1358.)

The court acknowledged that this might innovate upon state precedent and that a federal court could not authoritatively construe state law. Its conclusion predicted a saving construction by North Carolina's highest court. It did not falsely describe these protections as explicit findings already made in Grimes's original judgment. (1356–1358.)

## Twenty-day detention barrier invalid; petition starts the clock

Section 23-32 required twenty days' creditor notice before an imprisoned debtor could obtain a discharge proceeding, with possible further delay serving an out-of-state creditor. Even someone obviously without assets could remain confined, allowing vengeance rather than disclosure to drive detention. The court held that requirement unconstitutional insofar as it barred preliminary release. It permitted reasonable notice under judicial discretion while protecting a stronger liberty interest. (1356–1357 & n. 3.)

The key timing rule was **an actual hearing within three days of the debtor's petition for release, or release**, whether or not the creditor received notice. It was not a blanket three-day period measured from arrest. Sheriff or service failures could not extend detention beyond that protection. Continued confinement required examination and findings supporting actual concealment. (1357–1358.)

Preliminary release was distinguished from final statutory discharge. The creditor could continue investigating and seek a later determination of fraudulent concealment; an adverse finding could lead to later imprisonment until full disclosure. The court predicted that section 23-38's bar against renewed arrest after discharge would not equate the preliminary seventy-two-hour release with final discharge. This preserved further adjudication, not automatic cancellation of the debt or absolute immunity from later execution. (1357–1358.)

## Counsel notice and monetary relief

State section 7A-451(a)(7) supplied counsel to indigent persons in civil arrest and bail proceedings. The court agreed the state had failed to provide Grimes counsel but placed the duty at the first suggestion of indigency. Miller's complaint instead suggested concealed valuable property; attendance at trial might have presented indigency earlier. Because legislation supplied the right, the court **did not decide an independent constitutional right to counsel** for these proceedings. (1358.)

The warrant had to advise the debtor of counsel entitlement if indigent, the opportunity to petition for preliminary release, and a chance within seventy-two hours of petition to present indigency for both purposes. A judge should appoint counsel immediately on a finding of inability to pay, or provisionally before arrest if indigency was suggested then. The legislative entitlement survived preliminary release while concealment and indigency remained unresolved; apparent indigency warranted a provisional appointment. (1358–1359.)

Miller acted in good faith under the scheme and could not reasonably have anticipated the new construction. Damages against her were denied. The court requested a judgment embodying ten specific items: prejudgment challenge dismissed; money relief denied; postjudgment scheme constitutional with required findings; notice barrier invalid; counsel notice required; plaintiff and defendant classes denied; injunction denied; own costs; section 1988 fees denied. No separate writing or appendix appears. (1359.)

## Assignment and sources

The textbook correctly identifies the probable-cause safeguards but says the hearing occurs within three days **of arrest**. The actual rule runs from **petition for release**. The note also compresses the distinction between preliminary release and final discharge and omits the separate notice-to-counsel ruling. The brief uses the actual procedural clock and conditional result. The supplied note reports subsequent Supreme Court affirmance at 434 U.S. 978; that later summary stage is distinguished from this April district-court decision and is not treated as a new explanatory opinion. (Assigned 612/1209; actual 1357–1359.)
