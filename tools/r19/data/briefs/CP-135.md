Parties
The respondents were Black employees of Pullman-Standard's railway-car manufacturing plant in Bessemer,
Alabama. They challenged employment practices and the departmental seniority arrangement under federal
civil-rights law. Pullman-Standard and the United Steelworkers of America, with its local union, were the
petitioners defending the system. A different union, the International Association of Machinists, represented
other departments; its role in the creation of the plant's bargaining structure became important evidence
concerning the disputed system's origins. The Supreme Court's review concerned the seniority system, rather
than every job-assignment and promotion claim in the litigation. 456 U.S. at 275–279 & nn.1–5.
Material Facts
Pullman-Standard had maintained racially discriminatory job assignments before Title VII became effective.
The challenged seniority rules ordinarily measured an employee's accumulated service within a department. An
employee transferring to another department generally lost the seniority accumulated in the old department.
The rules therefore discouraged movement, and the employees contended that they preserved disadvantages
created when Black workers were assigned to less desirable jobs. The presence of an adverse racial effect and
the reason the seniority arrangement was adopted or maintained were distinct questions under the substantive
rule the Court applied. Id. at 277–280 & nn.5–7.
The departmental structure developed through the plant's unionization history. The Steelworkers organized a
workforce including Black and white employees; the Machinists represented other departments. By 1954 the
plant had 28 departments, 26 represented by the Steelworkers and two by the Machinists. The seniority
arrangement had shifted between departmental and occupational bases before returning to a departmental base
in 1954. Exceptions permitted certain transfers, and a 1972 agreement with the Department of Labor allowed
specified Black employees to transfer without losing seniority. These details mattered because the suit required
examination of how the particular system originated and operated, rather than an assumption that every
seniority rule had the same history. Id. at 277–279 & nn.3–7.
The district court examined whether transfers were discouraged equally, whether the departmental structure
was rational and consistent with industry practice, whether the system originated in racial discrimination, and
whether it was negotiated and maintained free of an unlawful purpose. It found no discriminatory intent. The
employees challenged that finding by pointing to unequal job desirability, the division of departments along
racial lines, and the Machinists' role. The Supreme Court emphasized that the four considerations were useful
evidentiary inquiries, not an exhaustive statutory checklist. Id. at 279–284 & n.8.

Procedural History
The employees filed suit in 1971. By the time of Supreme Court review, the litigation had involved three trials
and two appellate reviews. After the Court's intervening Teamsters decision clarified the treatment of seniority
systems under Title VII, the district court considered the system's bona fides and found that employment
differences produced by it did not result from an intention to discriminate. It therefore held the system
protected by § 703(h). Id. at 275–280 & n.2.
The Fifth Circuit reversed. It found defects in the district court's treatment of the racial effects and historical
origins of the arrangement, including its failure to consider the relevance of the Machinists' motives. The court
of appeals stated that it was firmly convinced a mistake had occurred, but also invoked circuit precedent
treating discrimination as an ultimate fact that could be determined independently on appeal. It then concluded
that discriminatory purpose had been established. Id. at 280–285.
The Supreme Court granted review on the standard of appellate review and the legal criteria governing the
seniority determination. It declined to review separate rulings that the company had discriminated in job
assignments and selection of foremen. Thus the appeal did not present the company's entire record of
compliance with Title VII. The Court reversed the Fifth Circuit's judgment in the seniority controversy and
remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion. Id. at 275–277 & n.1, 293.
Issue
May a court of appeals independently decide whether a seniority system was adopted or maintained with
discriminatory intent because intent is the ultimate fact determining liability? When a trial court has omitted
findings because it treated relevant evidence as legally irrelevant, may the appellate court make those findings
itself, or must it ordinarily remand? Id. at 285–293.
Rule and Holding
Rule 52(a)'s clearly erroneous standard applies to findings of discriminatory intent under § 703(h). The Rule
contains no general exception for ultimate facts. In this statutory setting intent means actual motive, a factual
question for the trial court, even though its resolution determines legal liability. A reviewing court may correct
an erroneous legal standard, but it may not use the importance of the factual question to conduct unrestricted
factfinding. Id. at 285–290.
When an erroneous legal view has caused the district court to omit material findings, the usual remedy is a
remand for findings under the correct standard. Appellate resolution without remand is appropriate when the
record permits only one resolution of the factual issue. The Fifth Circuit's treatment did not satisfy these limits:
it appeared to make an independent determination and supplied findings concerning the Machinists and the
Steelworkers that the district court had not made. Id. at 290–293.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Fifth Circuit and remanded the cases to that court for further proceedings. It
did not itself determine that the seniority system was lawful, reinstate every district-court finding as
substantively correct, or reject the employees' other discrimination claims. The judgment required the appellate
court to respect the proper review standard and the district court's factfinding role. Id. at 293 & n.1.

Reasoning
The Court began with the relationship between the substantive statute and the procedural standard. Under its
construction of § 703(h), a seniority system could produce racially disparate consequences without being
unlawful if those consequences did not result from a discriminatory purpose. The task was therefore to find
actual motive. Adverse impact could supply circumstantial evidence of that motive, but it could not
automatically replace the required finding. This characterization made the intent inquiry factual in the specific
controversy before the Court. Id. at 277–279, 287–290.
Rule 52(a) broadly protects trial findings from reversal unless clearly erroneous. Nothing in its language
creates one standard for subsidiary historical details and another for the fact that ultimately determines liability.
Many factual questions are outcome-determinative. Allowing independent review whenever a fact was labeled
ultimate would remove a substantial part of the Rule's operation. The Court drew support from decisions
treating motive, purpose, and the intention behind a transfer as factual matters. Id. at 285–289 & n.16.
The distinction between fact and law nevertheless remained important. An appellate court need not defer to a
district court's mistaken understanding of what the statute requires or what evidence is legally relevant. The
Court acknowledged the difficulty of separating factual questions, legal conclusions, and mixed questions in
other contexts. It expressly declined to settle the general standard for mixed questions. Its holding was that
actual discriminatory motive under this seniority provision was a pure fact question. Id. at 287–290 &
nn.16–19.
The employees argued that the Fifth Circuit had in fact applied clear-error review. It had quoted the familiar
formulation requiring a definite and firm conviction that a mistake had occurred. The majority found that
insufficient in light of the opinion as a whole. The appellate court had not expressly applied that standard
through its detailed disagreements with the district court, and its reference to independent review of ultimate
facts suggested a different method. The objection concerned the actual allocation of decisional responsibility,
rather than the absence of a ritual phrase. Id. at 290–291 & n.21.
The more fundamental problem concerned missing findings. The Fifth Circuit believed that the district court
had failed to give the Machinists' role proper consideration. That could be a valid legal reason to set aside a
finding. But recognizing a legal error did not ordinarily authorize the appellate court to decide the omitted
factual issue for itself. The appellate court needed to return the matter for findings unless only one conclusion
was possible on the record. Id. at 291–293.
The Fifth Circuit instead determined the Machinists' motives, found acquiescence by the Steelworkers, and
used that assessment to invalidate the system. The Supreme Court explained that even a finding of
discriminatory motive by the Machinists could not simply be imputed to the Steelworkers. It was relevant
insofar as it illuminated the purpose of the Steelworkers or the company in creating and maintaining their
arrangement. Acquiescence and discriminatory purpose were not automatically equivalent. This made careful
findings about the respective actors essential. Id. at 292–293 & n.23.
The opinion therefore preserves two forms of appellate correction while limiting a third. A court of appeals
may correct the governing law, and it may set aside factual findings that are clearly erroneous. It ordinarily
may not replace missing trial findings with its own assessment just because the error has opened the judgment
to review. The remand requirement gives effect to the trial court's assigned institutional function without
insulating its work from review. Id. at 287, 291–293.

Separate Opinions
Justice Stevens joined the Court except insofar as its preliminary statements about the burden in every
seniority-system challenge conflicted with his separately expressed views in American Tobacco Co. v.
Patterson. His brief statement does not supply a competing account of Rule 52(a) or endorse the Fifth Circuit's
independent ultimate-fact doctrine. Id. at 293 (Stevens, J., concurring in part).
Justice Marshall's Part I renewed his disagreement with the requirement that plaintiffs demonstrate
discriminatory purpose before challenging a seniority system perpetuating past discrimination. He considered
that burden inconsistent with Title VII's objectives and text. Justice Blackmun did not join that part. Their
agreement in the remaining dissent concerned the application of appellate-review principles to this record. Id.
at 293–295 (Marshall, J., dissenting).
Marshall accepted that Rule 52(a) governed the factual intent inquiry but argued that the Fifth Circuit had
respected it. The court had identified legal errors, expressly found the trial court's conclusion mistaken, and
concluded that the relevant evidence permitted only one result. In his view, the placement of the clear-error
statement late in the opinion did not negate its meaning. He treated the appellate court's conclusion as an
application of the majority's own one-permissible-result exception. Id. at 295–301.
The dissent also stressed that the trial determination rested overwhelmingly on documents rather than witness
credibility. It pointed to the racial organization of departments, disparities in job desirability, and historical
evidence of the unions' roles as compelling proof. Its position was not that documentary findings never receive
clear-error protection: a footnote expressly rejected that proposition. Rather, the nature of this record supported
appellate correction without another trial. Id. at 301–304 & nn.6–9.
