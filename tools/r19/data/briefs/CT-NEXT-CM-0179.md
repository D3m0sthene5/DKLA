# In re Hitz Restaurant Group
616 B.R. 374 (Bankr. N.D. Ill. 2020), Bankruptcy No. 20 B 05012; official opinion issued June 3, 2020 | Contracts

## Facts and bankruptcy setting
Hitz operated a restaurant in leased premises and filed Chapter 11 on February 24, 2020. The lease made rent due on the first of each month. February rent was therefore a prepetition obligation, while March 1 rent and later monthly installments arose after the order for relief. Hitz paid no postpetition rent. Kass Management Services sought enforcement of the lease obligations under 11 U.S.C. § 365(d)(3) and modification of the automatic stay under § 362(d)(1). It requested immediate payment, continued timely performance, or authority to recover the premises. (Official PDF pp. 2–3, 8.)

The lease excused either party while performance was prevented, delayed, retarded, or hindered by specified events, including governmental action and orders. It also stated that lack of money did not constitute force majeure. Illinois Executive Order 2020-7, effective March 16 at 9 p.m., prohibited on-premises consumption of food and beverages while allowing and encouraging takeout, delivery, and curbside pickup. The restrictions were extended. Hitz contended that they excused its rent obligations. It separately asserted that Kass's failure to repair the HVAC system excused payment. (PDF pp. 3–4, 8.)

## Governing obligations and issues
Section 365(d)(3) requires timely performance of obligations arising after the order for relief under an unexpired nonresidential real-property lease until assumption or rejection. The court distinguished those obligations from ordinary § 503(b)(1) administrative expenses: the lessor need not prove that each payment is an actual and necessary estate-preservation cost, and timely rent is required even in administrative insolvency. The statute directed the court to the obligations defined by the lease, including its applicable excuse provisions. (PDF pp. 2–3.)

The contract questions were whether the governmental order triggered this particular force-majeure clause and, if so, whether it excused all rent or only part. Cassling applied Illinois contract interpretation, under which the specified event must proximately cause nonperformance. The court treated the express clause as superseding common-law impossibility, rather than deciding a freestanding frustration claim. (PDF pp. 4–5.)

## Holding and rejected objections
The clause unambiguously applied in part. The executive order was governmental action and an order within its terms; prohibiting dine-in operations hindered Hitz's business and proximately caused an inability to generate revenue and pay rent, at least to a degree. That causal finding concerned the restrictions on operation, rather than a general inability to pay whenever business declined. (PDF p. 5.)

Kass argued that banks and post offices remained open, so Hitz could still write and mail a check. The court rejected that argument as unresponsive to the claimed hindrance and unsupported by the clause's language. Kass also invoked the lack-of-money exclusion. Hitz's position, which the court accepted in part, was that the governmental order, rather than an independent shortage of funds, caused the inability to pay. If the general money exclusion and specific governmental-event provision conflicted, the specific provision governed under Illinois interpretive principles. (PDF pp. 5–6 & n. 2.)

Finally, Kass argued that Hitz should have obtained a Small Business Administration loan. No lease term or cited authority required borrowing as a condition of invoking the governmental-order clause. The court would not add that obligation. (PDF p. 6.)

## Why the relief was partial and provisional
March rent had become fully due on March 1, before the March 16 order. The later force-majeure event did not excuse that already-due installment. April, May, and June were different, but the restrictions did not prohibit all restaurant operations. Hitz could still use its kitchen for takeout and delivery. Its continuing rent obligation was therefore reduced in proportion to its reduced ability to generate revenue caused by the order, rather than eliminated altogether. (PDF pp. 4, 6–7.)

Neither party supplied substantial evidence to calculate the reduction. Hitz estimated that the dining room and bar, comprising seventy-five percent of the restaurant's floor area, were unusable, while the kitchen comprised the remaining twenty-five percent and could serve permitted operations. Before an evidentiary hearing fixing the final amount, the court treated that estimate as an admission that at least twenty-five percent of rent remained due for those months. It did not make a final evidentiary finding that square footage always equals revenue, nor announce a seventy-five-percent reduction for every restaurant. Later obligations could increase as restrictions eased. Original note 3 discusses the state's reopening phases and Chicago's different timetable, including impending limited outdoor dining; expiration of one order's stated duration did not mean all relevant restrictions had vanished. (PDF pp. 7–8 & n. 3.)

## Other defense and operative directions
The court declined to adjudicate the HVAC-repair defense in this summary stay proceeding. Such hearings focus on adequate protection, equity, necessity to reorganization, and other cause for relief, rather than a full trial of largely unrelated non-core state-law counterclaims. The repair dispute belonged in state court. That procedural ruling was not a determination that the alleged repair failure never occurred or that no substantive state-law remedy existed. (PDF p. 8.)

Because Hitz had paid nothing, it had not adequately protected Kass. The court nevertheless allowed a short opportunity to comply with its first announced interpretation. By June 16, Hitz had to pay full March base rent of $10,561.25, plus full March common-area charges and taxes, and $2,640.31 per month for April, May, and June, plus twenty-five percent of the corresponding charges and taxes. The specified base-rent total was $18,482.18. Failure to pay would result in an order finding cause to lift the stay; the opinion did not immediately lift it or order eviction. A status hearing was set for June 23 at 10 a.m. (PDF pp. 8–9.)

## Source scope and limits
Cassling was the sole bankruptcy judge; there are no separate writings or panel votes. The complete official nine-page file, including its transmittal sheet and all three judicial notes, was read. The court's official transmittal and posting date is June 3, 2020; the signature-date line in that preserved file is blank.
