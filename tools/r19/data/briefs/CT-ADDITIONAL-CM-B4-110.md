# Tsakiroglou & Co. Ltd. v. Noblee Thorl G.m.b.H.
[1962] A.C. 93 (H.L., decided March 28, 1961) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
The House of Lords unanimously dismissed the sellers’ appeal. Viscount Simonds and Lords Reid, Radcliffe, Hodson, and Guest each delivered a speech. The dismissal preserved the buyers’ £5,625 damages award. The reproduced formal order affirmed the Court of Appeal, directed that the £5,625 deposited in court be paid to the buyers, and required the sellers to pay appellate costs. The report year is 1962; the decision date is March 28, 1961. (110, 116, 119, 125, 130, 135; reproduced formal order.)

## Contract, disruption, and arbitration
On October 4, 1956 the sellers agreed to sell approximately 300 tons of Sudanese groundnuts in shell at £50 per 1,000 kilos, including bags, c.i.f. Hamburg, for shipment in November or December. Ninety-five percent of the provisional invoice was payable against documents, with the balance after analysis and final invoice. The parties incorporated the Oil Seed Association’s form, including London arbitration. Although its form referred to shipment from a West African port, the case stated found that the parties meant Port Sudan, the suitable export port for these nuts. The contract did not expressly prescribe passage through Suez. (94–97, 110–111.)

The sellers acquired the goods and held them in Port Sudan warehouses from November 1. They booked space on vessels scheduled to call during the shipment period, but the company canceled those bookings November 4. The Suez Canal had been blocked November 2 and remained closed until April 1957. A Cape of Good Hope voyage remained available during the contracted shipment period. It was approximately 11,137 miles instead of 4,386. Freight, originally about £7 10s. per ton, carried a 25-percent surcharge from November 10 and a 100-percent surcharge from December 13. The sellers shipped no goods and treated the contract as canceled. (96–97, 110–111.)

The arbitrator awarded £5,625 damages and £79 15s. award costs. The association appeal board upheld the award and stated a special case. It found that transport via the Cape was possible and that performance through that route was not commercially or fundamentally different. Diplock J. upheld the award; the Court of Appeal affirmed. The sellers appealed to the House of Lords, asserting an implied route obligation, frustration, and the contract’s force-majeure exception. The proceeding therefore reviewed an existing award on a case stated; it was not a new damages trial. (94–97, 110.)

## Parties’ arguments and the contractual exception
The sellers argued that the ordinary and contemplated route at formation defined their obligation: a Cape shipment that would have departed from that route before closure could not become mandatory after closure. They maintained that the essential basis of the bargain had disappeared and challenged the board’s general conclusion as a legal matter, not a binding factual finding. The buyers responded that the sellers’ c.i.f. obligations included obtaining reasonable carriage and appropriate documents, with the route judged when shipment was due. Increased freight alone did not change the contract’s essential character. (97–109.)

Clause 6 extended shipment or delivery for up to two months when specified events or force majeure prevented performance and canceled the contract if the impediment then continued. The House held that it did not excuse these sellers. The canal closure did not prevent bringing the goods to the shipment port and loading them for their contractual destination through an available route. It prevented use of Suez, rather than shipment within this clause’s relevant meaning. The speeches agreed on the clause’s inapplicability; they did not declare every force-majeure clause ineffective whenever any physical route can be imagined. (111–112, 119, 125, 130, 135.)

## Simonds: contract construction and fundamental change
Simonds declined to imply either an exclusive Suez term or an unqualified obligation to use the route customary at formation. Both assumptions would treat buyers as agreeing to forgo the goods if Suez became unavailable, despite no contractual basis for that consequence. Customary performance must be considered when performance is called for. If no customary route is then available, a reasonable route must be selected, with regard to the goods and surrounding circumstances under the Sale of Goods Act. (112–114.)

He kept construction and frustration analytically distinct: impossibility of one assumed term does not invariably extinguish an entire contract, while absence of an implied term does not itself preclude frustration. Here the contract concerned sale on c.i.f. terms, with carriage only one component. No evidence showed that the longer trip would damage the nuts, defeat a seasonal market, or deprive the buyers of a material arrival requirement. The sellers might incur higher carriage and insurance expense or lose their profit, but the change did not fundamentally alter this bargain. Simonds rejected Carapanayoti’s contrary approach on comparable facts and stressed the doctrine’s narrow limits. (112, 114–116.)

## Reid: effects on both sides and the unresolved cost extreme
Reid accepted that, absent express terms, a usual route or a reasonable and practicable alternative was required, but treated the relevant route as that at performance. Fixing it at formation would terminate the contract whenever the route changed substantially, even if the change had little effect on the actual parties’ rights and obligations. His preferred analysis compared those rights and obligations after closure with the situation had Suez remained open. (116–118.)

Frustration operates without either party’s election, so Reid considered the buyers as well as the sellers: a falling market could have given the buyers a reason to invoke it. The record showed no relevant injury to the nuts or buyer loss from the extra transit time. For the sellers, the identified change was freight expense. They had to procure appropriate carriage, obtain a bill of lading, and tender documents; they were not themselves promising the carrier’s voyage. The sellers did not argue that this freight increase independently sufficed. Reid therefore expressly left an astronomical cost increase for another case. He also expressed no opinion on whether the altered voyage might frustrate a separate shipowner’s carriage obligation. (118–119.)

## Radcliffe, Hodson, and Guest
Radcliffe began with the obligation to procure carriage to the named destination. Usual commercial practice helps fill contractual gaps because it ordinarily expresses reasonable mercantile performance, but its formula cannot freeze an unavailable route into the contract. Once Suez closed, the question was whether Cape shipment was a reasonable way to carry out this commercial sale. The flexible two-month shipment window, variable vessel speeds, absence of a stipulated arrival date, and lack of evidence of deterioration or special packing needs supported that conclusion. His blocked-front-door analogy illustrated an available substitute method rather than a universal duty to use any alternative regardless of cost or risk. (120–123.)

Hodson emphasized reasonable carriage under the Sale of Goods Act and declined to imply Suez exclusivity. A needed arrival deadline or goods unable to withstand the alternative could matter, but neither appeared here. Increased freight remained a portion of the price, and the sellers principally relied on the character of the longer haul rather than the increase alone. Guest similarly judged the carriage obligation at the time chosen for shipment. He distinguished inability to use a contemplated route from the radically different obligation necessary for frustration; a potential breach involving one method and the destruction of the entire bargain were not interchangeable propositions. Each voted to dismiss. (125–135.)

## Arbitrators’ findings and the judicial question
All five speeches rejected treating the appeal board’s general statement of no fundamental difference as conclusively deciding frustration. Concrete findings concerning time, cost, risk, and condition were proper factual matters for arbitrators; whether their combined effect frustrated the contract remained a legal conclusion for the court. Simonds called the commercial opinion valuable evidence. Reid distinguished specific commercial facts from the ultimate inference. Radcliffe warned against too rigid a law/fact division in mercantile practice, while explaining that detailed component findings would have been preferable to a single summary conclusion. Hodson and Guest likewise preserved judicial resolution of the legal issue without ignoring the commercial tribunal’s relevant assessment. (116, 119, 123–125, 129–130, 134.)

## Assigned significance and reading scope
The assigned Other Suez Cases note at printed 1114 contrasts a seller’s c.i.f. obligation with a carrier’s possible excuse. The speeches require identifying each contract’s own obligations, rather than assuming the result for one necessarily controls the other. This decision rejected frustration of the sale; it did not adjudicate every separate carrier’s contract. (116, 119, 133–134.)
