# Moloney v. United States

**204 F.R.D. 16 (D. Mass. 2001)** — November 6, 2001; No. 99-10429-MLW. Chief Magistrate Judge Collings. Civil Procedure: timely and specific privilege objections during depositions. Assigned: CivPro.pdf physical pages 493–494, printed page 963, Note 3 and its continuation.

## Facts and procedural posture

Donald J. Moloney sued the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act for alleged medical malpractice by Dr. Lisa Adams. She worked at Neponset Health Care Center, a subsidiary of Harbor Health Services, which had been designated a federally supported facility and Public Health Service employee under 42 U.S.C. §233(g). The suit was filed February 25, 1999. The court did not decide medical negligence or damages in this discovery order. 16–18.

Moloney had reported abdominal symptoms and fever. His account of the February 1997 consultation differed from Adams's: he described severe pain and distress, while she recalled symptoms suggesting possible sexually transmitted disease and an upper respiratory infection, obtained cultures, administered and prescribed antibiotics, and advised further care if symptoms persisted. He subsequently underwent surgery for perforated sigmoid diverticulitis with fecal peritonitis and suffered serious complications. These competing medical accounts explain the relevance of the later communications without resolving the malpractice claim. 17–18.

At her deposition, Adams was asked about three conversations with her immediate medical supervisor, Dr. Robert Baratz, concerning her treatment of Moloney. One occurred within days of treatment; the other two occurred in summer 1998, one by telephone and one at Baratz's home. She also had one or two conversations with Harbor Health's chief operating officer, Dan Driscoll, months after the treatment. Defense counsel instructed her not to answer questions about their substance, asserting attorney-client privilege and work-product protection. Moloney moved to compel further testimony. 17–19 & n.3.

Opposing the motion, the United States made no effort to justify those two original protections. Instead, it invoked federal self-critical-analysis and medical-peer-review privileges and Massachusetts General Laws chapter 111, §§203–204. Further briefing left the court with the threshold question whether those newly asserted protections had been timely claimed. 18, 21.

## Issue, rule, and holding

**Could counsel block deposition testimony under two stated privileges, abandon their justification, and then rely on three different privileges in response to the motion to compel?** On these facts, no. The original protections were foreclosed when the government failed to justify them, and its unjustified failure to assert the later protections at the deposition prevented reliance on them afterward. The motion to compel was allowed. 20–21.

Historical Rule 26(b)(5) required an express privilege or trial-preparation claim and a description enabling other parties to evaluate it without revealing protected information. The court read that requirement as applying to depositions as well as documents and other discovery. Historical Rule 30(d)(1) required concise, nonargumentative, nonsuggestive objections. The claimed privilege had to be identified with enough supporting information for meaningful evaluation; a blanket claim was insufficient. 19–21.

## Why the objections failed

The deposition exchanges mattered. Plaintiff's counsel repeatedly asked which privilege was being asserted, challenged the first conversation's connection to litigation, and reserved a motion to compel. Defense counsel repeatedly named attorney-client privilege and work product. Although she promised further inquiry and possible arrangements to obtain testimony without inconvenience, she did not identify self-critical analysis, peer review, or the Massachusetts statutory protection. Her statement that her position might change did not make those unstated claims express. 18–19.

The failure deprived opposing counsel of an opportunity to explore whether the actual privileges applied and to develop a useful judicial record. The court rejected using one protection to stop testimony and then researching an entirely different protection after a motion to compel. That sequence defeated both the notice requirements and their practical purpose. 20–21.

Note 5 rejected ignorance of the law as an excuse: the statutes were at least nine years old and cited cases reached back to 1970. Counsel's asserted ignorance of the conversations was also inadequate. The court said she had a duty to discover them earlier; Baratz and Driscoll should have been identified in response to interrogatory 8, served in June 2000 before the February 2001 deposition. 21 n.5.

The decision did not adjudicate the general existence or substantive scope of a federal self-critical-analysis privilege, federal medical-peer-review privilege, or the Massachusetts statute. It resolved the ability to rely on these protections in this discovery dispute. Its express reference to the unjustified failure on these facts should not be converted into a universal rule that every late or imperfect privilege assertion automatically waives protection. The quoted advisory note itself described waiver as a possible consequence. 19–21.

The treatise passages also advised examining counsel to identify the exact asserted privilege and request its rationale. Those suggestions explain how to create a record. Here, plaintiff's lawyer actually sought identification, and the court faulted the government's inadequate assertions. The opinion did not hold that a requesting lawyer invariably forfeits a motion to compel by failing to use a prescribed script. 18–21.

## Exact order and monetary consequences

**Motion 20 to compel further deposition testimony was allowed.** The United States had to pay the plaintiff's reasonable expenses of bringing the motion, including attorney's fees. The court found the opposition was not substantially justified and that no other circumstances made an award unjust under historical Rule 37(a)(4). It additionally ordered the government to pay plaintiff's counsel the costs, including reasonable attorney's fees, of attending Adams's continued deposition. 21–22.

The opinion fixed entitlement, not a final dollar amount. Plaintiff's counsel had to submit supporting affidavits by close of business November 23, 2001; the government could respond to the amount or deficiencies by December 7. The court also invoked American Hangar for its statement that the party instructing a witness not to answer on privilege grounds had an obligation to seek a protective order instead of requiring the opponent to move to compel. That is this court's stated procedural rationale, not an independently updated description of present national practice. 21–22.

## Textbook comparison, notes, and verification

The textbook accurately states that counsel blocked testimony using attorney-client privilege and work product and switched to different protections when opposing the motion. Its quotation is on actual page 21 and continues across the two assigned physical pages. The actual order concerns further oral deposition testimony, and the textbook's shorthand about production should be understood in that setting. It omits the separate expense and continued-deposition awards. The textbook identifies the subsequent renumbering of the deposition-objection provision; the brief preserves the rule numbers actually used by the 2001 court. Assigned 493–494; actual 19–22.

All five judicial notes were read: the Moloney/Maloney spelling choice, correction of a pleading's treatment year from 1996 to 1997, the later conversations' forms, the transcript's Barrett/Baratz spelling, and counsel's asserted justifications.
