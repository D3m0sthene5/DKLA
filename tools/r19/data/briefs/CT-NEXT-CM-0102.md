# Doe v. Great Expectations
10 Misc. 3d 618, 809 N.Y.S.2d 819 (N.Y.C. Civ. Ct. 2005), 2005 N.Y. Slip Op. 25464 | Contracts

## Transaction and trial

Jennifer Doe and Debra Roe brought separate small-claims actions to recover the $1,000 and $3,790 they had paid for Great Expectations memberships. Judge Lebedeff issued a consolidated decision on October 31, 2005, after hearing testimony. Doe bought six months of service; Roe bought thirty-six months, later extended to fifty-four. The printed contracts offered photographs, video, a dating workbook, counseling, background checks, and dating etiquette. Yet they expressly promised zero social referrals and declared that members neither desired nor expected referrals. Members could view other members’ Internet profiles and approach one another. Roe’s handwritten “Marriage Program” and “Platinum Shopper” terms accompanied an oral assurance of twelve introductions during the program, rather than a specified monthly number. Roe testified that the service introduced her to nobody, although one person contacted her after seeing her information; Doe met nobody and eventually stopped checking for contacts. These were testimony-based small-claims findings, not allegations resolved on a motion. Published slip opinion at *1 & n.1.

## Coverage and violations

The court held that the service fell within New York General Business Law § 394-c. The statute included paid social matching by computer or other means. Earlier litigation involving Great Expectations had established that a service need not itself match particular people: supplying the place and mechanism for members to assess and approach one another sufficed. The company’s Internet method therefore did not avoid the Dating Services Law. Id. at *2–3.

The distinction between a service with specified monthly referrals and one without them controlled the price. Under the statute as applied in this decision, a contract charging more than $25 had to promise a specified certain number of referrals per month. Neither contract did so, making $25 the maximum charge for these contracts—not a monthly allowance. The forms also omitted the required refund option when promised referrals were not made, restrictions on disclosing personal information without written consent, the unilateral right to suspend membership for up to a year, return of personal materials by certified mail after termination, the maximum distance for face-to-face meetings, and the policy for a member who moved outside the service area. They omitted the separate Dating Service Consumer Bill of Rights as well. Only the three-day cooling-off cancellation notice complied. Id. at *3–4.

## Damages and statutory reasoning

Section 394-c(9)(b) allowed an injured consumer to recover actual damages or $50, whichever was greater. The court found that actual damages included the contract price minus the lawful $25 maximum. Recovering the remaining $25 required an additional factual explanation. Having observed both claimants’ testimony, the judge found that they carefully negotiated the services and would not have signed contracts with unlawful terms if the mandatory notice had informed them of their statutory rights. That finding justified returning the entire price as restitutionary actual damages and achieved substantial justice in the small-claims proceedings. Id. at *4–5.

The court distinguished the earlier approach of simply treating a noncompliant dating contract as void on public-policy grounds. A 1992 amendment supplied an express individual actual-damages remedy and authorized Attorney General enforcement with civil penalties of up to $1,000 per violation. The court read that change as providing consumers alternate routes to restitution without weakening protection against excessive charges. It grounded these awards in statutory injury and actual damages, rather than automatically voiding every noncompliant contract. Id. at *5. Footnote 2 explains why a damages-pleading decision from ordinary civil litigation did not defeat the concise small-claims statements. Footnote 3 distinguishes contracts actually promising monthly referrals: the statutory cancellation-and-refund calculation for missed monthly referrals was not the basis of relief here. The court also suggested in footnote 3 that ancillary services arguably should have no separate economic value, rather than making a categorical finding about every dating service. Id. at nn.2–3.

## Judgment and regulatory referral

The court awarded Doe $1,000 and Roe $3,790, with interest running from their respective contract-payment dates. Doe’s membership had expired; the judgment terminated Roe’s membership. The court suggested that the company should return their personal materials under the statute, even though its forms omitted that obligation. That passage was a warning about the statutory duty, not an additional adjudicated materials-return order. The court amended the defendant’s name to include GE Management Group of NY, Inc. before entering judgment. Id. at *5–6.

The judge also exercised discretion to send the decision to the New York Attorney General’s Consumer Fraud Unit and the New York City Department of Consumer Affairs. The considerations were the regulated industry, evidence of a recurring practice in boilerplate forms, earlier similar litigation, and the absence of any threatened referral that might have distorted testimony or pressured settlement. The cases had been decided, so transmitting the decision would not impair their truth-finding function. This was a regulatory referral, not a finding of criminal guilt or an award of the Attorney General’s civil penalties. Id. at *5–6.
