# Kornblut v. Chevron Oil Co. — Appellate Division
62 A.D.2d 831, 407 N.Y.S.2d 498 (1978) | Contracts

## Holding and judgment
On July 3, 1978, the Appellate Division, Second Department, reversed on the law a $519,855.98 judgment, including interest, costs, and disbursements, for a motorist's injuries and death following delayed roadside service. Although some contract provisions intended enforceable benefits for motorists, the majority found no undertaking to compensate this consequential personal injury and no reasonable contemplation of this loss under the contractual damages rule. The complaint was dismissed without costs or disbursements. Hopkins wrote, joined by Latham and O'Connor; Martuscello concurred in the result on a narrower beneficiary rationale. (831–832, 837–839.)

## Breakdown, calls for assistance, and injury
On an August afternoon in 1970, Fred Kornblut drove north on the Thruway with his wife and twelve-year-old son. A tire went flat near Sloatsburg around 3 p.m.; the temperature was 92 degrees. About an hour later a trooper radioed Ettinger, the contractor with exclusive service rights under an assignment of Chevron's agreement with the Thruway Authority. Other troopers also reported the disabled vehicle and repeatedly told Kornblut assistance would arrive within twenty minutes. (832–833.)

By 6 p.m., no service had arrived. Kornblut changed the tire himself with difficulty and complained of chest pain. His wife and son had to put the flat tire in the trunk. He drove to the next service area, was taken to a hospital, and was diagnosed with myocardial infarction. He died twenty-eight days later. His widow asserted negligence and contract theories, but the trial judge instructed the jury only on contract, and the damages recovered rested on that theory. The appellate court did not decide the negligence claim. (833.)

The jury implicitly found deficient and untimely service, and the appellate court concluded the evidence supported that determination. The decisive issues instead concerned the scope of beneficiary rights and the kind of loss contemplated by the contract. The court did not overturn the judgment by finding that prompt service had actually been provided. (833–834.)

## Public contract terms and motorists' particular benefits
The Thruway Authority was a state-created governmental body responsible for operation and service facilities. The majority examined the historical Restatement rule for public contracts and Moch's requirement of primary, immediate benefit showing an undertaking to make direct reparation, rather than benefit to the public in a broad sense alone. The claim had to fit the intended-compensation alternative; this was not treated as a city agreement whose nonperformance would itself subject the municipality to a damages duty. (834–835 & n. 1.)

Chevron promised rapid, efficient, round-the-clock assistance when notified by the authority or police. It had to maintain sufficient vehicles, equipment, and personnel to respond within thirty minutes from assignment of a call, subject to unavoidable extreme weather or traffic delays. Daytime and nighttime charges were specified, overcharges were refundable directly to the motorist, and service vehicles had to help other distressed vehicles encountered en route. (835–836.)

The authority benefited from clearing obstructions and maintaining convenient high-speed toll travel. Motorists also had a personal interest: limited access hindered outside assistance, and self-repair near rapidly moving traffic created hazards. The fixed charges and direct refunds clearly contemplated an individual benefit, allowing a motorist to sue for an overcharge. The majority therefore did not classify motorists as wholly incidental beneficiaries for every provision. (836.)

## The contractual damages limitation
The majority distinguished enforcement of a specific service-price benefit from damages for wrongful death, personal injury, or property loss not addressed by the agreement. Contract language, notice of special circumstances, and statutory provisions could support different results in other cases. The second footnote specifically noted the Uniform Commercial Code's treatment of personal-injury warranty damages; the opinion did not impose a universal prohibition on personal-injury recovery in contract. (836–837 & n. 2.)

Because recovery here rested on contract, the widow had to show a loss the defendants had reason to foresee as a probable breach consequence under Hadley v. Baxendale, rather than rely solely on tort proximate causation. No provision indicated an assumption of responsibility for this injury from delayed assistance. The majority concluded that death following exertion while changing a tire in unusual midsummer heat was not a reasonably contemplated consequence of the service breach. The potentially extensive responsibility to highway users reinforced its interpretation. Thus beneficiary rights under certain provisions did not establish a right to these consequential damages. (837–838.)

## Martuscello's concurrence in the result
Martuscello agreed that the complaint should be dismissed but viewed the trial instruction declaring Kornblut a beneficiary as incorrect. He emphasized the authority's reserved right to obtain substitute service from any source and charge its cost to Chevron. That provision did not indicate an undertaking to pay injured motorists directly. In his view, prescribing a response time described the contracted service without necessarily conferring beneficiary rights on motorists, much as municipal water or lighting contracts could benefit the public without such rights. His reasoning was distinct from the majority's recognition of enforceable benefits under some clauses. (838–839.)

The January 21, 1977 Kings County judgment was reversed on the law, without costs or disbursements, and the complaint dismissed. No new trial or reassessment of damages was ordered in this appellate judgment. The later Court of Appeals stage is separately recorded. (839.)
