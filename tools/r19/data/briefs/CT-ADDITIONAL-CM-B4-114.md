# Florida Power & Light Co. v. Westinghouse Electric Corp.
826 F.2d 239 (4th Cir. August 12, 1987) | Contracts

## Decision and issue

Judge Donald Russell, joined on the panel by Widener and Senior Judge Butzner, held that Westinghouse established impossibility or commercial impracticability excusing its spent-nuclear-fuel disposal obligation. The court reversed the judgment charging Westinghouse with future permanent-storage costs. It affirmed the division of interim reracking costs because neither party appealed that portion. No separate writing appears. This appeal concerned disposal of spent fuel, not the separately settled dispute over supplying uranium at a fixed price. (239–243, 262–264, 279–280.)

## Contract and failure of performance

Florida Power & Light sought nuclear generation at Turkey Point as a less expensive alternative to transporting coal or oil. After evaluating competing proposals, it chose Westinghouse’s pressurized-water reactor. Extended negotiations produced a 1966 contract covering construction, fixed-price uranium supply for the first ten operating years, and disposal options for spent fuel generated during that period. The project later expanded to two plants, reaching initial criticality in October 1972 and June 1973. Florida elected Option C, informally in 1970 and formally in 1972: Westinghouse would remove irradiated fuel from the site and dispose of it as it saw fit. This promised removal and off-site disposal, rather than perpetual retention at the plant. (240–242, 264–265.)

Beginning in 1969, Westinghouse negotiated with Allied General Nuclear Services for reprocessing at its proposed Barnwell facility. An April 1970 letter of intent remained subject to a mutually acceptable agreement. An agreement was drafted in 1974, but AGNS refused to sign as regulatory changes substantially increased anticipated costs and introduced uncertainty. When Florida requested disposal in 1975, Westinghouse responded that uncertainties concerning the AGNS facility prevented removal or long-range planning. Florida had already been reracking fuel onsite and sued for specific performance or damages. (241–243 & nn. 1–2.)

The historical regulatory setting was central to the appellate court’s assessment. The federal government promoted peaceful nuclear generation under the Atomic Energy Act and regarded reprocessing as the practical means of disposing of spent fuel, with valuable uranium and plutonium recovered. In 1957 the AEC assured utilities it would provide reprocessing when private services were unavailable on reasonable terms, and government facilities subsequently processed private reactor fuel. Commercial reprocessing also developed, including Nuclear Fuel Services’ operation beginning before this contract was signed. The court treated these assurances and practices as supporting both parties’ expectations. (243–249 & nn. 3–15.)

Environmental proceedings, licensing obstacles, policy changes, and President Carter’s indefinite deferral of commercial reprocessing then deprived the industry of that disposal route. Onsite reracking offered temporary accommodation, not permanent disposal. Although President Reagan lifted the indefinite ban and Congress enacted the Nuclear Waste Policy Act of 1982, neither measure supplied an available licensed reprocessing facility or off-site storage repository when performance was required. Future federal storage remained contingent and delayed at the time of this appeal. These are the opinion’s historical circumstances, not findings about present nuclear policy. (249–253 & nn. 16–24.)

## The three district-court rulings

The first district opinion, June 25, 1981, rejected Westinghouse’s defenses but deferred selecting a remedy. It treated reprocessing as speculative, reasoned that Westinghouse accepted the risk, and faulted its effort to secure disposal facilities. It also relied on shipping cases in which closing the Suez Canal did not make a reasonably available alternative route prohibitively expensive. The judge asked the parties to seek an equitable resolution. (254–257.)

After further evidence, expert review, and the 1982 legislation, the unreported second opinion concluded that performance had become vitally different from what either party could reasonably anticipate. It charged Westinghouse with reracking costs but left the remaining interim and ultimate disposal costs with Florida, noting the anticipated ability to recover such costs through utility rates. On Florida’s request for rehearing, the third opinion, November 8, 1984, revised that result. It reaffirmed much of the original rejection of excuse, divided reracking costs, and placed future permanent-storage costs on Westinghouse. That third judgment was the judgment under review. The appellate account of these earlier rulings does not substitute for separate full-source reading of each district judgment. (257–262.)

## Governing doctrine

Westinghouse had pleaded impossibility, force majeure, UCC § 2-615, frustration, and mutual mistake. The appellate court confined its decision to impossibility or commercial impracticability. Drawing on its Opera Company decision, it required an unexpected intervening occurrence, nonoccurrence constituting a basic assumption of the agreement, and resulting impracticability of performance. It discussed the Restatement, the UCC, and earlier impossibility decisions in developing that framework. (242–243, 262–264.)

The court emphasized the equitable basis of the doctrine: even an unqualified contractual promise may be affected by extraordinary circumstances making performance fundamentally different. Thus, the absence of an express term specifying reprocessing did not dispose of the defense. The question was what performance required and what means both parties assumed would be available, including whether there was a reasonable alternative and whether the promisor caused the difficulty. This is the reasoning of this historical opinion, rather than a universal claim that contractual allocation never matters. (262–266.)

## Basic assumption and governmental assurance

The appellate court rejected the distinction between expecting reprocessing and making it a basic contractual assumption on this record. There was no available permanent off-site storage alternative when the agreement was made. Negotiations, technical documents, other contractual references, and the negotiated fuel-cycle price all assumed reprocessing. Pricing included its expected costs and the value of recoverable materials. Florida itself remained responsible for later years of the plants’ anticipated operating lives, undermining the suggestion that it had no stake in disposal feasibility. (264–272.)

Gardner’s contemporaneous memorandum expressed concern about future plutonium prices, not whether reprocessing was technically possible. The later testimony suggesting technological uncertainty was inconsistent with that document, the demonstrated process, and industry expectations. The court also distinguished Wegematic’s unproved technological undertaking. Even if private reprocessing did not develop as hoped, government assurance provided a fallback. Its abandonment was the unforeseen contingency, rather than merely the fluctuation in the market value of recovered plutonium. (266–274 & nn. 38–50.)

The district court itself had used the government’s failure to honor its commitment to excuse timely performance in assessing interim costs. The appellate court found it inconsistent to recognize that failure for timing but disregard it when addressing the disposal duty itself. (273–274.)

## Diligence and the supposed alternative

The court rejected the theory that greater zeal would have made performance possible. Converting AGNS into an off-site storage facility required licensing, and AGNS was not interested in that conversion. The district court was itself unwilling to find that Westinghouse could force conversion or construct its own viable facility. The licensing and political obstacles that defeated governmental and other commercial efforts were not shown to be avoidable through more vigorous action by Westinghouse. Its negotiations had repeatedly sought a firm agreement; AGNS, confronting regulatory uncertainty, was delaying execution. Impossibility was therefore not attributable to Westinghouse’s lack of diligence on this record. (274–276 & n. 51.)

Future federal storage was also no reasonable alternative comparable to the Cape of Good Hope route in the Suez cases. It was not available when performance was due and remained uncertain. More fundamentally, anticipated reprocessing profit of approximately $18–20 million would be replaced by a loss exceeding $80 million. The district court’s comparison against revenue for the entire plant project distorted the relevant inquiry. The proper comparison concerned the separate disposal undertaking and its proposed replacement. (276–277.)

Hadley supplied an additional reason for rejecting future-storage damages. The court treated the loss as different in kind, rather than merely greater in amount: both parties contemplated reprocessing and associated price risks, not abandoned government reprocessing followed by compulsory expensive storage in a repository not yet available. It rejected the district court’s contrary foreseeability analysis. (278.)

## Equities and exact disposition

The court additionally considered the equities because it understood impossibility as an equitable defense. Florida’s ratepayers had enjoyed major savings from nuclear generation. The opinion reasoned that it was fair for those beneficiaries to bear unforeseen storage costs, consistently with congressional policy statements and treatment of disposal costs attributable to later operating years. It anticipated that Florida would probably recover the costs through rates; it did not itself establish a rate schedule or enter judgment against individual ratepayers. (279.)

Florida could not recover from Westinghouse the cost of future federal storage for fuel discharged during the initial ten-year period. That portion was reversed. The court affirmed the existing reracking-cost allocation because neither side challenged it, notwithstanding the broader reasoning supporting excuse. The formal result was affirmed in part and reversed in part; the court did not eliminate the unappealed interim-cost judgment. (279–280.)
