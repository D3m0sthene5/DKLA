# Tuazon v. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co.

**433 F.3d 1163 (9th Cir. 2006). January 11, 2006.** Judge McKeown wrote for a panel consisting of Judges Cudahy, T.G. Nelson, and McKeown. Cudahy sat by designation from the Seventh Circuit. No separate writing is reported. Assigned account: CivPro PDF 236–237, printed 431, Note 3.

## The controversy and the interlocutory appeal

Nilo Tuazon began smoking Salem cigarettes at seventeen and continued for more than forty years while living in the Philippines. He developed a chronic cough and was diagnosed with chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder in 2003. That year he immigrated to Washington through a petition supported by his daughter, a United States citizen, and settled with relatives in Renton. Seattle-area physicians confirmed the diagnosis and continued his treatment. He sued R.J. Reynolds, alleging participation in a worldwide conspiracy to conceal cigarettes’ addictive and harmful effects. The alleged conspiracy originated in the United States and operated abroad through affiliates and local entities, including the Philippine distributor of Reynolds brands. The opinion did not adjudicate those allegations or tobacco liability. (1166–1168.)

Reynolds, headquartered in North Carolina, challenged personal jurisdiction and sought dismissal in favor of the Philippines under forum non conveniens. The district court denied both requests, certified its order for immediate appeal, and the Ninth Circuit permitted the appeal. The panel affirmed, although it rejected the district court’s conclusion that the Philippines was not an adequate alternative forum. Adequacy alone did not establish that litigation there would be more convenient. (1168, 1177–1182.)

## Personal jurisdiction under the opinion’s historical framework

Because the claims arose from conduct outside Washington, the panel examined general jurisdiction. On written submissions without an evidentiary hearing, Tuazon needed a prima facie showing of supporting jurisdictional facts. The court separately considered Washington’s service-of-process statute and federal due process. Washington treated a corporation’s continuous and substantial participation in its markets as doing business under the statute. The court found much more than a few incidental sales: Reynolds had been licensed in Washington since 1940, maintained an office and permanent employees, advertised in local publications, researched the market, supported university research, and participated in political activity concerning tobacco regulation. Its annual net Washington sales in the relevant period were approximately $145–240 million. (1167–1171.)

Under the circuit’s then-applied due-process framework, the panel considered the longevity, continuity, volume, economic impact, physical presence, and local integration of the company’s activities. It distinguished sporadic purchases or isolated sales from Reynolds’s sustained physical, economic, and political presence. No single revenue figure or license supplied the entire basis. The court rejected the suggestion that it was subjecting every seller of products to general jurisdiction everywhere its goods appeared. (1171–1175.)

The panel then asked whether exercising jurisdiction was reasonable. Reynolds bore the burden of presenting a compelling case of unreasonableness after adequate contacts were shown. It identified no specific hardship in defending in Washington, where it had operated for decades. Washington had an interest in its resident’s welfare, although his claim had matured abroad. Evidence would come from both countries; neither court could conveniently compel all foreign witnesses. The potential need for Philippine law did not make Washington an inherently unreasonable forum. An American resident’s suit against an American company did not itself intrude on Philippine sovereignty. Footnote 5 also considered purposeful interjection and the alternative forum, concluding that Philippine availability did not override the other considerations. (1175–1177 & nn.4–5.) This describes the court’s 2006 analysis, rather than certifying that its general-jurisdiction formulation governs today.

## Why the Philippines was an adequate alternative

Forum non conveniens requires an adequate alternative and a balance of public and private interests strongly favoring dismissal. Reynolds had the burden of showing availability and adequacy. The district court thought the company had not consented to service in the Philippines. The panel read the record differently: seeking dismissal primarily without conditions and resisting waiver of all defenses did not negate the company’s alternative consent to service. The district court could have tailored dismissal conditions if otherwise appropriate. (1177–1178.)

A former Philippine appellate judge supplied an extensive affidavit describing available tort and contract relief, discovery, and procedural requirements. That established potential redress. Smaller damages than an American court might award did not make the remedy nonexistent. Tuazon asserted corruption and delays reaching thirty years, but identified no personally observed civil proceeding supporting those claims; his own successful use of Philippine courts was difficult to reconcile with the generalized condemnation. The State Department reports principally concerned human rights and criminal justice. Their general observations did not overcome the case-specific expert showing about civil relief. (1178–1180.)

The court did not hold that corruption or extreme delay could never defeat adequacy. It discussed decisions involving specific, substantial evidence of systemic failure and emphasized the powerful showing necessary to conclude that the forum offered no remedy at all. The evidence here did not meet that standard. (1179–1180.) The assigned account’s suggestion that the Country Report was the plaintiff’s only evidence compresses the record: the actual opinion also evaluates his deposition and asserted personal experience, finding those insufficiently specific.

## Why the adequate alternative did not require dismissal

Tuazon’s lawful Washington residence entitled his forum choice to the deference accorded a resident plaintiff; Philippine citizenship did not make him a foreign plaintiff for that purpose. Review of the district court’s reasonable balancing was deferential, with reversal requiring clear abuse of discretion. A plaintiff need not select the optimal forum, only avoid one so oppressive to the defendant that the burdens are disproportionate to the plaintiff’s convenience. (1177–1178 & n.6; 1180.)

The private-interest assessment supported Washington. Both parties resided in the United States. Evidence of the alleged conspiracy and company decisionmaking would largely be American, current medical evidence was in Washington, and smoking history and Philippine operations required Philippine proof. The inquiry concerned the importance and accessibility of witnesses, not counting how many lived in each country. Letters rogatory, voluntary testimony, and Tuazon’s assistance with personal records offered ways to obtain foreign evidence. Moving selected Philippine witnesses to Washington would cost less than moving the parties and the American trial apparatus overseas. Reynolds’s offer on appeal to satisfy a Philippine judgment came too late to bear on the district court’s exercise of discretion. (1180–1181 & n.9.)

Public interests also supported retention. Washington had an identifiable local interest in its resident and tobacco-related health concerns, even though the Philippines also had an interest. Philippine substantive law might apply, but the panel left that choice-of-law determination to the district court and treated foreign-law difficulty as one factor, not a command to dismiss. The district court had adequate resources and was not overburdened; Reynolds offered no persuasive response to those findings. (1181–1182.)

The Ninth Circuit **affirmed the denial of dismissal for lack of personal jurisdiction and forum non conveniens**. It corrected the alternative-forum reasoning while sustaining the independent convenience balance. The mandate did not establish conspiracy, causation, damages, or a final choice of substantive law.
