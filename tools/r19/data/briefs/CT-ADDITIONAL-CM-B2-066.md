# Extra Equipamentos e Exportação Ltda. v. Case Corporation
541 F.3d 719 (7th Cir. 2008) | Contracts

## Holding and divided result
The Seventh Circuit affirmed summary judgment for Case on Extra's fraud and promissory-fraud claims. A negotiated no-reliance clause applied to the alleged oral distributorship promise, and the language and circumstances made reliance unreasonable as a matter of law. The majority distinguished that clause from integration and the parol-evidence rule and did not adopt automatic enforcement against every unsophisticated signer. The costs award was partly affirmed, partly reversed, and remanded because written-document translation did not qualify as interpreter compensation under the statute. Posner wrote the September 3, 2008 opinion with Rovner joining. Ripple agreed on the ordinary fraud claim but would send promissory fraud to a jury. (719, 721–728.)

## Distribution dispute and negotiated exchange
Case Brasil, Case Corporation's wholly owned Brazilian subsidiary, hired Extra to distribute equipment in 1992. In 1999 Extra sued the subsidiary in Brazil over allegedly corrupt charge-backs, and objected before Brazilian authorities to a merger Case wanted. Case sought information permitting it to remove corrupt subsidiary employees without employment liability. Extra's president Briante negotiated in Illinois with Case vice president James Sharman, who signed a release on Case Brasil's behalf although employed by the parent. No subsidiary employee attended or signed. (721–722.)

The release ended the first Brazilian litigation, capped past-due claims, and required Extra's cooperation concerning corruption and withdrawal of merger objections. The majority described the cap as $2 million; Ripple's more detailed account identifies R$2 million. This brief retains that source difference rather than converting or equating currencies. Ripple describes negotiation over a full day in a Waukegan airplane hangar, with Extra's Brazilian and American commercial counsel and financial adviser present. His account also identifies written commitments to furnish standard Commercial Representative and Technical Services agreements and reverse charge-backs. (722, 729–730, 733.)

Extra alleged that Sharman promised orally to retain it as a distributor in good standing, without an intention to fulfill that promise. It claimed that Case manipulated the parent/subsidiary distinction to obtain information and releases while permitting Case Brasil to deny or evade the exchange. The subsidiary later terminated the distributorship, generating a second Brazilian suit. The majority reports a contract breach determination with specific performance denied and damages still being determined; it did not decide those Brazilian damages. Case disputed that it or its subsidiary failed to honor the release. Ripple emphasized that the summary-judgment record did not definitively settle performance or repudiation and described allegations of pressure to accept a much larger debt and surrender the cap. (722, 730–731.)

## Federal litigation and the clauses
Extra filed this fraud action against the American parent in 2001. The district court initially dismissed for failure to join Case Brasil as an indispensable party. A prior 2004 appeal reversed, allowing discovery. On remand the district court granted summary judgment, principally because the no-reliance clause defeated reliance, and awarded Case approximately $116,000 costs. This 2008 appeal reviewed merits and costs; the earlier joinder stage was procedural background, not newly decided. The parties agreed Illinois substantive law governed. (721–722, 727, 731–732.)

The release stated that parties relied on their own judgment, knowledge, and chosen counsel rather than representations by the other party or its representatives, except representations and warranties expressed in the release. A distinct integration clause made the release the entire agreement superseding previous negotiations. Reliance was essential to a fraud claim because an ignored representation could not cause injury. (722–723.)

The majority explained that parol evidence and integration principally protect the contractual writing against contradictory negotiated terms in a contract action. It assumed, while acknowledging uncertainty about Illinois law, that those devices did not themselves bar a tort claim alleging negotiation fraud. Fraud had distinct pleading, proof, limitations, and remedial consequences; it could nevertheless become a means to obtain terms omitted from the writing. No-reliance clauses addressed reliance directly and served to prevent that circumvention. The release did not contain the specific oral promise to retain Extra on which the majority focused. (723–724.)

## Sophistication and summary judgment
The majority recognized that a lay, unrepresented signer might not understand the significance of a clause cast in the technical language of reliance. Some courts examined negotiation circumstances before enforcement. Without deciding whether Illinois required that inquiry, the court assumed it did and held it could still be resolved without trial where no reasonable jury could find misunderstanding. (724–725.)

Briante headed a very large enterprise and had experienced Brazilian and New York commercial lawyers. Extra did not claim those lawyers misunderstood the clause, failed to explain it, or were deceived about its meaning. This was thus an informed commercial agreement rather than evidence of uncomprehended boilerplate. The court's reasoning did not announce a categorical bar to every fraud suit containing such language. (725.)

## Parent, subsidiary, and unreasonable reliance
Extra argued that Sharman spoke for the parent rather than for the subsidiary, the other party named in the release. The majority rejected that distinction: he signed expressly for Case Brasil and represented it in procuring the agreement. His employer and that represented party could both benefit from the alleged conduct. Actual or apparent authority to bind the subsidiary was also inconsistent with treating his statements as outside the clause merely because his employer was different. (725–726.)

The court further distinguished contractual enforceability from tort reliance. Even assuming the clause could not be enforced as contract law, its language and informed negotiation made reliance on contrary oral statements unreasonable. A representative's authority to sign and reasonable reliance on his oral promises were separate questions; absence of authority would not make reliance reasonable. The term representative was itself broader than authority to consummate a transaction. (726.)

As an additional defect, the majority noted that Extra did not want rescission and disclaimed a theory seeking to undo an induced contract. It sought damages associated with termination, already the subject of Brazilian contract litigation, without explaining why the supposed oral promise increased that loss or why it would have been better off without the release. The majority therefore saw neither a valid reliance path nor the necessary distinct injury theory. This was its characterization of Extra's case, contested in Ripple's account below, rather than a finding that all alleged promises were honestly made. (726–727.)

## Ripple's partial dissent
Ripple agreed with disposing of ordinary fraud, but viewed promissory fraud differently. Illinois generally rejected false future promises alone while permitting them as part of a fraudulent scheme. His note 4 and text set out knowledge, inducement, justifiable reliance, damage, and the additional scheme requirement. He regarded the record as sufficient for a jury on the latter theory, as the district court itself had rejected Case's alternative assertions of missing scheme evidence and causation. (728–729, 731–733 & nn. 2–4.)

Crucially, the no-reliance clause expressly excepted representations and warranties in the release. Ripple said that commitments to furnish distribution-related agreements, reverse charge-backs, and cap liability were written into it. Reliance on those commitments therefore was not disclaimed. Enforcing the writing's own exception also served the policy of keeping later litigation anchored to negotiated text. He believed the majority overlooked a scheme to obtain irrevocable cooperation and concessions while arranging for the subsidiary not to perform. Allegations of near-immediate reneging, threatened loss of credit and spare parts, and attempted novation from R$2 million to R$10,365,000 supported trial rather than adjudicated liability. His note 1 explained the alleged charge-back mechanism; note 3 retained the contextual limits of Rissman's no-reliance reasoning. (729–735 & nn. 1–4.)

Ripple also disputed the majority's remedial objection. Rescission could not recover already disclosed corruption information or undo abandoned merger opposition, and restoration of released Brazilian claims might be barred by time or laches. Damages could address these allegedly lost opportunities. Those were reasons for allowing the pleaded damages theory, not findings that limitations had actually expired. He would reverse only the promissory-fraud disposition and remand it for jury trial. (734–735.)

## Costs and exact judgment
The court rejected withholding all costs as punishment for Case's delay in asserting its winning defense. Cost assessment was meant to be summary, not an investigation of litigation strategy. Court-reporter attendance fees of about $8,700 were allowable components of necessary transcript costs. (727.)

Almost $76,000 had been awarded for translation-related services. Section 1920(6)'s ordinary reference to interpreters covered oral translation, not translation of written exhibits. The majority distinguished the noun from expansive meanings of the verb interpret and declined to create a broad purpose overriding the statute's selective list. It also noted that documentary translation could expand without a natural limit, complicating cost administration. Its conflict with a Sixth Circuit decision triggered full-court circulation under Rule 40(e); no judge voted for en banc consideration and Flaum did not participate. Costs were remanded for redetermination, rather than cancelled entirely. (727–728.)

The controlling result was affirmance of both fraud dispositions, with reversal only of the improper costs component and a costs remand. Ordinary fraud was unanimous; Ripple's dissent made promissory fraud two to one. The original report also records September 29, 2008 denial of rehearing and rehearing en banc, with a caption footnote noting Flaum's nonparticipation. Those are the report's procedural notations, without an invented additional rehearing opinion. (719 & caption note, 728–729, 735.)
