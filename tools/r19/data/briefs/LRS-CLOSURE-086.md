# Kosak v. United States
465 U.S. 848 (1984) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and procedure

While stationed in Guam, Kosak assembled a collection of oriental art. When transferred to Philadelphia, he brought it with him and declared that he intended to retain the collection for his personal use. Customs agents received information that he planned to resell some items, obtained a valid search warrant, and seized antiques and other objects from his house. Guam was outside the United States’ customs territory, so importing the goods was subject to customs duties. Kosak was prosecuted for smuggling under 18 U.S.C. § 545 but acquitted by a jury. The Customs Service nevertheless advised him that the goods were subject to civil forfeiture under the then-applicable version of 19 U.S.C. § 1592. On his petition under § 1618, the Service granted relief from forfeiture and returned the property. The acquittal and remission were separate from his subsequent damages claim. (849–850 & nn. 1–2.)

Kosak alleged that some returned items had been damaged while in Customs custody. After the Service denied his administrative claim, he sued under the Federal Tort Claims Act in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania, seeking approximately $12,000. The government sought dismissal or summary judgment under 28 U.S.C. § 2680(c). The district court granted that motion without specifying its grounds. The Third Circuit affirmed over Judge Weis’s dissent, concluding that the customs-detention exception encompassed damage to detained property. The Supreme Court granted review to resolve disagreement among the circuits. (850–851 & nn. 3–5.)

Justice Thurgood Marshall wrote the March 21, 1984 opinion for eight Justices: Chief Justice Burger and Justices Brennan, White, Blackmun, Powell, Rehnquist, and O’Connor joined him. Justice Stevens dissented alone. The judgment affirmed the Third Circuit; it did not decide that Customs had handled Kosak’s property carefully or that his claimed losses were untrue. It held the FTCA unavailable for this category of claim. (848–849, 862.)

## Statutory question and rival readings

The FTCA generally makes the United States liable for covered employee negligence to the same extent as a private party. Section 2680(c), as then written, excepted claims arising in respect of specified tax or customs activities, including detention of goods by customs officers. Kosak proposed limiting that language to injuries caused by detention itself, such as loss of use, depreciation, or economic consequences of unavailable goods. He distinguished those injuries from physical harm caused by negligent handling or storage while the government possessed the goods. The government argued that the exception covered all injuries to property associated with its customs detention. (851–853.)

The majority considered the encompassing prepositional phrase to be equivalent to a claim arising out of detention. On its ordinary reading, that language included negligent handling and storage of detained goods. The majority acknowledged that the narrower interpretation was linguistically possible, but found the government’s reading fairer. Because Kosak had conceded lawful detention followed by injury, the Court did not decide the meaning or limits of detention itself. It also reserved which other kinds of law-enforcement officers would fall within the exception’s broader text. (852–854 & nn. 6, 8.)

The Court declined to rest on a general rule that every exception to a waiver of immunity should receive the broadest possible reading. Footnote 9 explains that unduly generous exceptions could defeat the FTCA’s central purpose, just as nullifying an exception would disregard Congress’s limits. The interpretive objective was to identify the circumstances within the exception’s words and reason, without enlarging or narrowing that range through a categorical presumption. (853–854 n. 9.)

## Statutory context and legislative evidence

Kosak relied on the neighboring postal exception, § 2680(b), which specifically enumerated loss, miscarriage, and negligent transmission of mail. Drawing on Alliance Assurance Co., he reasoned that Congress knew how to exclude negligent property damage expressly and had not done so in the customs provision. The Court drew the opposite inference: the postal provision’s specificity suggested a narrower exception than the customs provision’s general language. A principal purpose of the FTCA was to permit recovery for vehicle accidents caused by postal employees. Carefully delineating postal misconduct left those motor-vehicle claims available; no comparable reason required narrowing the customs exception. This comparison is the passage reproduced in the assigned Katzmann account. The actual holding concerns detained customs goods, while the postal discussion explains the contrast between two provisions. (854–855.)

The majority also used limited historical evidence. A report by Alexander Holtzoff, who apparently drafted the relevant language, discussed immunity for losses connected with customs detention. Committee reports used the phrase arising out of when summarizing the exceptions. The Court found these clues consistent with its textual reading. It acknowledged that there was no definitive proof Congress relied on Holtzoff’s internal report and that a document never introduced into the public legislative record deserved limited weight. It nonetheless considered the apparent draftsman’s explanation relevant in the absence of direct evidence of legislators’ understanding. (855–858 & n. 13.)

The Court identified three general purposes of FTCA exceptions: protecting governmental activities from disruption by damages litigation, avoiding excessive or fraudulent claims, and leaving claims to already adequate remedies. Exposure to suits by owners of seized property could inhibit customs enforcement. Without records of each item’s condition at seizure, the government could have difficulty defending false damage claims. The Court did not find Kosak’s own claim fraudulent; it described a possible legislative rationale for preserving immunity across the category. (858–860.)

Existing remedies offered additional, qualified support. Owners could sue individual customs officers at common law, with Treasury payment of qualifying judgments under § 2006. But identifying the particular negligent official could be difficult. An implied-in-fact bailment contract might also support Tucker Act relief, subject to proof of such a contract. The majority did not hold either alternative automatically available to Kosak or decide the government’s assertion that suing a district director would avoid identifying the responsible official. It recognized the limits of those alternatives while concluding Congress could have considered them sufficient for purposes of this exception. (860–861 & nn. 20–24.)

## Stevens’s dissent

Stevens understood detention as the temporary interference with possession, distinct from physical injury to the goods. He emphasized that Congress used different formulations across § 2680 and that the distinction should ordinarily carry meaning. He accepted the majority’s reading as plausible but preferred a construction allowing recovery for negligently damaged property. (862–863, 868–869.)

His principal methodological objection concerned legislative intent. Holtzoff’s 1931 working paper was an internal executive document, with no demonstrated congressional awareness or adoption. A draftsman’s private intention could not be attributed to Congress; Stevens warned that treating such materials as legislative history would invite searches of dormant executive and private lobbying files. A committee’s compressed introduction to a list of exceptions likewise supplied little evidence about the precise reach of this one. (863–865 & nn. 1–2.)

Stevens also disputed the majority’s hypothetical policy rationales. Continuing officer liability sat uneasily with an assumed desire to avoid chilling enforcement, and possible Tucker Act recovery sat uneasily with a categorical concern about fraudulent claims. More fundamentally, the FTCA was enacted to transfer adjudication of tort claims from private congressional bills to courts equipped to assess their validity. General reasons for exceptions should not displace that broad remedial purpose without specific evidence. Much cited history concerned earlier bills that had not passed, whose proponents’ purposes might differ from the Congress that enacted the 1946 reform. Stevens would have returned to the enacted language and permitted the claim. (865–869 & nn. 3–6.)

## Judgment and preserved limits

The eight-Justice majority affirmed, holding that § 2680(c) barred FTCA recovery for negligent handling or storage of goods detained by Customs. Policy arguments for a more effective remedy were directed to Congress. Two other alleged injuries—the destruction of a cork pagoda during the search and seizure of a stereo receipt affecting warranty repairs—were not independently resolved: Kosak had not presented his segregation argument to the court of appeals. Nor did the Court decide the reach of § 1346(b) for the purely economic detention injuries in his proposed construction. (850 n. 3; 852–853 n. 7; 861–862.)
