# Schroeder v. City of New York
371 U.S. 208 (1962) | Civil Procedure

## Decision and the assigned account

The Supreme Court decided this appeal on December 17, 1962. Justice Stewart wrote the Court's opinion; the complete report contains six judicial footnotes and no separate writing or statement of nonparticipation. The assigned account is the Schroeder parenthetical in Mullane Note 4, Civil Procedure PDF page 148, printed page 246. It illustrates why newspaper publication and notices posted near property can fail when the government can readily identify and contact the affected owner. The complete decision explains that the owner sought an opportunity to present a compensation claim, rather than a right to stop the diversion of water. [Complete official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-371/pdf/USREPORTS-371-208.pdf).

## A diversion upstream and notice during the winter

The appellant owned a house and three and one-half acres on the Neversink River in Orange County, New York. Her family occupied the property only in July and August. In 1952, New York City began proceedings under the Water Supply Act to acquire the right to divert some of the river's water at a point in Sullivan County, approximately twenty-five miles upstream. The dispute concerned the effect of that diversion on her downstream property. 371 U.S. at 208–209.

The statute required six weeks of newspaper publication before the application to appoint appraisal commissioners. Publication had to appear in the City Record, two New York City newspapers, and two newspapers in each affected county. It also required handbills in at least twenty conspicuous places along the aqueduct or near the affected property. Claims for damages from the acquisition were barred after three years. These statutory provisions are reproduced or identified in notes 1–3. At 209–210 & nn.1–3.

The City complied with that statutory procedure. It published in the required newspapers and posted twenty-two notices on trees and poles along a seven- or eight-mile stretch of river near the appellant's premises. But none was on her property, and she never saw any. The postings occurred in January, when her summer home was vacant. The two Orange County newspapers were published in small communities many miles away, although larger towns nearby also had newspapers. At 210.

The City could readily have found her name and address in deed records and tax rolls. Yet the notices did not identify her or any other affected owner. They also did not explain how an owner could seek compensation or reveal the filing deadline. Compliance with the statutory publication and posting requirements therefore left a separate constitutional question: did this procedure reasonably notify the person whose claim would be barred? At 210–211.

## The pleadings and the relief actually at issue

The appellant filed no damages claim within the three-year period. In January 1960, she brought an equitable action alleging that she had not been notified of the condemnation proceedings and had known neither of them nor of her right to claim damages until consulting a lawyer in 1959. The trial court granted the City's motion for judgment on the pleadings. It treated the conceded statutory compliance as constitutionally adequate. The Appellate Division affirmed, and the New York Court of Appeals affirmed over two dissenting votes. The Supreme Court heard the federal constitutional appeal under the then-applicable section 1257(2). At 210–211 & nn.4–5.

Although the complaint requested an injunction against diversion, the New York courts construed it as a means of challenging notice and asserting a right to be heard on damages. In the Supreme Court, the appellant conceded that she was not entitled to an injunction. Note 5 preserves this distinction. The constitutional challenge consequently did not ask the Court to prohibit the City's water project or determine the amount of compensation. At 211 n.5.

## The notice rule and its application

Mullane required notice reasonably calculated, in the actual circumstances, to inform affected parties of the proceeding and allow them to present objections. The Court linked notice to the meaningful exercise of the right to be heard: an owner cannot choose whether to contest a proceeding without learning that it exists. This is a contextual standard, rather than a mechanically identical method of service for every situation. Missing or unknown persons can make personal notification impracticable. At 211–212.

But the reasons for relying on publication weaken sharply when an affected person's identity and address are known or easily ascertainable. A small newspaper announcement depends heavily on chance, particularly outside the newspaper's normal circulation area and when it does not name the intended recipient. Mullane's distinction between ascertainable and unknown parties therefore controlled this case. At 212–213.

Walker supplied the closely analogous condemnation application. There, newspaper notice of a hearing to fix compensation was inadequate for an owner known to the City and listed in official records; even a letter could have informed him of the proceeding and the need to appear. Here, adding trees and poles to the newspaper notices did not cure the deficiency. No sign appeared on the appellant's property or reached her, and the City still made no effort to contact this readily identifiable owner personally. At 213.

## Seeing a changed river is different from knowing about a claim

The New York Court of Appeals had also relied on an assumption that the diversion's effects must have become apparent before the three-year deadline. The Supreme Court identified two problems. First, the pleadings on which the trial court entered judgment contained no such allegation. Second, even awareness that a river looked different would not tell an owner that the City had diverted it or that she had a right to seek compensation. Those were the relevant facts the City needed to make a good-faith effort to communicate. At 213–214.

Note 6 further qualifies the underlying allegation. The claimed impairment of bathing, swimming, fishing, and boating resulted from reduced flow velocity, rather than a change in the river's course, depth, or configuration. The Court did not transform those pleaded damages into proven losses, or accept the lower court's assumption of readily visible injury. At 214 n.6.

The Court concluded that mailing a single letter would have discharged the City's obligation to make the required personal notification effort in these circumstances. That conclusion concerns an adequate method reasonably directed to this owner; it does not replace the contextual notice standard with a universal guarantee that every recipient actually reads the communication. At 211–214.

## Judgment and practical consequence

The Court reversed the New York Court of Appeals and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. The City's publications and postings were constitutionally inadequate in this setting. The appellant gained an opportunity to pursue the notice and compensation issues without the deficient notice being treated as sufficient; the Court awarded no damages and ordered no halt to diversion. At 214.

The assigned parenthetical accurately identifies the distant county newspapers, nearby tree postings, and readily ascertainable owner records. The full decision adds the seasonal timing, the omitted claim information, the posture of judgment on the pleadings, and the express concession against injunctive relief. These details explain why the judgment concerned a meaningful opportunity to seek compensation rather than a final finding about the project's legality or the value of the property loss. Assigned printed 246; actual 210–214.
