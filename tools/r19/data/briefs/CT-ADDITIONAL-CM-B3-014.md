# Shaffer v. Superior Court
33 Cal. App. 4th 993, 39 Cal. Rptr. 2d 506 (1995) | Contracts

## Court, issue, and result
The California Court of Appeal, Second District, Division Three, decided this discovery proceeding on March 30, 1995. Justice Croskey wrote for a unanimous panel, with Presiding Justice Klein and Justice Aldrich concurring. The court granted a nonparty attorney's petition for mandate: the hourly compensation that a law firm paid her was irrelevant to the client's claim that the firm's fees were unconscionable. The court resolved relevance and expressly declined to decide her constitutional privacy claim. This was a ruling about discoverability of the firm's costs, rather than a final determination that its fees were conscionable or its representation competent. (993, 995–996, 999, 1003.)

## Facts and procedural history
On June 13, 1989, Jeremy Simms retained Gibson, Dunn & Crutcher for advice about disputes with Alan Robbins and possible litigation. His fees were to equal the time of the individuals performing services multiplied by their generally applicable hourly rates when the work was performed. The firm represented him in the Robbins matters into 1991. In January 1993, he sued for breach of contract, fraud, breach of fiduciary duties, and professional negligence. He alleged that he had paid $2,018,067.63 in bills during July 1989 through December 1990; about $51,000 covered other work, with the remainder attributable to the Robbins matters. He alleged further bills of approximately $250,700 after litigation was stayed. These allegations were not adjudicated as malpractice findings in this proceeding. (996–997 & n. 3.)

Marcy Shaffer was a contract staff attorney, rather than a party to the malpractice action. Her services were billed to Simms at $215 to $250 an hour, for approximately 1,800 hours—apparently about nineteen percent of the time billed on the Robbins matters. She had previously been a full-time associate at the firm from 1980 into 1983. On returning as a contract attorney, she performed research, analysis, and writing. She testified that she had no permanent office or dedicated secretary, used temporarily vacant offices and sometimes the library, and did most of her own typing. Those facts supported Simms's proposed inquiry into the difference between what the firm charged and what it paid her. (997–998.)

At her deposition, Simms asked her hourly compensation. Counsel, representing both the firm and Shaffer, objected on relevance and privacy grounds and instructed her not to answer. Simms moved to compel, arguing that he needed the information to show an unconscionable markup and that requesting only an hourly rate was a modest intrusion justified by his having paid for her services. He also requested sanctions. A discovery referee recommended disclosure, follow-up compensation questions, a possible protective order, and $1,100 in sanctions. On August 12, 1994, the trial court adopted the report but reduced sanctions to $900. Its October 21 amendment explained that the client's need to investigate unconscionable fees and hours outweighed Shaffer's privacy interest. (997–998.)

Shaffer sought mandate against that order. The appellate court issued an alternative writ, heard argument, then requested further briefing. Her separate attempted appeal was dismissed because the discovery order was nonappealable. Simms did not file a proper return to the writ petition, so the court accepted its undenied factual allegations and disregarded unsupported factual assertions in his memorandum. Nevertheless, it rejected Shaffer's attempt to confine review to the particular deposition pages cited below: the entire deposition had been lodged with the trial court and remained part of the record. The rule excludes facts outside that record; it does not prevent reading uncited portions of an exhibit actually before the trial court. (995–996 & nn. 1–2; 997–998 n. 4.)

## Governing rules and the parties' positions
Under then-applicable Code of Civil Procedure section 2017(a), discovery reached unprivileged matter relevant to the action that was admissible or reasonably calculated to lead to admissible evidence. If relevant discovery implicated personal or financial privacy, the court balanced discovery needs against reasonable privacy. That balancing did not eliminate the threshold requirement of relevance. Shaffer argued that her compensation was a business cost having no bearing on the value of her work to the client; Simms argued that the firm's profit margin could demonstrate an unconscionable fee. (999.)

The court applied the ordinary distinction between procedural unconscionability—oppression from unequal bargaining power or surprise from hidden terms—and substantively harsh, unjustified allocations. It described their sliding relationship: greater substantive unfairness reduces the procedural showing required, though both ordinarily are necessary. Civil Code section 1670.5 permitted refusing an unconscionable agreement, enforcing its remainder, or limiting a clause, with an opportunity for commercial-context evidence. (1000 & n. 5.)

For legal fees, former Rule of Professional Conduct 4-200 supplied more specific guidance. Its nonexclusive factors included the fee's relationship to the services' value, sophistication, difficulty, skill, competing work, amount and results, timing, professional relationship, attorney experience and reputation, fixed or contingent structure, time and labor, and informed client consent. The particularly relevant factors here were value, experience and ability, and informed consent. None made the firm's profit margin an appropriate measure. (1000–1002 & n. 6.)

## Reasoning
The pertinent question was what Simms received for the $215–$250 charged: the quality and necessity of Shaffer's services and their value compared with similar work by lawyers of comparable experience and ability in the community. A fee substantially consistent with that market was difficult to characterize as unconscionable merely because the firm purchased the underlying labor cheaply. This did not foreclose a fee challenge based on deficient, unnecessary, or otherwise unjustifiably priced services; it rejected the proposed cost-based proof. (1002.)

Profit analysis would require an ancillary accounting inquiry with no stable stopping point: defining revenue and profit, choosing accounting methods, allocating salaries, facilities, equipment and other overhead to this case, and valuing reputation, goodwill, and service quality. A firm that negotiated favorable leases or operated efficiently would otherwise face greater scrutiny precisely because it could provide the same valuable services at lower cost. Courts would effectively regulate individualized profit margins rather than judge fees against service value and the market. (1001, 1003.)

The court drew support from Copeland and Margolin, which rejected a cost-plus method of awarding reasonable fees in public-interest litigation. Although those were fee-award settings, their distinction between the cost of producing a service and the service's value applied here as well. Paying less for research labor or office facilities did not reduce the value of useful research to the client. The rule's factors were nonexclusive, but that did not rescue profit margin as an appropriate additional factor. (1002–1003.)

## Exact disposition and assignment comparison
The court discharged the alternative writ and ordered a peremptory writ compelling the trial court to vacate the August 12 order as amended October 21, disapprove the referee's report, and deny Simms's motion to compel the hourly compensation information. The report records denial of Simms's petition for Supreme Court review on June 15, 1995. (1003.)

The assigned account on C&M printed page 699 reverses the actual procedural sequence when it describes the client appealing from denial of discovery. In the actual case, the trial court compelled discovery, and the attorney obtained writ relief against it. The assignment also compresses the more than $2 million of firm-wide bills into work by a contract lawyer; the actual opinion identifies Shaffer's work as roughly nineteen percent of billed time. The original assignment correctly prints 39 Cal. Rptr. 2d 506; OCR's apparent extra numeral is not a reporter error. The assignment's comparison with freezer-sale profits is an editorial question, not a holding of this court.
