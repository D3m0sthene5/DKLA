# Moses v. Macferlan
2 Burr. 1005–1012, 97 Eng. Rep. 676–681 (K.B. May 19, 1760) | Civil Procedure

## Notes, indemnity, and payment
Chapman Jacob made four promissory notes to Moses, dated November 7, 1758, for thirty shillings each. Moses endorsed them to Macferlan so that Macferlan could collect from their maker in his own name. Before endorsement, Macferlan promised that Moses would suffer no prejudice, liability, or costs; a signed agreement expressly protected him from payment arising from the endorsements. Macferlan nevertheless brought four separate proceedings against Moses as endorser in the Court of Conscience. (97 Eng. Rep. 676–677.)

Moses's agent tendered the indemnity and supporting evidence in the first proceeding. The commissioners refused to consider that collateral agreement, believing it outside their cognizance, and adjudged liability on the undisputed endorsement. Only one of the four causes actually reached a decree. Knowing the commissioners' position, the agent paid the whole £6 into court on all four notes; Macferlan withdrew it under their order. The full report therefore gives a more precise account than saying that four judgments compelled payment. (Id. 676–677.)

## Trial, reserved question, and arguments
Moses brought an action in King's Bench for money had and received to his use. At the Guildhall trial before Lord Mansfield, the material facts and Moses's entitlement on the merits were undisputed. The jury returned a £6 verdict, subject to the court's resolution of whether this general form of action was available or Moses had to sue specially on the indemnity. Macferlan moved on April 26 to set aside that verdict and enter judgment as for a nonsuit. The court heard argument, took time to consider, and gave its resolution on May 19. This was a reserved question following a trial verdict, rather than an appellate reversal of the commissioners. (Id. 677–678.)

Macferlan argued that assumpsit required a debt or actual contract, that no promise to refund could be inferred from an adverse judgment, and that a new action could not correct a competent court's judgment. In his account, the indemnity could support only a distinct special action. Moses answered that money unfairly received could be recovered by money had and received even when another remedy existed; the commissioners' final proceedings did not exhaust the collateral right. (Id. 677–678.)

## Holding and the implied obligation
Mansfield delivered the court's expressly unanimous opinion that the general action was maintainable. Assumpsit was available in cases where debt would lie and in cases where it would not. The absence of an actual agreement to refund did not defeat this species of action: an obligation arising from natural justice could support a legally implied debt and recovery as though upon a contract. The reasoning concerns an obligation imposed by law, not a factual finding that the defendant consented to a new repayment bargain. (Id. 678.)

The court described recoverable categories including payments by mistake, failed consideration, imposition, extortion, oppression, and undue advantage inconsistent with laws protecting the payer's situation. It also distinguished payments that the recipient could in conscience retain despite a legal obstacle to enforcement, giving examples of time-barred debts, infancy debts, principal and legal interest on a usurious transaction, and money fairly lost at play. Those are the court's historical examples, not a certification of their treatment under present statutes. (Id. 680–681.)

## Finality and the collateral ground
Mansfield accepted that a competent court's judgment remained conclusive on its subject matter until reversed or set aside. King's Bench could not reopen the merits determined by the commissioners or correct an error in their proceedings. Moses's action accepted the endorsement liability and relied on the separate indemnity, which the commissioners had declined to entertain. The question was whether Macferlan could keep the proceeds consistently with that collateral undertaking, rather than whether the endorsement judgment was wrong. (Id. 678–679.)

The court treated the commissioners' refusal as proper because litigating a potentially valuable collateral agreement within a thirty-shilling note proceeding could indirectly conclude a much larger account. Its illustrations of insurance recoveries followed by a ship's return, a supposedly dead person's reappearance, or discovery of fraud explain how retention can become unjust without treating the original recovery as invalid. The admitted availability of a special action also answered the finality objection: either form would leave the original judgment intact while enforcing the collateral obligation. (Id. 679.)

## Pleading, defenses, and election
The general count let Moses allege that the money was received to his use and prove the special circumstances at trial. Mansfield considered that flexibility beneficial to both sides. The defendant could present equitable defenses, allowances, and even a release under the general issue; recovery could not exceed the money received and justly owing. Choosing this route barred another action on the indemnity, even though the special agreement might have supported additional costs and expenses. (Id. 679–680.)

The court relied on Dutch v. Warren, a reported failure to transfer purchased shares, to show that the existence of a special agreement did not exclude money had and received. That example also illustrated adjustment of recovery to the benefit unjustly retained rather than automatic repayment of the entire purchase price. Mansfield regarded Macferlan's abuse of endorsements obtained for a different purpose as an even stronger case of fraud. These precedent facts are supporting reasoning, rather than a second principal decision being silently merged into this brief. (Id. 680.)

## Disposition, annotations, and assigned scope
The court allowed Moses to waive the broader indemnity demand and recover the £6. Its express rule directed delivery of the postea to the plaintiff, sustaining the reserved verdict. The report states unanimity but does not identify every participating judge or a numerical tally. No judicial dissent or separate opinion appears. (Id. 681.)

All printed annotations in the preserved English Reports reproduction were read. Several critical notes question the court's distinction between judgment and collateral restitution, distinguish general from special assumpsit, or dispute its Roman-law explanation of the share example. Those are annotations about the judgment, not statements by a dissenting judge. Bracketed later case references are also distinct from the 1760 reasons.
