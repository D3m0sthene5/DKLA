# Federal Trade Commission v. QT, Inc.
448 F. Supp. 2d 908 (N.D. Ill. 2006) | Contracts

## Court, trial, and judicial stage
United States Magistrate Judge Denlow issued this memorandum opinion and order on September 8, 2006, after a seven-day bench trial conducted between June 6 and July 11. The parties had consented to magistrate-judge jurisdiction. The opinion contains the court's Rule 52 findings and conclusions, rather than allegations accepted on a dismissal motion. It directs judgment for the FTC against three corporations and Que Te Park on all three counts, jointly and severally, and judgment for Jung Joo Park on all counts. It orders monetary relief and directs permanent relief against deceptive conduct, while requiring the parties to submit a proposed final judgment for later entry. No separate writing exists. (448 F. Supp. 2d at 908, 912–913, 954, 975–976.)

## Product, marketing, and litigation chronology
QT, Q-Ray Company, and Bio-Metal marketed a C-shaped metal bracelet manufactured in Spain. Que Te Park, also known as Andrew Park, was president of all three and controlled QT and Q-Ray Company. He first encountered the product in 1994, began American sales in 1996, and began direct-consumer infomercials in 2000. Retail prices ranged from $49.95 to $249.95, compared with wholesale costs of $7.50 to $28. The FTC sued May 27, 2003; an asset-freeze temporary restraining order followed May 29, and a stipulated preliminary injunction June 11. (Id. at 913–918, 952.)

Four versions of lengthy infomercials, accompanied by websites, brochures, and packaging, promoted immediate, significant, or complete relief from many kinds of pain. Testimonials claimed vanished back, joint, and other pain; hosts reinforced them and presented purported scientific explanations and demonstrations. The advertisements also promised an unconditional thirty-day refund. The court found these claims material and deceptive. Its findings about the defendants' marketing and unreliable substantiation should be distinguished from the underlying complaint's initial allegations. (Id. at 918–932, 948–952.)

## What consumers were told
The court examined each infomercial's overall impression, including repeated testimonials, onscreen text, hosts' statements, and the limited disclaimers. Brief, inconspicuous qualifications about varying results or lack of scientific consensus did not undo the message. The claims of efficacy and scientific proof were express or sufficiently conspicuous implied claims that the court could identify them directly; a consumer survey or consumer-psychology expert was unnecessary on this record. The court distinguished that proposition from the qualifications necessary if a party chooses to offer expert testimony on consumer perception. (Id. at 919–932, 957–960.)

An advertisement violated the applicable deception standard if a representation, omission, or practice was likely to mislead reasonable consumers and was material. Materiality concerned information likely to affect purchase or conduct. Express claims and important health-related claims were presumptively material under the authority applied. The efficacy and proof claims were central to selling this bracelet, and the refund guarantee reduced the perceived risk of purchase. (Id. at 957–960, 965–969.)

## Scientific evidence and the reasonable-basis claim
For Count I, the FTC principally proceeded on the theory that the sellers lacked a reasonable basis for the advertised pain relief. The defendants had to identify their claimed substantiation; the FTC retained the burden of proving it inadequate. A bare efficacy claim and an establishment claim promising a particular level of scientific proof were distinguished. For a non-establishment claim, the court considered the nature of the claim and product, consequences of error, benefits of truth, cost of substantiation, and standards accepted by relevant experts. For an establishment claim, the seller must possess the level of proof represented. (Id. at 958–962.)

Six experts testified. The court required competent and reliable scientific evidence for these strong medical claims, specifically a well-conducted, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study. This was a determination tied to the advertised health benefit, not a rule requiring a clinical trial before selling jewelry described merely as attractive or enjoyable. Defendants' own statistical expert agreed that several studies were seriously deficient. Weak blinding, unexplained populations and measurements, absent raw data, lack of valid comparison groups, and uncertain product identity undermined the studies offered. Anecdotes could not supply the missing scientific support. (Id. at 938–947, 961–963.)

The first study by graduate student DeAnn Trapp was particularly troubling. Its original report included pain and placebo data that did not substantiate the advertised benefit. At the company's request, the report supplied to QT was altered to omit that material, although it remained in her thesis. Her second study was also methodologically inadequate. Foreign reports sometimes tested a supposedly magnetic Bio-Ray bracelet without reliable evidence that it was the same product as the nonmagnetic Q-Ray. A two-paragraph Japanese letter did not furnish evidence about pain relief. (Id. at 933–936, 940–943.)

The Mayo study found improvement in both the purportedly active and placebo groups without a statistically significant difference. The defense identified flaws in that study, but treating it as invalid would not turn it into affirmative proof of efficacy. The court regarded it as the most thorough study available and concluded that no adequate clinical trial supported the advertised claims. QT nevertheless continued the challenged infomercial after learning the results. (Id. at 936–938, 943–944.)

The proposed mechanism fared no better. Park gave contradictory accounts of ionization and could not explain or substantiate the manufacturing process. The court preferred the FTC physicist's analysis to speculative testimony about implanted ions; no testing showed the bracelet maintained a physiologically meaningful charge. The traditional Chinese medicine witness did not identify ionization as part of that tradition and lacked a scientific basis for this bracelet's claimed benefit. The placebo expert supplied no product-specific proof of efficacy. (Id. at 916–917, 944–948.)

Following Pantron and Acu-Dot, the court rejected placebo response as substantiation for a representation of inherent effectiveness. Some consumers' subjective improvement did not validate the promised physical properties. Footnote 26 expressly assumes, for that portion of the discussion, that efficacy rests solely on placebo; it should not be read as a new independent clinical finding eliminating every possible mechanism in every setting. Count I therefore established materially misleading claims under §§ 5 and 12. (Id. at 963–966 & n. 26.)

Count II separately concerned assertions that tests proved pain relief. Because that assertion itself represented scientific substantiation, falsity and lack of reasonable basis converged: the studies did not provide the proof claimed. The court found this second medical claim materially misleading as well. (Id. at 966–967.)

## Refund guarantee and statutory coverage
The heavily promoted thirty-day guarantee did not match the policies actually imposed on many online purchasers and some bracelet styles. A less visible website link disclosed a shorter period; the infomercials directed viewers to that website without warning about the difference. The eventual uniform thirty-day policy operated prospectively, leaving earlier purchasers denied under the ten-day policy without refunds. The court found Count III proven. It expressly declined to treat ordinary processing delays during rapid growth as a separate violation; the false guarantee and nonretroactive correction were the decisive issues. (Id. at 948–952, 967–969.)

The bracelet qualified as a device because its advertised intended use was to mitigate pain and affect bodily function. Intended use depended on promotion, not successful actual physiological operation. The court found no basis to conclude its principal purpose operated chemically or through metabolism; it also explained the statutory drug alternative if chemical action were involved. Thus the sellers could not escape the false-advertisement provision merely by disputing actual physical efficacy or calling the product nonmedical. (Id. at 954–957 & nn. 17–19.)

## Preclusion and individual defendants
A prior Illinois consumer class action had produced a directed verdict for Park individually. That did not preclude this FTC enforcement case. The FTC was not a party or privy of private litigants seeking their own relief; it vindicated a public statutory interest. Fundamental fairness also opposed that application of preclusion. Issue preclusion additionally failed because Park did not show identical issues actually and necessarily decided: the private litigation's consumer-reliance analysis differed from federal individual-liability standards. (Id. at 969–972 & nn. 30–31.)

Individual liability required direct participation or authority to control the deception, together with the requisite knowledge; subjective intent to defraud was unnecessary. Park's advertising approval, control over substantiation and policy, and knowledge established liability. Jung Joo Park's title as secretary and bank-signature authority were insufficient. She did not participate in or control the challenged marketing, and the FTC failed to establish the knowledge requirement. Her office, shipping, and employee-assistance roles did not justify imposing liability merely because she was an officer or Park's wife. (Id. at 972–974.)

## Exact relief and assigned-summary qualification
Applying the § 13(b) equitable-remedy precedents then governing its decision, the court rejected an absolute net-profit limit on restitution. It distinguished direct sales, where consumer losses may measure unjust gains, from transactions where a nonparty intermediary retains part of consumers' payments. It ordered disgorgement of $22.5 million in profits plus interest and full-refund eligibility for purchasers during the January 1, 2000–June 30, 2003 period, with total restitution capped at $87,019,840 in net direct-consumer sales. The monetary remedies overlapped: $10 million refunded would leave a $22.5 million total obligation; $40 million refunded would produce a $40 million total, and $95 million claimed would be capped at $87,019,840. The opinion does not add a separate $22.5 million to all refunds. (Id. at 974–975.)

The court directed permanent injunctive relief to prevent further deceptive conduct concerning the bracelet or similar products. It did not announce a flat prohibition on every future sale of the bracelet. Claims concerning a newer November 2005 infomercial were expressly outside this trial. The September 8 memorandum contemplated a proposed final judgment by September 22 and later entry on September 28; those directions are not proof that the later final order was entered in a particular form. (Id. at 954, 975–976.)

The assigned account at printed 673–674 compresses the decision as enjoining sale and ordering refunds “also” with profits disgorgement. The actual opinion supplies the narrower anti-deception injunction and overlapping monetary structure. Its historical § 13(b) analysis is reported as the 2006 court's reasoning, not certified as present remedial authority.
