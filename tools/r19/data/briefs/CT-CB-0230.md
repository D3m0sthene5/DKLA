# Corinthian Pharmaceutical Systems, Inc. v. Lederle Laboratories

724 F. Supp. 605 (S.D. Ind. 1989)

## Citation, court, and assigned scope
Judge McKinney of the United States District Court for the Southern District of Indiana decided the summary-judgment motion on October 30, 1989. The casebook, printed pages 230–234, emphasizes the absence of an offer by the seller, the computer acknowledgment, and the accommodation-shipment rule. The full opinion also discusses an earlier settlement, summary-judgment standards, and alternative reasons why the seller's conditions would control even if a contract had formed. Those additional grounds are treated separately below.

## Parties and requested relief
Corinthian was a pharmaceutical distributor buying vaccine for resale to physicians and other providers. Lederle manufactured and distributed pharmaceuticals, including the DTP vaccine. Corinthian sought to compel delivery of the 950 vials remaining from its asserted 1,000-vial order at $64.32 per vial. Although the amended complaint referred generally to damages, costs, and fees, the plaintiff's summary-judgment brief sought only specific performance. The opinion notes that Corinthian had no identified downstream resale contracts when it ordered the vaccine. 724 F. Supp. at 606–608 & n.7.

## Material facts and commercial background
The parties had already litigated a large vaccine order in 1984. A written settlement required Lederle to supply a specified amount on specified dates, which it did. The settlement also allowed Corinthian to order additional vaccine at the market price and under the seller's terms and conditions in effect on the order date. That continuing agreement mattered to the court's alternative analysis; it was not itself the breached supply obligation alleged in the present action. Id. at 606, 611.

Lederle's published price lists stated that they were submitted without offer, that prices could change without notice, and that orders required acceptance at its home office. They further stated that unfilled orders would be invoiced at the price effective when shipped. The invoices repeated the seller's conditions and made acceptance conditional on the buyer's assent to them. Corinthian's president knew the conditions appeared on the invoices, although he had not read them. The largest individual order during the intervening period had been one hundred vials. Id. at 606–607 & n.3.

As vaccine-related litigation and insurance difficulties increased, Lederle decided to self-insure and increase prices. An internal sales-force letter dated May 19, 1986, described an increase from $51 to $171 effective May 20. Corinthian did not receive that internal communication until weeks later. It did, however, obtain advance knowledge on May 19 of a customer announcement dated May 20. The report uses both the internal $51 figure and Corinthian's requested $64.32 purchase price; they should not be silently converted into one supposedly uniform old price. Id. at 607.

On May 19 Corinthian ordered one thousand vials through Lederle's automated Telgo telephone system. The system returned a tracking number. Corinthian also sent two written confirmations insisting on $64.32 per vial. No communication at that stage stated that Lederle had agreed to fill the entire order at that price. Id.

On June 3 Lederle shipped fifty vials at $64.32 each. An accompanying letter explained that the lower-price shipment was an exception to its ordinary shipment-date pricing policy because of the magnitude of the increase. It stated that the balance would be billed at $171 and shipped during the week of June 16, and it invited cancellation by June 13. Corinthian received and accepted the fifty vials but asserted a right to the remaining 950 at the lower price. Id. at 607–608.

## Procedural history and governing-law posture
This was a diversity action before trial. Lederle moved for summary judgment, maintaining that it never accepted the asserted 1,000-vial bargain, that the partial shipment was an accommodation, and that its previously established terms controlled in any event. The court treated the material facts as undisputed and drew permissible inferences in Corinthian's favor. It explained that allegations and conclusory affidavits do not establish a genuine issue merely because intent is relevant to formation. Id. at 608–609; 606 n.1.

Both parties were merchants and the transaction concerned goods. The court used the UCC and principally Indiana decisions. Its footnote acknowledged Lederle's New Jersey choice-of-law clause, but no material difference between the relevant states' treatment had been established for the motion. The opinion therefore should not be described as a definitive conflicts-of-law determination that Indiana law necessarily overrides that clause. Id. at 609 & n.8.

## Issues
Did the price lists, advance price-increase notice, or internal memorandum give Corinthian power to conclude a thousand-vial contract at the old price? If Corinthian instead made the offer, did the tracking number accept it? Did shipping fifty vials accept the whole order as a nonconforming shipment, or did the accompanying notice make it an accommodation under § 2-206(1)(b)? Finally, did the earlier settlement and seller's stated conditions defeat the requested relief even on the assumption that a contract had formed?

## Rules and holdings
Corinthian's order was the first offer. The seller's lists reserved acceptance, and neither the internal memorandum nor the customer announcement was a specific offer to supply one thousand vials at Corinthian's selected price. The automated tracking acknowledgment did not manifest acceptance. Id. at 609–610.

An order inviting prompt shipment ordinarily may be accepted by shipping either conforming or nonconforming goods. Section 2-206(1)(b) creates an exception when the seller seasonably notifies the buyer that the nonconforming shipment is offered only as an accommodation. The fifty-vial shipment, only one-twentieth of the requested quantity, was nonconforming, and the explanatory letter brought it within that exception. It was a counteroffer rather than acceptance of the thousand-vial order. Id. at 610–611.

Alternatively, the court held that the prior settlement and the seller's conditions preserved Lederle's pricing and allocation defenses even assuming a contract existed. That alternative analysis is part of the full decision, but it should not be confused with the primary holding that the asserted bargain never formed. Id. at 611, pt. III.B.

## Actual judgment
The court granted Lederle's motion for summary judgment. Corinthian did not obtain specific performance of the claimed right to another 950 vials at $64.32. There was no jury verdict resolving disputed formation facts and no appellate remand in this reported decision. Id. at 611.

## Reasoning
### Published prices and advance information did not constitute a commitment to this order
The lists expressly reserved home-office acceptance and made prices subject to change. Those reservations are inconsistent with a general power in every recipient to demand any quantity at the listed price. The customer notice likewise announced a price change; it did not create a special old-price purchase right for whoever learned about it first. The internal letter was not intended for Corinthian, which in any event did not know of it when ordering. Id. at 609–610.

The court also found no course of dealing establishing an outstanding offer. The parties' earlier purchases and prior dispute did not convert the seller's general communications into an unlimited commitment. An unusually large order placed in anticipation of a price increase still required acceptance on the applicable terms. Id. at 610 & n.9.

### The tracking number recorded the proposal without accepting it
A tracking number showed receipt and administration of the order. It did not communicate approval of the quantity and price. The court classified this as a ministerial acknowledgment, consistent with the seller's express reservation of acceptance. Id. at 610.

The conclusion should be stated in its context. The case does not establish that machines or automated communications can never manifest assent. The important feature was what this response conveyed in this transaction. A system programmed and presented as issuing a final acceptance could raise a different question; that is an analytical variation, not a qualification decided by McKinney.

### Shipment and notice had to be read together
Shipping only fifty of the thousand vials would ordinarily raise the possibility of acceptance by nonconforming shipment, with liability for the deficiency. Lederle avoided that consequence by contemporaneously explaining that it was extending a limited lower-price accommodation and offering the remainder at the new price. The letter made the distinction explicit enough that the buyer could accept or reject the new proposal. Id. at 610–611.

Nonconformity here concerned quantity, not unsafe or defective vaccine. A reader who assumes that § 2-206 addresses only faulty products will miss why the statute applied. Likewise, the fifty vials were not a free gift: they were sold at the lower unit price. The court's use of accommodation described why that partial shipment did not accept the original order, not the absence of all commercial exchange in the fifty-vial transaction.

### The earlier settlement supplied an independent contractual constraint
Part III.B assumed formation only for argument. Corinthian had already agreed, in resolving the 1984 litigation, that subsequent orders would operate under the seller's applicable conditions. Its president was charged with knowledge of the signed settlement. The conditions preserved the shipment-date price and allocation defenses, defeating an insistence that the order date fixed the lower price. Id. at 611.

The opinion also invoked the invoice's expressly conditional acceptance language under § 2-207. This portion must be attributed accurately to the court without converting it into a general rule that any seller can impose all boilerplate simply by labeling it conditional. Other assigned cases distinguish formation through writings from formation by subsequent conduct. Here the preexisting settlement gave the court an additional basis absent from an ordinary first-time battle of forms. The doctrinal caution is a study comparison; it does not erase the alternative reasoning actually stated in Corinthian.

## Separate opinions
This is a single district judge's order. There are no separate opinions or appellate votes to report.

## Study use and limits
Work through the communications chronologically: invitation, buyer's offer, acknowledgment, nonconforming shipment with seasonable accommodation notice, and counteroffer. Beginning with the word “order” or with the physical shipment alone produces the wrong formation analysis. The case also distinguishes a reason to want a bargain—a looming price increase—from an existing legal entitlement to that bargain.

The holding does not turn on whether vaccine policy was wise or the new price socially desirable. The insurance background explains the commercial decision. The contractual question is what the parties actually committed themselves to supply and pay. The final order resolves the buyer's asserted supply right, not a general question about pharmaceutical price regulation.
