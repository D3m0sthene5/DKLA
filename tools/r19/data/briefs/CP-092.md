1985. Justice Rehnquist wrote for the Court; Justice Stevens joined Parts I and II and dissented from Part III.

Parties
Phillips Petroleum Company, the defendant and Supreme Court petitioner, was a Delaware corporation with
its principal place of business in Oklahoma. Irl Shutts and Robert and Betty Anderson represented a class of
royalty owners seeking interest on delayed natural-gas royalty payments. Shutts lived in Kansas; the
Andersons lived in Oklahoma. The final class contained approximately 28,100 owners, including many with
no connection to Kansas. 472 U.S. at 799–803; CB 873–874.
Material Facts
During the 1970s, Phillips produced or purchased gas from leases in eleven states. While federal regulators
considered proposed price increases, Phillips collected increased prices subject to possible refunds with
interest. It withheld the corresponding royalty increases unless owners supplied indemnity for possible
refunds. When the increases were approved, Phillips paid the suspended royalties without interest despite
having retained and used the money for years. The regulatory and indemnity details come from the full
opinion; the casebook summarizes the delayed-payment dispute. 472 U.S. at 800–801; CB 873.
The Kansas court initially certified roughly 33,000 royalty owners. First-class mailed notices described the
suit, allowed personal or counsel participation, and explained that recipients would be represented and
bound unless they returned an exclusion form. More than 3,400 opted out; roughly 1,500 were excluded
because their notices were undeliverable. The remaining owners lived throughout the United States and
abroad; approximately 97 percent of them, and over 99 percent of the leases, lacked an apparent Kansas
connection apart from the litigation. 472 U.S. at 801–803, 813, 815; CB 873, 878–879.
Procedural History
Following a bench trial, the Kansas trial court applied Kansas contract and equity law to all class claims and
awarded interest on the suspended royalties. The Kansas Supreme Court affirmed. It treated notice,
participation, exclusion, and adequate representation as sufficient jurisdictional protections, and applied
forum law absent compelling reasons to use another state's law. Phillips obtained certiorari, challenging both
jurisdiction over the absent owners and the universal application of Kansas law. 472 U.S. at 802–804; CB
873, 879–881.
Issue
Could Kansas bind absent plaintiff-class members who lacked defendant-style minimum contacts without
requiring them affirmatively to join the class? Independently, could Kansas apply its own substantive law to
every owner's claim merely because the nationwide class action was properly before its courts?

Rule and Holding
For a class seeking to bind known plaintiffs on claims wholly or predominantly for money judgments,
defendant-style minimum contacts and affirmative opt-in are unnecessary when procedural due process is
supplied. Absent plaintiffs must receive the best practicable notice, an opportunity to be heard and
participate, an opportunity to opt out within a reasonable time, and adequate representation throughout.
Kansas satisfied these requirements. The Court expressly reserved other forms of class relief and defendant
classes. 472 U.S. at 811–814 & n.3; CB 877–879.
Choice of law requires a separate inquiry. Where potentially applicable laws materially conflict, the selected
state's contacts with each class member's claim must create sufficient state interests to make the selection
neither arbitrary nor fundamentally unfair. Class certification cannot supply missing substantive contacts.
Kansas could not apply its law indiscriminately to all transactions on the reasoning used below; the Court
did not prescribe the particular law governing each claim on remand. 472 U.S. at 816–823; CB 879–881.
Judgment
Affirmed insofar as Kansas had jurisdiction over the plaintiff class; reversed insofar as Kansas law was
applied to all transactions; remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion. The Court did not
enter a merits judgment eliminating the owners' entitlement to interest. 472 U.S. at 823; CB 881.
Reasoning
Phillips had standing to challenge absent plaintiffs' jurisdiction because its own interest was at stake. A
judgment against Phillips that did not bind the owners could expose it to later suits on the same claims. Its
objection therefore sought reciprocal finality rather than merely asserting the constitutional rights of
litigation opponents. 472 U.S. at 804–806; CB 874.
An absent plaintiff has a property interest in a claim that adverse adjudication can extinguish, but ordinarily
bears substantially fewer litigation burdens than a defendant. The absent owner need not travel, retain
counsel, defend against coercive relief, or incur the ordinary risks of a personal damages judgment. Judicial
supervision and the class representatives protect the owner's interests. Those differences justify different
jurisdictional safeguards without denying that due process protects plaintiffs. 472 U.S. at 807–811; CB
875–877.
Notice and a meaningful opportunity to leave the class protected individual autonomy while preserving
practical access to adjudication. Claims averaging about $100 would often be uneconomical individually; an
opt-in rule could exclude owners through unfamiliarity or inaction. The thousands of actual exclusions
showed that Kansas's procedure was functional, and adequate representation was undisputed. The Court did
not resolve hypothetical counterclaims, discovery obligations, or costs imposed on absent owners. 472 U.S.
at 809–814 & n.2; CB 876–879.
Kansas's procedural power did not establish a substantive interest in every royalty transaction. Phillips
identified potentially important differences in other states' interest rules. The majority required proper
consideration of those conflicts and relevant contacts. Kansas's familiarity with such suits, the plaintiffs'
failure to opt out, and the administrative convenience of uniform law could not replace that analysis. The full
opinion additionally explains that the commingled royalties were not an identifiable Kansas fund justifying a
single governing law. 472 U.S. at 816–822; CB 879–881.

Fairness also concerns the parties' expectations when their transactions occurred. Nothing showed that
owners and Phillips expected Kansas law to govern leases and owners elsewhere. The Constitution may
permit more than one reasonable choice, but aggregation does not enlarge the permitted range for an
otherwise unrelated claim. 472 U.S. at 821–823; CB 880–881.
Separate Opinions
Justice Stevens joined Parts I and II and dissented from Part III. His reasoning, omitted from the casebook
except for a notation, is supplied from the full opinion. He thought Kansas had already examined other
states' laws and that Phillips had not demonstrated an unambiguous, established conflict. He distinguished
full-faith-and-credit concerns about other states' interests from due-process concerns about unfair surprise
and objected to converting debatable state-law interpretations into constitutional error. Justice Powell took
no part in the decision. 472 U.S. at 823–845 (Stevens, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part); id. at
823; CB 881.
Notes
Keep four questions separate: jurisdiction over the defendant, jurisdiction over absent plaintiffs, satisfaction
of class-certification requirements, and permissible governing law. This decision resolves the second and
fourth questions on the record presented. It does not create nationwide jurisdiction over a defendant merely
because a plaintiff class is nationwide. 472 U.S. at 811–814 & n.3, 821–823.
The narrow holding concerns known plaintiffs and predominantly monetary claims with the stated
protections. Its broader significance is that procedural aggregation can make small claims practicable while
leaving constitutional limits on substantive law intact. The casebook's later-remand note is subsequent
history, not part of this 1985 holding. 472 U.S. at 811–814 & n.3, 821–823; CB 881–882.
