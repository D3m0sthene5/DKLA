# Donovan v. RRL Corporation (Court of Appeal)
88 Cal. Rptr. 2d 143 (Cal. Ct. App. 1999), No. G024997, August 23, 1999 | Contracts

## Decision and historical position
The Fourth District, Division Three, reversed a judgment for an automobile dealer and remanded for a determination of the buyer’s damages. Justice Rylaarsdam wrote; Presiding Justice Sills and Justice Crosby concurred, with no separate writing. The court held that a sufficiently specific used-car advertisement, read in light of California’s advertised-price statute, constituted an offer accepted by the buyer’s attempted tender. It rejected the dealer’s mistake defense and itself found negligence in the failure to check the advertising proof. This is the 1999 intermediate appellate decision. The California Supreme Court subsequently reversed it in Donovan v. RRL Corp., 26 Cal. 4th 261, 27 P.3d 702 (2001), separately briefed; the conclusions below describe the earlier court’s reasoning, not the final disposition of the controversy. (Actual opinion, “Disposition,” opening discussion, and concurrence lines.)

## Facts and procedural history
Donovan saw a Saturday Daily Pilot advertisement listing a particular 1995 Jaguar XJ6 Vanden Plas, identified by VIN 720603, for $25,995. The next day he and his wife visited Lexus of Westminster, RRL’s dealership, found the advertised car, and took a permitted test drive. Donovan then said that he would buy it for the advertised price. A representative immediately responded that the price was a mistake. Donovan accused the dealer of bait-and-switch advertising and spoke with the sales manager. Although Donovan offered to write a check for the full advertised price, the manager instead offered to sell for $37,016. Donovan declined, left, unsuccessfully sought completion through correspondence, and sued in municipal court. (“Facts,” first four paragraphs.)

The advertisement had resulted from a newspaper error. The dealer previously advertised the 1995 car without a price. On Tuesday its advertising manager requested another run of that advertisement; on Thursday she requested a replacement advertisement for a 1994 Jaguar XJ6 priced at $25,995. The newspaper’s composing room combined the 1995 vehicle with the 1994 vehicle’s price. The advertising manager did not review a proof because she did not work Fridays, when the advertisement went to press. Both the manager and newspaper representative testified to this sequence. (“Facts,” advertising testimony.)

After a bench trial, the municipal court found that the advertisement could produce a contract upon tender, that a good-faith unilateral mistake negated contractual intent, and that Donovan did not know about the mistake. It entered judgment for the dealer. The superior court’s appellate division reversed, relying on Vehicle Code section 11713.1(e), prohibiting a dealer’s failure to sell at its advertised total price, and on Germain’s allocation of mistaken transmission risk. Following certification, the Court of Appeal transferred the case for hearing and decision. These are actual trial findings and appellate steps, rather than allegations deemed proved through a pleading dismissal. (“Facts,” final three paragraphs.)

## Offer and acceptance
The court acknowledged the ordinary rule that advertisements invite bargaining. It examined Estate of Lynch’s invitation for bids, Harris v. Time’s offer of a watch for opening an envelope, and Brown’s statement of the general advertising rule. The exception required sufficient commitment and sufficiently definite requested action without additional negotiation. This advertisement identified one unique car and a price, rather than a generic inventory of cars. Nothing on its face told a buyer to do more than tender the price. The possibility of several customers responding did not necessarily produce several contracts: only one buyer could accept the offer for the particular vehicle. (“Is an Advertisement an Offer?”)

The court did not make unilateral versus bilateral classification decisive. Donovan’s statement after the test drive could be treated as acceptance of a bilateral offer; alternatively, the advertisement invited tender as the act of acceptance. The dealer’s refusal excused actual presentation of the money under Civil Code section 1440. Nor did the prospect of a later written sales agreement necessarily prevent present formation: sufficient manifestations of assent can bind parties before a contemplated memorial. Although vehicle sales often involve financing, warranties, trade-ins, insurance, and delivery negotiations, Donovan sought none of those additional terms. His willingness to buy without them did not create a legally mandatory negotiation. Nevertheless, the court called the offer question close and relied on the advertised-price statute to tip the balance. It did not establish that every price advertisement independently constitutes an offer. (Same section, bilateral-contract, contemplated-writing, and final paragraphs.)

## Statutory policy and mistake
The dealer argued that an innocent publication error lacked the intent required for a statutory violation. The court distinguished the duty concerning false advertising under section 11713.1(b) from the duty to sell at the advertised price under subdivision (e). The dealer did not intend the erroneous advertisement, but knowingly refused the requested advertised-price sale. Relying on Ford Dealers Association’s description of the statute as protecting the public from irresponsible as well as unscrupulous dealers, the court declined to reward failure to review proofs. It also rejected a newspaper amicus’s Smith v. California First Amendment objection: its analysis concerned a knowing refusal to sell, rather than criminal liability for unknowingly publishing false contents. (“The Effect of Section 11713.1, subdivision (e).”)

The court accepted the good-faith mistake finding but disagreed that unilateral mistake necessarily erased the offer. It distinguished Kemper, where the accepting city already knew of the bidder’s clerical mistake, and invoked Hedging Concepts’ objective-contract reasoning concerning an unknown unilateral misunderstanding. The dealer and amici additionally argued that the newspaper was not its agent, so Germain’s mistaken-transmission rule should not apply. The court expressly avoided deciding the newspaper’s agency or attributing its concurrent negligence. (“Did the Mistake Vitiate the Offer?” first three paragraphs.)

Instead, the court used Code of Civil Procedure section 909, allowing appellate factual determinations in a case tried without a jury. The trial court’s finding of good faith was not a finding of freedom from negligence. The uncontradicted testimony showed an opportunity to review the proof that the advertising manager did not take. Given the advertising duties imposed on automobile dealers, the appellate court found that omission negligent and considered the record incapable of supporting the contrary conclusion. This appellate factfinding was a distinct part of its analysis, not a trial finding that the dealer negligently prepared the advertisement. (Same section, final five paragraphs.)

## Judgment and source scope
The court reversed and remanded to the trial court to determine Donovan’s damages, awarding him appellate costs. It did not award a specified damage sum or order delivery of the car in this opinion. The later Supreme Court reversal must accompany any historical use of this decision. (“Disposition.”)
