# Clarke, Comptroller of the Currency v. Securities Industry Association
479 U.S. 388 (1987) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and votes
On January 14, 1987, the Supreme Court held that the Securities Industry Association could obtain review of the Comptroller's approval of national-bank discount-brokerage operations, but that the Comptroller's statutory interpretation was permissible. The judgment affirming standing was preserved; the adverse merits judgment was reversed. White wrote the opinion. Brennan, Marshall, Blackmun, and Powell joined it fully. Rehnquist, Stevens, and O'Connor joined Parts I and III and the judgment; Stevens wrote for those three explaining a narrower basis for standing. Scalia did not participate. Thus all eight participating Justices agreed on the result, while five endorsed the general zone-of-interests discussion in Part II. (389–390, 394, 409–410.)

## Banks, brokerage offices, and proceedings
In 1982 Union Planters sought to acquire an existing discount brokerage, and Security Pacific proposed to establish a brokerage affiliate. Both would offer services at bank branches and at other sites, including sites outside their home states. Discount brokers execute trades without giving investment advice and generally charge less than full-service brokers. The question was whether the separate brokerage offices were bank branches subject to the National Bank Act's geographical restrictions. Section 81 confined a national bank's general business to its headquarters and permitted branches; section 36(c) limited branching by reference to home-state and state-bank law, and section 36(f) defined branches to include locations receiving deposits, paying checks, or lending money. (390–392 & n. 1.)

The Comptroller concluded that the brokerage locations performed none of those core functions. They might intermediate margin lending, but loan approval would occur at chartered offices. Their customer credit balances were incidental to brokerage, were not solicited like ordinary deposits, and were subject to securities-law restrictions. Treating them as branches also appeared inconsistent with longstanding nonbranch securities operations. The Comptroller approved Security Pacific and later Union Planters. (391–392 & nn. 2–3.)

The association of securities brokers, underwriters, and investment bankers argued that these offices were branches. The Comptroller challenged its zone-of-interests standing, saying the McFadden Act protected competitive equality between state and national banks rather than securities firms. The district court found standing and rejected nonbranch brokerage; a divided circuit panel affirmed and rehearing was denied over three judges' dissent. The Supreme Court consolidated the Comptroller's and Security Pacific's petitions. The association's separate argument that banks could not offer discount brokerage at all under Glass-Steagall had been rejected below and was not before the Court. The Comptroller had abandoned his separate Article III injury objection. (392–394 & nn. 4–6.)

## The majority's APA zone-of-interests analysis
Part II understood the test as a gloss on APA § 702's authorization of review for persons adversely affected or aggrieved by agency action within the meaning of a relevant statute. Injury alone did not entitle everyone to challenge an agency, but Congress's generous review provisions did not require a narrowly construed legal interest or an express review provision in the substantive statute. Data Processing had allowed competitors to challenge an expansion of banking activities and had considered related legislation to identify the banking scheme's overall purposes. (394–398 & nn. 7–13.)

For a plaintiff not directly regulated, the test excludes interests so marginally related to, or inconsistent with, the statute's purposes that congressional permission to sue cannot reasonably be inferred. It is not especially demanding and does not require proof that Congress intended to benefit that plaintiff class. Still, satisfying it does not end the inquiry: other statutory indicators may demonstrate an intent to preclude review, as in Community Nutrition's consumer challenge to a specialized pricing scheme. Note 16 explains that the APA inquiry is not a universal test for every statutory or constitutional claim and differs from establishing an implied private cause of action outside the APA. (399–401 & nn. 14–16.)

The Comptroller focused too narrowly on § 36's concern with state-bank equality. Section 36 created a limited exception to § 81's earlier restriction, and the broader statutory history showed concern about concentration of financial power through unrestricted national-bank branching. Brokerage competition bears a plausible relationship to that policy: credit balances and margin opportunities give banks greater access to money and lending. Competitors whose alleged injury implicates that regulatory policy were reasonable parties to challenge the Comptroller. There was no comparable indication that Congress intended to exclude their review. Being a proper challenger did not establish that the challenged interpretation was wrong. (401–403 & n. 17.)

## Why the Comptroller prevailed on the merits
Part III applied the then-prevailing principle of substantial weight for a reasonable construction by the agency administering the banking law. The association said § 81's general-business language unambiguously covered every activity at every bank office. The Court found a plausible narrower meaning limited to core banking functions. A 1911 Attorney General opinion, later approved by the Court, had distinguished a branch conducting general banking business from an agency conducting a particular incidental activity. The 1927 change from “usual” to “general” business supplied no shown legislative intent to reject that understanding. (403–406.)

Nor did statutory authorization of an activity necessarily subject every site conducting it to branch restrictions. Congress specifically allowed safe-deposit business while deleting potentially restrictive location language; contemporaneous debate supported operations away from the bank's premises. The Court gave little weight to Representative McFadden's expansive postpassage description of branches: Congress had not seen it when acting, and it would imply an unexplained reversal of the established distinction. (406–407 & nn. 18–19.)

Congress had expressly authorized national-bank securities dealings against a background of widespread interstate operations. It was unlikely to have imposed geographical restrictions silently. Note 21 addresses why earlier affiliates were not materially different in control from subsidiaries; note 22 distinguishes Glass-Steagall's later restrictions on types of transactions from location restrictions. The Comptroller reasonably confined competitive equality to core banking, rather than every incidental service. The Court held discount brokerage not a core banking function and expressly reserved whether core functions extended beyond the three enumerated in § 36(f). Accordingly, it affirmed standing and reversed the merits ruling. (407–409 & nn. 20–23.)

## Stevens's narrower concurrence
Stevens, joined by Rehnquist and O'Connor, agreed with the merits and with standing but declined Part II's broad exposition. Detailed examination of branch-banking history already established this class of competitors within the protected zone. The McFadden Act was a compromise retaining a general restriction while permitting enough branching to let national banks compete with state banks. The antibranching position reflected concerns about concentrated credit, financial power, and public consequences, not merely protecting local banks. The 1933 expansion and later bank-holding legislation showed continuing broader concerns. Thus securities competitors fell within the policy against enlarging national-bank power, and no sweeping reformulation of the test was necessary. This is a concurrence in part and in the judgment, not a dissent from the decision to permit review. (409–417 & concurrence nn. 1–8.)
