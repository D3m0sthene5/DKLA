# Automotive Parts & Accessories Association, Inc. v. Boyd

**Course:** Legislation, Regulation & the State  
**Citation:** 407 F.2d 330 (D.C. Cir. 1968)  
**Court and date:** United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, December 27, 1968  
**Opinion:** Judge McGowan for the unanimous panel of Chief Judge Bazelon and Judges McGowan and Robinson; no separate opinion appears.  
**Assigned source:** LRS November pages 1–165 scan, PDF pages 5–15, printed pages 579–589; principal opinion ends before Notes, with judicial footnote 18 continuing beneath them.  
**Brief ID:** LRS-MAX-AUTOMOTIVE

## Case orientation

The agency could use informal rulemaking to require factory-installed front-seat head restraints in new passenger cars. That procedural choice did not eliminate its duty to explain the major policy choices or the court's duty to examine the record. The promulgation materials and the agency's letter denying reconsideration together explained why a vehicle standard better protected consumers than a standard governing optional accessory equipment. The court denied the petitions for review.

## Facts and rulemaking

Under the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 1966, the transportation authorities issued Motor Vehicle Safety Standard No. 202. Effective January 1, 1969, new passenger cars manufactured for sale in the United States had to have factory-installed front-seat head restraints meeting federal requirements. A vehicle standard required the car itself to meet the standard before manufacture for sale; an equipment standard governed the accessory manufactured for sale without necessarily ensuring its installation in every car. (407 F.2d at 332; note 1.)

The challengers were accessory manufacturer Sterling Products and trade associations APAA and ASIA, rather than automobile manufacturers. ASIA intervened in the consolidated review proceedings. Their principal concern was that factory installation would displace aftermarket sales. In comments and reconsideration submissions they supported the safety benefits of head restraints while disputing how the government should require them.

The agency proposed twenty-three standards in November 1966, sought comments, and issued twenty standards in January 1967. It withheld a final head-restraint standard to obtain more information, sought additional comments, and met with manufacturers and other interested persons in November 1967. A further proposal in December 1967 invited comments through January 26, 1968. The final standard issued February 12, 1968, reflected some comments but rejected the accessory interests' central proposal. The agency denied their reconsideration petition in a reasoned three-page letter on May 20, 1968. (At 334.)

Petitioners preferred a standard that would certify accessory products, allow consumers to select the brand and fit they wanted, and permit installation before delivery if installation was mandatory. They argued that factory installation raised prices, strengthened automobile-industry concentration, slowed improvements, encouraged minimum designs, and restricted consumer choice. They also claimed aftermarket restraints were easy and inexpensive to install and offered faster implementation. The agency answered that optional equipment did not assure installation in every car, that restraint performance depended on seat structure and anchorage, and that a vehicle standard allowed integral-seat designs as well as add-on restraints. (At 338–341.)

## Procedural posture and questions

In the consolidated direct-review proceedings, petitioners argued that the Safety Act required formal APA hearings, that the statement of basis and purpose was insufficient, and that the substantive requirement lacked adequate justification. They had not presented their formal-hearing objection during the agency proceedings or reconsideration. The court identified a serious preservation question but decided the merits because the government defended them and effective administration of the new statute presented a public interest. The decision therefore is not a holding that the objection was waived. (At 334.)

The court held that informal rulemaking was authorized, that the agency adequately explained its material choices, and that the record supported the standard. It denied review, leaving the standard in operation. There was no district-court judgment to affirm and no damages award.

## Informal rather than mandatory formal rulemaking

APA § 553 makes §§ 556 and 557 applicable when a statute requires rules to be made on the record after an opportunity for an agency hearing. The Safety Act instead stated generally that the APA applied to orders establishing, amending, or revoking safety standards. The statute's use of “orders” did not settle the APA classification: a future rule of general application remains rulemaking despite that statutory label. (At 334–336; note 6.)

The legislative history supplied the missing procedural specificity. The Senate version expressly excluded the formal-hearing provisions. The House version referred generally to the APA, but its committee report contemplated a choice between informal and formal procedures. The conference adopted the House language. Senator Magnuson explained that it permitted formal procedures if the Secretary considered them desirable; it did not require them. The Act's reliance on informal consultation, research, and experimentation reinforced that understanding. Setting general safety policy differed from resolving a particular factual dispute through an adjudicatory hearing.

Petitioners argued that requirements to file the agency record in court, APA judicial review, and a possible remand for additional evidence implied a formal evidentiary record. The court rejected that inference. Written comments and other materials could form an informal-rulemaking record, and that record could be supplemented on remand. Judicial review did not independently create a hearing requirement contradicted by the statute's history and structure. (At 335–337.)

APA § 706(2)(E)'s substantial-evidence provision concerned the formal proceedings specified in its terms and did not apply here merely because the Safety Act provided review. Other § 706 grounds still governed informal rulemaking. Their application could be searching and strict while examining the legislative materials appropriate to the proceeding, rather than treating the agency's action as trial findings. The opinion later described substantial support for the standard; that merits description did not silently convert the proceeding to formal substantial-evidence review. (At 336–338.)

## An explanation of major issues, not a bare purpose sentence

APA § 553(c) required a concise general statement of basis and purpose. The promulgation's short statement identified the objective of reducing neck injuries, but the court considered the agency's promulgation explanation and its denial of reconsideration together. Note 12 expressly identifies the latter agency explanation as relevant to compliance. The court did not hold that the short purpose sentence alone would always suffice. (At 338 and note 12.)

The statement need not discuss every item in the submissions or supply formal findings on every material issue. It must allow the reviewing court to see the major policy issues and why the agency resolved them as it did. Courts should not have to reconstruct the agency's rationale in the first instance through a laborious search of the record. The court warned the new agency against treating “concise” and “general” as permission for an explanation too thin to permit meaningful review.

Here the agency identified why it rejected the equipment-only alternative. Universal installation and enforceability mattered, as did the engineering relationship among restraint, seat, and anchorage. It explained the design flexibility of a vehicle performance standard and why the public safety benefit outweighed the challengers' commercial disadvantage. These were agency explanations furnished during promulgation and reconsideration, rather than a rationale first invented by reviewing counsel or the court. (At 338–341.)

## Substantive justification and limits

The court found conscientious consideration and reasoned rejection of petitioners' alternatives. Assuring installation in every new car was a practical enforcement advantage, while expert evidence supported the integrated structural relationship. The record also supported the January 1, 1969 effective date. Petitioners' demand for greater speed would have reduced protection by shifting to an optional add-on model. (At 340–342; notes 14, 16, and 17.)

The Administrator had also asserted that he lacked statutory authority to require purchasers to install equipment after establishing an equipment standard. The court expressly declined to decide that legal question in note 15, because the other grounds were sufficient. The brief therefore does not turn that agency assertion into a judicial holding.

In court, petitioners shifted to arguing that restraints themselves were unnecessary or hazardous, drawing on submissions from automobile manufacturers who were not challenging the final rule. The court sharply criticized the inconsistency with petitioners' earlier support for restraints and demand for quicker implementation. It nevertheless reached an alternative merits conclusion: independent research, medical information, and departmental statistics supported reduced neck injuries in rear-end crashes, while asserted visibility and other safety harms were speculative. The agency could reasonably weigh those competing consequences. This was not solely a judicial estoppel or preservation disposition. (At 341–343.)

The standard satisfied the Act's requirements of practicability, safety need, and objective terms, with attention to available safety data, consultation, appropriateness for the vehicle, and the statutory purpose. Economic loss to accessory producers did not require abandonment of a supported safety choice. The court did not conduct an antitrust trial or announce that any safety rationale automatically defeated all economic objections. (At 342–343; note 18.)

## Other procedural objections and footnotes

The full opinion also rejected the claim that the wrong official promulgated the standard. The transportation statutes, executive organization, and delegations allowed the relevant authority to be exercised by the Federal Highway Administrator; the statutory bureau director had not been unlawfully bypassed. The court noted the bureau director's actual research, consultation, and reporting participation. (At 333–334; note 2.)

The consultation objections also failed. The statute required a majority of the advisory council to represent the general public but did not compel a representative of every listed private interest. The record supported consultation with the council and the Vehicle Equipment Safety Commission. These conclusions are specific to the statutory organization and record then before the court. (At 334; note 5.)

## Judgment and exam use

The unanimous panel denied the petitions for review. Standard No. 202 remained effective. Identify separately whether formal procedure is required, whether the actual informal procedure provided the required explanation, and whether the substantive policy has record support. An informal proceeding can require serious judicial scrutiny without becoming a trial. When reviewing explanation, distinguish agency reasons actually supplied during the decision process from a court's own reconstruction, and do not confuse an agency's reserved statutory theory with an issue the court decided.
