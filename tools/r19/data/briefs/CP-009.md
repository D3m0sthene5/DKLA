# Texas Employers' Insurance Association v. Price
336 S.W.2d 304 (Tex. Civ. App.—Eastland 1960) | Civil Procedure

## Workplace injury and compensation judgment

Loyal Grant Price sought to set aside Industrial Accident Board awards and recover workers' compensation from the insurer, Texas Employers' Insurance Association. Price was a thirty-year-old welder and fitter employed by Port Houston Iron Works. He testified that in October 1957 a sledgehammer rebounded while he was working on a scaffold, struck his face, and caused him to fall six or seven feet, injuring his back. He received initial treatment, reported the accident to supervisory personnel, and continued working, although his pain increased. Full opinion, opening procedural and accident-history paragraphs.

Price later worked for other employers and underwent surgery for a herniated disc in February 1958. His surgeon removed slightly less than one-quarter of the disc. Price returned to welding work in April, but testified that he worked in pain because of his bills and progressively lost his ability to lift, stoop, and perform the trade. At trial he wore a brace and described worsening back and leg pain and numbness. His doctor considered the permanent impairment approximately twenty percent and not total, but would not pass him to practice his trade. Continued earnings and medical estimates thus supplied arguments against total disability, while Price's account of functional limitations supported it.

The jury found an accidental employment injury on October 15, 1957, producing total and permanent incapacity beginning November 27, with no partial incapacity. It also found grounds for lump-sum compensation, notice, a failure to furnish medical services, and $967.25 in reasonable necessary medical expenses. The court entered judgment for $13,415.96 plus interest. The insurer's motions for judgment notwithstanding the verdict and for a new trial were denied, and it appealed.

## Challenges rejected in the full opinion

The insurer challenged jurisdiction on the ground that Price's general allegation of compliance with the compensation statute did not specifically plead notice, a claim, and notice of dissatisfaction with the Board award. The court held that the allegation covered the required statutory steps and, as to facts not specifically denied under oath, the contemporary Texas pleading rules treated them as true. A legal conclusion could suffice where the adversary was not misled and no special exception attacked it. The compensation requirements were sufficiently familiar that the insurer was not misled. Full opinion, point-five discussion.

The insurer had separately denied timely injury notice under oath. On that disputed issue, proof—not merely the general allegation—showed that the superintendent or yard foreman and Price's immediate supervisor had actual notice on the accident date. Despite disputes over details and dating, the evidence described the same accident the jury identified. Timely notice to the foreman counted as notice to the employer and insurer. The alternative jurisdictional objection also failed. Full opinion, point-six discussion.

The court rejected both no-evidence and factual-insufficiency attacks on total and permanent incapacity. Price's continued paid work did not necessarily defeat total-disability recovery where he performed it painfully from economic necessity. The jury could weigh his functional account against the doctor's percentage estimate; the extent of incapacity was not exclusively a subject on which expert conclusions controlled. The same doctor's refusal to approve Price for his trade also supported Price's account. The court considered the finding supported by probative evidence and not against the great weight and preponderance of the evidence. Full opinion, discussion of points one through four.

A further complaint concerned the requested form of an issue about post-injury earning capacity under an amended compensation provision. The jury had found no partial incapacity and therefore did not answer contingent partial-incapacity questions. Any error in declining the insurer's preferred question was harmless in light of that finding. This did not become the reason for reversing the judgment. Full opinion, point-seven discussion.

## The established jury misconduct

The court distinguished allegations of misconduct supported only by conflicting evidence from one act established without dispute. It deferred to supported implied findings that the disputed misconduct had not occurred. The proven incident involved a juror who supplied specialized employment information during the deliberations on whether Price was totally or only partially incapacitated. Full opinion, final misconduct discussion.

The juror drew on union work and a letter from his company discussing caution in hiring people with back injuries. He told the other jurors that employers would demand a statement about an obvious back scar and probably refuse an applicant mentioning a back injury. He claimed that Price could not obtain work at named companies, including Rohm and Haas, Shell, and Sinclair, or other companies with union contracts or employee benefits, because of strict physical examinations. He acknowledged that he was trying to persuade another juror to change sides and regarded this as sharing beneficial personal knowledge. The assertions supplied original evidence about employers' actual practices and Price's labor-market prospects that had not been introduced at trial. Id.; misconduct testimony continuing at 310.

This was misconduct because the juror used his private experience as evidence of material facts, rather than simply using ordinary experience to evaluate the testimony heard in court. The court did not treat the other jurors as forbidden to exercise judgment or weigh expert testimony. Its earlier sufficiency ruling expressly recognized their authority to reconcile Price's testimony with the doctor's opinion. The impermissible step was adding a supposedly informed factual account of specific hiring practices to resolve the close incapacity question.

## Materiality, probable injury, and disposition

The court found both materiality and probable harm under the contemporary Rule 327 framework. Whether Price was totally and permanently incapacitated or only partially impaired was close; his own doctor estimated partial disability at approximately twenty percent. The juror's assertions addressed precisely whether Price could obtain employment and were made to persuade a dissenting juror. Considered with the whole record, the court concluded that injury probably resulted to the insurer. The proved misconduct therefore required reversal even though the admitted trial evidence was sufficient to support Price's verdict. Full opinion, final misconduct and disposition paragraphs.

Collings wrote the appellate opinion; the reproduced complete text identifies no separate opinion or individual joining judges, so none is inferred. The court reversed the trial judgment and remanded the cause. It did not render judgment for the insurer, hold Price's disability evidence legally insufficient, or conclusively establish that Price lacked total incapacity. It required further proceedings because the jury's deliberation process had been materially affected by an improper factual contribution.

## Assigned scope and historical limits

The assigned excerpt occupies PDF pages 59–60, printed pages 59–61. It focuses on evidence sufficiency and jury misconduct and omits much of the accident history, pleading-and-notice rulings, and requested earning-capacity issue. The complete actual opinion was read before drafting; the reproduction contains no judicial footnotes or separate opinions.

The following casebook discussion quotes Head v. Hargrave to distinguish general experience in weighing evidence from specific private facts, and reproduces a later Texas Rule 606(b) limiting testimony about deliberations. Those are editorial comparisons rather than additional holdings of Price. Price's use of juror testimony and its 1960 compensation and procedural rules must be understood in their historical setting. The opinion does not establish that the same testimony would be admissible under every later juror-impeachment rule.
