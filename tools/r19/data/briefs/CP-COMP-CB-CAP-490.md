# Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. General Electric Co.

**446 U.S. 1 (1980).** Chief Justice Burger wrote for a unanimous Supreme Court on April 22, 1980. The assigned account is CivPro physical pages 617–618, printed pages 1222–1223. The decision concerns Rule 54(b) certification for immediate appeal of a final decision on one claim while other claims remain pending.

## Facts and procedural path

Between 1968 and 1972, General Electric entered twenty-one contracts worth approximately $215 million with Curtiss-Wright for components used in nuclear-powered naval vessels. Curtiss-Wright sued in diversity in 1976, alleging fraud, misrepresentation, and breach and requesting damages and reformation. It also sought an outstanding $19 million balance on work already performed. General Electric counterclaimed for $1.9 million in costs allegedly incurred through extraordinary efforts that helped Curtiss-Wright avoid default, and $52 million in alleged unjust enrichment from those efforts. Most claims involved disputed facts. The balance claim presented an isolated question about a contractual release. (3–4.)

General Electric did not dispute the balance’s amount or that it would eventually have to be paid. It argued that payment could await resolution of the other claims because each contract made release of liabilities to the buyer and government a condition of final payment. The district court applied the parties’ chosen New York law, rejected that argument, and awarded Curtiss-Wright summary judgment for $19 million plus statutory prejudgment interest at six percent. The Supreme Court’s decision reviewed certification of that judgment, not the correctness of the underlying release interpretation. (4, 11.)

After an earlier certification request had failed because prejudgment interest was unresolved, Curtiss-Wright sought certification again. The district judge expressly directed final judgment and found no just reason for delay. The judge supplied a written explanation: the adjudicated claim was separate from the remaining claims and counterclaims, later developments would not moot its review, and later appeals would not require a second decision on the same issues. The large liquidated sum, the difference between market and statutory interest rates, and potentially lengthy remaining litigation also favored allowing an appeal now. Both parties appeared financially sound. The judge considered the possible counterclaim setoff but found the other factors outweighed it, and stayed enforcement without bond pending appeal. (4–6 & n.1.)

A divided Third Circuit panel instead treated pending nonfrivolous counterclaims as requiring preservation of the status quo absent unusual or harsh circumstances such as insolvency or economic duress. It dismissed for want of an appealable order and directed the district court to vacate certification. Rehearing was denied, with four judges dissenting. These were lower-court divisions; the Supreme Court’s own ruling was unanimous. (6–7.)

## Issue and two steps under Rule 54(b)

Had the district court abused its discretion by certifying the $19 million judgment despite unresolved counterclaims and a possible setoff? The Supreme Court held that it had not. A nonfrivolous counterclaim did not categorically prevent certification, and the court of appeals had improperly substituted its equity assessment for a reasonable district-court judgment. (7–13.)

The Court separated finality from the timing of appeal. First, the district court must have an ultimate disposition of a cognizable individual claim within a multiple-claims action. A certification cannot simply turn an unfinished claim into a final decision. Second, once finality exists, the judge must determine whether any just reason warrants delay. Even separable final claims do not invariably qualify for immediate appeal. The district court acts as a dispatcher, choosing the appropriate time in light of sound judicial administration. (7–8.)

That second step considers both administrative interests and the parties’ equities. Separability and the danger of repeated appellate consideration matter because the rule must respect the policy against piecemeal appeals. Note 2 adds an important qualification: even a possibility of repeated review is not necessarily an absolute bar. A sufficiently important countervailing reason—potential facilitation of settlement, for example—might support certification. The Court avoided a rigid universal checklist for the many situations that could arise. (8 & n.2, 10–11.)

## Why the counterclaims did not bar certification

Counterclaims, compulsory or permissive, are evaluated by their interrelationship with the certified claim rather than their label or mere presence. Here the district court found factual and legal severability, and the court of appeals did not dispute that conclusion. Its objection instead concerned the possible setoff and the parties’ financial positions. The possibility of an eventual counterclaim recovery was significant, but it was a factor to weigh, not an automatic prohibition. (9, 11–12.)

The Third Circuit had converted the Advisory Committee’s description of the “infrequent harsh case” into a restrictive appellate standard. The Supreme Court rejected that use of the phrase. Appellate courts must scrutinize the relationships among claims to guard against inappropriate piecemeal review. Once those juridical concerns are satisfied, however, substantial deference applies to the district court’s assessment of equities. The reviewing court checks that the assessment is supported by the record and legally sound; it does not reweigh the facts merely because it would strike the balance differently. The equity conclusion should be disturbed only if clearly unreasonable. Certification remains discretionary rather than routine. (9–11.)

The interest-rate differential was a permissible equity consideration. Although setting the statutory prejudgment rate belonged to the legislature, that did not make the difference from market rates irrelevant to timing an otherwise certifiable judgment. The district judge also considered the debt’s large and liquidated character, General Electric’s lack of dispute over its amount or eventual payment, and the months or years of litigation likely to remain. Certification addressed who would benefit from retaining that money while unrelated claims proceeded. It did not revise the statutory interest rate. (11.)

Solvency cut in more than one direction. Curtiss-Wright’s financial soundness meant it could satisfy an eventual judgment on the counterclaims; its threatened insolvency would actually weaken that assurance and could count against certification. Conversely, a threat that General Electric might become unable to pay could favor prompt judgment. Its ability to pay either now or later was not itself a just reason to delay. With neither party likely to become insolvent, the judge reasonably treated solvency as neutral and balanced the remaining factors. (12.)

## Judgment, safeguard, and assigned emphasis

The Supreme Court **vacated the Third Circuit’s judgment and remanded**, concluding that the district court did not abuse its discretion in granting certification. It did not enter judgment on the unresolved claims or counterclaims. The body earlier used “reverse” to describe its conclusion, but the formal mandate at page 13 is vacatur and remand. There were no separate opinions. (7, 13.)

Note 3 explained an additional safeguard under the then-existing Rule 62(h): a certifying court could stay enforcement and prescribe protections, potentially requiring a deposit invested in government obligations pending the remaining litigation. That was a suggested method of protection, not an order directing such a deposit in this case. The actual stay here was without bond and lasted only through the appeal. (13 n.3.)

The assigned note accurately develops the counterclaim and deferential-review reasoning, and identifies the interest-rate differential and likely litigation delay. Its statement that the Third Circuit “reversed” is less precise than the actual procedural history: the circuit dismissed for lack of an appealable order, which the Supreme Court vacated.
