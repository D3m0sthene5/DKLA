# Rush v. City of Maple Heights

**Civil Procedure · CP-136 · 167 Ohio St. 221, 147 N.E.2d 599 (1958) · January 29, 1958**

Rush holds that one wrongful act causing personal injury and property damage to the same person produces one cause of action, with different items of damages. Once the plaintiff obtains judgment on that claim, she cannot bring another action for the omitted damages. The Ohio Supreme Court overruled paragraph four of Vasu’s syllabus, reversed the personal-injury judgment, and entered final judgment for the city.

The assigned editors supply the immediate factual background: the plaintiff fell from a motorcycle and first obtained a $100 municipal-court judgment for property damage caused by the city’s negligent street maintenance. That judgment was affirmed. In a separate personal-injury action arising from the same accident, she invoked the earlier negligence determination to secure trial on damages alone, recovered $12,000, and again prevailed on appeal. The full judicial opinion begins with the city’s eighth assignment of error: allowing separate actions for property and personal injuries improperly split one cause of action. The court found that objection dispositive and did not reach the other assigned errors. (167 Ohio St. at 224, 235; assigned printed page 1262.)

The difficulty was Vasu v. Kohlers, Inc. There, an insurer paid automobile damage and took an assignment before suing the alleged tortfeasor. The insurer lost. The insured later pursued personal-injury damages, and Vasu held that the insurer’s adverse judgment did not bar him. Its syllabus also declared that personal and property injuries invaded different rights and therefore constituted distinct causes of action, subject to issue estoppel from a prior adverse determination. Later decisions had explained and distinguished Vasu without changing that declaration. In particular, Mansker enforced preclusion of a controlling issue actually determined in prior litigation, even where the next action asserted another cause of action. (Id. at 224–29.)

Justice Herbert’s majority examined the competing definitions of a claim. Under the majority rule in other jurisdictions, a single wrongful act supplies the cause of action; injuries to person and property are its consequences. Requiring all available damages in one suit prevents repeated litigation, expense, and delay. The alternative rule focused on different protected rights or different evidence needed to establish bodily injury and property loss. The court found that distinction unpersuasive under modern code pleading. It endorsed the reasoning that different damage evidence does not make the underlying tort into multiple wrongs. The Lord Coleridge dissent quoted in the opinion belongs to the older English Brunsden case, not to Rush’s own dissent. (Id. at 230–33.)

The majority also distinguished Vasu’s actual subrogation setting from Rush. The issue necessary to Vasu’s outcome was the effect of an insurer’s separately acquired interest and litigation upon the insured, not whether a single plaintiff could divide his own recovery against one tortfeasor. Jurisdictions accepting one tort claim commonly recognize insurer-assignment or subrogation situations in which the insurer may separately recover what it has paid, or join to recover that portion. Ohio’s real-party-in-interest statute supported such treatment. A person need not surrender insurance protection as the price of retaining a personal-injury claim. Rush involved no insurer and therefore did not eliminate that distinct situation. (Id. at 233–35.)

The court expressly overruled paragraph four of Vasu’s syllabus. Merely winning the first action did not preserve the remaining damage demand: the omitted personal-injury recovery belonged within the same claim. This distinguishes claim preclusion from issue preclusion. The plaintiff attempted to use the first negligence finding favorably in the second suit, but claim preclusion prevented the second suit itself. (Id. at 235.)

Six justices supported reversal: Herbert, Chief Justice Weygandt, and Justices Stewart, Taft, Matthias, and Bell. Stewart separately concurred, emphasizing that Vasu’s two-claim discussion was unnecessary to its decision. He would have been more reluctant to displace a necessary holding. Justice Zimmerman dissented. He acknowledged respectable arguments on both sides but stressed stability: a unanimous court had announced the earlier rule less than thirteen years before, and subsequent cases had not displaced it. He found no compelling changed conditions or experience justifying reversal of that settled guidance. (Id. at 235–37.)

The disposition was **reversal and final judgment for the city**, not a new trial. The full reported corpus includes the majority, concurrence, and dissent, without judicial footnotes; the assigned passage retains shortened versions of all three on printed pages 1262–1266. This brief addresses the historical Ohio decision and its identified subrogation distinction without an unchecked current-law conclusion.
