Citation
Hanna v. Plumer, 380 U.S. 460 (1965). Supreme Court of the United States; argued January 21, 1965; decided
April 26, 1965. Chief Justice Warren wrote the Court’s opinion. Justice Harlan concurred in the judgment with
an opinion; Justice Black concurred in the result without an opinion. Assigned casebook pp. 468–476. Full
opinion: Full judicial opinion . Rule 4(d)(1) is the numbering used in this historical decision. The account
principally follows the assigned opinion; the precise bond date and discussion of actual notice supplement it
from the full opinion’s first footnote.
Parties
Hanna, an Ohio citizen, was the injured plaintiff and petitioner. Plumer, a Massachusetts citizen, was executor
of the estate of Louise Plumer Osgood, the Massachusetts driver whose negligence allegedly caused Hanna’s
injury. Plumer defended in his representative capacity; he was not alleged to be the driver responsible for the
accident. Hanna sought damages exceeding the then-applicable $10,000 diversity threshold. 380 U.S. at 461.
Material Facts
The underlying automobile accident occurred in South Carolina. Osgood had died before Hanna sued her
executor in federal district court in Massachusetts. On February 8, 1963, copies of the summons and complaint
were left with Plumer’s wife at his residence. The parties agreed that this method complied with Federal Rule
4(d)(1), which allowed service at the defendant’s dwelling upon a resident of suitable age and discretion.
Whether that method was legally sufficient, despite the competing Massachusetts provision, was the issue;
Osgood’s negligence was not adjudicated by the Supreme Court. 380 U.S. at 461–463.
Massachusetts General Laws chapter 197, § 9 combined a one-year period for commencing claims against an
estate with particular notice requirements. In-hand service on the executor was one method; the statute also
permitted acceptance of service or filing specified notice in the probate registry. Hanna conceded that she had
not complied with those methods. The commencement deadline itself was not disputed: Plumer had filed his
bond on March 1, 1962, and Hanna filed the complaint on February 6, 1963. The controversy concerned the
manner of service within the relevant period, not an attempt to revive an already late complaint. 380 U.S. at
461–463 & n.1.
Both the state rule and the Federal Rule sought to provide notice, but they accepted different means. Plumer’s
affidavit asserted that he had not been personally served and had not accepted service; it did not allege that he
lacked actual notice of Hanna’s action. That fact supported the Court’s explanation of why abode service need
not undermine the state’s notice objective. 380 U.S. at 462 n.1.
Procedural History
Hanna filed in the District of Massachusetts on February 6, 1963. Plumer’s answer asserted noncompliance
with the Massachusetts statute. On October 17, the district court granted his motion for summary judgment,
relying on York and Ragan to treat the state requirements as controlling. The ruling disposed of the suit
because of service rather than resolving the underlying negligence and damages questions. 380 U.S. at
461–462.
The First Circuit affirmed. It regarded the Massachusetts legislature’s insistence on personal notification within
the year as substantive and therefore controlling under Erie. Hanna acknowledged her noncompliance with

Issue
When a Federal Rule expressly permits abode service and a state provision governing suits against executors
requires a different method, must a federal diversity court disregard the Federal Rule because state law would
terminate the action? More generally, does the ordinary Erie outcome inquiry determine the validity of an
applicable Federal Rule, or must that Rule be tested under the Rules Enabling Act and the Constitution? 380
U.S. at 464–474.
Rule and Holding
Rule 4(d)(1) governed. It regulated the method of notifying a defendant of litigation, fell within the Rules
Enabling Act’s authorization of federal practice and procedure, and did not violate constitutional limits. The
state provision therefore could not displace the applicable and valid Federal Rule. 380 U.S. at 464–465,
469–474.
The Court distinguished two inquiries. When a Federal Rule actually covers the disputed issue, its applicability
depends on its scope and validity under the Enabling Act and constitutional authority. When no Federal Rule
covers the issue, ordinary Erie analysis considers the policies against forum shopping and inequitable
administration of law. A procedural difference’s capacity to affect the outcome after a party has failed to
comply does not, by itself, settle either inquiry. 380 U.S. at 466–472.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the First Circuit. Hanna’s conceded compliance with Rule 4(d)(1) supplied the
governing measure of service, so the adverse judgment based on Massachusetts service requirements could not
stand. The decision did not establish negligence, assess damages, or give Hanna judgment on her
personal-injury claim. 380 U.S. at 464, 474.
Reasoning
The Court first examined what the Federal Rule regulated. Service provisions establish how a person is
informed that judicial proceedings have begun. That is part of the machinery for enforcing rights, rather than a
rule defining the underlying duty of care. Applying the Rule could affect the course of litigation, but incidental
effects on a litigant’s position do not automatically abridge a substantive right within the Enabling Act. The
Court used Sibbach and Murphree to distinguish procedural implementation from alteration of the rules
deciding liability. 380 U.S. at 464–465.
Plumer offered a straightforward outcome argument: state law would give him immediate victory, whereas the
Federal Rule would allow Hanna’s claim to continue; therefore, York required state law. Warren rejected that
reasoning because nearly every procedural rule can become decisive after someone violates it. A missed
pleading deadline can end a case without transforming every timetable into substantive law. Erie asks about the
character of the difference and the policies it affects, rather than treating the eventual consequence of
noncompliance as conclusive. 380 U.S. at 466–469.
The Court’s discussion of the two Erie policies was prospective in an important sense. When Hanna chose a
forum, the Massachusetts rule did not make recovery unavailable; she could have complied by using a different
service method. Allowing abode service instead of in-hand service had little apparent connection to choosing a
federal court for an advantage in the governing rights. Nor did the difference substantially discriminate against

Massachusetts citizens when both methods were designed to provide notice. Warren therefore doubted that
ordinary Erie analysis would compel the state method even without an on-point Federal Rule. 380 U.S. at
467–469.
That discussion was not the decisive reason for enforcing Rule 4. Warren separately explained that a court
confronting a promulgated Federal Rule is not exercising the same relatively unguided law-selection function
as a court confronting a gap in federal procedure. Congress authorized a national rulemaking system. A court
must apply a Rule that covers the matter unless it exceeds the Enabling Act or constitutional authority.
Ordinary Erie policies are not a free-standing license to disregard an otherwise valid Rule whenever state law
differs. 380 U.S. at 469–471.
Scope must be established before validity. Earlier decisions applying state law despite an argument about a
Federal Rule had often interpreted that Rule as addressing a different question. A Rule about pleading
contributory negligence, for example, need not allocate the burden of proving it. Here the competing provisions
actually addressed the manner of service: the Federal Rule permitted leaving process with a suitable resident,
while Massachusetts did not accept that method for this purpose. The collision could not be avoided by
assigning the two provisions separate functions. 380 U.S. at 470 & n.12.
The constitutional argument also differed from Erie’s rejection of general federal common law. Erie concerned
a substantive duty of care and no applicable Federal Rule. Constitutional authority to establish federal courts,
together with the Necessary and Proper Clause, permits Congress to regulate their practice and pleading. That
power extends to matters rationally classifiable as procedural even when they lie near the substance–procedure
boundary. It does not permit rules unsupported by federal authority, but service within federal litigation was
well within the relevant power. 380 U.S. at 471–472.
Warren finally linked the result to uniform federal administration. Federal courts need not import every local
procedural detail merely because it can affect enforcement of a state-created claim. Enforcing the valid national
service rule preserved Congress’s rulemaking design while leaving the governing tort law intact. The Court did
not suggest that state substantive rights generally disappear in diversity cases. 380 U.S. at 472–474.
Separate Opinions
Justice Harlan agreed with reversal but objected to the breadth of the majority’s framework. He understood
Erie principally as an allocation of authority over people’s primary conduct. His proposed question was
whether the competing rule would substantially affect conduct that the constitutional system leaves to state
regulation. A Federal Rule could not override such regulation simply because a reasonable person could label
the Rule procedural. He also regarded a bare forum-shopping test as overinclusive because litigants routinely
choose courts for legitimate procedural advantages. 380 U.S. at 474–476 (Harlan, J., concurring).
Harlan illustrated his distinction through Ragan and Cohen. He thought Ragan wrongly elevated a short
difference in the time at which defendants could regard claims as ended, whereas Cohen’s security requirement
materially deterred shareholder strike suits. In Hanna itself, the state policy of enabling reliable estate
administration would suffer little from requiring an executor to check his household or the federal courthouse
as well as the probate registry. He therefore reached the same result through his primary-conduct analysis. 380
U.S. at 476–478.
Justice Black concurred only in the result. The report supplies no explanatory Black opinion, so an account of
his supposed reasoning would be speculative. 380 U.S. at 474.

Notes
The decision must not be reduced to either “outcome never matters” or “the Federal Rules always win.” The
majority’s method requires identifying the precise issue, construing the Rule, and testing an actually applicable
Rule’s validity. Its ordinary Erie discussion remains relevant where federal law does not cover the question.
Harlan’s concurrence offers a competing account of the constitutional boundary, rather than the Court’s
governing rationale. 380 U.S. at 469–478.
The statutory text and rule numbering above describe the provisions litigated in 1965. The case concerns a

with Walker’s separate question about tolling a state limitations period. Hanna, 380 U.S. at 462 n.1, 470;
Walker, 446 U.S. 740, 749–752 (1980).
