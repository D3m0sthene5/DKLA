# Trump v. Wilcox

**145 S. Ct. 1415 (2025); No. 24A966 • Supreme Court of the United States • May 22, 2025**

The Court issued an unsigned stay order. Justice Kagan dissented, joined by Justices Sotomayor and Jackson; the recorded split was six to three. ## Facts and procedural posture

President Trump removed Gwynne Wilcox from the National Labor Relations Board and Cathy Harris from the Merit Systems Protection Board without identifying qualifying cause under their respective statutory removal protections. Section 153(a) protected NLRB members against removal except for neglect of duty or malfeasance; § 1202(d) protected MSPB members against removal except for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance. District-court orders of March 4 and March 6, 2025, enjoined the removals. The Government applied for a Supreme Court stay of those orders while appellate review proceeded. The Chief Justice referred the application to the Court. Court order, slip p. 1; Kagan dissent, slip pp. 2–3.

This was an application for interim relief, not a final decision on the agencies' constitutional structure. The assigned editorial account interprets the order as signaling the vulnerability of Humphrey's Executor. That interpretation should be distinguished from what the order formally resolved at this stage.

## Issue and interim ruling

Should the orders protecting the officers against removal remain operative pending appeal and possible Supreme Court review?

**The Court granted the stay.** It reasoned that the President generally may remove executive officers exercising executive power on his behalf, subject to narrow exceptions recognized by precedent. The Government was likely to show that the NLRB and MSPB exercised considerable executive power. But the Court **expressly reserved** whether either agency fell within a recognized exception, leaving that issue for full briefing and argument. Court order, slip p. 1.

Accordingly, this order did not itself formally overrule Humphrey's Executor or finally adjudicate the constitutional validity of either agency's removal protections. The dissent argued that its practical effect nevertheless revised existing law without the normal process.

## Court's reasoning

The unsigned order relied on Article II's vesting of executive power and cited Seila Law for the President's general removal authority and its exceptions. Its merits assessment concerned the substantial executive functions of these agencies while reserving their ultimate fit within an exception. Court order, slip p. 1.

On the equities, it considered the Government's risk from a removed officer continuing to exercise executive power greater than the risk to an officer temporarily prevented from performing her statutory duties. It characterized interim equitable relief as balancing interests while litigation continued rather than conclusively determining rights. Avoiding repeated removal and reinstatement also supported a stay. Court order, slip pp. 1–2.

The respondents argued that the Government's reasoning necessarily implicated the Federal Reserve Board and other Federal Open Market Committee members. The Court disagreed, describing the Federal Reserve as a uniquely structured, quasi-private entity connected with the historical tradition of the First and Second Banks of the United States. Court order, slip p. 2. This was an express distinction in the stay order, not a final merits adjudication of a Federal Reserve officer's removal. The assigned excerpt's quotation of that passage finishes on p. 15.

## Kagan dissent, joined by Sotomayor and Jackson

Kagan would deny the stay under Humphrey's Executor. She described the NLRB and MSPB as multimember, bipartisan expert bodies within the longstanding exception recognized for independent agencies. Their statutory protections therefore remained enforceable, and the President's concededly cause-free removals lacked a valid basis under existing precedent. The emergency docket should not overturn or narrow that precedent without full briefing, argument, and deliberation. Dissent, slip pp. 1–4.

She criticized the order's merits analysis for acknowledging executive power while declining to answer whether the agencies fit the exception, even though a stay required a sufficient likelihood-of-success assessment. If considerable executive power alone excluded the agencies, that would signal a substantial change to Humphrey's Executor through an abbreviated proceeding. Dissent, slip pp. 4–5.

The equities, she argued, were not merely the officers' personal interest in holding jobs against an undifferentiated Government interest. Congress and the public had an interest in the independent structure Congress enacted. The President's interest was only one component of Government, and decades of experience under removal restrictions undermined the asserted urgency. The dissent's asterisk note also challenged the disruption rationale: reinstatement had already occurred, so removing the officers again itself disrupted the situation unless the Court assumed their eventual removal. Dissent, slip pp. 5–7 & p. 7 n.*.

Finally, Kagan questioned the Federal Reserve distinction. Its independence rested on similar legal foundations, while Seila Law's cited footnote had assumed rather than established a special historical status. A reassurance concerning the Federal Reserve did not explain abandoning the existing protections for the other agencies. Dissent, slip pp. 7–8.

## Exact disposition and limits

The March 4 and March 6 district-court orders were **stayed pending the D.C. Circuit appeal and disposition of a timely certiorari petition**. If certiorari were denied, the stay would terminate automatically; if granted, it would terminate upon transmission of the Supreme Court's judgment. Court order, slip p. 2. Those conditions concern the scope of this May 2025 order and should not be described as a permanent injunction, a final reversal on the merits, or a present-status report about the later litigation.
