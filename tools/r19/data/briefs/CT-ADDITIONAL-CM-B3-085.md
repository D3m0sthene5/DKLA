# Shear v. National Rifle Association of America
606 F.2d 1251 (D.C. Cir. 1979), decided August 15, amended August 22, 1979 | Contracts

## Court, posture, and disputed facts
Judge MacKinnon wrote for a panel comprising Judges Tamm, MacKinnon, and visiting District Judge John H. Pratt. The published report contains no separate opinion or numerical vote. The district court dismissed Richard Shear’s complaint without an opinion under Rule 12(b)(6). The appeal concerned contract, fraud, and rescission claims; claims for harm to professional reputation and quantum meruit apparently were not pursued on appeal. All factual allegations described below were accepted for purposes of testing the pleading. The court expressly declined to decide whether Shear could prove them and rejected the NRA’s effort to substitute its account of the actual facts at this stage. (1251, 1253 & star note, nn. 2–5, 1259, 1261 n. 45.)

The NRA planned to move its headquarters from Washington to Colorado Springs, bought land, and held a groundbreaking ceremony in November 1976. Its treasurer, Binswanger, instructed Shear to obtain offers for the Washington building; he found two prospective purchasers. On April 1, 1977, the Management Committee established a sealed-bid procedure and promised unanimously to recommend the winning bid to the Board at its May 23 meeting. Board approval was necessary for a sale. On April 29 the committee selected the highest bid, procured by Shear. The purchaser and NRA president signed a sales contract subject to Board approval. On May 6 the executive vice president signed a brokerage agreement promising Shear $150,000, instead of four percent of the sales price, contingent on settlement. (1253–1254 & nn. 6–13.)

Later in May, dissident members and officers took control at the annual meeting. New bylaws prohibited moving the headquarters and stripped the Board of authority to approve a sale of NRA real property. Only afterward was the proposed sale put to the Board for a vote; the Management Committee declined to recommend approval and the Board did not approve. No settlement occurred and no commission was paid. Shear alleged that Binswanger and other officers had known of the intended obstruction during negotiations but continued to promise a favorable recommendation without disclosing that intention. (1254 & nn. 14–18.)

## Conditional commission and the prevention doctrine
Ordinarily, a conditional duty does not become enforceable before its condition occurs. But a promisor cannot wrongfully prevent the condition and then rely on its absence to escape the promised performance. The court explained this through Corbin, Williston, earlier cases, and the tentative Second Restatement’s rule excusing nonoccurrence when a party’s breach substantially contributes to it. The draft provision was then numbered section 269; the opinion is an account of that historical source, rather than a claim that the tentative numbering remains current. (1254–1255 & nn. 19–21.)

The NRA invoked Dixon v. Bernstein, in which a broker had assumed the risk of a sale’s failure under the relevant contractual arrangement. The court explained that prevention must be wrongful: if the contract authorizes the conduct that stops the condition, that conduct is not a breach. Calling assumption of that risk an exception to prevention is therefore somewhat imprecise; it follows from the doctrine’s premise. The controlling question was which risks Shear’s arrangement assigned to him. (1255–1256 & nn. 22–26.)

Shear assumed that an authorized Board could reject a sale even after the Management Committee unanimously recommended it. He did not, on the pleaded facts, assume that the committee would withdraw its promised recommendation or that new bylaws would deprive the Board of power before submission. Those two alleged failures supported wrongful prevention, even though approval and settlement never occurred. The NRA’s argument that it never agreed to sell missed the claim: Shear alleged obstruction of the agreed approval process, not an unconditional obligation to convey the building. (1256–1257 & nn. 25–27.)

The NRA further argued that whether settlement would have occurred was speculative. The court gave three answers. Some counterfactual reasoning is inevitable in prevention cases; the pleaded allegation of prevention had to be accepted at this stage; and the tentative Restatement required substantial contribution rather than the older strict but-for formulation. Its comment nevertheless reserved a defense where the condition would not have occurred regardless of the lack of cooperation, placing that showing on the party in breach. Even under a but-for test, the complaint did not rule out evidence that the Board routinely approved recommended sales or that its members would have approved this one. Those were possible forms of proof, not evidence already received. (1257–1258 & nn. 28–32.)

## Corporate authority and fraud
The NRA argued that New York’s Not-For-Profit Corporation Law section 509 required Board approval of a property sale and therefore left officers without authority to hire Shear or promise a recommendation. The court separated the transactions: a statutory restriction on selling property did not itself bar hiring a broker to find a purchaser. Section 712 also permitted delegation of Board authority to designated committees, subject to express statutory exceptions reproduced in note 34. Alternatively, the NRA’s groundbreaking, purchaser-selection procedure, and officers’ knowledge of Shear’s sustained work could support apparent authority reasonably relied on in good faith. The pleading entitled him to prove authority; it did not finally establish that every promise was authorized. (1258–1259 & nn. 33–35.)

The fraud count alleged a deliberate misstatement, deceptive intent, reasonable reliance, and resulting damage. If Binswanger knew about the impending takeover and promised a recommendation anyway, a factfinder could infer an intention to induce Shear to accept a settlement condition while allowing the NRA to abandon the sale without paying him. The alleged assurance concerned the committee’s recommendation, not a guarantee that the Board would approve. Although promises of future conduct ordinarily do not establish fraud merely because they are broken, note 39 explained the exception for a contemporaneous intention not to perform. Corporate authority remained a matter Shear could try to prove. His allegation that officers who signed the May 6 agreement lacked knowledge of the takeover was not necessarily inconsistent with fraud based on Binswanger’s knowledge. (1259–1260 & nn. 36–44.)

## Rescission, judgment, and significance
The mutual-mistake count failed. The alleged mistake was a belief that the committee would recommend the sale in the future, rather than an erroneous belief about facts existing when the agreement was made. A prediction or promise about future conduct did not become a mutual mistake of existing fact because it proved wrong. This analysis did not defeat the distinct allegation that Binswanger misrepresented his contemporaneous intention. (1260 & nn. 43–44.)

The court reversed dismissal of the contract and fraud counts and remanded for further proceedings, while affirming dismissal of rescission. It awarded no commission and expressly made no prediction about success at trial. Shear illustrates how a settlement condition can allocate ordinary approval risk without authorizing a party to dismantle the promised approval process. It also separates breach through prevention, fraud about present intention, and an unsuccessful mistake theory about future performance. (1260–1261 & n. 45.)
