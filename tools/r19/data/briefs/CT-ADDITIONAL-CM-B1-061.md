# Barfield v. Commerce Bank, N.A.
484 F.3d 1276 (10th Cir. May 1, 2007) | Contracts

## Decision and posture

The Tenth Circuit reversed dismissal of James and Chris Barfield’s § 1981 claim, affirmed denial of permission to amend their complaint, and remanded for further proceedings. Offering a large bill in exchange for smaller bills could constitute a proposed contract supported by consideration, even without a fee or an immediate profit for the bank. That conclusion permitted the discrimination claim to proceed; it did not establish intentional discrimination or award damages. McConnell J. wrote the sole opinion for the Kelly, McConnell, and Holmes panel; no separate writing or numerical division was reported. (1276–1277, 1279–1281.)

## Alleged transactions and proceedings

Chris Barfield, an African-American man, requested change for a $50 bill at a Wichita Commerce Bank branch and was refused because he did not hold an account. The next day his father, James, sent a white friend, John Polson, to request change. Polson received it without being asked about an account. A few minutes later James requested change for a $100 bill and was refused unless he was an account-holder. James then enlisted a white news reporter and the reporter’s African-American colleague. In separate visits, only the African-American visitor was asked whether he held an account. These were the transaction accounts underlying a complaint dismissed for failure to state a claim, rather than appellate findings following a trial. (1277.)

The Barfields sued under 42 U.S.C. § 1981. While the dismissal motion remained pending, the parties engaged in unsuccessful mediation and negotiations, and the Barfields sought class certification and amendment. The introductory procedural account mentions an effort to add a Title VI claim; the amendment analysis specifically addresses proposed class allegations. The district court denied the plaintiffs’ motions and dismissed the original action. The appellate court’s express disposition reversed the dismissal, affirmed denial of amendment, and remanded; it did not grant class certification. (1277, 1280–1281.)

## Protected contracting activity

The opinion recognized that the 1991 statutory addition extended “make and enforce contracts” to performance, modification, termination, and the enjoyment of contractual benefits and conditions. The circuit’s test required membership in a protected class, intent to discriminate because of race, and interference with an activity protected by § 1981. Only the third requirement was disputed in this appeal. Retail customers’ tangible attempts to enter transactions could trigger the statute even before a purchase was completed. The question was therefore whether the proposed bill exchange was a contractual transaction rather than a legally gratuitous service. (1277–1278.)

Under Kansas common law, consideration could consist of a benefit, interest, right, detriment, forbearance, or responsibility bargained for in exchange. The district court had accepted the bank’s position that providing change produced no benefit or detriment and therefore no consideration. The appellate court disagreed. The customer proposed exchanging something of value—a large-denomination bill—for other bills the customer preferred. Equality in face value did not eliminate the exchange or make its consideration insufficient. Parties could attach value to what they received without a court assigning the bargain a separate financial gain. (1278–1279.)

The bank urged a different characterization: it supplied a free service, so the absence of remuneration defeated contracting protection. But a profit-making business may offer a transaction producing no immediate gain, or even a loss, to encourage other business. Such a transaction does not for that reason lack consideration. Potential goodwill or future dealings can carry value without a precisely measurable return. The alleged practice of making bill exchange available to one race while denying it to another therefore fell within § 1981’s contracting protection. This reasoning did not eliminate consideration from contract law or decide that every gratuitous act creates an enforceable contract. (1279.)

## Hampton distinguished

The bank relied on Hampton v. Dillard Department Stores, involving coupons for fragrance samples offered to an aunt and niece. The aunt had made the purchase required for the contractual benefit, whereas the niece had neither made nor attempted a purchase. The denial of the niece’s claim reflected her failure to satisfy that particular offer’s terms, rather than a categorical rule excluding all free samples and services from § 1981. Hampton had reserved the broader question, and its discussion recognized the commercial benefit of attracting a customer through a store to obtain a sample. The analogy thus did not justify dismissal of the Barfields’ proposed exchange. (1279–1280.)

## Amendment and exact result

The proposed November 5, 2005 amendment listed generalized accusations that the bank denied African-American customers accounts, several kinds of loans, currency exchanges, and check-cashing services. Unlike the specific original exchange allegations, the proposed allegations identified no affected persons, dates, or incidents supporting those categories, and did not allege that the Barfields themselves were denied the additional services. Applying the notice-pleading authorities then cited, the court concluded that the proposed pleading did not provide enough information for the bank to respond. Denial of leave to amend was therefore not an abuse of discretion. (1280–1281.)

The court reversed the ruling on the motion to dismiss, affirmed denial of permission to amend, and remanded. The original discrimination claim survived its consideration objection, while the defective broader amendment did not. This is an account of the May 1, 2007 decision and its cited procedural framework, without a certification that its historical pleading discussion states current law. (1281.)
