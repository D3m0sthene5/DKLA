# English v. Trump
279 F. Supp. 3d 307 (D.D.C. Jan. 10, 2018) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Competing succession claims and litigation

Richard Cordray resigned as Director of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau effective at midnight on November 24, 2017, before completing his statutory five-year term. Earlier that day, he appointed his chief of staff, Leandra English, Deputy Director effective at noon and publicly explained that her appointment would provide an orderly succession. Dodd-Frank provided that the Deputy Director should serve as acting Director during the Director’s absence or unavailability. President Trump instead designated Mick Mulvaney, the Senate-confirmed Director of the Office of Management and Budget, to perform the CFPB Director’s functions under the Federal Vacancies Reform Act. The presidential memorandum made that designation effective at 12:01 a.m. on November 26. Those different effective dates should not be collapsed into one appointment event. (311–315.)

The Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel and the CFPB’s General Counsel concluded that the FVRA remained available despite Dodd-Frank’s succession provision. Senior CFPB management agreed to recognize Mulvaney. On November 27 he began working at the Bureau, receiving requests for decisions and issuing directives that staff followed, while continuing to serve at OMB. English continued working as Deputy Director and held herself out as acting Director in emails and meetings. Mulvaney asked her to stop doing so and to perform customary deputy duties; he publicly stated that he was not considering terminating her. This was a contest over exercising the directorship, rather than a completed dismissal from employment. (313–315.)

English sued Trump and Mulvaney on November 26 for declaratory and injunctive relief. After hearings on November 27 and 28, Judge Timothy Kelly denied her temporary restraining order. She amended her complaint and sought a preliminary injunction on December 6; the court heard that motion on December 22 and decided it on January 10, 2018. She sought to restrain the President from appointing anyone other than her, require withdrawal of Mulvaney’s designation, and prohibit his acting service. Her statutory and Appointments Clause claims depended on her contention that the President lacked statutory authority for the designation. This reported decision denies that preliminary injunction; it is not the earlier TRO ruling or a final dismissal of every claim. (307, 315, 316–317, 337.)

## Governing statutes, arguments, and preliminary-relief standard

The FVRA applied when a Senate-confirmed officer of an executive agency died, resigned, or otherwise became unable to perform the office’s functions. Its default made the first assistant the acting officer, but § 3345(a)(2) permitted the President to choose another officer already serving in a position requiring presidential appointment and Senate confirmation. The statute generally limited acting service to 210 days and restricted certain nominees’ eligibility. Section 3347 made the FVRA exclusive unless, among other things, another statute expressly supplied an acting-officer mechanism. Section 3349c excluded specified offices, including appointed members of governing multimember independent bodies. (312.)

Dodd-Frank created the CFPB as an independent bureau within the Federal Reserve System, with a single Senate-confirmed Director. As the law then stood, the Director had a five-year term and presidential removal protection for cause; the constitutionality of that restriction was before the D.C. Circuit en banc. Section 5491(b)(5) provided that the Director appointed the Deputy Director, who would serve as acting Director during the Director’s absence or unavailability. Section 5491(a) also applied federal laws dealing with federal officers to the CFPB unless otherwise expressly provided by law. (313 & n.1.)

English argued that Dodd-Frank’s mandatory, later-enacted, agency-specific succession provision displaced the FVRA’s permissive general rule. Alternatively, she argued that a still-serving OMB Director could not lead an independent CFPB. Defendants maintained that the statutes provided compatible alternatives and that neither prohibited Mulvaney’s selection. (317, 319, 331–333.)

A preliminary injunction required likely success, likely irreparable injury, favorable equities, and consistency with the public interest; the last two considerations merged because the government opposed relief. Kelly did not decide whether Winter had eliminated the circuit’s earlier sliding-scale approach or whether English sought a mandatory injunction subject to a higher standard. She failed even the less demanding alternatives. Lack of likely success and lack of irreparable injury each independently supported denial. (315–317, 333–336.)

## Why the FVRA remained available

The FVRA covered the vacancy on its own terms: the CFPB was an executive agency, its Director a Senate-confirmed officer, and Cordray had resigned. The multimember-body exception did not apply simply because the CFPB Director also sat on the FDIC board. That seat was held ex officio by operation of statute, whereas the exception concerned members appointed to the multimember body by the President with Senate consent. Dodd-Frank had not added the CFPB Director to the FVRA’s express exclusions. The court declined to infer a further exception from a generalized conception of agency independence. (317–319 & n.2.)

The court then read the two statutes together. Section 3347’s removal of FVRA exclusivity where another acting-officer statute existed made the FVRA a nonexclusive means, not an unavailable means. Hooks, involving the NLRB General Counsel, supported that general interaction, although the court acknowledged that its agency-specific provision did not contain Dodd-Frank’s particular mandatory language and predated the FVRA. Hooks therefore supplied an analogy, rather than resolving the precise statutory conflict. (319–320.)

Dodd-Frank’s own express-statement provision reinforced continued FVRA availability. English’s reliance on decisions allowing later Congresses to depart from earlier statutory express-statement requirements missed the temporal distinction: here the later Congress itself specified how preexisting federal officer laws applied to its new bureau. The requirement therefore guided interpretation of the legislation being enacted rather than purporting to bind a future Congress. No provision expressly prohibited presidential use of the FVRA. The court questioned whether absence or unavailability necessarily encompassed a permanent resignation, but did not decide that question: even assuming it did, those words did not expressly exclude the FVRA. (320–323.)

Nor did “shall” end the inquiry. Read in statutory context, Dodd-Frank’s direction that the Deputy Director serve was a default qualified by the President’s FVRA choice. Other mandatory service provisions coexisted with removal or alternative-designation provisions. Harmonious reading and the presumption against implied repeal favored giving both statutes effect; the later-specific-over-earlier-general canons did not control without an irreconcilable conflict. The FVRA was itself particularly specific about vacancies caused by resignation. (323–326 & n.3.)

That interpretation did not render the deputy succession provision superfluous. Dodd-Frank did not expressly impose the FVRA’s time limits or its relevant nominee restrictions, and its absence language could cover temporary absences beyond the FVRA’s apparent reach. Constitutional avoidance supplied additional confirmation, not a constitutional holding: English’s theory could let an outgoing, removal-protected Director choose a successor insulated from presidential control, creating serious Take Care Clause concerns. The court expressly declined to decide whether that theory actually violated the Constitution. Its text-based conclusion did not depend on avoidance. Notes 4 and 5 examined the FHFA comparator and judicial appointment of inferior officers rather than announcing new categorical constitutional rules. (326–329 & nn.4–5.)

Agency independence did not override the enacted mechanisms. Funding through the Federal Reserve, specified communications protections, and other statutory features remained. The FVRA also required the President’s selected officer to have received Senate confirmation for an existing office and limited acting service, while English’s proposed succession mechanism involved no Senate confirmation of the deputy. Legislative history added little: omission of an earlier bill’s explicit FVRA reference, without explanation, did not reliably establish an intent to displace that statute. (329–331.)

## Mulvaney’s eligibility and equitable grounds

Dodd-Frank’s description of an independent bureau did not itself prohibit simultaneous OMB service. Congress expressly barred the Director or Deputy Director from holding positions in specified financial institutions and regulated entities, but did not include OMB. Provisions requiring provision of financial information to OMB while disclaiming a consultation or approval obligation likewise did not create the asserted separation wall or an eligibility bar. Mulvaney’s Senate-confirmed OMB position qualified him under § 3345(a)(2). The court reserved defendants’ separate objection concerning the de facto officer doctrine and quo warranto because English already lacked likely success on her substantive theory. (331–333 & n.6.)

English also failed to demonstrate irreparable injury, even assuming her merits theory was correct. She remained employed, faced no identified imminent termination, and supplied no declaration facts describing personal harm. Berry, concerning removed Civil Rights Commissioners and a commission about to expire without completing its report, differed: the CFPB continued operating, and any injury to it or third parties did not establish English’s own irreparable injury. Her claim that a permanent appointment would soon eliminate her acting-service opportunity was speculative. Moreover, she had never been the uncontested acting Director; the last uncontested position was her deputy office, so an injunction placing her in the directorship would not merely preserve that status quo. (333–336.)

Equities and public interest also favored denial. Even accepting her asserted statutory right to function, Mulvaney asserted the corresponding competing right and English was unlikely to prevail. The President, Bureau counsel, and senior management recognized Mulvaney, and the agency was operating on that basis. An injunction would disrupt that arrangement rather than produce greater clarity. The order therefore denied the preliminary-injunction motion. Kelly was the sole deciding judge, with no separate opinion. (336–337.)
