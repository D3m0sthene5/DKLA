# International Ore & Fertilizer Corp. v. SGS Control Services, Inc.

**Civil Procedure · CP-133 · 38 F.3d 1279 (2d Cir. 1994) · October 24, 1994**

International Ore illustrates the difference between defending a judgment on another legal theory and obtaining a larger judgment. The Second Circuit rejected the district court’s negligent-misrepresentation theory but affirmed its $713,666.27 award on a contract theory. Interore had not appealed the dismissal of its contract claim, so the court declined to increase the award to its full contract damages. It also affirmed sanctions imposed on SGS’s lawyers for repeatedly relitigating causation.

Interore sold 22,202 metric tons of fertilizer to East Coast Fertilizer in New Zealand for $4,118,287, including cost and freight. It chartered the vessel Adelina and engaged SGS to inspect the holds and certify their suitability for the cargo. The vessel had carried coal immediately before this shipment and barley before that. Cleaning and painting left barley behind structural stringers. A Swedish SGS affiliate inspected two holds; SGS’s Tampa inspector spent approximately half an hour examining the other three, walking through them without looking behind the stringers. He later learned about the earlier cargoes but did not return. SGS issued a certificate of readiness and charged $150 for the Tampa inspection. When the ship reached New Zealand, authorities found barley contamination. They would permit unloading only if buyers were informed and restrictions protected barley-growing areas. East Coast refused the shipment. After efforts to find other buyers failed, Interore sent the fertilizer to Antwerp and sold it in smaller lots, incurring additional expense and losses. The facts concern an inspection service: SGS neither owned the vessel nor undertook the transportation. The assigned editors’ bracketed introduction inaccurately describes SGS as the transporter and shipowner. (38 F.3d at 1281–82.)

Interore pleaded contract, warranty, negligence, and negligent misrepresentation. Following a bench trial on liability, Judge Tenney dismissed the contract claim, reasoning that the small fee and informal transaction did not warrant imposing large consequential liability. He rejected negligence because SGS’s inspection duty arose from contract, but imposed liability for negligent misrepresentation in the certificate. He also attributed half the responsibility to Interore for not warning SGS about New Zealand’s strict requirements. A later damages proceeding before Magistrate Judge Gershon produced the $713,666.27 judgment. SGS appealed. Interore cross-appealed the contributory-negligence finding, but did **not** cross-appeal the contract dismissal. Thus, saying that Interore filed no cross-appeal at all would overstate the actual procedural history. (Id. at 1282–83, 1285–86.)

Judge Winter’s majority opinion, joined by Judge Van Graafeiland, held that the contract governed SGS’s duty and that no independent tort duty supported negligent misrepresentation. The same inadequate inspection could not coherently be treated as a tort while being excused as a contractual failure. The majority then concluded that SGS had breached its obligation to inspect with reasonable care. The certification was the service’s central purpose, and SGS could foresee that cargo interests would rely on it. The parties’ repeated professional transactions and formal certificates were significant even though the particular arrangements were brief and the fee small. A low fee alone did not excuse foreseeable consequential loss: SGS could price the risk or insure against it. The court distinguished a vessel-classification certificate intended for insurance purposes from the cargo-readiness certification here. It also explained that the district court had given excessive precedential force to language in Vitol that the other panel members had not joined. The court did not decide whether federal maritime or New York law controlled because the relevant rules produced the same result. (Id. at 1283–85 & n.1.)

The appellate problem was whether this rejected contract claim could nevertheless sustain the existing judgment. Under the rule drawn from American Railway Express, an appellee may defend a decree on any ground supported by the record, including a ground the district court rejected. A cross-appeal is required to enlarge the appellee’s rights or diminish the opponent’s rights under that decree. The majority discussed decisions treating the cross-appeal requirement as discretionary in certain circumstances, particularly litigation involving multiple parties, but did not need a broad exception to affirm here. Substituting contract liability for tort liability preserved the award already entered. Increasing the damages would change the parties’ rights and was therefore unavailable in the circumstances. Interore’s challenge to the contributory-negligence finding did not supply the missing appeal from the contract dismissal. (Id. at 1285–86.)

The majority separately upheld Rule 11 sanctions against SGS’s counsel, Charles B. Updike and Scott M. Riemer. Causation had already been resolved in the liability phase. The damages proceeding was limited to remaining damage and mitigation issues, yet counsel repeatedly attempted to reopen causation, including in objections and post-trial motions. The district court could regard these efforts as groundless attempts to fight a settled issue again rather than correction of manifest error. Counsel had notice of the request for sanctions in Interore’s papers and an opportunity to respond; counsel did respond by affidavit without addressing the request. An additional evidentiary hearing was not required on this record. The sanctions ruling survived review for abuse of discretion. (Id. at 1286–87.)

District Judge Mishler, sitting by designation, concurred in the rejection of the independent tort theory, the availability of a contract ground without a cross-appeal, and affirmance of sanctions. He dissented from imposing contract damages and would have dismissed the complaint. In his view, the majority insufficiently respected the trial judge’s findings about the transaction’s informality and the extraordinary disparity between the $150 charge and the claimed loss. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 351(3) allowed a court to limit otherwise foreseeable damages to avoid disproportionate compensation. He considered Interore better positioned to explain New Zealand’s requirements and disputed the majority’s treatment of the vessel-classification precedent. His four notes clarify the pleaded damages, the findings, the comparison case, and the distinction between pleaded and actual loss. (Id. at 1287–89 & nn.1–4.)

The judgment and sanctions were **affirmed**. The instructional point is that appellate relief turns on the judgment’s effect on the parties, not merely on whether the appellate court accepts the trial judge’s reasoning. The assigned principal passage, printed pages 1246–1247, emphasizes that point and omits most substantive and sanctions analysis. The later Jennings discussion is an editorial note, not part of this 1994 opinion. This brief describes the historical decision; it does not supply an unchecked current-law or citator conclusion.
