# Foley v. Interactive Data Corp.
47 Cal. 3d 654, 765 P.2d 373 (1988) | Contracts

## Three claims arising from one discharge
Daniel D. Foley sought compensatory and punitive damages after Interactive Data Corporation discharged him. His second amended complaint asserted three distinct theories: discharge contrary to public policy, breach of an implied-in-fact agreement allowing discharge only for good cause, and tortious breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. The trial court sustained demurrers without leave to amend and entered judgment for the employer. The Court of Appeal affirmed all three dismissals. The California Supreme Court's December 29, 1988 decision treated those causes of action separately. (47 Cal. 3d at 662,665,700.)

Chief Justice Lucas wrote for himself and Justices Panelli, Arguelles, and Eagleson. Broussard and Kaufman each concurred in Parts I and II but dissented from Part III. Mosk agreed with their opinions except that he also dissented from Part I. Thus the public-policy result was 6–1, the implied-contract result was unanimous, and the rejection of tort remedies for this covenant claim was 4–3. The complete report contains Lucas's 43 notes, Broussard's 16 notes, Kaufman's 2 notes, and Mosk's full dissent. (Id. at 700–701,715,723–724.)

## Alleged employment assurances and discharge sequence
The complaint alleged that Interactive Data hired Foley in June 1976 as an assistant product manager at $18,500. It required an agreement restricting competition and requiring disclosure and assignment of computer-related information and innovations for one year after termination. That writing did not expressly define permissible grounds for discharge. Over six years and nine months Foley allegedly received promotions, raises, bonuses, favorable evaluations, and assurances of continued employment while he performed adequately. He became Los Angeles branch manager, earning $56,164 annually, and received a $6,762 merit bonus two days before discharge. Written termination guidelines allegedly prescribed grounds for dismissal and a mandatory seven-step procedure. Foley understood them to apply to him and allegedly declined other employment opportunities in reliance on job security. (Id. at 663–664.)

In January 1983 Foley privately told former supervisor Richard Earnest that new supervisor Robert Kuhne was under FBI investigation for embezzlement from Bank of America. Foley alleged that he acted for the employer's benefit because its business involved confidential dealings with the financial community. Earnest told him to disregard the rumors. In March Kuhne announced replacement for performance reasons and a possible Massachusetts transfer; Foley was told refusal might mean demotion rather than firing. Subsequent discussions offered continued branch management under a performance plan. At the meeting intended to present that plan, however, Kuhne instead gave him a choice between resignation and discharge. (Id. at 664–665.)

These were allegations assumed true on demurrer, not findings that an actual job-security agreement existed or that the employer's motives had been proved. The employer's counterassertions that Foley sought to oust his supervisor and rejected an available transfer were not proper facts for this review. The report also notes Kuhne's later September 1983 guilty plea, distinguishing that later event from what Foley knew at the time of his disclosure. (Id. at 662–665 & nn.1–2.)

## Public-policy tort: this disclosure served a private interest
The at-will presumption of Labor Code section 2922 did not authorize discharge in violation of fundamental public policy. Tameny allowed a tort claim based on a duty imposed by law, exemplified by refusing an employer's demand to violate criminal law, independent of any contractual job-security promise. The majority distinguished those duties from a requirement arising only from a particular employment agreement. (Id. at 665–669.)

Foley argued that an employee-agent had a duty to communicate relevant business information to management. The court did not finally decide whether that disclosure obligation derived from statutory ordinary-care duties or uncodified agency law. Nor did it decide the broader question whether a public-policy discharge claim always required a statutory or constitutional source. Even assuming a statutory touchstone, the complaint had to implicate a fundamental duty benefiting the public rather than only a particular employer or employee. (Id. at 669–670.)

The majority found this disclosure served the employer's private interest. It contrasted refusing crimes, reporting wrongdoing to authorities, and disclosing unsafe conditions. In footnote 12 it reasoned that the parties could permissibly agree to dispense with disclosure of adverse background information about a fellow employee, whereas they could not contract around prohibitions on perjury or antitrust violations. The public-policy dismissal was therefore affirmed. This was an application to the pleaded disclosure, not a rejection of all internal whistleblowing or all wrongful-discharge torts. (Id. at 670–671 & nn.10–13,700.)

## Implied contract: at-will status was a rebuttable presumption
Foley's allegation of an oral agreement was more accurately an implied-in-fact agreement because he relied on the employer's course of conduct and representations rather than particular words expressly agreeing to a good-cause restriction. Ordinary contract principles allowed the parties' actual understanding to be inferred from conduct. Section2922 supplied an at-will presumption when no contrary agreement existed; it did not prevent proof of an implied agreement restricting discharge. (Id. at 675–678.)

The court rejected a substantive requirement that job-security promises always have separate consideration beyond services. One consideration can support several promises, including both wages and a limit on arbitrary dismissal. It likewise rejected a supposed need for equivalent restrictions on the employee's right to leave: adequate consideration does not require equivalent values or mutual restrictions. Historical permanent-employment constructions did not prohibit the parties from making enforceable good-cause agreements. (Id. at 672 & n.14,678–680.)

Relevant evidence included personnel policies and practices, service longevity, assurances of continued employment, and industry practices. Six years and nine months was sufficient time for conduct supporting such an agreement; the court did not adopt a minimum-years rule. Oblique language alone would not suffice, but the totality of assurances, promotions, favorable treatment, and written termination guidelines could support the alleged understanding. Foley's postemployment competition and information undertakings were additional evidence of a continuing relationship with limits on dismissal, although separate consideration was not indispensable. (Id. at 680–682.)

The court held the allegations sufficient to permit proof of an implied good-cause agreement and breach. It did not find a contract after weighing evidence or determine whether good cause actually existed. Footnote 23 distinguished situations involving an express written at-will provision; the writings alleged here were silent on dismissal grounds. The decision did not resolve every potential conflict between express at-will language and an alleged implied modification. (Id. at 680 & n.23,681–682.)

## The one-year statute of frauds did not bar that claim
Civil Code section 1624's one-year provision applied only to contracts that, by their terms, could not possibly be performed within a year. An indefinite good-cause employment agreement could be completed within that period if the employee left or the employer discharged for cause. Anticipation of a longer relationship did not establish impossibility of performance within one year. The inquiry was prospective at formation, not retrospective from how long the job actually lasted. (Id. at 671–675; 675 n.18.)

The Court of Appeal's contrary Newfield reasoning confused expected duration and unequal termination powers with the applicable performance test. The Supreme Court disapproved Newfield to the extent inconsistent with its decision, relying on White Lighting, longstanding precedent, and the Legislature's adoption of language already construed narrowly. The oral or implied nature of the alleged agreement therefore was not fatal. The contract dismissal was reversed so Foley could attempt to prove his claim. (Id. at 672–675; 675 n.19,700.)

## Good-faith covenant: contractual protection did not imply tort damages
The covenant applied to employment contracts and protected their promised benefits, but the majority declined to extend the insurance exception allowing tort damages. It distinguished contract law's enforcement of agreed expectations from tort law's enforcement of broader social duties. Insurance decisions rested on features such as protection against calamity, unequal bargaining, and insurer obligations; earlier employment decisions had too readily transferred that model without examining the different relationship and remedial policies. (Id. at 682–690.)

The majority found ordinary employment insufficiently analogous to insurance. An insured could not buy replacement coverage for an already incurred loss, whereas a dismissed employee could seek replacement work and had a mitigation duty. Employers did not sell loss protection or perform the same quasi-public function, and ordinarily benefited from retaining good employees rather than withholding a payment from a claimant. The dissenters disputed these premises, but they were central to the majority's refusal to extend tort liability. (Id. at 692–694.)

Predictability, business stability, the breadth of motive disputes, and existing statutory/public-policy protections also counseled caution. The majority acknowledged that conventional contract damages could be inadequate but identified competing remedies, including expanded contract recovery, fees, arbitration, and legislative measures. It did not adopt any proposed alternative or hold that contract damages could never include a particular additional loss. Footnote 24 expressly left the measure of damages for the surviving contract claim unresolved. (Id. at 682 n.24,694–700.)

The majority also separated Seaman's narrow bad-faith denial of a contract's existence from attempts to extend that cause of action to disputes about whether good cause existed under a conceded contract. It declined the proposed extension. In footnote 39 it emphasized that the covenant could not itself turn every genuine at-will relationship into a good-cause contract: it protected benefits actually agreed upon. Cleary and its progeny were disapproved insofar as they permitted tort remedies for breach of the covenant in employment. (Id. at 687–690,698 n.39,700 n.42.)

## The three separate writings
Broussard agreed with the public-policy and implied-contract results but objected to eliminating a tort remedy he viewed as established by consistent appellate authority. He argued that employee dependence, security, dignity, and potential distress made the insurance analogy persuasive. A knowing, arbitrary violation of contractual discharge protections differed from an innocent breach; tort principles could address that difference. He criticized the majority's appeal to legislative restraint because it first removed existing remedies. He separately suggested exploring foreseeable emotional-distress recovery within contract law, while acknowledging that the measure of contract damages should not be decided in this case. These were his proposals and objections, not new remedies adopted by the court. (Id. at 701–715 & nn.1–16.)

Kaufman likewise agreed with Parts I and II but would recognize tort remedies for willful, malicious discharge. He characterized good faith as a duty imposed by law rather than merely a consensual contract term, considered employee vulnerability at least as compelling as an insured's, and defended the courts' common-law responsibility against legislative-deference and flood-of-litigation arguments. He viewed Koehrer's good-faith/probable-cause distinction as workable, rather than requiring tort liability for every mistaken contract breach. (Id. at 715–723 & nn.1–2.)

Mosk agreed with those opinions except their concurrence in the public-policy dismissal. He believed that informing a corporation that a supervisor faced potential criminal wrongdoing benefited society as well as the employer. He found it incongruous to protect reports to law enforcement while permitting retaliation for going first to the employer. That public-interest view did not command the court. (Id. at 723–724.)

## Exact result and historical scope
The Court of Appeal judgment was affirmed as to dismissal of the public-policy and tortious-covenant causes of action, reversed as to dismissal of the implied-in-fact good-cause contract claim, and remanded for proceedings consistent with the opinion. No damages were awarded and no employment contract was finally found. Footnote 43 expressly reserved prospective versus retroactive application because the parties had not briefed or argued it. (Id. at 700 & n.43.)
