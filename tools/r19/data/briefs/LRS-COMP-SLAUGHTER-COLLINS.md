# Collins v. Yellen

**Citation and court:** 594 U.S. 220 (2021), Supreme Court of the United States, Nos. 19–422 and 19–563, decided June 23, 2021. Justice Alito wrote the Court's opinion. Roberts, Thomas, Kavanaugh, and Barrett joined it in full. Kagan and Breyer joined all but Part III–B; Gorsuch joined all but Part III–C; Sotomayor joined Parts I, II, and III–C. The statutory ruling was unanimous. Six Justices joined the removal rationale; Kagan separately agreed that the removal restriction was unconstitutional under precedent, producing a 7–2 result on that question. Sotomayor and Breyer dissented from it. Eight joined the Court's remedial discussion, with Gorsuch taking a different remedial approach.

Thomas concurred separately. Kagan concurred in part and in the judgment, with Breyer and Sotomayor joining only Part II of her writing. Gorsuch concurred in part. Sotomayor concurred in part and dissented in part, joined by Breyer. The complete opinion and all four separate writings were read, including twenty-six Court notes, seven Thomas notes, two Gorsuch notes, and four Sotomayor notes. Kagan's writing has no judicial footnotes.

**Facts and statutory structure.** Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac operated under federal charters as privately owned, for-profit mortgage-finance companies. Purchasing and securitizing mortgages supplied liquidity and stability to home lending. Their combined portfolios approached $5 trillion in 2007; the 2008 housing collapse caused extraordinary losses and threatened the broader economy. Congress responded through the Housing and Economic Recovery Act. 594 U.S. at 228–229.

The Act created the Federal Housing Finance Agency, with a Senate-confirmed Director serving five years and removable by the President for cause. The FHFA could regulate the companies, investigate, issue subpoenas and orders, impose penalties, and appoint itself conservator or receiver. As conservator it acquired extensive control of their property and operations. Its budget came from assessments rather than annual appropriations. Id. at 229–231 & nn.1–2.

In September 2008, the FHFA placed the companies into conservatorship and negotiated stock-purchase agreements with Treasury. Treasury initially committed up to $100 billion to each company. In exchange it obtained senior preferred shares, liquidation preferences, warrants for up to 79.9% of common stock, and cash dividends initially calculated at a 10% annualized rate. Treasury waived commitment fees. Later amendments expanded the capital commitment. Id. at 231–233 & nn.3–6.

The companies repeatedly drew Treasury funds to pay Treasury dividends. By mid-2012 they had drawn over $187 billion, including $26 billion used for dividends. The August 2012 third amendment replaced the fixed-rate formula with a quarterly payment of net worth exceeding a specified reserve. It eliminated payments in loss quarters and the resulting circular funding, while preventing the companies from accumulating capital above the reserve. As performance improved, about $200 billion flowed to Treasury in 2013–2016, at least $124 billion more than the earlier formula would have required during that period. These were historical transactions, not damages awarded by the Court. Id. at 233–235 & nn.7–9.

**Claims and procedure.** Three shareholders sued in 2016, claiming the net-worth sweep exceeded the FHFA's conservator authority and that the Director's removal protection violated separation of powers. They sought declaratory relief, invalidation of the third amendment, prospective restrictions, and either return of dividends to the companies or recharacterization of payments as reducing Treasury's investment. The district court dismissed the statutory claim and entered summary judgment for the FHFA on the constitutional claim. A fractured en banc Fifth Circuit revived the statutory claim, found the removal restriction unconstitutional, and severed that restriction without undoing the third amendment. Both sides sought review. Separate statutory and arbitrary-and-capricious claims against Treasury were not presented for Supreme Court review. Id. at 235–237 & nn.10–11.

In January 2021, the fourth amendment suspended the challenged variable-dividend formula while the companies built specified capital levels, changing future payment arrangements. That development removed the basis for prospective relief but left retrospective relief at issue. Id. at 235 & n.10, 244 & n.15. These were the arrangements before the Court in 2021, rather than a statement of their present status.

**Statutory issue and holding.** Section 4617(f) prohibits judicial action restraining or affecting the FHFA's exercise of its powers as conservator or receiver, subject to the Act's specified qualifications. It does not protect action exceeding those powers. The third amendment fell within the FHFA's broad conservator authority, so the anti-injunction provision barred this statutory claim. Id. at 237–242.

The Act authorizes action in the best interests of either the regulated entity **or the Agency**. The conservator could thus pursue rehabilitation that served public interests without maximizing shareholder interests. Eliminating circular draws preserved Treasury's remaining commitment as a backstop for the mortgage market. The FHFA could reasonably choose that approach despite surrendering the companies' future capital accumulation. Id. at 238–239 & n.12.

Shareholders argued that imminent improved earnings made the sweep counterproductive. The Court found that prediction insufficient to put the choice outside statutory authority. They also proposed dividends in kind; the agreements instead required cash, with unpaid dividends increasing liquidation preference and the rate to 12%, leaving the underlying payment risk. Finally, the sweep did not itself liquidate the companies: they continued substantial mortgage operations. The Court decided statutory authority, expressly declining to decide whether the amendment was the best or even a particularly good business decision. Id. at 239–242 & nn.13–14.

**Standing, mootness, and succession.** The shareholders alleged a concrete financial injury traceable to adoption and implementation of the amendment, with potential redress through retrospective relief. Traceability concerned allegedly unlawful government conduct, rather than requiring the standing inquiry itself to establish that the removal provision caused the financial injury. Entitlement to a remedy remained a separate question. Id. at 242–244, 258 n.24.

The fourth amendment mooted prospective relief but not the requested retrospective remedies. The Act's succession clause transferred shareholder rights concerning the entities and their assets to the conservator; it did not transfer away the shareholders' constitutional right, shared with other people who have standing, to challenge separation-of-powers violations. The Court left the succession clause's application to the statutory claim undecided because the anti-injunction provision already disposed of it. Id. at 244–246 & nn.15–16. This reasoning did not grant every citizen standing without injury.

**Acting Director.** An Acting Director adopted the third amendment, but confirmed Directors continued implementing it and could have renegotiated it. Therefore, the adoption-stage circumstance did not defeat the entire constitutional claim. It did affect the potential scope of relief. Id. at 246–247 & n.17.

The Act expressly protected the confirmed Director but neither protected an Acting Director nor incorporated that protection into its acting-officer provision. Applying the ordinary presumption of presidential removal, the Court held the Acting Director removable at will. Describing an agency as independent did not itself establish tenure protection: other enabling statutes used that word in differing ways. Wiener involved an adjudicatory body with a distinct need for freedom from executive interference; the FHFA was not such a body. Id. at 247–250 & n.18. Harm from adoption by the at-will Acting Director could not rest on the challenged removal restriction.

**Removal holding.** Applying Seila Law, the Court held the confirmed Director's for-cause removal restriction unconstitutional. It rejected proposed distinctions concerning the FHFA's smaller regulatory domain, its conservator role, the government-sponsored character of regulated entities, and the asserted modesty of for-cause protection. Agency importance was not a workable determinant of presidential removal authority; even conservatorship involved implementing a special federal statute and exercising special government powers. Indirect effects on homeowners and markets mattered to accountability, and a for-cause standard still differed from removal at will. Id. at 250–256 & nn.19–20.

The Court expressly declined to decide the constitutionality of removal restrictions for other agencies, including the Social Security Administration, Office of Special Counsel, other commissions, or civil service. Id. at 256–257 n.21. The assigned account's later discussion of Commissioner Saul and an OLC memorandum is an account of subsequent executive action and advice, not an additional holding in Collins.

**Retrospective relief.** The removal defect did not automatically make the amendment or all subsequent implementation void. Each Director was properly appointed and possessed the office's lawful powers. The Court distinguished defective appointments, exercise of powers belonging to another branch, and other actions an actor had no authority to take. Seila Law had not resolved whether ratification was necessary; Bowsher involved a different defect and an express statutory remedial mechanism. Id. at 257–259 & nn.22–25.

Nevertheless, a removal restriction might cause compensable harm. Examples included a President actually prevented from removing an officer, or announcing that the restriction obstructed a desired removal. The shareholders argued that different Directors or incentives would have produced different decisions. The Government countered that the President already controlled Treasury, the agreement's other party. The Court left those arguments to the lower courts, together with the disputed, fact-dependent laches defense. Id. at 259–260 & n.26. It did not order repayment, guarantee recovery, or categorically deny all retrospective relief.

**Thomas's concurrence.** Thomas joined in full but stressed that identifying an unconstitutional statute is insufficient: plaintiffs must identify unlawful government action and a right to redress. He distinguished an executive officer's lawful appointment and powers from an invalid limit on removal. He questioned whether a misunderstanding about removal power makes otherwise authorized conduct unlawful, because the Constitution supersedes the invalid statutory restriction. He also found no textual basis for treating the conservator powers as inseverable from removal protection. Id. at 261–271.

His notes address a potential equitable cause of action and an asserted APA theory, standing based on allegedly unlawful action, branch and appointment distinctions, and his continuing objection to judicial rewriting through severability. He recognized that blocked presidential removal might present an unauthorized officer, and that an arbitrary-and-capricious theory based on mistaken legal understanding would raise different statutory questions not presented here. Id. at 263 nn.1–2, 265–266 nn.3–4, 267–269 nn.5–7. He doubted eventual entitlement to relief but accepted resolution of the issues presented and further consideration on remand.

**Kagan's separate opinion.** Kagan accepted the removal result under stare decisis: the FHFA wielded significant executive power and was not meaningfully distinguishable from Seila Law. She rejected the majority's political theory and its unnecessary elimination of the earlier significant-executive-power boundary. Precedent required fidelity from both former dissenters and prevailing Justices. Id. at 271–274.

In Part II, joined by Breyer and Sotomayor, she supported the remedy analysis. Restoring plaintiffs to their position absent a removal defect requires showing that the defect affected the challenged decision; automatic unwinding could place them in a better position and disrupt decisions a President supported or never noticed. She believed the Fifth Circuit's existing analysis of Treasury's presidential accountability might already establish that the restriction made no difference. That was her assessment, not the Court's final resolution of the remanded question. Id. at 274–276.

**Gorsuch's separate opinion.** Gorsuch agreed on the merits but rejected Part III–C's remedy. He regarded actions by an unconstitutionally insulated officer as unauthorized and ordinarily subject to being set aside, with traditional equitable defenses such as laches still possible. He disputed the distinction between defective appointment and defective supervision, invoking Bowsher and Seila Law. Id. at 276–279 & n.1.

He criticized assumptions about which agency design Congress would have selected and about what a President would have done under different legal advice. Counterfactual inquiries might require sensitive executive evidence and leave plaintiffs without effective relief. His note 2 disputed Thomas's application of his earlier severability reasoning. Gorsuch's preferred invalidation approach was not the remedy adopted by the Court. Id. at 279–283 & n.2.

**Sotomayor's separate opinion.** Joined by Breyer, Sotomayor accepted the statutory holding and remedial discussion but would sustain the Director's tenure protection. She read Seila Law as distinguishing agencies with significant coercive power over private actors. Compared with the CFPB and the 1935 FTC, the FHFA regulated a limited set of government-sponsored enterprises, had a narrower mandate, and often acted as conservator. Its authority was not absent, but in her account it lacked the constitutionally significant breadth on which Seila Law relied. Id. at 283–290 & nn.1–3.

She invoked Morrison's inward-focused independent-counsel power, the entities' public missions and government relationships, and the historical tradition of independent financial regulation. She criticized the majority for abandoning its recent distinctions and intruding on Congress's power to design agencies. She joined Kagan's remedial Part II and considered Thomas's lawfulness question worthy of future consideration. These were separate views, not an exemption adopted for the FHFA. Id. at 284 n.1, 290–294 & n.4.

**Exact disposition.** The Court **affirmed in part, reversed in part, and vacated in part the Fifth Circuit's judgment, and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion**. Id. at 261. The statutory claim had to be dismissed; the removal restriction was unconstitutional; potential retrospective relief and associated disputed issues required lower-court consideration. No blanket invalidation of FHFA actions or payment award was entered.

**Course significance.** Keep statutory authority, constitutional structure, standing, and remedy separate. Collins applied removal doctrine beyond the CFPB while limiting the inference that a tenure defect invalidates every past action. The assigned discussion emphasizes the scope dispute following Seila Law; the full opinion supplies the Act's unusually broad conservator powers, the Acting Director distinction, and the disagreement over remedy.
