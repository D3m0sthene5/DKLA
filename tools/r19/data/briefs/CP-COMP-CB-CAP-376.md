# Pyle v. Selective Insurance Co. of America

**Citation:** 2016 WL 5661749 (W.D. Pa. Sept. 30, 2016), No. 2:16-cv-335, Document 29. **Court/judge:** Western District of Pennsylvania, Senior District Judge Terrence F. McVerry. **Course:** Civil Procedure—cooperation over electronic discovery. **Assigned location:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 479, printed pages 933–934, Note 8.

## Facts and procedural posture

The assigned textbook identifies Pyle’s action as employment discrimination. The order concerns discovery relating to her employment, receipt of disability benefits, termination, and medical treatment; it does not decide liability on those underlying claims. Two motions by Selective were before the court: ECF 22 sought medical records and executed HIPAA authorizations; ECF 24 sought plaintiff’s cooperation in selecting electronic search terms. Pyle opposed both. (Order 1–3.)

For medical discovery, Pyle’s initial disclosures named three treating doctors, and Selective later learned of a fourth. Counsel requested records by letter July 7, provided releases August 30, and formally served a document request September 6. Although no records or releases had yet been produced, the thirty-day response period had not expired. (1–2.)

For electronic discovery, Pyle’s July 28 request sought emails, correspondence, memoranda, and other documents from various Selective employees relating to her employment and benefits. Selective produced some documents and retrieved additional email archives. It asked opposing counsel to agree on search terms, renewing the request September 6. Pyle’s counsel refused, saying she had not requested direct account access requiring a protocol and that Selective had shown neither supporting authority nor burden. Selective requested an order compelling terms or, alternatively, relieving it of any ESI production obligation. (2–3.)

## Issues and holdings

**Medical records:** Should production be compelled before the response deadline? **No.** The motion was premature given Pyle’s representation that records and authorizations would be supplied within the prescribed period. (2.)

**Electronic search:** Could counsel refuse to cooperate over the search methodology? **No.** The court compelled Pyle to confer and reach agreement on terms for searching the identified additional email archives. It did not grant Selective a blanket exemption from producing ESI. (3–4.)

## Rule and reasoning

Electronic discovery should ordinarily be driven by the parties. Rule 26(f)(3)(C) expressly requires counsel to confer about ESI disclosure, discovery, and preservation, including production forms. The court drew on Romero and its quotation of Trusz concerning practical agreement on terms, dates, and relevant people. Local Civil Rule 26.2(C) more specifically required counsel to confer and attempt agreement on ESI protocols, including filters such as terms and date ranges. Cooperation was consistent with these obligations; the court should not need to manage every search detail. (3.)

Pyle’s argument that no authority supported cooperation therefore failed. The order did not itself prescribe particular terms, decide the adequacy of a completed search, require predictive coding, or establish that every document matching a keyword must be produced. It addressed a refusal to participate in developing the methodology for already retrieved archives. (3–4.)

The medical motion illustrates a separate procedural limit: a party’s expected compliance is not yet a failure warranting a preemptive production order. The court expected timely compliance and allowed Selective to raise the issue again if Pyle failed to comply when due. It did not uphold permanent withholding or decide any particular privilege objection. (2.)

## Exact order and textbook connection

**ECF 22 denied as premature; ECF 24 granted insofar as it required conferring and agreement on search terms.** No specific agreement deadline was set, although prompt resolution was expected to avoid unreasonable discovery delay. No merits judgment or sanction was entered. (2, 4.)

The textbook accurately presents the search-term ruling. Its explanation that the federal rules do not directly prescribe this specific methodology should be read alongside the order’s reliance on the general federal conference duty and the more specific local rule. The neighboring predictive-coding discussion and Rule 35 material are separate topics.
