# Herrera v. Wyoming

**139 S. Ct. 1686 (2019), No. 17–532.** Supreme Court, May 20, 2019. **Civil Procedure — issue preclusion, intervening legal change, and alternative grounds; Indian treaty hunting rights.** Assigned account: CivPro, physical PDF pages 658–659, printed pages 1305–1307, Note 7 through the end of the Alito quotation before The Evergreens. Pinpoints below use each writing's separate pagination in the preserved official slip opinion.

## Treaty, events, and litigation

In an 1868 treaty, the Crow Tribe ceded more than thirty million acres in what became Montana and Wyoming. In return, among other promises, the United States reserved the Tribe's right to hunt on unoccupied federal lands while game remained and peace continued on the hunting districts' borders. Negotiations emphasized continued hunting as central to the Tribe's way of life. The treaty did not name statehood as a termination event. Congress created Wyoming Territory later that year, expressly preserving unextinguished Indian rights; the 1890 statehood legislation did not mention treaty hunting rights. President Cleveland subsequently reserved the Bighorn forest lands from entry or settlement in 1897. Sotomayor opinion 1–4.

Clayvin Herrera, a Crow member living on the Montana reservation, and other members pursued elk into Bighorn National Forest in Wyoming. Wyoming charged Herrera with taking elk out of season or without a state license and being an accessory to the same. His pretrial treaty defense was rejected, and he could not present it to the jury. Conviction brought a suspended jail sentence, a fine, and a three-year hunting-privilege suspension. The majority describes the events as occurring in 2014; the dissent begins its investigative account in winter 2013 and adds trophy photographs, discovery of carcasses with substantial meat abandoned, and Herrera's admission. These are the respective writings' accounts, not a basis to infer a chronology they do not jointly establish. Sotomayor 4–5; Alito dissent 6–7.

A prior case complicated the defense. In Crow Tribe of Indians v. Repsis, 73 F.3d 982 (10th Cir. 1995), the Tribe and Thomas Ten Bear had litigated the same treaty right. The Tenth Circuit upheld Wyoming's position on two independent grounds: statehood terminated the right, relying on Ward v. Race Horse, 163 U.S. 504 (1896), and the creation of Bighorn National Forest made the land occupied within the treaty's meaning. Sotomayor 5–6; Alito 5–6.

The Wyoming district court, acting as an appellate court in Herrera's case, upheld his conviction. It rejected his contention that Minnesota v. Mille Lacs Band of Chippewa Indians, 526 U.S. 172 (1999), had repudiated Race Horse. It also treated Repsis as precluding Herrera, whose right derived from membership in the Tribe, and concluded that the national forest was occupied in any event. The Wyoming Supreme Court declined review; the United States Supreme Court granted certiorari from the state district court's judgment. Sotomayor 5–6.

## Judgment and questions resolved

The Court held that Wyoming's statehood did not terminate the Crow hunting right and that creating the national forest did not categorically make its lands occupied. Mille Lacs had sufficiently changed the applicable law to remove Repsis's preclusive force on the statehood issue. The Court **vacated the Wyoming district court's judgment and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion**, rather than finally acquitting Herrera or resolving every possible justification for state regulation. Sotomayor 6–22.

Justice Sotomayor wrote for a five-Justice majority joined by Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, Kagan, and Gorsuch. Justice Alito dissented, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas and Kavanaugh. No other separate opinion or appendix accompanies the official judgment.

## Why the intervening decision defeated this preclusion claim

Race Horse had relied on two ideas: Wyoming's admission on an equal footing with other States was inconsistent with continuing special hunting rights, and the treaty created a temporary right not intended to survive statehood. Mille Lacs undermined both. Treaty hunting rights could coexist with state sovereignty because necessary, reasonable, nondiscriminatory conservation rules could still apply. Abrogation required clear congressional intent, or satisfaction of a termination event found in the treaty itself. Describing rights as temporary or precarious did not make statehood an unstated expiration date. Sotomayor 6–11.

The majority acknowledged that Mille Lacs had not expressly overruled Race Horse. It nevertheless found their reasoning impossible to harmonize and formally repudiated Race Horse to the extent it held treaty rights impliedly extinguished at statehood. That intervening shift mattered to preclusion: the Repsis court had been obliged to follow Race Horse and could not predict the later repudiation. Id. at 11–13.

Issue preclusion ordinarily binds parties to issues actually litigated, resolved, and essential to a valid prior judgment. Even if those conditions otherwise exist, an intervening change in the applicable legal context can justify an exception. The majority drew on Bies, Limbach, Sunnen, Montana, and Restatement principles, reasoning that mechanically enforcing the obsolete determination could undermine equitable administration. This was not a marginal change in atmosphere or a merely inconsistent lower-court decision; the foundation of Repsis's statehood holding had been repudiated. Id. at 11–13, including n.2.

The Court did not reopen the original Repsis judgment or invalidate all final judgments whenever precedent changes. It addressed the use of that prior determination as a bar to litigating the treaty issue in Herrera's later case. Note 3 further reserved whether compelling reliance or repose concerns could justify a different outcome. Wyoming had not identified such concerns convincingly: conservation authority remained possible, and the right concerned unoccupied federal lands rather than private property. Id. at 13 n.3.

## Treaty interpretation and limits on remand

The Statehood Act supplied no clear evidence that Congress had considered and chosen to abrogate Crow treaty rights. The treaty specified four terminating conditions: occupation, loss of federal ownership, absence of game, and loss of peace on the relevant borders. Statehood was absent. Negotiators had promised continued hunting and knew that additional western state admissions were foreseeable; the record supplied no proposal to make statehood the endpoint. Wyoming could not treat statehood as a proxy for the supposed disappearance of the frontier. Id. at 13–17.

The national forest question required understanding occupation as the Crow would naturally have understood it. The treaty's contrasts between hunting districts and settlement, its other uses of occupation, and the negotiations pointed to non-Indian residence or settlement. Reserving the lands from settlement did not categorically cause that condition. Federal dominion and regulation were not synonymous with the relevant settlement; the State's mining and logging evidence did not establish occupation of the forest as a whole. Id. at 18–21.

There remained two express limitations. Wyoming could argue that the particular hunting site was occupied, even though national-forest status did not automatically occupy every acre. Wyoming could also pursue necessary conservation regulation; the trial court had addressed that issue, but the appellate court had not, and the Supreme Court did not decide the viability of the State's position. Id. at 21–22.

A separate preclusion limitation is essential. Note 5 declined to decide whether Repsis's alternative occupied-land ruling independently precluded Herrera. The majority read the judgment below as applying preclusion only to the statehood ground, found the question outside the grant of review, and emphasized unresolved gateway matters such as full and fair litigation and possible forfeiture. It did not hold that the alternative ruling categorically lacked preclusive effect. Id. at 18–19 n.5.

## Alito's dissent

Alito would resolve preclusion first and would not reach treaty interpretation. In his view, Repsis conclusively determined the Tribe's right to hunt in Bighorn, and Herrera was bound by that collective determination even though he was not personally a plaintiff. He criticized the majority's narrow definition of the precluded issue, reading the state decision as giving effect to the whole Repsis judgment. Alito dissent 7–9, 12–13 & n.6.

He questioned whether Mille Lacs had changed the law enough to warrant an exception: it had unequivocally rejected equal-footing reasoning, but reasonably could be read as distinguishing rather than overruling Race Horse's second ground. He also distinguished Bies and the tax cases the majority cited and warned that an aggressive change-in-law exception would sacrifice finality. He ultimately found it unnecessary to settle that question because Repsis's independent occupation ground remained. Id. at 9–13 & n.5.

Alito favored giving both independently sufficient grounds preclusive effect, as the First Restatement recommended, rather than the Second Restatement's contrary recommendation. Courts can carefully deliberate alternative grounds, and concerns about an unappealed weaker ground did not establish unreliability here. The Tribe had actually briefed the occupation question extensively, and Repsis was a declaratory action intended to settle legal rights. Those features reinforced his conclusion. Id. at 13–16 & nn.7–8.

He rejected a categorical bar on applying a civil declaratory judgment in a later criminal prosecution. Such judgments let parties determine their rights without first risking prosecution; their conclusiveness should operate against the losing party as well as in its favor. Civil appellate review had been available and used in Repsis, unlike concerns surrounding an unreviewable criminal acquittal. Note 9 additionally distinguished preclusion of an offense element from Herrera's affirmative defense under Wyoming's burden framework. These are dissenting conclusions, not majority holdings resolving the broader questions. Id. at 16–19 & n.9.

## Assigned significance

The textbook correctly emphasizes changed law as an exception to issue preclusion and reproduces the majority's central reasoning and Alito's caution. Its short account should be read with the complete opinion's qualifications: statehood preclusion failed, while alternative-ground preclusion was not decided; treaty interpretation did not dispose of site-specific occupation or conservation. All five majority notes, all nine dissent notes, the complete separate writing, and the entire supplied assigned account were read for this historical judgment. Later law and proceedings are not certified.
