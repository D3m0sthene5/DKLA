# Kenford Co. v. County of Erie
73 N.Y.2d 312, 537 N.E.2d 176 (1989) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Designated Judge Mollen wrote for the New York Court of Appeals. Chief Judge Wachtler and Judges Alexander, Titone, and Bellacosa concurred; Judges Simons, Kaye, and Hancock, Jr., took no part. The five participating judges reversed the relevant portions of the Appellate Division's 1988 final order and its 1985 order brought up for review, with costs, and struck Kenford's award for anticipated appreciation of land surrounding a proposed stadium. There is no separate writing. The decision does not vacate every damages award in this long litigation. 73 N.Y.2d at 315–316, 322; designation note.

## Facts and chronology
Erie County authorized a domed sports stadium and $50 million in financing in May 1968. Kenford, whose president and sole shareholder was Edward Cottrell, acquired options on Lancaster land and initially offered to sell a stadium site to the County. The County declined. Kenford then enlisted Roy Hofheinz, who had helped develop the Houston Astrodome. Hofheinz and Cottrell formed Dome Stadium, Inc. (DSI). Kenford proposed donating the site in exchange for DSI's ability to lease or manage the stadium. The County accepted that proposal in June 1969, and Cottrell exercised options on surrounding parcels while negotiations proceeded. Id. at 316.

The August 8, 1969 contract called for Kenford to donate 178 acres for the stadium and access roads, and for the County to begin construction within twelve months. The County would negotiate a forty-year DSI lease expected to produce at least $63.75 million in revenues, including stadium taxes, rent, and increased taxes on peripheral lands. The latter category covered Lancaster lands owned, contracted for, or later acquired by Cottrell or Kenford. If no satisfactory lease emerged within three months, the parties were to execute an annexed twenty-year management agreement. Footnote 1 confirms that the forty-year lease was never agreed. Id. at 316–317 & n.1.

Construction bids reached approximately $72 million, $22 million beyond the authorized financing. Efforts to increase the appropriation failed. The County terminated the contract in January 1971; Kenford's alternative financing attempts also failed. Kenford and DSI sued in June for specific performance or $90 million in damages. The requested damages were later increased to $495 million, as footnote 2 records. Liability was established by summary judgment, and a damages trial lasting roughly nine months produced $18 million in anticipated peripheral-land appreciation and over $6 million in out-of-pocket expenses for Kenford, plus $25.6 million in twenty-year management profits for DSI. Id. at 317 & n.2.

## Procedural history and issues
In 1985, the Appellate Division sustained much of Kenford's reliance and mitigation award, rejected DSI's lost profits and some Kenford expenses, and ordered a retrial of the land-appreciation amount. Its majority considered appreciation recoverable but rejected an appraisal that assumed improvements such as a theme park, offices, and golf course. The proper retrial measure would compare raw acreage value after stadium construction with its purchase value. Two justices dissented on foreseeability and speculation. On a separate earlier appeal, the Court of Appeals rejected DSI's management profits because liability for them was not contemplated and the proof was too speculative. Id. at 317–318.

The land-appreciation retrial yielded $6.5 million. The Appellate Division affirmed in 1988, with two justices concurring solely because its earlier recoverability ruling was law of the case. The County's appeal from that final order brought up the necessarily affecting 1985 nonfinal order. Footnote 3 explains that other orders the County sought to challenge were not reviewable on this CPLR 5601(d) appeal. The operative question was whether the County had assumed liability for Kenford's surrounding-land appreciation if the stadium never materialized. Id. at 318–319 & n.3.

## Rule and competing positions
Ordinary damages compensate the natural and probable consequences of breach. Unusual or extraordinary damages require contemplation at or before formation as a probable consequence of breach. In assessing that contemplation, the court considers the contract's nature, purpose, and known circumstances, together with the liability the defendant consciously assumed or reasonably led the plaintiff to believe it assumed. Id. at 319.

Kenford maintained that the expected appreciation was actually foreseen and inherent in the bargain. The contract expressly contemplated higher assessments and peripheral-land tax revenues; all participants expected a local economic boom. Kenford also defended its retrial appraisal and the underlying liability determination. The County disputed the legal recoverability of appreciation and sought review of other rulings, but the court confined the present resolution to the cognizable appellate issue. Counsel's assertions about evidence and validity were arguments, not additional Court of Appeals findings. Id. at 313–315, 318–320.

## Reasoning
Knowledge that a stadium could increase property values did not establish a promise to compensate Kenford if that hoped-for increase never occurred. The revenue provisions showed shared expectations of economic growth. They did not demonstrate assumption by the County of the investment risk attached to Kenford's peripheral lands. The court used its earlier rejection of DSI's twenty-year profits as an analogy: contractual default remedies had not contemplated so heavy a responsibility, and the evidence did not show the parties would have accepted it had they addressed the issue. Id. at 319–320.

The distinction became especially clear because Kenford had no contractual duty to acquire or retain surrounding land. Its obligation concerned the 178-acre donation. The County knew of the additional purchases and intentions, but awareness alone did not make the associated special consequences part of the agreement. The court compared cases involving sugar supplies, telegraph delays, and Hadley's broken mill shaft, where the information necessary to connect an extraordinary loss to the bargain was absent or insufficient. Id. at 320–321.

Kenford voluntarily bought peripheral lands hoping to capture the stadium's benefits. Without proof transferring that risk to the County, the investment could gain value if the project succeeded and fail to appreciate if it did not. Kenford's theory would effectively guarantee the anticipated increase whether or not the stadium was built. The court found no contractual or evidentiary basis for that guarantee. Limiting damages for unassumed risks also reduced the uncertainty confronting businesses entering contracts. Id. at 321–322.

## Holding and relationship to certainty
Kenford could not recover the peripheral-land appreciation as a matter of law because the County had not reasonably contemplated liability for that loss. The court struck the award rather than remanding for another appraisal. Its operative rationale here was the absence of contemplated assumption of liability. The earlier DSI ruling had both contemplation and reasonable-certainty grounds; those two grounds should not automatically be attributed as independent holdings about this retrial's land-valuation evidence. Id. at 318, 322.

The casebook discusses the court's reliance on the assumption-of-liability approach and contrasts it with the UCC's rejection of a tacit-agreement test. That discussion is useful doctrinal context but is editorial, rather than a holding that this land-and-stadium agreement was governed by the UCC.
