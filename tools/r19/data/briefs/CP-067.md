# Kearns v. Ford Motor Co.

**567 F.3d 1120 (9th Cir. 2009) · Civil Procedure · CP-067**

William Kearns alleged that Ford and its dealers persuaded consumers to pay a premium for certified pre-owned vehicles by misrepresenting the value of certification. The Ninth Circuit affirmed dismissal because his complaint rested on a unified fraudulent scheme but did not identify the circumstances of the alleged deception with the particularity Federal Rule 9(b) requires. Calling that scheme an unfair business practice, or describing part of it as nondisclosure, did not remove the pleading defect. The decision concerns the adequacy of the allegations; it does not establish whether Ford's certification program actually delivered its advertised benefits.

Ford's certified pre-owned program covered late-model used vehicles. Ford represented that inspection and certification made them safer, more reliable, and more roadworthy than ordinary used vehicles, and sold them at a premium. Ford charged participating dealers an annual fee and a fee for each vehicle, supplied marketing and instructional materials, and advertised nationally. Dealers implemented sales and service locally. Kearns alleged that the resulting consumer reassurance cost $1,080 and was worth less than that premium. He disputed the quality of vehicle-history reports, technician training, and inspection, and alleged inadequate disclosure of Ford's limited oversight. These were the complaint's assertions, rather than findings that the program was defective. (Opinion ¶¶8–11, 567 F.3d at 1122–23.)

Kearns filed a proposed class action in California state court under the Consumers Legal Remedies Act and Unfair Competition Law. Ford removed it under the Class Action Fairness Act's diversity provision, 28 U.S.C. §1332(d). Kearns unsuccessfully sought remand under the local controversy exception. An amended complaint was dismissed with leave to amend, followed by dismissal of a second amended complaint under Rule 9(b). The third amended complaint was again dismissed with leave to amend. The district court also struck its first footnote. Kearns elected not to amend further, the court dismissed the case, and he appealed. The appellate court reviewed the Rule 9(b) dismissal de novo. The remand history supplies procedural context; this opinion's developed analysis decides pleading sufficiency. (¶¶12–15.)

Kearns advanced three principal reasons to avoid dismissal. He argued that California pleading precedent did not impose the federal fraud rule on these consumer statutes; that portions of his complaint, especially nondisclosure allegations, did not sound in fraud; and that the court should separately consider the UCL's unfairness branch. He also challenged the striking of the complaint footnote. Ford maintained that the asserted consumer claims were founded on fraud and lacked the specific circumstances needed to answer that charge.

The court first distinguished Rule 8's ordinary requirement of a short and plain statement from Rule 9(b)'s particularity requirement for circumstances constituting fraud. Fraud need not be an indispensable element of a statutory claim for Rule 9(b) to apply. Where a complaint mixes fraudulent and nonfraudulent conduct, only the fraudulent averments receive the heightened treatment; deficient fraud averments are stripped out, and remaining allegations are assessed under Rule 8. But when a plaintiff relies entirely on a unified fraudulent course of conduct, the claim as a whole sounds in fraud and must satisfy Rule 9(b). Merely omitting the word fraud does not change the substance of allegations necessarily constituting it. (¶¶16, 24, 29.)

Particularity requires identifying who committed the misconduct, what happened, when and where it occurred, and how it was fraudulent. Neutral facts locating a transaction are insufficient. This specificity gives defendants meaningful notice of the charge, restrains complaints used to discover unknown wrongs, protects reputations, and prevents substantial litigation costs without an adequate factual basis. The court treated these purposes as reasons to require particulars about the deception itself, rather than simply identification of a vehicle purchase. (¶¶17–18.)

California supplied the substantive elements of fraud, but federal law governed how its circumstances must be pleaded in federal court. The Federal Rules applied regardless of whether jurisdiction rested on state-law diversity claims or federal law. State consumer statutes therefore did not carry California procedural pleading standards into this federal action. In judicial footnote 2, the court distinguished Kearns's reliance on Swierkiewicz: decisions rejecting extra pleading requirements for claims without fraud or mistake did not contradict application of Rule 9(b) where fraud was actually alleged. (¶¶20–21 & n.2.)

The complaint asserted a coordinated effort by Ford and its dealers to misrepresent certification benefits and induce purchases. Kearns said national television advertising, dealership sales materials, and dealership personnel led him to buy a certified vehicle. Yet he did not specify the contents or timing of the advertisements and materials, identify those he regarded as material, or explain which sales materials he relied on. He did allege being told that certified vehicles were selected and rigorously inspected and carried a Ford-backed extended warranty, but did not identify the speaker or time. Those omissions prevented Ford from responding to particular misconduct. General exposure to promotional channels could not substitute for the missing particulars. (¶¶25–26.)

Nor did the nondisclosure theory preserve a separate, adequately pleaded claim. Kearns invoked Vess, which had distinguished nonfraudulent omissions from fraud allegations. The panel examined California fraud elements, including false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure, knowledge, intent to induce reliance, justified reliance, and resulting damage. It contrasted Vess's earlier formulation with the California Supreme Court's formulation in Engalla. Here, the pleaded omissions were part of an alleged intent to conceal that certified vehicles were essentially ordinary used vehicles. Those general concealment allegations were therefore fraudulent in substance and failed Rule 9(b). The reasoning does not establish that every omission in every consumer complaint necessarily constitutes fraud; it addresses the omissions alleged as part of this scheme. (¶¶27–30.)

The court acknowledged that unlawful, unfair, and fraudulent practices are distinct UCL theories, and that unfairness can independently support relief. Nevertheless, this complaint relied on one fraudulent course of conduct across its theories. Recasting that course under the unfairness branch did not furnish nonfraudulent allegations that escaped particularity. The district court consequently did not err by failing to conduct a separate unfairness analysis of this deficient complaint. (¶¶32–34.)

The panel affirmed dismissal. It did not decide the merits of the challenge to striking the first complaint footnote: dismissal of the entire complaint made that issue moot. Judicial footnote 1 reproduced the complaint's allegations about another family's fatal vehicle accident; that reproduction was neither an appellate factual finding nor an adjudication of that other case. Judge N. Randy Smith wrote for Judges Pregerson, Hall, and Smith; no separate writing appears. Both judicial footnotes were read, along with the complete assigned principal passage and its relocated editorial alteration notices. The rule to carry forward is the distinction between a mixed claim containing separable nonfraudulent allegations and a claim resting wholly on a fraudulent course of conduct. This brief describes the 2009 decision without claiming a current citator result. (¶¶36–39 & nn.1–2.)
