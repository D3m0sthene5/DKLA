# Snyder v. Harris; Gas Service Co. v. Coburn

**394 U.S. 332 (1969). Decided March 25, 1969. Nos. 109 and 117.** Justice Black delivered the Court’s opinion resolving both cases. Justice Fortas dissented, joined by Justice Douglas. The complete report supplies those two dissenters but no separate majority-membership list. Assigned account: Civil Procedure casebook, physical PDF page 448, printed pages 869–870, the Snyder portion of Note 3 and the subsequent comparison question.

## Two controversies and opposite appellate results

Margaret Snyder owned shares in Missouri Fidelity Union Trust Life Insurance Company. She sued directors in federal court, alleging that they sold their own shares above fair market value, with the excess representing payment for company control that Missouri law required distributing among all shareholders. Her own asserted recovery was $8,740. She sought to represent approximately 4,000 shareholders, whose potential claims together would total approximately $1.2 million. The district court dismissed because her individual demand did not exceed the historical $10,000 diversity threshold, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed. **394 U.S. at 333.**

The companion case concerned a different small claim. Kansas resident Otto Coburn alleged that Gas Service Company improperly charged him and other customers a city franchise tax despite their living outside city limits. His own alleged overcharge was $7.81. He sought relief for approximately 18,000 other customers and alleged that their aggregated claims exceeded $10,000, although the individual amounts remained unknown. The district court rejected the company’s jurisdictional challenge. On interlocutory appeal, the Tenth Circuit agreed that the 1966 amendment to Rule 23 permitted aggregation. The Supreme Court reviewed both cases to resolve the circuit conflict. **Id. at 334.**

These were jurisdictional determinations, not adjudications establishing that directors unlawfully retained control premiums or that the gas company collected illegal taxes.

## Issue and historical rule

Did the 1966 revision of the class-action rule permit separate and distinct class members’ claims to be added together to satisfy the jurisdictional amount under § 1332?

The Court held that it did not. The traditional interpretation permitted aggregation when one plaintiff combined multiple claims against one defendant, or when multiple plaintiffs enforced a single title or right in which they possessed a common **and** undivided interest. It did not permit adding separate and distinct demands of different plaintiffs simply because they shared factual or legal questions and could be litigated together. **Id. at 335–338.**

## Why changing Rule 23 did not change jurisdiction

Old Rule 23 used the categories “true,” “hybrid,” and “spurious.” The Court explained that common-and-undivided rights permitted aggregation in true class actions, while spurious actions functioned more like permissive joinder of distinct claims. The amended rule replaced those categories with functional criteria, notice procedures, exclusion opportunities, and broader binding judgments. The Tenth Circuit reasoned that abolition of the old distinctions meant aggregation formerly allowed for some classes now should be allowed for all. **Id. at 335–336.**

The Supreme Court rejected that inference because the nonaggregation doctrine arose from the statutory phrase “matter in controversy,” not from Rule 23’s categories. It existed before the Federal Rules and applied to ordinary joinder as well as class actions. A binding judgment did not by itself make distinct claims a single jurisdictional demand: plaintiffs joined under Rule 20 also were bound, yet their separate demands traditionally could not be aggregated. **Id. at 336–337.**

Rule 82 prohibited interpreting procedural rules to extend or restrict district-court jurisdiction. The Court’s rulemaking authority could not alter Congress’s statutory jurisdictional grant by revising Rule 23. Whatever procedural advantages the revised class device offered, it did not independently erase the amount requirement or change the Court’s established construction of it. **Id. at 337–338.**

## Why the Court declined to change its own construction

The plaintiffs separately urged the Court to reconsider the old doctrine itself. They pointed to difficult distinctions between separate and common claims, uncertainty, additional litigation over classification, and the inability to bring important collections of small claims to federal court. The majority acknowledged those arguments but thought them insufficient to displace a long-settled statutory interpretation. **Id. at 338.**

Congress repeatedly had increased the jurisdictional dollar amount while retaining the operative language against that interpretive background. Although legislative silence and reenactment did not always adopt every prior construction, the majority thought the 1958 choice of $10,000 reflected workload estimates relying on nonaggregation. Allowing aggregation broadly could undermine the purpose of limiting federal caseloads and bring many local state-law disputes into federal court. The concern was particularly pronounced where only the named representative’s diversity was relevant to citizenship, even if the other class members shared the defendant’s state. **Id. at 338–340.**

The Court also considered the claimed practical harm overstated. It described lower-court standards as largely workable, observed that many federal-question suits did not depend on a jurisdictional amount, and pointed to state forums for these state-law disputes. A Missouri suit involving the underlying shareholder issues already existed, and Kansas supplied a class procedure for the gas customers. Broader federal access, if necessary, was for Congress to authorize. **Id. at 341–342.**

## Fortas’s dissent: statutory interpretation and procedural change

Fortas and Douglas would measure the controversy by the monetary value of the entire class claim once the action satisfied amended Rule 23. Fortas argued that the majority restored the very conceptual distinctions the reform sought to replace. His disagreement proceeded in three connected parts. **Id. at 342–357.**

First, Congress had not defined the bare phrase “matter in controversy” through a detailed aggregation code. Courts had developed those subsidiary rules. Fortas rejected the inference that repeated dollar-threshold amendments froze every judicial doctrine, particularly where the 1958 hearings and reports did not discuss aggregation in class actions. Judicially developed applications could be reevaluated in changed circumstances without disregarding an express statutory command. **Id. at 346–350 & nn.6–12.**

Second, revised Rule 23 changed the legal structure of the proceeding. Unlike the older spurious class action, a properly maintained class action could bind absent class members, subject to due process and any applicable exclusion rights. Thus, in Coburn’s case, the judgment could settle the legality of charges to the whole represented group, not merely his $7.81 demand. The relevant controversy should reflect that binding resolution. Fortas’s lengthy footnote 3 reproduced the amended rule’s prerequisites, class categories, notice, judgments, management orders, and settlement controls; it did not suggest that merely asserting a class label dispensed with those requirements. **Id. at 344–346 n.3, 350–354 & nn.13–20.**

Third, he distinguished altering subject-matter jurisdiction from changing procedural occasions for its exercise. Rules affecting joinder, representation, and who became bound necessarily changed what litigation came before federal courts. Recognizing the value of a class-wide controversy in that new framework, he argued, applied the statutory grant rather than enlarged it. He also warned that the majority’s conclusion affected some constitutional and other federal-question cases then dependent on § 1331’s amount requirement; the availability of many special jurisdictional statutes did not eliminate that problem. **Id. at 342–343 n.2, 354–357 & nn.21–23.**

## Exact judgments and limits

In **No. 109, Snyder**, the Supreme Court **affirmed** the Eighth Circuit judgment rejecting aggregation. In **No. 117, Gas Service**, it **reversed** the Tenth Circuit judgment permitting aggregation. These are two expressly numbered dispositions in one jointly reported opinion; they should not be collapsed into a statement that both appellate judgments were affirmed. Neither judgment awarded the underlying shareholders or customers damages. **Id. at 342.**
