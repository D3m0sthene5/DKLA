# Harriscom Svenska, AB v. Harris Corp.
3 F.3d 576 (2d Cir. 1993) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
The Second Circuit affirmed the two final judgments entered on December 18, 1992, rejecting Harriscom's contract claims against its supplier and precluding its second action. Judge Cardamone wrote for a panel including Judges Lumbard and McLaughlin; no separate writing is reported. This August 23, 1993 merits judgment differs from the 1991 appeal dismissed for want of appellate jurisdiction. (576–577, 579, 581.)

## Facts and chronology
Harris Corp.'s RF Systems division appointed the Swedish company Harriscom its exclusive distributor in Iran in 1983. On December 6, 1985, U.S. Customs detained a shipment of model 2301 radio spare parts, worth $663,869, destined for Iran through Sweden. Although Commerce had determined in 1982 that this radio could generally be exported under a general license, the State Department began a commodity-jurisdiction proceeding to determine whether it belonged on the Munitions List. For more than six months RF Systems negotiated with Commerce, State, and Defense officials. The problem emerging from those negotiations was the Iranian destination rather than simply the radio's technical classification. In July 1986 RF Systems agreed to withdraw from further Iranian sales. It represented that this concession meant losing $5.9 million of existing orders and $10 million of potential orders. In exchange, the government determined in September that the radio was not a Munitions List product. (578.)

Only three of eight outstanding Harriscom orders, involving models 2301 and 301, were allowed to proceed. RF Systems' $240,000 performance guarantees expired with those shipments. Harriscom, which had separately guaranteed performance to Iran in the amount of $550,000, lost $270,000 on the five unfilled orders and claimed lost profits. It argued that the supplier had voluntarily sacrificed its contracts, should have supplied substitutes through its Indian licensee, and should have applied under a November 1987 regulation permitting certain existing contracts to be fulfilled. Those were Harriscom's theories of breach, not findings of bad faith. (578.)

A related 1984 agreement required Harriscom to buy the entire model 301 inventory for $3 million, while RF Systems promised to stop manufacturing that model. A September memorandum and October side letter provided for shipments in December 1984 and April 1985, with some flexibility for the second date. Harriscom had paid a $150,000 deposit expressly characterized as nonrefundable. The Iranian restrictions interrupted this arrangement too, although one model 301 order was shipped in June 1986 under the governmental compromise. Harriscom contended subsequent negotiations changed the agreement and sought its deposit back. (578–579.)

## Procedure and questions presented
Harriscom sued in June 1988 and amended in September. Negligence and fraud claims were dismissed that December. A December 1990 summary judgment initially disposed of the remaining complaint, but the district court revived the deposit claim, Count X, in February 1991 and left RF Systems' inventory-purchase counterclaim for trial. Rule 54(b) certification did not cure the resulting jurisdictional difficulty: the Second Circuit dismissed that first appeal because related claims remained unresolved. Harriscom then filed a second action in November 1991, alleging a promise to resume Iranian business when conditions permitted and failure to seek the 1987 export permission. (579.)

The district court granted summary judgment on Count X in May 1992, making Harriscom's trial-evidence motion moot, and stayed the second action on preclusion grounds in June. RF Systems voluntarily dismissed its counterclaim without prejudice on December 17; final judgments followed the next day. The present appeal challenged excuse, good faith, the deposit provision, evidentiary rulings, and claim preclusion. The court reviewed summary judgment de novo for a genuine material factual dispute. Voluntary dismissal of the counterclaim was not a merits adjudication that Harriscom had defeated that claim. (579–580.)

## Holding and reasoning
RF Systems established commercial impracticability under New York UCC § 2-615 because it complied in good faith with informal governmental requirements. The description of its withdrawal as voluntary did not overcome overwhelming, uncontradicted evidence that the government would not permit continued sales to Iran and had power to compel compliance. Comment 10 recognized such good-faith compliance; the seller need not adopt a foolhardy refusal to cooperate before claiming excuse. The express force-majeure provisions independently covered governmental interference beyond the parties' control. This was a decision on the actual record and contractual language, not a rule that every request from an official excuses performance. (580.)

Good faith also did not automatically create a triable issue. Harriscom produced no evidence of bad faith, and speculation was insufficient. For substantially the same reasons, the covenant of good faith did not require the proposed Indian substitute. The November 1987 regulation was no help because it applied to existing contracts predating October 22, 1987; performance had been excused in July 1986, and the unfilled orders were treated as cancelled losses. Harriscom supplied only its principal's assertions, not evidence of a surviving contractual obligation. (580.)

The deposit was an unambiguous nonrefundable first installment of the $3 million price. Extrinsic evidence was unnecessary to establish its meaning, and calling the term unreasonable did not create ambiguity or a factual dispute. The court did not convert the deposit into a judicial damages award. Challenged affidavits rested on personal knowledge and perceptions, and supporting documents were admissible business records. Affirmance of summary judgment left no trial for the proposed in-limine evidence. (580–581.)

The second action repeated allegations already argued between the same parties in the first. Summary judgment had rejected those contentions on their merits. Their absence from an explicit discussion in an earlier district-court opinion did not mean they had escaped adjudication. Claim preclusion therefore supported dismissal of the second complaint. (581.)
