# Harrow v. Department of Defense

**601 U.S. 480 (2024) · Supreme Court of the United States · May 16, 2024**

## A late appeal after a prolonged administrative delay

Stuart Harrow, a longtime Department of Defense employee, challenged a six-day furlough before the Merit Systems Protection Board in 2013. An administrative judge upheld the furlough in 2016, describing it as regrettable but not improper. Harrow sought review by the full Board. The Board lost its quorum in early 2017, and could not decide cases for more than five years. After its quorum was restored, it affirmed the administrative judge’s decision in May 2022. 601 U.S. at 482.

Under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1), Harrow had sixty days after the Board issued notice of its final order to petition the Federal Circuit for review. He filed in September 2022, more than 120 days after the order issued. Harrow explained that his work email address had changed during the lengthy delay and that his old address eventually stopped forwarding messages. The Board sent notice to the old address, so he learned of its decision only through a website search after the sixty-day period had expired. He asked the court to excuse the lateness in light of those circumstances. Id. at 482–483.

The Federal Circuit treated the statutory deadline as jurisdictional and therefore beyond equitable tolling. It dismissed the appeal despite recognizing that Harrow’s circumstances might be sympathetic. The Supreme Court granted review to decide the jurisdictional question. It did not take the case to determine whether the furlough was proper or whether Harrow had already established entitlement to tolling. Id. at 483, 489–490.

## Question and holding

Does § 7703(b)(1)’s sixty-day deadline for seeking review of a Board decision limit the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction?

No. The filing deadline is nonjurisdictional. Its mandatory wording and its connection to a separate jurisdictional statute do not clearly establish that missing the deadline deprives the court of adjudicative authority. Whether this particular deadline permits equitable tolling, and whether Harrow qualifies for it, remained for remand. Id. at 485–490.

## The clear-statement requirement

Justice Kagan’s unanimous opinion distinguished ordinary procedural rules from rules defining a court’s power. A jurisdictional requirement must be enforced even without a party’s objection and cannot be excused for equitable reasons. Given those consequences, a procedural requirement is jurisdictional only when Congress clearly gives it that character. Congress need not use particular words, but ordinary tools of interpretation must plainly establish the jurisdictional effect. Mandatory or emphatic language, standing alone, does not suffice. Id. at 483–485.

Section 7703(b)(1) tells a litigant where and when to file. It requires filing in the Federal Circuit within sixty days after the Board issues notice. It does not speak to the court’s jurisdiction or its authority over an untimely appeal. The command that a petition shall be filed within the stated period therefore supplies a filing requirement without converting it into a restriction on judicial power. Id. at 485–486.

## Why the jurisdictional cross-reference did not change the result

The Government relied on 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(9), which gives the Federal Circuit jurisdiction over an appeal from a final Board order or decision “pursuant to” § 7703(b)(1). It argued that the phrase required complete conformity with every requirement of the referenced provision, including the sixty-day deadline. On that reading, only timely appeals would fall within the jurisdictional grant. Id. at 486.

The Court rejected the claim of clarity. In legal usage, an act done pursuant to a statute can simply be an act taken under that statute, rather than an act satisfying every statutory requirement. The Court’s own opinions used the phrase that way. Congress did too: the removal statute permits review of specified remand orders in cases removed pursuant to particular provisions even though the question on appeal may be whether removal under those provisions was proper. Treating the phrase as identifying the asserted legal basis makes sense without presupposing compliance with every procedural condition. At minimum, the Government’s alternative did not plainly establish jurisdictional consequences. Id. at 486–488.

The broader structure reinforced that conclusion. Section 1295 uses comparable cross-references to statutes containing deadlines, service and notification requirements, and other procedural conditions. The Government’s interpretation would make that collection of ordinary procedural rules jurisdictional as well. The Court declined to read those cross-references as indiscriminately switching the Federal Circuit’s adjudicative power on and off. Id. at 488.

The Government also invoked *Bowles v. Russell*, which treats statutory deadlines for civil appeals from district courts as jurisdictional. The Court confined that established exception to statutory deadlines for appeals from one Article III court to another. Harrow sought review of an agency decision, so the usual clear-statement rule applied. Id. at 488–489.

An asterisked judicial footnote addressed *Lindahl v. Office of Personnel Management*. Although that earlier decision had described the relevant provisions as jurisdictional, it concerned the categories of Board claims reviewable by the Federal Circuit, rather than the timeliness of an appeal. Its terminology reflected the Court’s historically loose use of jurisdictional language and did not settle the question here. Id. at 489 n.*.

## Exact disposition and unresolved equitable relief

The Supreme Court vacated the Federal Circuit’s judgment and remanded. The Government advanced a fallback argument that the deadline remained unavailable for equitable tolling even if nonjurisdictional. The Court recognized a presumption favoring equitable tolling of nonjurisdictional timing rules, but did not decide whether the Government could rebut it here. The argument had not been raised below, the Federal Circuit had not addressed it, and it fell outside the question presented. Those issues, including possible waiver, belonged on remand. If the Federal Circuit found tolling available, it would then determine whether Harrow’s particular circumstances justified it. Id. at 489–490.

Thus the judgment removes a jurisdictional barrier; it does not itself excuse Harrow’s late filing or award relief from the furlough. Nonjurisdictional status and entitlement to an equitable exception remain distinct questions.

## Authorship and assigned context

Kagan wrote for a unanimous Court. There were no separate opinions. The complete judicial opinion contains the asterisked *Lindahl* footnote; the preliminary report also has a separate asterisked counsel and amici note after the syllabus. Both were read, and the editorial syllabus is distinct from the Court’s opinion.

The assigned supplement’s captioned account appears on printed pages 507–508, within physical PDF page 342. It highlights the mandatory-language distinction and the absence of a reference to the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction. The complete opinion supplies the prolonged administrative history, cross-reference and statutory-structure reasoning, limited *Bowles* exception, *Lindahl* footnote, and express reservation of the tolling questions. Those reservations prevent a shorthand description of the decision from becoming an unwarranted claim that every nonjurisdictional deadline can be extended.
