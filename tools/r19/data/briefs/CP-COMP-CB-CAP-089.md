# Republic of Sudan v. Harrison

**587 U.S. 1, 139 S. Ct. 1048 (2019) — March 26, 2019. Civil Procedure.** Justice Alito wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh. Justice Thomas alone dissented. The judgment was 8–1. Assigned account: CivPro, physical pages 158–159, printed pages 268–269, Note 5. Pinpoints below refer to separately numbered pages of the preserved official slip opinions, not an inferred final-reporter page conversion.

## Background, service, and enforcement proceedings

Victims of the October 2000 bombing of the USS Cole and their family members sued Sudan in 2010, alleging that it materially supported al Qaeda’s attack. The bombing killed seventeen crewmembers and injured dozens more. The suit invoked the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act’s terrorism provisions. The service question did not require the Supreme Court to determine Sudan’s responsibility for the attack. [Alito 3.]

At the plaintiffs’ request, the court clerk mailed a summons, complaint, and other service materials, with a return receipt requested, to the named Sudanese foreign minister at Sudan’s embassy in Washington, D.C. The clerk certified that the packet was sent and that a signed receipt returned. Sudan later questioned both whether the correct minister was named and whether the embassy actually received it. The Court assumed those points in plaintiffs’ favor because the selected destination was deficient in any event; it did not find those disputed matters conclusively established. [Alito 3–4 n.1.]

Sudan did not appear. After an evidentiary hearing, the District Court for the District of Columbia entered a $314 million default judgment. The clerk mailed that judgment in the same manner. The plaintiffs then registered it in the Southern District of New York and sought Sudanese assets through bank turnover orders. After confirming that sufficient time had elapsed following the judgment and its notice, that court issued three such orders. Sudan appeared to contest jurisdiction and appealed each order, arguing that the original judgment lacked personal-jurisdiction support because service had not complied with the FSIA. [Alito 4.]

The Second Circuit upheld embassy-directed mailing, reasoning that the statute did not expressly identify a location and that the method could reasonably be expected to reach the foreign minister. It again adhered to that position after supplemental briefing and argument on rehearing, including the United States’ support for Sudan. A Fourth Circuit decision took a contrary view. The Supreme Court granted review to resolve the conflict. [Alito 4–5.]

## Statutory issue and service hierarchy

Under the FSIA, an applicable exception to immunity permits subject-matter jurisdiction, while §1330(b) links personal jurisdiction to service under §1608. Section 1608(a), governing a foreign state or political subdivision, supplies four methods in hierarchical order: an existing special service arrangement; an applicable international service convention if there is no special arrangement; prescribed mailing if neither earlier method is available; and diplomatic-channel service through the Secretary of State if the mailing method cannot be completed within thirty days. A served foreign state has sixty days to respond. These alternatives form an actual hierarchy, unlike the independent Rule 4(f) methods discussed in Rio Properties. [Alito 1–3.]

The parties agreed that the first two methods were unavailable. The third, §1608(a)(3), required the clerk to address and dispatch a translated service packet by signed-receipt mail to the head of the foreign state’s foreign-affairs ministry. The issue was whether naming that minister but using the Washington embassy’s address satisfied this provision. It was not whether a reasonable prospect of actual notice alone excused statutory compliance. [Alito 2–3, 9–10.]

## Majority: the foreign minister’s own office is required

The Court held that the most natural reading required direct mailing to the foreign minister at the minister’s office in the foreign state. It acknowledged that this was not the only linguistically plausible interpretation. But an address ordinarily identifies the recipient’s residence or customary workplace, and dispatch connotes sending directly and promptly, rather than routing the letter through a third-party office. An embassy in the United States ordinarily is neither the minister’s residence nor usual workplace. [Alito 5–9.]

The signed-receipt requirement supported that reading. A subordinate at the foreign ministry would presumably know how to receive and route the minister’s mail. Receipt at an embassy mailroom offered less assurance of prompt handling by the right officials. The Court did not require the minister personally to sign; its concern was sending the packet to the proper office. [Alito 8–9.]

Related statutory provisions reinforced the interpretation. Section 1608(b), addressing an agency or instrumentality rather than the foreign state itself, expressly used a reasonable-actual-notice standard and authorized certain agents, including specified domestic recipients. The omission of comparable language from §1608(a)(3) argued against importing those options into this method of serving the state. The Court also avoided treating the “addressed and dispatched” requirement as merely duplicating a general notice test, which would make separate language in §1608(b) redundant. Note 2 adds that an earlier proposal expressly contemplating service through an ambassador or chief of mission had not become the operative formulation. [Alito 9–11 n.2.]

Section 1608(c)’s completion dates supplied another comparison. Service depended on specified certifications or receipts; diplomatic service had its own transmittal-based date. Mailing to the foreign ministry better aligned completion with a strong likelihood that an appropriate foreign official would promptly handle the packet. The plaintiffs’ suggestion that Sudan could later try to reopen a default was a poor substitute for timely, properly routed initial service. [Alito 11–13.]

## Diplomatic context, contrary text, and practical objections

The majority also found its reading avoided potential anomalies with Federal Rule 4 and tension with the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations. If receipt at an embassy sufficed under this provision, serving the foreign state could appear easier than serving a person abroad under comparable mail requirements. The State Department’s longstanding view opposed embassy-directed service and warned about reciprocal implications for United States embassies abroad. The Court paid attention to those executive views but did not decide whether the Department’s interpretation of embassy inviolability was independently correct; its statutory construction avoided the potential dispute. [Alito 13–15.]

The plaintiffs proposed mailing to a location with a direct communication line to the minister. Alito found that criterion difficult to administer consistently: consulates, ambassadorial residences, and United Nations missions could invite similar arguments. He preferred a definite office-based rule. He also acknowledged the plaintiffs’ strongest textual counterargument: §1608(a)(4) expressly specifies Washington for mailing to the Secretary of State, while subsection (3) contains no similarly express place. That contrast favored the plaintiffs, but the majority considered it outweighed by the statute’s ordinary meaning and surrounding structure. It did not pretend the argument lacked force. [Alito 15–16.]

Finally, possible Sudanese awareness of highly publicized litigation and the plaintiffs’ understandable frustration did not excuse these requirements. Consistent compliance mattered in suits with sensitive diplomatic implications. The holding did not foreclose another attempt under subsection (3) or, if unsuccessful, subsection (4). [Alito 16–17.]

## Thomas’s dissent

Thomas agreed that notice likelihood alone was insufficient, that subsection (3) was not simply an agent-service provision, and that the minister need not personally receive the packet. He disagreed about the eligible address. In his view, the text identified a person without imposing an exclusive foreign-office location. An embassy’s distinctive role as a secure communication channel made it a place where the foreign minister could be reached, absent contrary evidence or rejection of the packet. He relied on the returned receipt and lack of evidence that Sudan’s minister could not be reached through the embassy, rather than presuming every embassy must accept all service papers. [Thomas 1–5.]

He emphasized the expressly specified location in subsection (4) and rejected the majority’s office-routing analogies as insufficiently attentive to embassies. His note 1 also disputed the premise that routing an Attorney General letter through a local United States Attorney would necessarily be inappropriate. Efficient diplomatic communications undermined the claim that sending papers physically overseas was always more direct. [Thomas 5–7 n.1.]

On the Vienna Convention, Thomas distinguished inviolability against physical intrusion from immunity against judicial authority. Receipt of ordinary mail, including service papers, did not intrude upon embassy premises. His note 2 added that, even if a conflict existed, the later FSIA would control to that extent. Those were dissenting propositions; the majority avoided deciding the treaty issue. Thomas would have affirmed the Second Circuit. [Thomas 7–9 n.2.]

## Judgment and assignment

The Court **reversed the Second Circuit and remanded for proceedings consistent with its interpretation of §1608(a)(3)**. It did not decide the terrorism claim’s merits, order payment of a different sum, or bar renewed proper service. [Alito 17.]
