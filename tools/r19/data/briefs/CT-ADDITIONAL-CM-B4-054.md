# Seitz v. Mark-O-Lite Sign Contractors, Inc.
210 N.J. Super. 646, 510 A.2d 319 (Law Div. 1986) | Contracts

## Court, stipulated trial, and judgment
Acting Superior Court Judge Milberg decided this contract action in the Law Division, Monmouth County, January 31, 1986. Counsel submitted the dispute at trial on stipulated facts. The court rejected the sign subcontractor's force majeure and impossibility defenses, held its refusal a material anticipatory breach, and directed entry of $7,200 judgment for George Seitz against Mark-O-Lite. This was a trial-court determination, despite the reproduction website's appellate-division metadata. (210 N.J. Super. at 648, 654.)

## The subcontract and the employee's disability
Seitz was the low bidder on renovations of the Strand Theater for the Ocean County Center for the Arts. He signed the owner's $51,200 contract December 26, 1983; specified sign work accounted for $19,500. Mark-O-Lite had given him an oral $10,000–$12,000 estimate, but they had not yet executed a subcontract. Another sign company's January 1984 quotation was $20,228. Seitz and Mark-O-Lite ultimately signed an April 18, 1984 subcontract for $12,800, and Seitz gave the company a $3,200 deposit check. (Id. at 648–649.)

Within days, Mark-O-Lite discovered that Al Jorgenson, its only employee capable of the detailed sheet-metal work, had to enter the hospital for an unknown period. Jorgenson was diabetic; progressive infection led to partial amputation of his foot. The condition was not a sudden stroke or heart attack. The company advised Seitz by telephone and, on May 3, returned the uncashed deposit with a letter offering whatever portion of the work it could perform. It contacted other sign companies, whose $18,000–$20,000 prices would make subcontracting economically infeasible for it. Seitz instead contracted with City Sign Service for $20,000 and claimed the $7,200 difference from the agreed $12,800. (Id. at 649–650.)

The stipulated replacement description expressly identified added rail, neon, and a neon border as extras beyond Mark-O-Lite's original specifications. The opinion nevertheless used the full $20,000 replacement price in its final $7,200 calculation, without explaining a separate adjustment for those extras. Both the stipulation and the actual judgment must be preserved; the report does not support inventing an allocation or changing the award. (Id. at 649, 654.)

## Why the force majeure clause did not excuse performance
Mark-O-Lite relied on a clause excusing failures resulting from labor strikes, fires, floods, earthquakes, acts of God, and other conditions or contingencies beyond its control. The opinion's later quotation also listed war. It argued that the employee's disability fell within the broad residual language. (Id. at 649.)

The court applied ejusdem generis: general language following listed events reaches contingencies of the same general nature, rather than every event outside the obligor's control. Jorgenson's illness was not in the class of the listed disasters. Nor did the progressive consequences of diabetes qualify as an act of God. His disability was not sudden and was reasonably foreseeable given his condition. The court's reference to a sudden stroke or heart attack identified circumstances that might receive different treatment in a particular case; it did not establish a universal exemption for every sudden illness. The force majeure defense failed. (Id. at 649–650.)

## Independent impossibility analysis and delegation
Failure under the clause did not itself eliminate the common-law impossibility defense. The court examined both the original Restatement's rule for death or illness of a necessary person and the Second Restatement's §§ 261 and 262 concerning supervening impracticability and personal incapacity. Under either formulation, this asserted defense required that the parties understand performance to depend on a particular person's services. If another person could perform the obligation without violating the contract, the expected employee's illness would not discharge it. (Id. at 650–652.)

The subcontract did not require Jorgenson personally, and the circumstances did not make his peculiar skill or discretion indispensable. Mark-O-Lite's own efforts to hire outside shops demonstrated that the sheet-metal work was delegable; Seitz's replacement contract confirmed that it remained possible. The problem was that Mark-O-Lite could not perform with its existing staff at a satisfactory cost, rather than that the promised work could not be done. The court distinguished subjective from objective impossibility and rejected marginal profit or a loss on replacement subcontracting as an excuse for this fixed-price obligation. (Id. at 652–654.)

## Repudiation and the exact remedy
A definite, unconditional declaration that essential promised performance will not be rendered constitutes material anticipatory breach. The May 3 letter and returned deposit unequivocally communicated that Mark-O-Lite would not supply the contracted sheet-metal work. Its offer to perform some work did not oblige Seitz to accept a divided substitute. Especially given deadlines under the owner's contract, replacing the entire sign subcontract was reasonable. (Id. at 654.)

The court calculated the expectation shortfall as $20,000 less $12,800, directed judgment for Seitz in the amount of $7,200, and required him to submit an appropriate judgment form. It did not affirm a lower-court judgment or merely remand for damages. (Id.)
