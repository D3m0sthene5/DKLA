# Nelson v. Colorado

**581 U.S. 128 (2017), 137 S. Ct. 1249 — Supreme Court of the United States, April 19, 2017.** Civil Procedure. Decided together with Madden v. Colorado. The assigned account is CivPro physical page 172, printed page 296, Note 4 before Note 5. Justice Ginsburg wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy, Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan. Justice Alito concurred only in the judgment; Justice Thomas dissented. Justice Gorsuch did not participate. The result was 7–1, with six Justices joining the Court’s reasoning.

## Facts and procedural history

Colorado imposed monetary assessments on Shannon Nelson and Louis Alonzo Madden solely because of their criminal convictions. Nelson was convicted in 2006 of two felonies and three misdemeanors involving alleged abuse of her children. Her sentence included 20 years to life in prison and $8,192.50 in costs, fees, and restitution. The conviction was reversed for trial error; a new jury acquitted her of all charges. Madden was convicted in 2005 of attempting to patronize a prostituted child and attempted third-degree sexual assault. His sentence included $4,413 in monetary assessments. One conviction was reversed on direct review and the other vacated on postconviction review; Colorado elected not to appeal or retry him. 581 U.S. at 131.

The requested refunds were amounts actually paid, rather than the entire assessments. Colorado withheld $702.10 from Nelson’s inmate account, allocating $287.50 to costs and fees and $414.60 to restitution. Madden paid $1,977.75, consisting of $1,220 in costs and fees and $757.75 in restitution. The Court’s first two footnotes identify the public funds receiving particular fees. Its third explains that conviction was the legal basis for these exactions and that, although Colorado restitution operated as a separate civil judgment, a conviction-dependent restitution order was vacated when the underlying conviction was reversed. 581 U.S. at 131–132 & nn.1–3.

After their convictions were invalidated, both defendants requested refunds. Nelson’s trial court denied any refund. Madden’s postconviction court allowed costs and fees but refused restitution. The Colorado Court of Appeals held that both could seek return of all payments. The Colorado Supreme Court reversed, reasoning that courts needed statutory authority to refund public money and that the 2013 Exoneration Act supplied the exclusive remedy. Neither defendant had filed a claim under that Act. A state dissent argued that the restored presumption of innocence required a workable refund mechanism without requiring proof of innocence. The United States Supreme Court granted certiorari. 581 U.S. at 132–133.

## Statutory obstacle and constitutional issue

The Exoneration Act was designed to compensate actually innocent people for wrongful incarceration. Eligibility required imprisonment pursuant to a felony conviction and excluded reversals based merely on insufficient evidence or legal error unrelated to actual innocence. The claimant had to establish actual innocence by clear and convincing evidence. Successful claimants could obtain specified compensation for imprisonment and related harms, as well as reimbursement of conviction-based payments. Treating that demanding compensation proceeding as the only refund route excluded misdemeanor-only cases and made small refund claims financially unrealistic. 581 U.S. at 133–134 & n.6, 137 & n.12.

The issue was whether due process permitted Colorado to retain conviction-based costs, fees, and restitution after the convictions were invalidated and no further prosecution would occur, unless defendants independently proved actual innocence under that scheme.

## Holding and majority reasoning

The Court held that the scheme violated due process. It applied Mathews v. Eldridge: the affected private interest, risk of error under the procedures, and government interest all weighed against Colorado. Colorado urged Medina v. California’s more historically oriented test for state criminal procedures. The Court distinguished continuing deprivation of money after the criminal prosecution had ended from rules governing the criminal process itself. It also stated in footnote 9 that the scheme would fail Medina because the presumption of innocence was fundamental. That alternative discussion should not obscure Mathews as the majority’s governing framework. 581 U.S. at 134–136 & nn.7–9.

First, the defendants had an obvious interest in recovering their money. Invalidating their convictions restored the presumption of innocence. Colorado could not treat them as guilty enough to justify retaining monetary exactions simply because a conviction had existed when payment occurred. The Court rejected distinctions between reversal at trial and on appeal, and between supposedly void and merely voidable convictions. Otherwise, a successful appeal from a conviction carrying only a fine would leave the defendant with no practical relief from that monetary punishment. 581 U.S. at 135–137 & nn.8–11.

Second, the refund mechanism created an unacceptable risk of continued erroneous deprivation. A defendant whose conviction had been invalidated should not have to prove innocence to recover payments resting solely on that conviction. The Act’s felony limitation left some assessments without any remedy, while litigation expense deterred small claims even though successful claimants could recover attorney’s fees. Probable-cause review, a jury trial, and proof beyond a reasonable doubt did not solve this problem: those safeguards addressed conviction, not whether money would be returned after a conviction was overturned. A possible stay pending appeal was likewise inadequate, because few defendants could satisfy its requirements and the court could still require a deposit of costs. 581 U.S. at 137–139 & nn.12–13.

Colorado also improperly equated the refunds with damages for wrongful imprisonment. Petitioners sought their own payments back, not interest or compensation for their temporary loss of money. The Court distinguished restoring something taken under a conviction from compensating all harms caused by that conviction. 581 U.S. at 138.

Third, Colorado identified no countervailing interest in retaining money for which it currently had no claim of right. It mentioned equitable considerations but supplied none relevant to these defendants or incorporated into the Act’s restrictions. Due process allowed no more than minimal procedures for refunding exactions dependent on a subsequently invalidated conviction. This was not a holding that every related civil judgment or every loss caused by prosecution must automatically be undone. 581 U.S. at 139.

## Separate opinions

Alito agreed with reversal but would apply Medina. Refund requests remained part of the criminal process, and historical practice gave courts authority to restore payments after reversal while considering equitable exceptions. Colorado’s inflexible scheme abandoned that tradition and effectively prevented most successful appellants from seeking refunds. He thought history explained the distinction between refunds and compensation for imprisonment better than the majority’s broad balancing approach. 581 U.S. at 139–146 & nn.1–2.

Alito also disputed sweeping treatment of restitution. Unlike fees kept by the State, restitution goes to victims and resembles a civil judgment, potentially resting on standards different from criminal conviction. He discussed cases in which collateral reversal would not necessarily erase a separate civil entitlement or require the State to reimburse money already distributed as an intermediary. Those were his cautions and hypotheticals, not majority-adopted exceptions or findings that Nelson’s or Madden’s victims had a surviving independent judgment. 581 U.S. at 146–148 & n.3.

Thomas would affirm. In his view, procedural due process presupposed an independently established property entitlement. Colorado’s highest court had treated lawfully collected payments as public funds or victims’ money and made the Exoneration Act the source of any refund right. Petitioners had not shown entitlement under that Act or another source. The majority therefore assumed the very right requiring proof and used procedural due process to alter substantive entitlement. His first footnote reserved an original-understanding argument not advanced by Colorado. He did not dispute that unconvicted persons could not initially be charged these payments or that petitioners could not now be compelled to make additional payments. 581 U.S. at 148–154 & nn.1–2.

## Judgment and assigned significance

The Court **reversed both Colorado Supreme Court judgments and remanded** for consistent proceedings. 581 U.S. at 139. The textbook correctly presents the Mathews factors and Thomas’s threshold objection. Its then-incomplete official citation is now 581 U.S. 128. The full opinion also records Alito’s distinct approach and notes new Colorado legislation effective September 1, 2017, which had no effect on these cases. 581 U.S. at 133 n.4. This brief describes the historical judgment without certifying current law.
