# Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay

**437 U.S. 463 (1978) — Supreme Court of the United States — June 21, 1978.** Justice Stevens wrote for a unanimous Court. The assigned Civil Procedure account is Note 5, printed page 1228, physical PDF pages 620–621. The decision rejects immediate appeals of class-certification orders under both the collateral-order doctrine and the historical “death knell” doctrine.

## Investment and litigation history

Coopers & Lybrand certified financial statements used in a prospectus for Punta Gorda Isles' 1972 public securities offering, which had an aggregate price exceeding $18 million. The plaintiffs purchased securities in reliance on that prospectus. The company's next annual report restated its reported 1970 and 1971 earnings, reducing net income by more than $1 million for each year. The plaintiffs later sold their investments at a $2,650 loss. They sued the accounting firm and other defendants for alleged violations of the federal securities laws, seeking to represent similarly situated purchasers. The Supreme Court did not decide whether any defendant violated those laws. 437 U.S. at 465–466 & n.4.

The District Court initially certified a class but subsequently decertified it. The plaintiffs did not seek certification of that order under § 1292(b). Instead, they filed a notice of appeal under § 1291 and also sought mandamus to recertify the class. The Eighth Circuit concluded that it had appellate jurisdiction because decertification would sound the litigation's economic death knell. It then reversed on the merits and dismissed the mandamus petition because it had accepted the appeal. Id. at 466–467 & nn.5–7.

The appellate court's economic inquiry considered the plaintiffs' $26,000 combined yearly gross income, approximately $75,000 net worth with only $4,000 in cash, expenses already incurred, and an estimated $15,000 future litigation cost. It thought they would not pursue their individual claim without a possible class recovery. That prediction was the lower court's jurisdictional rationale, not a finding that their securities claim lacked merit. Id. at 466–467 n.7.

Other defendants separately petitioned for Supreme Court review and tentatively settled, but Coopers & Lybrand did not stipulate to dismissal of its petition. The tentative settlement therefore did not moot this case. Id. at 465 n.3.

## Issue and final-judgment framework

The question was whether the refusal or withdrawal of class certification constituted a final decision appealable as of right under § 1291 before judgment on the individual claims. The Court held that it did not. Such an order does not itself end the action: the named plaintiff remains free to pursue an individual claim. An exception would therefore be necessary to obtain immediate § 1291 review. Id. at 465, 467–468.

The collateral-order doctrine requires conclusive determination of the disputed question, resolution of an important issue completely separate from the merits, and effective unreviewability after final judgment. Class-certification orders failed that test for three independent reasons. Under the then-applicable Rule 23(c)(1), class status could be revised before the merits decision, making the order inherently tentative. Certification questions such as typicality, adequacy, and commonality also overlap the case's factual and legal merits. Finally, the named plaintiff or intervening class members could obtain effective review after final judgment. Id. at 468–469 & nn.9–13.

## Why the economic death-knell theory failed

The plaintiffs defended immediate review as a means of protecting valuable small-claim class litigation. Coopers & Lybrand criticized class actions and disputed the need for special appeal rules. The Court treated those broad policy arguments as matters for legislative consideration. At the time, class-action rules supplied no special appellate mechanism; ordinary appealability standards applied. The Court did not deny that decertification might economically induce abandonment. It rejected making that prediction a substitute for legal finality. Id. at 469–470.

The proposed doctrine would also reach beyond class actions. Discovery, venue, or summary-judgment rulings can make continued litigation economically unattractive without ending it. Appealability based on predicted abandonment would depend on resources, claim size, likely costs, willingness to finance litigation, prospects of success, and ability to share costs. That would make jurisdiction turn on a variable assessment of litigants' circumstances. Id. at 470–471 & nn.15–16.

The Court examined two implementations. An amount-based threshold was arbitrary and improperly judicially created: Congress had made finality, not an amount in controversy, the criterion for appeal. Such a threshold ignored other economic variables and could make appellate jurisdiction depend on counsel's joinder decisions. A fuller factual examination was somewhat less arbitrary but administratively costly. It required evidence and findings in the trial court, review of that record on appeal, and potentially a remand merely to decide appellate jurisdiction. Id. at 471–474 & nn.17–22.

That individualized inquiry also invited repeated interruption. Reversal of a decertification ruling based on adequacy would not prevent a later refusal based on predominance, with another asserted appeal of right. Similar appeals could arise from other economically important orders. The resulting disruption conflicted with the final-judgment rule's protection of the trial process. Id. at 473–474.

## Statutory screening and unequal treatment

Section 1292(b) already supplied a carefully limited discretionary path for appropriate nonfinal orders. It required initial district-judge certification and appellate permission; certification did not compel acceptance. The death-knell doctrine bypassed those screens by substituting an appeal of right based on factual predictions. The Court explained that the historical appellate court could decline a certified appeal for reasons including docket congestion, and that some certification orders had in fact been reviewed through § 1292(b). Id. at 474–475 & nn.23–27.

The doctrine also favored plaintiffs alone. Granting certification could substantially increase a defendant's exposure and litigation costs, inducing settlement despite a meritorious defense. Yet that economic pressure had not converted grants of certification into final orders. Selecting which pressures should permit immediate review implicated legislative policy, not a general judicial license to expand jurisdiction. Id. at 476 & nn.28–29.

Footnote 30 rejected a broad reliance on *Gillespie*. Its unusual circumstances did not justify extending immediate review to inherently tentative, fact-dependent class orders. A general extension would empty § 1291 of practical significance. Id. at 477 n.30.

## Exact judgment, later rule, and assignment

The Court **reversed the Eighth Circuit with directions to dismiss the appeal**. It did not affirm the decertification order on its merits or decide the securities claims. There were no separate writings or dissents. Id. at 477.
