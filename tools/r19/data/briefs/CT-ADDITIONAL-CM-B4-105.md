# R. L. Kimsey Cotton Company, Inc. v. Ferguson; R. L. Kimsey Cotton Company, Inc. v. Evans
233 Ga. 962, 214 S.E.2d 360 (1975) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and disposition
Georgia’s Supreme Court decided four related appeals on March 17, 1975. Justice Jordan wrote, and all justices concurred. The court reversed the orders in the buyers’ appeals, Cases 29541 and 29543, because the trial court should have granted their motions for summary judgment. It dismissed the farmers’ cross-appeals, Cases 29542 and 29544, without deciding their issues. This was review of certified interlocutory rulings, not an affirmance of an existing final specific-performance decree or a judgment fixing a damages amount. (962–963, 968.)

## Facts and procedural history
In March 1973 farmers contracted to sell their cotton crop to Kimsey. In one action Kimsey sued four farmers for specific performance and an injunction preventing sale elsewhere. In the other, Kimsey and its own cotton buyer, W. B. Dunavant & Co., sought the same relief against five farmers. The contracts were materially alike except for names, some prices, farm locations, and acreage. The reproduced Ferguson agreement covered all cotton produced during 1973 on approximately 230 acres in Bartow and Polk Counties, with identified farm numbers. It specified 30 cents for the stated grades and staples, 24.5 cents for below-grade cotton, methods of picking and ginning, and delivery by December 20. The farmer promised normal good farming methods; the buyer could reasonably control heat and cleaning equipment in ginning. The agreement also declared integration and required written modifications. (962–964.)

Before the appealed rulings, a restraining order and interlocutory injunction prevented violation of the contracts while allowing delivery and sale of cotton and requiring bonds to secure any damages pending final determination. At the hearing the parties stipulated execution, repudiation, uniqueness of the cotton, and propriety of injunctive relief. The farmers nevertheless asserted indefiniteness, lack of consideration or mutuality, unconscionability, and fraudulent procurement. After considering pleadings, depositions, and affidavits, the trial judge denied the buyers’ summary-judgment and defense-striking motions and denied the farmers’ motions for judgment on the pleadings and dismissal. The judge certified immediate appeals and contemplated a jury trial on fraud. (962–963, 967.)

## Output obligation, consideration, and remedy
The contracts were enforceable output contracts under the then-numbered UCC provision, Code section 109A-2-306(1). Quantity was defined by the farmer’s crop on specified acreage, rather than an arbitrary later election. Following Harris v. Hine, the court held the description sufficiently definite as to quantity and subject matter and sufficient for the UCC statute of frauds. Both sides made binding promises: the farmer would sell the output and the buyer would buy it. The buyer’s promise supplied consideration for the seller’s, and mutuality was present. (964–965.)

Specific performance was available under section 109A-2-716 because the parties had stipulated that this cotton was unique. The assigned account uses that stipulation to illustrate equitable relief. The decision does not announce that every crop or fungible commodity is necessarily unique, or that parties can obtain equitable enforcement of any otherwise invalid bargain merely by selecting that remedy. Here the court also rejected each challenge to the underlying contracts. (965.)

## Unconscionability and market change
The farmers relied on Campbell Soup v. Wentz, where objectionable provisions could free the buyer from accepting goods while preventing the producer from selling them elsewhere without the buyer’s consent. Those provisions were absent here. The court assessed these contracts against the cotton trade’s commercial background and the circumstances when they were made. A forward contract can benefit the producer by guaranteeing both a market and a price for a fluctuating commodity. March’s price was fair when agreed, and the later dramatic market rise did not make the original terms unconscionable. Kimsey itself sold most of the contracted cotton months later at prices below the eventual market peak. Neither the price nor the production, harvesting, or ginning requirements were so one-sided as to warrant refusing enforcement. (965–966.)

## Fraud allegations and summary-judgment evidence
The farmers alleged that Kimsey falsely represented the agreed price as fair, claimed other farmers had already signed, and predicted a lower fall price. Those allegations were not findings of deceit. The buyers supported their motions with uncontradicted evidence, much of it the farmers’ own testimony. The farmers described 30 cents as satisfactory and above or close to prevailing prices. March futures quotations of 36–38 cents did not contradict that evidence because the local crop was valued about 10 cents below futures owing to quality and grade. Market information was publicly available, and most farmers followed it. Other local farmers were in fact contracting at similar prices, with approximately 75 percent of the area’s cotton crop under contract. Predictions about fall prices or later mill prices, even if made, were treated as conjecture about future events rather than actionable fraud on this record. (966–967.)

Once that evidence pierced the allegations, the farmers could not avoid summary judgment by relying on pleadings alone. Under section 81A-156(e), the responding party had to identify specific facts showing a genuine issue. The court read the depositions as showing dissatisfaction only after prices rose in August and September; the farmers first sought upward renegotiation and repudiated when that failed. Their unsupported fraud allegations did not require a jury trial. (967–968.)

## Exact result and reading scope
The court concluded the contracts were not invalid or unenforceable on any asserted ground and were not induced by actionable fraud. It reversed the buyers’ adverse rulings and dismissed the cross-appeals as unnecessary to decide. It did not report a final amount of recovery or direct a new finding that every cotton contract is unique.
