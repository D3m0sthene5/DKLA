# H. R. Moch Co. v. Rensselaer Water Co.
247 N.Y. 160, 159 N.E. 896 (1928) | Contracts

## Holding and judgment
On January 10, 1928, the New York Court of Appeals affirmed dismissal of a warehouse owner's action against a water company for allegedly failing to supply enough water and pressure to prevent a fire's spread. The city's hydrant-service contract did not demonstrate an intention to compensate individual property owners for nonperformance. The pleaded negligent omission also did not establish either a common-law tort duty to this plaintiff or a statutory right to recover. Cardozo wrote; Pound, Crane, Andrews, Lehman, and Kellogg concurred, and O'Brien did not sit. The judgment was affirmed with costs. (161, 163–170.)

## Contract, alleged fire loss, and procedure
The company contracted with the City of Rensselaer to provide water for a term of years. Its promises addressed sewer flushing, street sprinkling, schools, public buildings, and fire hydrants, the last at $42.50 annually for each hydrant. A separate branch provided water to private homes, factories, and other industries at reasonable rates subject to a stated schedule. That division between municipal services and direct supply to private takers became important to the court's interpretation of the undertaking. (163–164.)

A fire began in another building and spread to Moch's nearby warehouse, destroying it and its contents. The complaint alleged that the company received prompt notice and had equipment capable of providing water and pressure sufficient to prevent the spread, but negligently failed to furnish it. These were pleading allegations, not findings after a trial. Moch claimed damages for the company's failure to perform its city contract. Special Term denied a motion in the nature of a demurrer; a divided Appellate Division reversed and dismissed the complaint. Moch appealed, relying alternatively on third-party contract enforcement, common-law tort liability, and breach of statutory duty. (161, 163.)

## A public benefit did not itself create an enforceable contractual right
Cardozo began from the then-applicable premise that the city had no legal duty to supply inhabitants with fire protection. The company therefore had not assumed an existing city obligation to compensate this owner. A public-contract claimant needed more than the proposition that sound municipal contracts benefit the public: the agreement had to indicate an undertaking to make reparation directly to individual members of the public when the benefit was lost. The hydrant promise ran to the city in its corporate capacity; the distinct private-service promise addressed takers at their homes and factories. No intention to compensate all property owners for loss caused by deficient hydrant performance appeared. (164.)

The court used comparisons to explain the limiting principle. A supplier of fuel to a public building would not, merely because the contract benefited visitors, owe contract damages to one who became ill because the building was cold. Mail carriers and suppliers of firefighting equipment likewise would not acquire contractual responsibility for every consequential loss to everyone who would benefit from performance. Cardozo found the great preponderance of water-company authorities consistent with treating this kind of fire-protection benefit as incidental. (164–165.)

The potential scope of loss reinforced the inference about contractual intent. A hydrant-service undertaking for a small annual payment would otherwise expose the company to damages if an entire city burned. Cardozo compared that disproportion with New York's then-existing limitation on liability for negligently originated fires spreading to other premises. That historical analogy is part of the 1928 reasoning, not a representation of present fire-liability law. The contract was not read as an assumption of an overwhelming and indefinitely extended risk for the limited reward. (165–166.)

Nor did this analysis prohibit all enforcement of municipal contracts by private persons. The court distinguished contracts setting private water rates, fixing streetcar fares, or expressly assuming public-work liabilities owed to inhabitants. Those arrangements demonstrated an intention that the affected individuals could enforce the promise as occasion arose. The decisive difference was the intention to undertake direct responsibility, rather than public purpose alone. (166.)

## Tort: withheld benefit versus a newly created source of harm
Moch argued that once the company began furnishing water, negligent continuation or withdrawal without reasonable notice became actionable in tort. The court acknowledged that a voluntary undertaking, even gratuitous, can create a duty of care. It rejected a mechanical equation of omission with immunity: where a legally recognized relationship already carries a duty, negligent omissions can be tortious just as affirmative acts can. A surgeon's failure to sterilize instruments illustrates that point. (167.)

The antecedent question was whether the conduct generated the relevant relationship with this plaintiff. The court distinguished action advancing far enough that stopping would actively cause injury from conduct whose cessation only withheld a benefit. Beginning performance of a contract did not automatically create tort duties to an indefinite series of persons who expected to benefit from the promisee's receipt of performance. Otherwise a coal supplier would acquire duties to the shop's customers, and a manufacturer to people relying on its buyer for their own supply. That expansion was not justified by the water company's contractual undertaking. (167–168.)

The decision concerned a mere negligent omission without aggravating allegations. The court expressly reserved possible remedies for malicious withholding or reduction of water pressure, and for reckless and wanton indifference to measured and foreseen consequences. It did not decide those questions against claimants. On the pleaded facts, insufficient water denied a benefit rather than constituting an actionable wrong to Moch. (169.)

## Statutory duty and exact disposition
Transportation Corporations Law section 81 required the company, according to its capacity, to provide water at reasonable rates through suitable connections when demanded by inhabitants and through hydrants or public buildings when demanded by the city. That service obligation did not enlarge the relevant zone of liability to include indirect loss suffered by another person because hydrant pressure was deficient. The breach would be owed to the person entitled to service at the time and place where it was due. A statutory obligation to accept a shipper's goods similarly would not make a railroad liable to a different person relying on prompt delivery for the success of its work. (169–170.)

The Court of Appeals affirmed the appellate judgment dismissing the complaint, with costs. There was no finding that the company's performance was adequate and no adjudication of damages; the pleaded allegations failed to establish the asserted duties to this owner. (170.)
