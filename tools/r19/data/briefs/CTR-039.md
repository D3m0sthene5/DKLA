# Speakers of Sport v. ProServ

178 F.3d 862 (7th Cir. 1999) | Contracts

## Citation and Parties

Speakers of Sport, Inc. v. ProServ, Inc., 178 F.3d 862 (7th Cir. 1999). Seventh Circuit; Chief Judge Posner. Assigned excerpt: CB 521-523. Speakers was the former sports agency and plaintiff; ProServ was its competitor and defendant. Baseball player Ivan Rodriguez was the client whose switch generated the dispute, not the plaintiff suing on an endorsement guarantee.

## Material Facts

Beginning in 1991, Rodriguez retained Speakers under successive one-year agreements terminable at will. In 1995, ProServ sought to expand its baseball representation and told him it could obtain between $2 million and $4 million in endorsements if he switched. He terminated Speakers and signed with ProServ. ProServ obtained no significant endorsements, and after approximately a year he moved to another agent. That later agent negotiated a five-year, $42 million playing contract. CB 521-522; 178 F.3d at 864.

Speakers claimed the endorsement representation was fraudulent and induced loss of its client. But Rodriguez's departure did not breach his at-will agreement. That fact limited the former agency's theory: it could not simply claim ProServ had induced a contractual breach or that Rodriguez owed it continued retention. CB 521-523; 178 F.3d at 865.

## Procedural History

Speakers sued in federal court under diversity jurisdiction for tortious interference and related Illinois-law violations. The district court granted summary judgment to ProServ. The Seventh Circuit affirmed, construing the record favorably to Speakers to the extent supported. This posture differs from assuming all pleaded facts at a motion-to-dismiss stage. CB 521-522; 178 F.3d at 864.

## Issues

Was the endorsement statement an actionable false promise or an aspirational competitive sales pitch? Even if treated as a promise, did it satisfy Illinois's requirements for promissory fraud? Could Speakers establish compensable loss of fees on a later contract negotiated by a different agent?

## Rules and Holding

Competition for an at-will client is generally privileged, although the privilege does not authorize fraud or other unlawful means. Illinois's promissory-fraud doctrine, as the court describes it, requires a scheme to defraud supported by more than the allegedly false promise alone. The court held the statement to be aspirational puffery in context and, independently, insufficient to establish the required fraudulent scheme. CB 522-523; 178 F.3d at 865-867.

## Reasoning: The Meaning of the Supposed Promise

The court distinguished ProServ's statement from a commitment to employ Rodriguez at a fixed salary or to pay any shortfall below a guaranteed level of endorsements. Third-party advertisers would decide whether to make endorsement deals. Nothing indicated that Rodriguez understood ProServ to be guaranteeing the minimum from its own funds if the deals failed. The reasonable reading was that ProServ would try and was optimistic about the result. CB 522-523.

Lack of control over advertisers was relevant context, not an absolute legal bar to promising an outcome. The court expressly acknowledged that parties can make binding warranties about matters outside their control. A guarantee shifts that risk. The problem was that the actual communication was not reasonably understood as such a guarantee. CB 522-523.

The competitive setting also mattered. Allowing every disappointed forecast to support an inference of fraud would expose competing agents to litigation merely for saying they could serve a client better. The court worried that the old agent could turn an unfulfilled prediction into a jury question about what the new agent secretly intended. The Illinois scheme requirement and the interpretation of the statement as puffery helped limit that risk. 178 F.3d at 865-867.

## Full-Opinion Additions: Related Theories and Damages

The full opinion rejects a broader interference theory based on unethical competition without unlawful means. The court worried that vague trade or professional standards could protect incumbent competitors at customers’ expense. A major-league-baseball rule against agents’ misrepresentations protected players, not the displaced agency; even assuming the rule established an enforceable norm, Speakers was outside its protected interest. Its violation also was not itself a violation of law establishing wrongful interference under the court’s analysis. 178 F.3d at 867.

Speakers also invoked the Illinois Consumer Fraud and Deceptive Business Practices Act. As a nonconsumer competitor, it had to establish by clear and convincing evidence that the conduct implicated consumer-protection concerns. It made no effort to do so. Rodriguez did not claim injury or deception and testified favorably about his ProServ agent’s honesty and service. The court observed that potential harm to consumers might support an injunction in a different case, but Speakers sought only damages. The Act’s incorporation of FTC standards protected consumers and competition, rather than protecting an agency from competition. The puffery here was not an unfair act or method of competition. 178 F.3d at 867–868.

Independently, Speakers could not establish entitlement to its fee on the later $42 million playing contract. Another agent negotiated that contract after Rodriguez had left both Speakers and ProServ. Given the at-will relationship and Rodriguez's decision not to return to Speakers, retention through the later negotiation was too speculative to support the requested damages. “He once was our client” did not establish the counterfactual causal chain. 178 F.3d at 867-868.

## Distinctions for Class

Puffery concerns what the communication reasonably conveys: some language expresses hope rather than a commitment or factual assurance. A broken binding promise creates a contract issue without automatically proving that the promisor lacked intent to perform at formation. Promissory fraud instead concerns a deceptive representation of then-existing intention, subject to jurisdiction-specific restrictions. CB 519-523.

Application. “We will secure endorsements and pay you the difference if your annual total is below $2 million” is much closer to a warranty than the statement as construed here. “We already have three signed sponsors” is an assertion about an existing fact. A knowingly fabricated list of sponsors could also supply conduct beyond a lone unfulfilled promise. These changes alter the analysis; they do not establish liability without reliance, causation, and other applicable elements.
