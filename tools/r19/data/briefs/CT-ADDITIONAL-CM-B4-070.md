# Dutton and Wife v. Poole
2 Lev. 210–212, 83 Eng. Rep. 523–524 (K.B. 1677); affirmed, T. Raym. 302–303, 83 Eng. Rep. 156–157 (Exch. Ch. 1679) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural stages
The King's Bench allowed a daughter and her husband to enforce the heir's promise to her father to pay her £1,000. The father had supplied the requested consideration by preserving timber that otherwise would have been sold to provide portions for his younger children. Although the daughter neither made the bargain nor personally supplied that consideration, the court eventually entered judgment for the plaintiffs after a verdict in their favor. All four judges ultimately agreed, following an initial division and a change in the court's personnel. A separate report of the 1679 error proceeding records unanimous affirmance in the Exchequer Chamber. These are two judicial stages, both preserved here, rather than a single undifferentiated decision. (2 Lev. 210–212; T. Raym. 302–303.)

## Bargain, reliance, and the verdict
The father's intended sale would have raised portions for his younger children. The defendant, his heir, asked him to refrain and promised instead to pay the daughter £1,000. The declaration averred that the father did refrain, but the promised payment was not made. The defendant pleaded non assumpsit; the jury found for the plaintiffs. The reported King's Bench argument therefore concerned whether judgment could be entered on that favorable verdict, rather than whether the plaintiffs had merely alleged a plausible promise. (2 Lev. 210–211.)

The later error report identifies the father as Sir Edward Pool, the daughter as Grizil, her husband as Ralph Dutton, and the defendant as Nevil Pool. It describes timber in Oaksey Park, Wiltshire, and places the father's intention to sell on May 1 in the twenty-sixth regnal year of Charles II, 1674. The defendant's request and undertaking were at Sherborn, Gloucestershire. Payment was not made either while Grizil was unmarried or after her marriage. The error report expressly records a verdict for £1,000 damages and judgment on that verdict. Those additional details come from the subsequent judicial record, not from the shorter assigned account. (T. Raym. 302–303.)

## Objection in arrest of judgment
The defendant argued that the father, or his executors if he had died, was the proper claimant. The promise had been made to the father. The daughter was neither privy to that promise nor interested in the consideration, and no debt was already due to her. The defendant also argued that the father could still cut the timber, leaving the son without a remedy, while the daughter could not release a promise made to someone else. On that account, the daughter's intended receipt of money did not give her the necessary legal relationship to sue. (2 Lev. 210–211.)

The defense invoked decisions denying recovery by children on a promise to pay them money in consideration of a marriage. The report specifically discusses Norris and Pine as a recent contrary adjudication. The objection thus combined the identity of the promisee, the source of consideration, and precedent; it was not a denial that the son would benefit economically from preserving the timber. (2 Lev. 211–212.)

## Plaintiffs' arguments and the court's reasoning
The plaintiffs compared the daughter to a person entitled to receive money or goods delivered through an intermediary: the intended recipient could sue because the transaction was for that recipient's benefit. They also used the example of a transfer of a horse in return for a promise to pay the transferor's son. They argued that natural affection obliged a father to provide for his children and could support a use of his estate for them. Here, the son obtained the preserved timber, while the daughter lost the portion that its sale otherwise would have provided. These arguments connected her interest in the promised payment to the father's actual forbearance and the defendant's resulting benefit. (2 Lev. 211–212.)

At the first argument, Wylde and Jones inclined to require suit by the father or his executors, even if recovery would benefit the daughter. Twysden and Rainsford took the contrary position. Subsequently, Scroggs replaced Rainsford as Chief Justice, and Dolben replaced Twysden. On reargument, Scroggs reasoned that the father's natural obligation and affection toward his children permitted the consideration and promise to the father to extend to them. He rejected the contrary Norris and Pine resolution; Dolben agreed that the daughter could maintain the action. Jones and Wylde initially hesitated but agreed the next day. Jones acknowledged his doubts about Norris and Pine while explaining his reluctance to depart from a resolved case. Judgment was therefore given for the plaintiffs with the final agreement of all four judges. (2 Lev. 212.)

The rationale should remain tied to the reported transaction. There was requested forbearance, an heir who obtained the benefit of the preserved wood, and a daughter deprived of the anticipated portion. The report does not establish a universal rule that any person incidentally benefited by another person's contract can sue. Nor does its reference to natural affection mean that the father supplied no consideration: his forbearance was expressly pleaded and supported the verdict.

## Separate error proceeding and exact outcome
The King's Bench report states that error was immediately brought and that the judgment was affirmed in Trinity term of the thirty-first regnal year, 1679. The separate Raymond report confirms the proceeding and supplies its arguments. Holt, representing the plaintiff in error—the defendant below—renewed the objection that the promise was to Sir Edward, while the action was by Grizil and her husband. Pollexfen, for the defendants in error, argued that either the promisee or the person for whose use the promise was made could maintain the action. All the justices and barons agreed with that position, and the judgment was affirmed. Their individual names are not given in this report. (2 Lev. 212; T. Raym. 302–303.)

The Exchequer Chamber disposition affirmed the existing £1,000 verdict and judgment. It was not a new damages assessment or an equitable decree in Chancery. The report's references to the person for whose use a promise was made belong to the assumpsit controversy presented on the writ of error. The two reports do not provide a separate quantified costs award. (T. Raym. 303.)

## Assigned treatment and reading scope
The assigned account, printed 1164–1165, uses Dutton to explain an early allowance of enforcement by a daughter who personally supplied no consideration. It then contrasts later English restrictions. The bracketed reference to Tweddle in the English Reports reprint is likewise later editorial material, not part of the 1677 judges' reasoning. Later statutory discussion in the textbook is outside these historical judgments.
