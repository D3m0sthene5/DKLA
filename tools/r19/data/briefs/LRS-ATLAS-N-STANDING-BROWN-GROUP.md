# United Food and Commercial Workers Union Local 751 v. Brown Group, Inc.

**517 U.S. 544 (1996) — Supreme Court of the United States — May 13, 1996.** Justice Souter wrote for a unanimous Court. Assigned account: November second casebook, physical PDF pages 132–133, printed pages 1189–1190.

Congress could authorize a union to recover WARN Act damages for its members even though determining those damages required individual participation. The usual third requirement for associational standing was a judicial prudential restriction, which Congress could remove, rather than an Article III requirement. This ruling allowed the litigation to proceed; it established neither a notice violation nor any employee's entitlement to a particular award. (517 U.S. at 546–547, 554–558.)

## Facts and procedural history

On January 17, 1992, Brown Shoe Company wrote to a representative of the international union that its Dixon, Missouri, plant would close and 277 employees would be permanently laid off beginning March 20. Local 751 alleged that layoffs had actually begun before the letter and continued through February into March. The union sought the WARN Act's statutory remedy of 60 days' backpay for affected members. Because the complaint had been dismissed, the Supreme Court assumed the truth of the early-layoff allegation for this appeal. It did not decide whether the allegations were ultimately correct. (546–547 & n.1.)

The notice statute required covered employers to wait 60 days after notifying affected employees' representatives—or the employees themselves if unrepresented—before ordering a plant closing or mass layoff. Other recipients included the state dislocated-worker unit and the relevant local government's chief elected official. An employee's statutory remedy included backpay and benefits for the violation period, subject to a maximum of 60 days and no more than half the employee's days of employment. The backpay rate could not fall below the higher of the employee's average regular rate over the last three years or final regular rate. Benefits included qualifying medical expenses. The requested 60-day award therefore did not establish that every worker would recover that amount. (548–549 & n.3.)

The district court dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6), reasoning that monetary claims on members' behalf required individual participation. The Eighth Circuit affirmed: each employee's entitlement and amount would depend on particularized proof, defeating the third requirement of the *Hunt* associational-standing test. It also upheld the district court's refusal to add individual employees as plaintiffs. The Supreme Court reviewed the associational-standing issue, expressly leaving that amendment ruling outside the questions presented. (547 & n.2.)

## Arguments and questions presented

Brown Shoe first argued that WARN did not authorize this union damages suit at all. Because the certiorari question assumed statutory authorization, the company asked the Court to dismiss the writ as improvidently granted. It also relied on legislative history: an earlier bill had allowed a union to recover its own civil penalty, but Congress removed that provision. According to the company, unions consequently were not similarly situated to employees with recoverable losses. (548–550.)

The union maintained that the enacted statute authorized it to enforce its members' claims and that Congress could remove the individual-participation barrier. Alternatively, it argued that the damages were sufficiently simple to satisfy the ordinary third requirement even without congressional alteration. The Court found statutory abrogation and therefore did not decide that alternative. The union's separate claim of standing based on injury to itself also remained unresolved. (551 n.4, 554 n.5.)

The two controlling questions were whether Congress authorized a representative damages action and, if so, whether Article III nevertheless prohibited it. (546, 548–551.)

## Statutory authorization

The Court affirmed its earlier reading in *North Star Steel* that WARN's plaintiffs included employees' unions as representatives. Section 2104(a)(1) imposed liability to each aggrieved employee. Section 2104(a)(5) authorized a person enforcing that liability, including an employees' representative, to sue for that person, similarly situated persons, or both. WARN defined the representative by reference to the employees' exclusive representative under federal labor law. Those provisions gave the union authority to enforce liability owed to its members. Since the statute did not give the union its own relevant monetary remedy, interpreting its express authority as useless would frustrate the enacted text. (548–549.)

Removing a union's separate penalty did not necessarily remove its authority to recover for employees. The union and employees were similarly situated as recipients entitled to notice. The company's contrary explanation depended on treating the express authorization as legislative oversight. A straightforward construction giving effect to all enacted provisions prevailed over that speculative possibility. The Court accordingly rejected the request to dismiss certiorari. (550.)

## Constitutional and prudential limits

*Hunt* required that members have standing in their own right, the protected interests be germane to the organization's purposes, and neither the claim nor requested relief require individual members' participation. The Eighth Circuit accepted the first two requirements here. Only the third was disputed. Earlier cases had generally allowed representative prospective relief and had been understood to bar associations' damages claims. Brown Group distinguished that established limitation from a constitutional prohibition. (552–555.)

The first requirement directly ensures an actual member's injury, causation, and redressability and therefore is an Article III necessity. Germaneness complements it by giving the association a stake in the dispute and ensuring an effective adversary. Once both are satisfied, requiring individual participation adds no necessary constitutional component. The third requirement instead addresses administration and efficiency: an organization might lack individualized evidence, be unable to calculate damages adequately, or fail to distribute recoveries to injured members. Those concerns matter, but they are not equivalent to absence of a case or controversy. (555–557.)

Existing representative damages litigation supported that distinction. Class actions, bankruptcy trustees' suits, state parens patriae actions, and estate litigation permitted representatives to recover for others. Statutes also authorized the EEOC to seek employee backpay and the Labor Secretary to recover unpaid wages. Nothing showed that these arrangements inherently produced nonadversarial litigation, and the Court saw no greater Article III danger in WARN union suits. Congress thus could remove the judicially imposed third requirement and had done so. (557–558 & nn.7–8.)

## Judgment and limits

The Court **reversed the Eighth Circuit and remanded** for proceedings consistent with its opinion. All nine Justices joined; there were no separate opinions. The ruling settled the union's authority and associational standing, rather than establishing liability or awarding damages. (545, 558.)

Several reservations prevent overstating the result. Whether the January notice was improperly addressed to an international-union representative instead of Local 751 was not decided. Nor did the Court decide the union's direct-injury theory or whether a simplified damages claim would independently satisfy ordinary *Hunt* requirements. It also did not decide whether Congress could conclusively establish germaneness in another setting. Footnote 6 distinguished standing from adequate representation sufficient to bind absent members: germaneness alone does not establish that a representative judgment should have preclusive effect. Adequacy was undisputed for this duly selected collective-bargaining representative. (547 n.1, 551 n.4, 554 n.5, 556 n.6.)

The assigned account quotes the explanation of the first two *Hunt* requirements and the absence of a further constitutional necessity. The complete opinion supplies the statutory construction, concrete layoff allegations, damages restrictions, and reservations omitted from that selection. The preceding *Maritime* discussion and following *Students for Fair Admissions* account are different cases. This brief uses the Court's 1996 distinction between constitutional and prudential requirements without claiming to settle every later standing doctrine.
