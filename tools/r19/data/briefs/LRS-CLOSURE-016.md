# Public Citizen v. Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration
374 F.3d 1209 (D.C. Cir. 2004) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, question, and result

On July 16, 2004, the D.C. Circuit unanimously decided No. 03-1165. Sentelle wrote for Edwards, Sentelle, and Tatel. Public Citizen and other public-interest groups challenged FMCSA's 2003 revisions to commercial-driver hours-of-service rules. The court granted review, vacated the entire rule, and remanded because the agency wholly failed to consider its effects on drivers' physical health, a statutorily required factor. The court discussed other serious defects but expressly withheld final judgment on them. There were no separate opinions. (1210–1211, 1216–1217, 1223.)

## Old limits and proposed changes

The prior rules restricted both daily driving and the hours a driver could work before becoming ineligible to drive. They allowed ten hours of driving following eight hours off, or driving within fifteen hours on duty before another eight hours off. Breaks could extend the elapsed workday because they stopped the on-duty clock. The weekly limits barred driving after sixty on-duty hours in seven consecutive days or seventy in eight. A sleeper-berth exception allowed the nominally consecutive rest to be divided into two periods of at least two hours each. Drivers kept logbooks subject to inspection. These were limits on working and still driving, not absolute prohibitions on all further nondriving work. (1211–1212.)

Congress had directed revision to address fatigue, sleep, rest cycles, recorders, and other safety issues. The agency's governing law separately required consideration of safe vehicle operation, drivers' fitness to operate safely, effects of operation on drivers' physical condition, and regulatory costs and benefits. FMCSA took responsibility after its creation in 1999 and proposed changes in May 2000. (1211–1212.)

The proposal relied on the importance of regular twenty-four-hour circadian schedules and uninterrupted restorative sleep. For long-haul drivers it proposed twelve hours of combined duty and driving, two hours off during the workday, and ten consecutive hours off afterward. It would eliminate split sleeper-berth rest for solo drivers and lengthen the minimum split period for teams. A mandatory weekly weekend covering two nighttime periods would address accumulated sleep debt. Electronic on-board recorders would replace easily falsified logbooks. (1212–1214.)

## The materially different final rule

The April 2003 final rule instead permitted eleven driving hours, required ten hours off, and barred driving fourteen hours after work began. It increased off-duty time and removed the break-based extension of the driving-eligible day, but increased permitted driving from ten to eleven hours and dropped the proposed mandatory two-hour daytime break. A driver maximizing driving rather than all duty time could operate on a twenty-one-hour cycle, while a full fourteen-hour workday plus ten hours off produced a twenty-four-hour cycle. FMCSA acknowledged the scientific ideal of a twenty-four-hour schedule but considered mandatory adherence too disruptive to trucking. (1214–1215.)

The weekly sixty- and seventy-hour limits remained, but a new thirty-four-hour rest period restarted the weekly calculation. This allowed more work within an actual seven- or eight-day interval; the opinion illustrates seventy-seven hours in seven days using permissible driving/rest rotations and the restart. The agency rejected the mandatory weekend, reasoning partly that discouraging nighttime travel would worsen daytime highway congestion. It also retained split sleeper-berth rest for both solo and team drivers and declined to mandate electronic recorders at that time. Industry practice, convenience, uncertain evidence and costs, implementation difficulties, and privacy concerns figured in those choices. (1214–1216.)

## The decisive omitted factor: drivers' health

Under State Farm, the agency had to connect its findings rationally to its choice and consider important aspects of the problem. Section 31136(a)(4) expressly required protection against vehicle operation's deleterious effects on operators' physical condition. A factor Congress makes mandatory is necessarily important to the agency's mission. The record disclosed no consideration of this distinct health question. That omission alone required vacatur. (1216.)

FMCSA argued that driver health permeated its safety analysis and that health need not override costs and benefits. The court did not demand exclusive priority for health. It demanded consideration. Whether an overworked driver causes crashes is different from whether prolonged driving and lost sleep damage that driver's physical condition. The two matters overlap but are not interchangeable; other statutory provisions already address vehicle-operation safety. Potential long-term back or immune-system effects illustrated the inquiry, rather than constituting adjudicated medical findings. The agency could conclude that particular health effects were not problematic or were outweighed, but had to explain that conclusion. Silence throughout the rulemaking could not satisfy Congress's direction. (1216–1217.)

## Other concerns, expressly left without final adjudication

The court doubted the justification for increasing driving time to eleven hours. FMCSA acknowledged worsening performance late in a duty period but identified no particular studies explaining why a shorter overall workday compensated for more driving. Lawyers' specific citations in the appellate brief could not substitute for the agency's own explanation. Increased weekly hours also might offset any daily benefit. The cost-benefit model excluded time-on-task effects because their magnitude was uncertain, effectively assuming away the very fatigue effect it purported to justify. Uncertainty did not justify treating a conceded effect as nonexistent; informed estimation or extrapolation remained possible. These were reasons for doubt, not a separately entered holding invalidating eleven-hour driving. (1217–1219.)

The sleeper-berth reasoning likewise appeared weak. Evidence that berth sleep was less restorative than bed sleep, or that teams used berths better than solo drivers, did not establish that split rest was safe. Another study involved sleep in hospitals and motels rather than sleeper berths. Convenience might justify adjusting the length of a continuous rest period, but said little about splitting it. Entrenched use of berths was also different from entrenched split rest: eliminating the exception would not prohibit using the berth for uninterrupted sleep. The court expressed grave doubts without finally adjudicating this objection. (1219–1220.)

The electronic-recorder discussion distinguished a duty to evaluate from a duty to adopt. Congress's direction to deal with automated, tamper-proof recording devices required serious analysis of costs and safety benefits, but did not necessarily require mandating devices. FMCSA had neither tested available equipment nor attempted adequate benefit estimation despite recognized widespread logbook cheating. Unknown prices, inconsistent readout formats, privacy concerns, and potential litigation use focused on costs without weighing possible compliance benefits. Regulators operate under uncertainty; they must exercise expertise rather than use uncertainty as a reason to disregard a congressional command. The court again described likely flaws rather than deciding an independent ground of invalidity. The sole judicial note illustrates logbook noncompliance with a cultural reference; it supplies no additional operative legal test. (1220–1222 & n. 1.)

Finally, the restart explanation described useful rest and schedule flexibility but did not acknowledge or justify the resulting increase in maximum weekly work. That likely was another important aspect of the problem. The agency could reconsider all these interrelated matters after assessing physical health, which was why the court declined final decisions on the remaining challenges. (1222–1223.)

## Exact judgment and assigned limits

The closing judgment vacated the rule in its entirety and remanded for proceedings consistent with the opinion. It did not merely require an explanatory supplement while leaving the rule intact, mandate electronic recorders, or prescribe a replacement driving schedule. The final rule's omitted statutory health analysis supplied the dispositive ground. (1216–1217, 1223.)
