# Republic of Philippines v. Pimentel

**553 U.S. 851 (2008) — decided June 12, 2008.** Justice Kennedy wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Scalia, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer, and Alito. Justice Souter joined except Parts IV–B and V; Justice Stevens joined Part II only. Both filed opinions concurring in part and dissenting in part. Seven Justices ordered dismissal; the two separate writers favored further proceedings. Assigned Civil Procedure account: physical PDF pages 408–409, printed pages 784–786, followed by editorial Notes on printed pages 786–787.

**Central holding.** Where required parties asserting sovereign immunity have nonfrivolous claims to disputed assets and adjudication could injure their interests, a court cannot effectively adjudicate those claims against them in their absence by predicting they will lose. Here the Rule 19(b) analysis required dismissal of a statutory interpleader action. The Court reversed the Ninth Circuit and remanded with instructions that it order the district court to dismiss. The decision did not award the assets to the Philippines or undo the victims' separate damages judgment against Marcos. (864–873.)

## Assets, claimants, and the parallel proceedings

In 1972, Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos incorporated Arelma under Panamanian law. Arelma deposited $2 million in a Merrill Lynch brokerage account in New York. By 2000, the account held approximately $35 million. A class of about 9,539 victims of Marcos's human-rights abuses obtained a nearly $2 billion judgment in federal court in Hawaii against Marcos and his estate. These creditors, called the Pimentel class, sought to attach the Arelma assets. Separate Roxas claimants asserted rights based on their own judgment against Imelda Marcos. (857–858.)

The Republic and its Presidential Commission on Good Governance claimed that Philippine law had forfeited assets derived from misuse of public office to the Republic from the moment of misappropriation. After Marcos fled in 1986, the Commission pursued assets in Switzerland, including Arelma shares. Switzerland froze assets, and they were later placed in escrow at the Philippine National Bank pending proceedings in the Sandiganbayan, the Philippine court with special jurisdiction over corruption cases. The Commission had initiated those forfeiture proceedings in 1991. The Republic and Commission asked Merrill Lynch to transfer the brokerage assets into a comparable escrow account; it did not. (858–859.)

Merrill Lynch instead filed statutory interpleader under § 1335 in Hawaii, naming the Republic, Commission, Arelma, PNB, the Pimentel class, and other claimants. Merrill Lynch was the stakeholder **plaintiff**; the competing claimants were defendants. Judge Manuel Real, who had presided over the human-rights litigation by designation, directed the filing and presided over the interpleader. The distinction between the original victims' suit and this new stakeholder action is essential to understanding Rule 19(b)(4). (859, 871.)

## The procedural problem sovereign immunity created

The Republic and Commission invoked the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and moved to dismiss because the interpleader could not proceed without them. Arelma and PNB also sought Rule 19(b) dismissal. The district judge initially rejected the sovereigns' dismissal request without deciding immunity. On the first appeal, the Ninth Circuit held them immune and required parties under Rule 19(a), and stayed proceedings pending the Sandiganbayan's decision. (859.)

The district judge later vacated the stay, reasoning that the Philippine litigation could not determine entitlement to these assets, and awarded the fund to the Pimentel class. A week later the Republic asked the Sandiganbayan specifically to declare the Arelma assets forfeited. That court had not ruled when the Supreme Court decided this case. On a second appeal, the Ninth Circuit affirmed. Although the sovereign entities were required parties, it predicted their claims would fail, principally because a New York six-year limitations period supposedly barred a misappropriation action. It also emphasized their delay in obtaining a Philippine judgment and the victims' probable lack of another enforcement forum. (860–861.)

## Standing and the Rule 19 framework

The Pimentel class argued that the Republic and Commission, having secured dismissal on immunity grounds, could not appeal or seek Supreme Court review. The Court left that question open because Arelma and PNB remained proper petitioners. They had pursued the nonjoinder issue throughout. Seeking vacatur of the entire judgment on procedural grounds gave them a stake even though they had not petitioned for review of the substantive denial of their competing claims. Generally any party may seek Rule 19(b) dismissal, and a court with jurisdiction may address an absent required party itself. All nine Justices joined this procedural holding. (861–862.)

Rule 19(a) identifies persons who should be joined if feasible. If joinder is impossible, subdivision (b) asks whether equity and good conscience permit proceeding. Its nonexclusive factors address prejudice, measures to reduce prejudice, adequacy of a judgment rendered without the absentee, and whether the plaintiff would have an adequate remedy following dismissal. The Court explained that the 2007 changes from “necessary” to “required,” and deletion of “indispensable,” were stylistic rather than substantive. The pre-2007 Rule appears in the judicial appendix. Required-party status does not automatically mean dismissal in every case: the analysis remains sensitive to substantive, procedural, and institutional interests. (855–857, 862–864, appendix 873–875.)

Here, however, required-party status and sovereign immunity were uncontested. The Court also reserved the precise appellate standard of review for Rule 19(b) rulings. Although the equitable inquiry implies some deference, the lower courts' legal errors required reversal under any appropriate standard. (863–865.)

## Prejudice: respecting immunity means more than dismissing the sovereign

The first factor required appropriate weight for the sovereigns' interests. Sovereign immunity protects comity, equality, and dignity, not merely freedom from a named place in a caption. The Philippines had a concrete interest in adjudicating claims arising from historically significant abuses and determining whether, and how, recovered assets should compensate victims. Seizing property it claimed through a foreign decree could prejudice that interest even if the sovereign itself had been dismissed. (865–867.)

The Ninth Circuit's prediction that the Republic would lose under New York limitations law effectively diminished its immunity by adjudicating a consequential substantive interest without its consent. Drawing on earlier governmental-immunity decisions, the Court stated that dismissal is required when an immune required sovereign has nonfrivolous claims and there is potential injury to its interests. It left room to disregard genuinely frivolous claims and acknowledged that Rule 19 sometimes requires a preliminary assessment of claims. The defect was the definitive adverse treatment of this nonfrivolous sovereign claim, not an absolute prohibition on examining any merits-related matter when applying Rule 19. (867–869.)

Even the limitations prediction was uncertain. A Philippine ownership ruling might permit the Republic to proceed on behalf of Arelma for breach of the brokerage contract, with accrual when Merrill Lynch refused delivery, or to seek enforcement of a foreign judgment. The Court did not determine whether any such action would succeed. These alternatives demonstrated why the claim could not simply be assumed barred. It respected the victims' need for recovery and international anticorruption policy, but held that the courts had underweighted immunity rather than merely overvalued the victims' interests. (867–869.)

## The remaining factors and why dismissal followed

The second factor did not supply a protective alternative. None had been proposed or appeared available that would allow adjudication of competing ownership claims while avoiding prejudice to the absent sovereigns. A contest over ownership of the same assets could not be fairly resolved by treating one set of claimants as certain to lose. (869–870.)

Under the third factor, “adequacy” meant the public interest in resolving the dispute as a whole, not simply satisfying the Pimentel class's judgment. The Republic and Commission would not be bound by a judgment entered in their absence. Paying the victims through that judgment therefore would not settle the entire dispute. (870–871.)

The fourth factor concerned the alternative remedy of Merrill Lynch, the interpleader plaintiff. The victims' interests remained relevant to the equitable balance, but their lack of an enforcement forum was not a substitute for identifying the statutory plaintiff correctly. Dismissal would leave Merrill Lynch without a definitive ownership judgment; nevertheless, in later piecemeal suits it could try to join the sovereigns and obtain Rule 19(b) dismissal if immunity persisted. The Court described that defense as likely effective, not guaranteed. Any remaining stakeholder prejudice was outweighed by prejudice to the immune absentees. Sovereign immunity sometimes does leave claims without a United States forum. (871–872.)

## Exact judgment and separate opinions

The Court ordered reversal and remand with instructions to dismiss, rather than merely requiring the lower courts to rebalance. It recognized that the equities might change: unreasonable delay by the Sandiganbayan or an eventual ruling could alter the strength of the sovereigns' claims or their willingness to litigate. Later litigation could be brought elsewhere if jurisdiction and venue were proper. These possibilities did not permit this action to proceed now, and they did not decide ownership. (872–873.)

Stevens agreed that the limitations analysis was faulty but opposed giving immunity near-dispositive force here. He preferred a stay pending a reasonably prompt Philippine ruling or reassignment to a new district judge for further proceedings. He described record-based concerns about Judge Real's control of the litigation and impartiality, not a Court finding of judicial misconduct. He thought immunity carried less weight because the Republic would eventually need affirmative United States proceedings to recover the assets and had previously participated in Marcos litigation, even encouraging victims' claims. A fresh judge could reconsider how substantial the competing claim actually was. (875–879.)

Souter joined all of Kennedy's opinion except the alternative-remedy analysis in IV–B and the final dismissal disposition in V. He preferred vacatur and a reasonable stay awaiting the Sandiganbayan's decree, with further district proceedings before a judge new to the case. His narrower disagreement should not be conflated with Stevens's broader objections to the balancing approach. (879.)
