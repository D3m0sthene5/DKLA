# Triple Up Limited v. Youku Tudou Inc. — Appellate Judgment

**Civil Procedure · CP-COMP-CB-TRIPLE-UP-APPEAL**  
**2018 WL 4440459 (D.C. Cir. 2018)** · July 17, 2018 · No. 17-7033  
**Per curiam:** Circuit Judges Rogers, Millett, and Sentelle, unanimous.  
**Assigned:** CivPro.pdf, physical PDF page 126, printed pages 202–203, Note 2, linking this affirmance to the district opinion at 235 F. Supp. 3d 15.

## Dispute as it reached the appellate court

Triple Up, a Seychelles company, claimed American broadcast rights to three Taiwanese films displayed on websites operated by Youku, a Chinese video company. Youku’s headquarters and more than 3,300 employees were outside the United States. Its largely Mandarin-language platforms permitted users to watch and upload videos; company-uploaded material could be restricted by territory. Triple Up’s attorney watched the films from Washington, D.C., and reported advertisements accompanying the streams. Youku removed the identified material promptly after notice. The district court dismissed for lack of personal jurisdiction and refused discovery. Official appellate judgment, pp. 1–2.

The appeal presented a narrower remedial controversy than the initial complaint. Triple Up abandoned statutory copyright damages during the litigation, leaving actual damages and an injunction. Its American broadcast licenses expired at the end of November 2017, approximately eight months before this decision. The appellate record also described American views as less than one-quarter of one percent of monthly views. That later figure must not be substituted silently for the district opinion’s less-than-one-percent description. The appeal was resolved after examination of the record, briefs, and oral argument in a per curiam judgment under the court’s rules governing dispositions without a published opinion. Id. at 1–2.

## Prospective relief required a continuing threat

The panel addressed jurisdiction over injunctive relief separately from personal jurisdiction for actual damages. Youku had removed the videos promptly, and Triple Up offered no allegation of an actual or imminent future injury establishing a real prospect of reposting. At oral argument, Triple Up also conceded that expiration of its licenses mooted prospective relief. An injunction protects against a sufficiently concrete future injury; past allegedly unauthorized availability alone did not provide standing for that remedy. The court therefore rejected the injunction request on its own jurisdictional ground, rather than deciding it merely by borrowing the damages analysis. Id. at 1–2.

This distinction is especially useful procedurally. An alleged completed copyright injury can remain the basis of a damages claim while failing to support prospective relief. Conversely, one cannot assume that a corporation’s contacts with the forum answer whether this plaintiff has a continuing legally cognizable interest in a particular remedy.

## Actual damages and national contacts

For actual damages, the panel considered Rule 4(k)(2) and Fifth Amendment contacts with the United States as a whole. Even that broader geographic frame did not supply sufficient suit-related contacts. Triple Up had to offer plausible facts connecting Youku’s own activity with the forum and these claims; it could not rest on universal website access or independent actions of users and advertising intermediaries. Id. at 2–4.

Youku had no significant American physical presence and no employees here. Its American process agent and American software arrangements did not connect these freely accessible films to American business activity. It had no registered Copyright Office service agent, because it did not target the American market or regularly conduct business here; its designated location for copyright takedown notices was Beijing. The panel did not treat these observations as an adjudication of copyright safe-harbor eligibility. They supported its assessment of the company's relevant American operation. Id. at 2–3.

No alleged American viewer paid Youku to see the films, including Triple Up’s attorney. The websites were predominantly Mandarin-language and not generally aimed at an American audience. Accessibility therefore did not establish a persistent course of forum conduct. Triple Up likewise alleged no harm to its own American business operations: its location was Seychelles. The court contrasted a paid, deliberately American-facing video service in another copyright case, without deciding whether the conduct here infringed any copyright. Id. at 2–4.

## Why advertising and geoblocking were insufficient

Youku did receive advertising revenue, but Triple Up supplied no plausible facts showing that Youku materially selected the pairing of particular advertisements with these particular films or itself directed those advertisements toward American viewers. Advertising agencies' independent choices were insufficient to attribute forum targeting to Youku. Two of the three advertisements shown to the attorney concerned Mandarin-language Chinese video games; the English-language advertisement did not, by itself, establish the necessary defendant-created connection. Id. at 3–4.

Nor did an ability to geoblock prove intentional American exploitation. The panel found no plausible allegation of deliberate bad-faith efforts to stream these works into the United States. It contrasted another case in which the defendant intentionally disabled default restrictions, deleted non-geoblocked formats, and altered records to disguise American availability. Those acts were comparison facts from the other decision, not conduct found against Youku. The current record instead involved disputed company and user uploads, limited American viewing, and prompt removal after notice. Id. at 3–4.

The distinction is between positive evidence of deliberate forum conduct and an inference drawn merely from failure to prevent all access. It does not create blanket immunity for online copyright defendants, nor establish that knowledge, control, targeting, or technological restrictions can never support jurisdiction in a properly evidenced case. The appellate reasoning is its own record-based evaluation; the judgment does not separately adopt every district discussion of causation, waiver, or technological policy.

## Discovery and disposition

The panel reviewed denial of jurisdictional discovery for abuse of discretion and found none. Triple Up's requests were speculative or sought unrelated contacts. Possible American VIP subscribers would not connect the alleged free viewing of the films to American transactions. Its suggestion of American servers was a hunch, unsupported by a factual basis, despite the absence of offices and employees here. The court accepted that the existence of American servers could be relevant in an appropriate case; it upheld denial because discovery was not justified by these allegations and would not establish the missing connection merely through the categories requested. Id. at 4–5.

The exact judgment was **affirmance** of the district court's judgment. The panel unanimously rejected prospective relief for want of a continuing jurisdictional basis and rejected actual-damages jurisdiction for insufficient American contacts. It affirmed the discovery denial as well. It did not decide copyright infringement, secondary liability, or the ultimate measure of damages. The complete five-page judicial disposition contains no footnotes, separate judicial opinions, or appendices; the clerk's signatures are administrative authentication, not separate judicial reasoning. Id. at 5.

## Relation to the assigned account

The casebook identifies the appellate result through its Westlaw citation while developing the district court's geoblocking discussion. The complete appellate judgment adds a separate injunction problem, a later license-expiration event, the revised traffic figure, a closer look at advertising intermediaries, and an abuse-of-discretion discovery analysis. Keeping the stages separate prevents the trial court's original allegations from being mistaken for the full appellate record or every trial rationale from being attributed to the panel.
