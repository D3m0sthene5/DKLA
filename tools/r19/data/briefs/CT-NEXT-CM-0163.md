# Wasserman's Inc. v. Township of Middletown
137 N.J. 238, 645 A.2d 100 (1994) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Justice Pollock wrote for a unanimous seven-member New Jersey Supreme Court: Chief Justice Wilentz and Justices Clifford, Handler, Pollock, O'Hern, Garibaldi, and Stein. It upheld the validity of a municipal lease and a $55,748.27 improvement-cost award, but remanded the enforceability of a separate gross-receipts cancellation payment. The court did not finally declare that provision a penalty or finally enforce it. It affirmed in part, reversed in part, and returned the matter to the Law Division for the necessary damages proceedings. There is no separate writing or numbered judicial footnote in the preserved report. 137 N.J. at 240–241, 258.

## Facts and contractual setting
Wasserman's operated a general store on Township land from 1948 to 1968. The parcel comprised approximately 20,500 square feet, with a 3,200-square-foot store. The Township advertised a new lease in 1969, received only Wasserman's bid, rejected it, and advertised again in May 1970. Wasserman's again was the sole bidder. Negotiations produced a September 1970 approval resolution and a lease signed May 21, 1971. The Township valued the property at $47,500. The lease ran thirty years at fixed monthly rent of $458.33, without escalation. Id. at 241–242.

The bid specifications contemplated proportional reimbursement of improvements if the Township cancelled. The signed agreement also included a second cancellation payment not specified in the original solicitation: twenty-five percent of average annual gross receipts, calculated using the preceding three complete fiscal years. Adding three years of receipts and dividing by twelve produced that quarter-of-an-annual-average amount. Improvements were reimbursed proportionately to the remaining lease term. Id. at 241–242.

Wasserman's spent $142,336.01 expanding and renovating the store to roughly 5,600 square feet in 1971. In 1973 it sold the business and sublet to Rocco Laurino doing business as Jo-Ro, Inc., for $1,850 monthly. The parties agreed to allocate any cancellation payment between them. Later purchasers acquired Jo-Ro's stock for $95,000; the seller represented that the lease remained effective, and the Township permitted the subletting. The Township gave cancellation notice in December 1987, effective December 31, 1988. The occupants vacated without another place for their business. In June 1989 the Township sold the land at auction for $610,000. Id. at 242.

## Proceedings and lease-validity dispute
Plaintiffs sought the contractual cancellation amounts. The Township attacked the gross-receipts provision and initially disputed the improvement reimbursement, although it conceded that latter obligation on this appeal. On cross-motions for summary judgment, the Law Division upheld the lease and cancellation provision, later awarding $346,058.44 plus ten-percent prejudgment interest. Its component calculation was $55,748.27 for renovations and $290,310.18 for gross receipts. The report's displayed total is one cent higher than its recital of the judgment, a source discrepancy rather than a reason to change either component. The Appellate Division affirmed. Id. at 243–244.

The Township argued that adding gross-receipts compensation after bidding violated public-contract requirements and that officials lacked authority to agree to it. The Supreme Court applied the statute in force when the lease was signed, N.J.S.A. 40:60-42. That statute did not require public bidding for this private lease. Voluntarily advertising did not impose all the requirements of an inapplicable bidding statute. Wasserman's was the only bidder twice, and nothing established bad faith, corruption, detriment to municipal interests, or that the amended terms would have attracted another bidder. The record supported negotiations of concessions necessary to secure the lease. Id. at 244–247.

The Township also sought retroactive application of a replacement bidding statute effective six weeks after signing. Its language and history supplied no basis for retroactivity. Invalidating a lawful lease years later also risked constitutional contract-clause problems. The court relied on the formation-time statute and upheld the agreement; it did not adjudicate a constitutional violation. Id. at 247.

## Liquidated-damages standard
The gross-receipts provision remained subject to the prohibition on penalties. Reasonableness under the circumstances was the governing standard: a valid agreed remedy fairly estimates compensation; an excessive punishment cannot be enforced merely because parties included it in their bargain. Difficulties of estimating loss were part of the reasonableness inquiry rather than a mechanically independent requirement. Greater difficulty made a stipulated figure more likely to be reasonable. Labels and subjective descriptions were not dispositive. Id. at 248–251.

The court discussed evaluation at formation or breach and the relevance of actual loss to the reasonableness of predicted harm. It invoked the UCC and Restatement formulations addressing anticipated or actual harm and difficulties of proof. In commercial dealings between parties with comparable bargaining power, stipulated remedies were presumptively reasonable; the challenger bore production and persuasion burdens. The court reserved consumer-contract enforceability and remained alert to unfairness and unconscionability. Id. at 251–254.

## Application and remand
Gross receipts ordinarily omit operating expenses and expenses specifically caused by termination, making them a risky proxy for loss. Other decisions had rejected gross-revenue measures that would pay a supplier for unperformed services or award business revenue without deducting saved costs. Nevertheless, this court did not hold that any percentage of gross receipts is automatically invalid. It lacked the factual basis to determine whether this particular twenty-five-percent amount reasonably approximated cancellation losses. Id. at 254–257.

Jo-Ro's receipts for 1985–1987 totaled $3,483,722.25, yielding average yearly gross receipts of $1,161,240.75 and the claimed $290,310.18 payment. Its tax returns instead showed profits of $3,649 and $414 in the first two years and a $323 loss in the third. The court recognized that tax and actual economic losses are not identical. To the extent the returns reflected economic results, however, they raised a substantial concern about a payment exceeding $290,000. Operating expenses, relocation, and other termination-related losses could not be evaluated from gross receipts alone. Id. at 257.

Enforceability was a legal question that could require underlying factual findings. The Law Division was directed to consider the appropriateness of using gross receipts regardless of cancellation date; the choice of one-quarter of one year's average instead of receipts over all remaining years; the parties' reasons for the formula; mitigation; market rent; and available replacement space. The trial court could decide what further evidence was needed. The Township, as challenger, retained the burden of showing the clause unreasonable. Id. at 257–258.

## Significance and source scope
The decision preserves the lease and improvement reimbursement while allowing a fact-sensitive challenge to the separate revenue-based payment. It illustrates both presumptive respect for negotiated commercial remedies and scrutiny of a figure that could compensate avoided expenses or confer a windfall. The final holding is a remand to determine enforceability, not a completed penalty adjudication.
