# A. v. B. AG — Competition Covenant and Contractual Penalty
Swiss Federal Supreme Court, 4A_202/2024 & 4A_212/2024 (Sept. 24, 2024) | Contracts

## Facts and bargain

B. AG provided personnel, strategy, management, and planning services and traded under Construction21, Care21, and PrimeSelect. On December 11, 2020 its directors and A., chairman of the newly established Arkadium AG, jointly informed construction-industry customers that Arkadium would take over employees, ongoing candidate-placement processes, and customer management retroactively from October 1. A week later, A., B. AG, its directors, and other parties entered a share-purchase arrangement. A. sold his shares in B. AG to that company and a company controlled by its directors; B. AG sold its shares in a company led by A. to him. The parties were separating related business interests, and protecting B. AG's business names formed part of their bargain. Facts A.

Clauses 5.1–5.2 barred A. and connected businesses from using Prime, GetTemp, Construction, Care, and 21 to compete with B. AG or presenting themselves under its name or brands. The prohibition extended to suggesting the former connection with B. AG. It allowed references to past employment in personal résumés and social media, and information indispensable to fulfilling legal or contractual duties to third parties, including data-protection explanations about personal information and contract adjustments necessary to implement the agreement. Clause 5.3 set a CHF 100,000 penalty for each individual violation, payable by A. and/or Sevenedge AG, without releasing compliance obligations or liability for further damage. The litigation concerned this tailored exception and penalty, rather than a ban on every truthful description of the transferred business. Reasoning §4.2.1.

On January 11, 2021 A. sent a letter to Construction21's experts, meaning workers placed with construction-industry businesses. It announced Arkadium's takeover of Construction21's operational business and existing employment contracts. Its letterhead displayed CONSTRUCTION21 beside Arkadium's name, and A.'s signature used Arkadium AG/B. AG and his chairman's title. It also presented Arkadium as a leading headhunter in Construction & Property and advertised its specialized fields. B. AG viewed this as prohibited competitive use of its identity and sued for CHF 100,000 plus five-percent interest. Facts B; §§4.2.3–4.2.4.

## Procedure and the parties' appeals

The Zug cantonal trial court dismissed the claim on September 20, 2023. On February 23, 2024 the Obergericht partly allowed B. AG's appeal, found covenant violations, and awarded CHF 10,000 plus five-percent interest from November 19, 2021; it dismissed the balance. B. AG's original claim sought interest from January 12, 2021, but the resulting judgment and its federal appellate request used the later date. Facts B–C.

A. brought civil appeal 4A_202/2024 seeking dismissal, alternatively payment of CHF 500 or CHF 1,000, and alternatively remand. B. AG brought civil appeal 4A_212/2024 seeking CHF 100,000 plus interest or remand, and also filed a subsidiary constitutional appeal. Each opposed the other's appeal. The Federal Supreme Court consolidated the two proceedings because they involved the same parties and judgment. The civil appeals were available against this final cantonal civil decision, satisfied the CHF 30,000 amount threshold, and were timely. Because civil review was available, the court did not entertain the subsidiary constitutional appeal. §§1–2; Facts C.

## Issues, rules, and result

The issues were whether the expert letter violated the contractual competition restriction despite its informational purpose, and whether the lower court properly reduced the penalty to CHF 10,000. Contract meaning turns first on the parties' actual shared intent; only absent proved agreement does objective interpretation under the principle of trust determine how the declarations reasonably had to be understood in their full circumstances. Here the parties' shared understanding permitted an expert letter only to the extent indispensable to informing third parties. §§4.1.1, 4.2.2.

Swiss Code of Obligations article 163 allows parties to set a contractual penalty freely, but requires discretionary reduction of an excessive penalty. Article 161(1) permits the obligation to mature even without actual damage. Respect for contractual fidelity and freedom requires restraint: reduction is justified by an amount beyond reasonable law and equity, particularly a gross disproportion between the sum and the creditor's interest in full enforcement at the breach. The court considers the contract's nature and duration, fault and severity of breach, the protected interest, economic circumstances, dependency, and business experience. §§4.1.2, 4.3.3.

The First Civil Law Division's panel consisted of President Jametti and Judges Kiss and Rüedi, with Matt as clerk. It dismissed both civil appeals insofar as admissible and did not entertain the subsidiary constitutional appeal. The CHF 10,000 award with five-percent interest from November 19, 2021 consequently remained in place. The reproduction reports no separate opinions or numerical vote. Caption; §5; dispositive orders 1–6.

## Why the letter breached the covenant

The lower court did not simply find that claiming an operational-business takeover was false. It accepted the statement as not incorrect: B. AG had identified no Construction21 operational activity it was carrying on or intended to carry on when the expert letter was sent, and the transferred processes and infrastructure described substantially the same thing. Subsequent resumption of activity did not change that historical assessment. The jointly signed December customer letter also supported the permissibility of corresponding neutral information. The dispositive distinction was necessity under the parties' exception, rather than bare factual truth. §4.2.2.

The expert letter went further than necessary in announcing an operational-business takeover in two places. It could have explained that organizational changes meant candidates in ongoing placements should contact Arkadium from October 1. A. had not supplied a persuasive reason for changing the customer letter's more limited title and formulation. The broader phrasing suggested Arkadium's distinct position relative to B. AG, and in that context using CONSTRUCTION21 in the letterhead and B. AG under A.'s signature was also unauthorized. The factual accuracy of the underlying transfer did not make those additional uses indispensable. §§4.2.3, 4.3.1.

The leading-headhunter language and the list of Arkadium's specialties were promotional. They followed neutral descriptions of processes and management, drew attention to Arkadium's claimed merits, and reinforced the suggestion that candidates now belonged with Arkadium. Even a single use could exceed what third-party information required. A. did not dispute the promotional character, and neither the claimed truth of the advertisement nor Arkadium's interest in retaining experts in an expensive database supplied contractual permission to use B. AG's names in that advertising. The lower court identified another violation but disregarded it because B. AG had not raised it; the federal court did not enlarge the case with that unasserted ground. §§4.2.4, 4.3.1.

A.'s fault argument also failed. He had not established an absence of fault, and his business experience and deliberate adaptation of the earlier letter supported at least conditional intent. His federal arguments repeatedly defended the transfer's truth and the right to communicate database changes, points the lower court already accepted. They did not undermine the narrower finding that the contested presentation was unnecessary and promotional. §§4.2.5, 4.3.1.

## Why neither side changed the CHF 10,000 penalty

The federal court's factual review was limited. It generally relied on the cantonal findings, including procedural facts, and required a clear, substantiated showing of arbitrariness or another qualifying legal error that could affect the result. A party seeking additional facts had to identify where it had properly introduced them in the cantonal record. Simply reassessing evidence or adding a different factual account did not satisfy that standard. §§3.1–3.2.

The lower court found the breach very minor, occurring on one occasion with recurrence practically excluded. B. AG had neither suffered damage nor demonstrated a risk of damage. It offered a single candidate email as evidence of confusion but failed to explain why that confusion followed from the expert letter. It also failed to substantiate how the advertising affected its business, how many experts were in the database or ongoing placements, or whether other experts stopped using its services. It did not need a numerically proved damages claim, but it did need a substantive response to A.'s assertion of no adverse consequences and some outline of damage or risk. Those findings supported a substantial reduction. §4.2.5.

B. AG's appellate assertions did not show those findings arbitrary. It supplied its own assessment of one email rather than establishing multiple confused candidates. Registered trademarks, a reciprocal restriction, sophisticated contracting parties, and the deal's financial importance did not alone establish why CHF 50,000 or CHF 100,000 was proportionate to this marginal breach. Its claim of an intent to inflict harm was unsupported and late; additional expert-confusion assertions departed from the governing factual record. Nor had it shown that violating the covenant was profitable to A. §§4.3.2–4.3.3.

The court separated forfeiture from reduction. No actual loss was necessary for the penalty obligation to arise, but absence of loss and demonstrated risk remained relevant to whether enforcing the full amount would be excessive. This did not reverse the burden of proof. A. had expressly invoked excessiveness and sought judicial reduction, so the reduction also did not offend the principle that parties determine the relief sought. §§4.2.5, 4.3.3.

A.'s attempt to reduce the sum further relied on the same lack of operational activity and adverse effects that the lower court already weighed. Those considerations did not eliminate the penalty's punitive function. The financially substantial transaction provided context for a meaningful sanction, and CHF 10,000 remained appropriate despite the marginal breach. The court rejected his proposal of CHF 500 for each violation, totaling CHF 1,000. That is a rejected litigant proposal, not a judicial rate or a separate award for each sentence in the letter. §§4.2.5, 4.3.4.

## Exact disposition and source scope

The defendant bore CHF 2,000 in federal court costs on his appeal; B. AG bore CHF 5,000 on its appeal. Their respective party-compensation obligations were CHF 2,500 to B. AG and CHF 6,000 to A.; after offset, B. AG owed A. CHF 3,500. These appellate costs and compensation are distinct from the surviving CHF 10,000 contractual penalty. §5; dispositive orders 4–5.
