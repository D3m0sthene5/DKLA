# University of Tennessee v. Elliott

**478 U.S. 788 (1986) · Supreme Court of the United States · July 7, 1986**

Justice White wrote the Court’s opinion. All eight participating Justices joined Parts I–III, which rejected preclusion of the Title VII claim. Five joined Part IV concerning the Reconstruction civil-rights statutes: Chief Justice Burger and Justices White, Powell, Rehnquist, and O’Connor. Justice Stevens concurred in part and dissented in part, joined by Justices Brennan and Blackmun. Justice Marshall did not participate. The Court affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded. The assigned account appears on CivPro PDF page 694, printed 1380; its three attached editorial notes occupy PDF 695, printed 1380–1382.

## Facts and procedural history

In 1981, the University of Tennessee informed Elliott, a Black employee of its Agricultural Extension Service, that it intended to discharge him for inadequate performance and misconduct. Elliott requested a hearing under Tennessee’s Uniform Administrative Procedures Act. Before that hearing began, he also sued in the Western District of Tennessee, alleging racial motivation and seeking relief under Title VII, §1983, and additional civil-rights provisions. His requested remedies included damages, an injunction against discharge, and classwide relief from alleged discriminatory patterns. The complaint also invoked §§1981, 1985, 1986, and 1988 and the First, Thirteenth, and Fourteenth Amendments. These were pleaded allegations, not Supreme Court findings that discrimination occurred. 478 U.S. at 790–791 & n.1.

The district court initially restrained job action, but later lifted that restraint and permitted the administrative proceeding to continue. An administrative assistant to the University’s Vice President for Agriculture presided as an administrative law judge. The proceeding addressed ten charges against Elliott. Although the ALJ said he lacked jurisdiction to adjudicate the federal civil-rights claims themselves, he permitted Elliott to present racial motivation as an affirmative defense to the proposed discharge. The hearing continued intermittently for more than five months, involved over 100 witnesses and 150 exhibits, and produced more than 5,000 transcript pages. Id. at 791 & n.2.

The ALJ found some charges proved and others unproved, and found that the charges were not racially motivated. He nevertheless considered discharge too severe and ordered Elliott transferred to another assignment under different supervisors. The Vice President affirmed on administrative appeal, including the finding of no racial motivation. Elliott did not seek Tennessee judicial review. Instead, he returned to federal court. The University asserted that the agency’s finding precluded renewed litigation of discrimination, and the district court granted summary judgment. Id. at 791–792.

The Sixth Circuit reversed. It distinguished a state-court judgment reviewing an agency ruling, as in Kremer, from an unreviewed agency finding. It held that the latter did not preclude the Title VII action. For §1983 and the related claims, it held that §1738 did not govern administrative findings and declined to create a federal common-law preclusion rule. The Supreme Court reviewed both conclusions. Id. at 792–794.

## Statutory framework and holdings

Section 1738 requires federal courts to respect the preclusive effect of state-court records and judgments. It does not govern the unreviewed administrative findings here. That conclusion, however, does not end the analysis: the Court has developed federal common-law preclusion rules where no statute directly governs. Because §1738 predates the development of administrative agencies, its silence was not a congressional determination that agency findings must always remain nonpreclusive. Id. at 794–795.

The Court then reached different answers for different statutory claims. Congress did not intend unreviewed state administrative proceedings to preclude a Title VII action. For the Reconstruction civil-rights claims, federal courts must give qualifying state-agency factfinding the same preclusive effect it would receive in the state’s courts. Qualification matters: the agency must have acted in a judicial capacity, decided disputed factual issues properly before it, and afforded the parties an adequate opportunity to litigate. Id. at 795–799.

## Why Title VII receives independent federal consideration

Title VII instructs the EEOC to give substantial weight to final state or local findings in discrimination proceedings. Giving those findings binding, rather than evidentiary, effect in federal court would be difficult to reconcile with that statutory arrangement. Kremer had recognized this distinction even though it held that the state-court judgment actually reviewing the agency ruling was entitled to §1738 treatment. Elliott lacked that intervening judicial judgment. Id. at 792 n.3, 795.

Chandler supplied another important comparison. There, a federal employee could obtain a trial de novo on a Title VII claim after the employing agency rejected discrimination at an administrative hearing. Chandler’s examination of the statute and the 1972 amendments supported equivalent access for private, federal, and state employees. Applying agency preclusion to Elliott would contradict that arrangement. Id. at 795–796.

The University argued against implied repeal and distinguished Chandler because a federal agency’s decision would not receive §1738 full faith and credit. White answered that this argument assumed the very proposition already rejected: §1738 did not govern the unreviewed state-agency ruling either. The issue was whether a common-law bar would fit Title VII’s design, and the Court held that it would not. Elliott’s voluntary request for the agency hearing did not change that result; the statute contemplated independent pursuit of rights under Title VII and other applicable laws. Id. at 796 & n.5.

## Why qualifying factual findings may bind the other claims

Allen and Migra established the ordinary preclusive effect of state-court judgments in §1983 litigation. They were not directly controlling because §1738 did not apply here, but they supported the conclusion that the Reconstruction statutes had not displaced traditional preclusion principles. The majority saw no reason Congress would have prohibited adapting those principles to later administrative adjudication. Id. at 796–797.

Utah Construction provided the model for administrative factfinding conducted with judicial characteristics and a fair litigation opportunity. Preclusion can protect both parties from repeated expense, conserve judicial resources, and promote repose. Giving qualifying state-agency findings their state-law effect also serves federalism by reducing conflicting adjudications between forums. The majority drew policy support from the Full Faith and Credit Clause without claiming that the Clause itself directly binds federal courts. Id. at 797–799 & nn.6–7.

The Court did not hold that this particular hearing necessarily satisfied every prerequisite. Elliott disputed both whether the administrative process met the Utah Construction standard and whether Tennessee courts would themselves treat the findings as preclusive. Neither contention had been resolved below. Both remained for consideration on remand. Id. at 799 n.8. The Court therefore affirmed the Sixth Circuit as to Title VII, reversed its categorical rejection of preclusion for the other civil-rights claims, and remanded. It did not decide whether Elliott had actually suffered unlawful discrimination.

## Stevens’s partial dissent

Stevens agreed that §1738 did not govern and that Title VII remained independently litigable. He disagreed with the judicial creation of a preclusion barrier for the Reconstruction statutes. Their history reflected concern that prejudice and neglect in state institutions could deny federally protected rights; he thought the majority insufficiently considered that history. Id. at 799–800, 802–803.

He also questioned the promised efficiency. Since the Title VII claim would still be tried, the same discrimination issues might be examined in federal court even while being treated as settled for companion claims. His first note developed the risks of inconsistent findings, distorted litigation, and additional proceedings over the fairness of the administrative hearing. He argued that the rule could discourage use of state administrative processes. Finally, he distinguished Utah Construction: its binding factfinding rested on congressional arrangements governing federal contract disputes, not an equivalent congressional choice to bind civil-rights claimants to state administrators. Id. at 800–802 & nn.1–2.

## Assigned account and limits

The textbook says Elliott was discharged; the actual opinion describes a proposed discharge followed by an administrative transfer order. That difference matters to the factual chronology. Its accompanying notes separately consider Astoria’s statutory-intent inquiry, Gjellum’s distinction between administrative claim and issue preclusion, McInnes’s federal definition of a “court” under §1738, and arbitral adjudication. These are editorial extensions and other authorities, not additional holdings of Elliott. PDF 694–695, printed 1380–1382. Elliott’s holding concerns qualifying factual issue preclusion and preserves the remand inquiries; it does not make every administrative decision a state-court judgment or automatically bar every claim arising from the same events.
