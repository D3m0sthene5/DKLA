# Virginia House of Delegates v. Bethune-Hill
139 S. Ct. 1945 (2019) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and procedural posture

Virginia redrew its Senate and House districts in 2011 after the 2010 census. Voters in twelve House districts sued state agencies and election officials, alleging unconstitutional racial gerrymandering under the Equal Protection Clause. The House of Delegates and its Speaker intervened as defendants and took a substantial role in defending the districts through a bench trial, an earlier Supreme Court appeal, and a second bench trial. In June 2018 a three-judge district court, divided two to one, held eleven districts unconstitutional, enjoined further elections under that map, and allowed the General Assembly approximately four months to adopt a lawful replacement. (Court slip 1–2.)

Virginia’s Attorney General determined that continuing the litigation would not serve the Commonwealth or its citizens and declined to appeal. The House nevertheless appealed, and the state defendants moved to dismiss for lack of standing. After the General Assembly failed to enact a replacement within the allowed time, the district court itself prescribed districts for the 2019 election. The House also noticed an appeal from that remedial order. The present opinion decided the standing issue in the appeal from the judgment invalidating the districts and explained that the same standing claim would fare no better in the remedial appeal; it did not adjudicate the substantive racial-gerrymandering merits. (Court 2–3 & n. 1.)

## Holding and judgment

The Supreme Court **dismissed the House’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction**. Justice Ginsburg wrote for a five-Justice majority comprising herself and Justices Thomas, Sotomayor, Kagan and Gorsuch. Justice Alito dissented, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Breyer and Kavanaugh. The majority held that the House lacked authority to represent Virginia in the appeal and lacked standing in its own institutional right. It did not affirm the district court’s constitutional reasoning on the merits. (Court 2–12; dissent 1.)

Standing must exist throughout litigation, including an appeal, and cannot be waived or forfeited. When the primary party declines to appeal, an intervenor seeking appellate review must demonstrate its own standing. The House’s earlier role as an intervening defendant, and as an appellee in the prior appeal, had not itself required the House to invoke the court’s jurisdiction. Its earlier participation therefore did not establish an entitlement to continue the case when the state defendants chose to stop. The House had to identify a concrete, particularized, legally cognizable injury, traceability and redressability, rather than merely allege an unexplained institutional harm. (Court 3–4.)

## Why the House could not represent the Commonwealth

Virginia itself could defend its statute and could designate an agent to do so. But Virginia law assigned civil litigation on behalf of the Commonwealth and its institutions to the Attorney General, subject to exceptions inapplicable here. The Commonwealth could have authorized a legislative chamber to litigate for it, as other states had done; the federal courts could not substitute the House’s preferred arrangement for Virginia’s contrary choice. (Court 4–5 & n. 2.)

The House relied on Vesilind, a Virginia proceeding in which it had intervened to defend legislation, and on Karcher’s recognition of legislative officials’ authority to represent New Jersey. Neither overcame Virginia’s statute. In Vesilind the House had occupied a defensive posture, with no demonstrated state-court determination that it represented the sovereign itself. Karcher did not identify a statute like Virginia’s assignment of exclusive authority to the Attorney General. Moreover, throughout this case the House had claimed to defend its own interests, not to appear as Virginia’s agent. A litigant cannot switch representative capacities for the first time on appeal. The state defendants’ alleged acquiescence could not waive a jurisdictional standing requirement or an objection to a capacity the House had not asserted below. (Court 5–7 & n. 3.)

## Why the asserted institutional injuries were insufficient

Participation in passing a law does not by itself give every governmental component a judicially cognizable injury when a court invalidates the law. Virginia’s constitution vested redistricting authority in the General Assembly, not solely in the House. Arizona State Legislature was different: both chambers had acted together to challenge an alleged permanent transfer of their redistricting authority to an independent commission. Here a single chamber sought to litigate an interest assigned to the whole legislature, and the judgment preserved the General Assembly’s initiating and ongoing redistricting role by first allowing it to produce a valid plan. (Court 7–9 & n. 4.)

Coleman also did not establish standing. The opposing senators there asserted that a disputed tie-breaking vote had nullified votes that otherwise would have defeated ratification. The Virginia House did not challenge whether its votes had been counted or whether the map had been enacted. It challenged a judicial constitutional ruling about a concededly enacted plan. Chadha’s descriptions of both congressional chambers as proper or adverse parties likewise did not conclusively establish standing here; additionally, Chadha involved a veto authority each chamber could exercise independently. (Court 9–10 & n. 5.)

The possibility that new boundaries would change the House’s membership or future legislation did not establish an injury belonging to the institution. The House does not select its own members; the electorate does. Beens, involving an order reducing Minnesota’s Senate from sixty-seven members to thirty-five, was distinguishable even assuming it remained authoritative on appellate standing: such a reduction would change leadership arrangements, committees and voting rules. Changes in electoral boundaries did not establish comparable disruption of the Virginia House’s internal operations, and the House offered insufficient explanation of the allegedly nonobvious harm. The Court recognized an institutional interest in the manner of doing legislative business while rejecting an interest in which legislation future members might enact. (Court 10–11 & nn. 6–7.)

Finally, redistricting did not create the claimed representational confusion. Virginia’s constitution provided that incumbents continued representing the districts that elected them for the remainder of their terms. Greater difficulty or expense in seeking reelection, if legally cognizable at all, would injure particular legislators or candidates, not the House as a body. The Court left that individual-candidate question undecided. (Court 11–12.)

## Alito’s dissent

Alito addressed the House’s standing in its own right and expressly did not reach its claimed authority to represent Virginia. In his view, a districting map determines constituents, communities of interest and the members who produce a legislature’s work. Substituting a judicial map for the legislative map thus concretely injured the House’s institutional function; that injury was particularized, imminent, traceable and redressable. The magnitude of Beens’s injury did not justify denying a smaller but real injury here. Whether the House’s map was constitutional was a merits question, distinct from whether replacing it affected the institution. (Dissent 1–5 & n. 1.)

He also rejected transferring federal separation-of-powers limitations to state legislative institutions. States need not follow the federal allocation of governmental powers. The fact that the whole General Assembly could assert a redistricting interest did not logically foreclose a single chamber from asserting its own concrete injury. The majority’s reliance on judicial cognizability, he thought, obscured an additional limitation beyond the ordinary Lujan injury requirements. These were the four dissenters’ reasons to entertain the appeal, not rulings validating the challenged districts. (Dissent 5–7 & n. 2.)
