# Armstrong v. Pomerance
423 A.2d 174 (Del. 1980) | Civil Procedure

## Court, source, and assigned account

The Delaware Supreme Court decided this interlocutory personal-jurisdiction appeal on October 29, 1980. Justice McNeilly wrote for the three-justice panel, which also included Justices Quillen and Horsey. The reported decision contains eight judicial footnotes and no separate writing. The assigned account is the Armstrong portion of Shaffer Note 1, Civil Procedure PDF page 135, printed page 223. The complete judicial opinion supplies an important qualification omitted from that capsule: the court sustained jurisdiction over three directors but held that six others should have been dismissed because the statute did not yet apply to their appointments. [Complete reported opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/delaware/supreme-court/1980/423-a-2d-174-4.html).

## Corporation, directors, and procedural chronology

Two consolidated shareholder derivative actions asserted that Morrison-Knudsen's directors had breached duties owed to the corporation in transactions repurchasing its stock from Crane Company. A derivative action seeks relief on the corporation's behalf. The jurisdictional decision did not establish that the directors had committed the alleged misconduct. Morrison-Knudsen was incorporated in Delaware but maintained its principal place of business in Boise, Idaho. It did no Delaware business beyond what was necessary to maintain its corporate status. None of the individual defendants lived in Delaware, no board meeting had occurred there, and the complaints alleged no Delaware connection beyond the directors' relationship with this Delaware corporation. 423 A.2d at 174–175.

After Shaffer invalidated Delaware's earlier sequestration procedure, the legislature enacted section 3114. As reproduced in judicial note 1, the statute treated specified acceptance of, or service in, a Delaware corporate directorship as consent to appointing the corporation's registered agent—or the Secretary of State if there was no registered agent—for service in designated corporate or fiduciary-duty actions. The directors entered limited appearances challenging jurisdiction. The Court of Chancery rejected their objection, and the Supreme Court accepted this interlocutory appeal. The question concerned personal jurisdiction and service, not ultimate fiduciary liability. At 175 & nn.1–2.

## The statutory gate came before the constitutional question

Although the defendants raised constitutional objections, the court first asked whether section 3114 authorized service at all. Its two timing provisions mattered. Service as a director after June 30, 1978 could supply consent under one clause. But the summonses here had issued before that date; that clause could not justify the jurisdiction already asserted. The other clause covered acceptance of election or appointment after September 1, 1977. At 175–176.

Armstrong, McMurren, Stuart, Morrison, Scott, and Spencer had been elected to their then-current terms before September 1, 1977. Note 3 indicates that they apparently held multiyear terms. Thus, being directors when the litigation began did not make these six subject to the statute. The court held they should have been dismissed. Lilly, McCabe, and Woodhead, however, had been elected at the annual meeting in early May 1978. Their acceptance fell within the second clause, so service on them was statutorily proper. Only then did the court reach whether exercising jurisdiction over those three was constitutional. At 175–176 & n.3.

## Why accepting the directorship supplied the relevant contact

The remaining directors argued that Shaffer required minimum contacts in every jurisdictional setting and that director status alone could not satisfy that requirement. They emphasized their lack of physical Delaware activity. The court agreed that their contacts were numerically slight, but assessed their quality and connection to this particular litigation. The directorships existed under Delaware corporation law, and the suits challenged conduct undertaken through the powers those laws conferred. Acceptance of the position after the statute's effective date also supplied express statutory notice that a director might be called to defend alleged violations of those responsibilities in Delaware. At 176.

The court connected that voluntary legal relationship to benefits as well as burdens. Note 4 identifies management authority, opportunities for qualifying interest-free unsecured corporate loans, and indemnification rights under Delaware statutes. These were benefits available under the statutory framework; the opinion does not find that each defendant actually received a loan or indemnity payment. Their acceptance of corporate offices invoked Delaware's protections and made the related fiduciary suit a foreseeable consequence of that relationship. At 176 & n.4.

This reasoning was tied to the nature of the action. Note 5 stresses that section 3114 was being used in corporate or fiduciary-duty litigation bound up with Delaware law. If the allegations were true, the directors' acts had foreseeably injured a Delaware corporation, even though the acts occurred elsewhere. The decision therefore does not authorize unlimited Delaware jurisdiction over every unrelated personal dispute involving a corporate director. At 176–177 & n.5.

## Delaware's interest and the distinction from Shaffer

Delaware had a substantial interest in supervising duties created by its corporate law and in furnishing shareholders an effective forum to enforce them. The court reasoned that other states might decline to apply Delaware law, interpret it inconsistently, or impose procedural obstacles to derivative suits. Its extended quotation from Ratner and Schwartz explained additional practical problems: no single alternative state might have jurisdiction over all necessary directors, and security-for-expenses requirements might prevent small shareholders from proceeding elsewhere. These were reasons for Delaware's forum interest, not findings about obstacles actually encountered by these plaintiffs. At 177–179 & nn.6–7.

The directors relied on Shaffer's discussion rejecting jurisdiction based simply on officers' and directors' relationships with a Delaware corporation. The court considered much of that discussion unnecessary to Shaffer's sequestration holding. It also identified a changed legal basis: this suit invoked an explicit director-service statute rather than treating shares as property constructively located in Delaware. Drawing on the North Carolina decision Swenson, it distinguished a director's knowing acceptance of a fiduciary position from jurisdiction predicated on ownership and sequestration of shares. Note 8 reproduces the Delaware legislation's stated purpose and its reliance on other states' director statutes. At 179–180 & n.8.

The court consequently upheld the statute as applied to Lilly, McCabe, and Woodhead. Its reasoning combines the defendants' voluntary corporate relationship, statutory notice, the connection between that relationship and the alleged wrong, and Delaware's regulatory interest; the statute's existence did not eliminate the need to analyze fairness. At 176–180.

## Exact judgment and the assignment's limits

The judgment was affirmed in part and reversed in part. The rejection of the jurisdictional challenge stood for the three directors elected in May 1978. The six directors elected before September 1, 1977 should have been dismissed under the statute's timing limitations. No damages, stock-repurchase misconduct, or final fiduciary-liability determination followed from this interlocutory ruling. At 175–176, 179–180.

The assigned note accurately uses Armstrong as an example of jurisdiction over nonresident directors whose relevant Delaware connection was their directorship. The complete decision adds both the temporal division among defendants and the action-specific limit. The note's later discussion of a 2004 extension to high officers is editorial subsequent history, not part of the 1980 holding or an independent statement of present law. Assigned printed 223.
