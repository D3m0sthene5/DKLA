Citation
Clearfield Trust Co. v. United States, 318 U.S. 363 (1943). Supreme Court of the United States; argued
February 5, 1943; decided March 1, 1943. Justice Douglas wrote the opinion; Justices Murphy and Rutledge
did not participate. Assigned casebook pp. 528–530. Full opinion: Full judicial opinion . The assigned excerpt
supplies the federal-common-law analysis and loss-allocation reasoning. Exact transaction dates, the $24.20
amount, the intervenor’s role, and the distinction between notice and demand supplement its compressed
factual introduction from the full opinion.
Parties
The United States was the plaintiff seeking reimbursement of money paid on a federal check bearing a forged
payee endorsement. Clearfield Trust was the defendant bank that presented the check for collection after
guaranteeing prior endorsements; it became a petitioner after the Third Circuit ruled against it. J.C. Penney had
initially cashed the check and intervened as a defendant. The actual payee, Clair A. Barner, had neither
endorsed the check nor received its proceeds. 318 U.S. at 364–366.
Material Facts
On April 28, 1936, the Government issued a $24.20 check for Barner’s work on a Works Progress
Administration project. It mailed the check to his Pennsylvania address, but he never received it. An unknown
person obtained it, represented himself to a J.C. Penney store as Barner, signed Barner’s name, and exchanged
the check for cash and merchandise. Penney endorsed it to Clearfield, which accepted it for collection,
guaranteed prior endorsements, and collected from the Government through the Federal Reserve Bank of
Philadelphia. Both Penney and Clearfield acted without knowledge or suspicion of the forgery. 318 U.S. at
364–365.
By May 10, Barner had reported nonreceipt to officials on his project, and the information reached other
government agents. He executed an affidavit about the forgery on November 30. Clearfield received notice of
the forgery on January 12, 1937, and received the Government’s reimbursement demand on August 31. Notice
that a check had not reached its payee, formal confirmation of forgery, notice to the bank, and demand for
repayment were different events. Collapsing them into a single discovery-and-demand date would distort the
delay issue. 318 U.S. at 365.
The defendants relied on the Government’s delay. Penney’s employees could no longer remember the
transaction. The stipulated facts stated generally that employees can often locate a customer when a check is
returned shortly after cashing. They did not establish that this particular forger would have been located or that
timely notice would have produced reimbursement. Clearfield apparently retained its claim against Penney.
318 U.S. at 369–370.
Procedural History
The United States sued Clearfield in 1939 on its express guaranty of prior endorsements. Federal jurisdiction
rested on the provision for suits brought by the United States, not on diversity. Penney intervened, and the
district court decided the case on the pleadings and stipulated facts. 318 U.S. at 365–366.
The district court applied Pennsylvania law and dismissed because it considered the Government’s notice
unreasonably delayed. The Third Circuit reversed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari in light of the issue’s

Issue
Are the federal Government’s rights and duties on commercial paper it issues governed by state law under Erie,
or by federal law? If federal law governs and no applicable Act of Congress supplies the answer, what rule
should govern the consequences of delayed notice of a forged payee endorsement? 318 U.S. at 366–369.
Rule and Holding
Federal law governs the United States’ rights and duties on commercial paper issued in the exercise of its
constitutional and statutory functions. In the absence of an applicable congressional rule, federal courts may
fashion the governing rule. State law can sometimes supply the content of a federal rule, but the need for
uniformity in federal commercial-paper transactions justified a nationally uniform rule here. 318 U.S. at
366–367.
Under that rule, the Government’s right to recover money paid on a forged payee endorsement accrued at
payment. Delayed notice could provide a defense if the defendant established resulting damage; damage could
not simply be presumed from delay. Clearfield and Penney had not shown the required causal loss, so the
Government’s claim was not barred. 318 U.S. at 368–370.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the Third Circuit’s reversal of the dismissal. The Government was entitled to
proceed on the federal rule, and the stipulated record did not establish a delay-based defense. The reported
disposition is an affirmance of the appellate judgment, not an independent damages assessment by the Supreme
Court. 318 U.S. at 366, 370.
Reasoning
The Court first identified the source of the Government’s authority and obligation. Paying for work under a
federal relief program was an exercise of federal power. The check’s issuance did not depend on Pennsylvania
law, and the associated governmental rights and duties arose from the same federal sources. Erie’s requirement
to respect state law on state-governed matters therefore did not resolve this federal transaction. 318 U.S. at
366–367.
Identifying federal law did not automatically require rejecting every possible state rule. The Court
acknowledged that federal courts sometimes adopt state law as the governing federal standard. It then asked
whether that choice was appropriate here. Government checks circulate in enormous numbers and commonly
cross state lines between issuance and payment. Different rules depending on each transaction’s location would
create uncertainty about identical federal instruments. A uniform rule was justified by that specific practical
and governmental interest. 318 U.S. at 367.
The Court could consult earlier commercial-law decisions without reviving Swift’s general federal common
law. Those decisions were useful sources of reasoning for formulating a rule in an independently federal area.
The authority to select the rule came from the federal character of the Government’s rights, rather than from a
claim that federal judges possess general superiority in determining commercial law. 318 U.S. at 367–368.
Turning to the governing rule, the Court relied on decisions recognizing that a presenter of a check warrants
entitlement to receive payment. A forged payee endorsement defeats that entitlement. The drawee’s repayment

claim’s existence. The Court also distinguished forgery of the payee’s endorsement from forgery of the
drawer’s signature: the drawee is not charged with knowing every payee’s signature. 318 U.S. at 368–369.
The conclusion that notice was not a prerequisite to suit did not eliminate every defense based on delay.
Clearfield could show that the Government’s failure to give prompt notice after learning of the forgery caused
damage. Government status did not immunize the United States from ordinary commercial responsibilities, and
the scale of its operations did not excuse an established harmful delay. Federal law thus supplied a rule for the
transaction without automatically privileging the Government in every application. 318 U.S. at 369.
The decisive question was proof of resulting harm. A rule presuming harm from delay would shift the loss
merely because the party that paid the forged check waited to notify the presenter. The Court instead placed the
burden on the party seeking to shift the loss to show that the delay caused an actual disadvantage. The initial
loss arose when the forged endorsement was accepted; later delay had to be shown to have made that loss
unrecoverable or otherwise caused damage. 318 U.S. at 369–370.
Clearfield had not made that showing. Its recourse against Penney apparently remained available. Penney’s
generalized statement that prompt notice can help employees find customers did not demonstrate that this
forger could have been found, that recovery would have followed, or that the delay caused a manifest loss. The
possibility of better recollection was insufficient on the stipulated record. The Court therefore rejected the
defense without holding that delay could never matter. 318 U.S. at 370.
Separate Opinions
No separate opinion was filed. Justices Murphy and Rutledge did not participate. Their nonparticipation is
different from dissenting from the rule or judgment. 318 U.S. at 370.
Notes
The opinion separates three questions: whether federal law governs, whether that federal law should borrow
state content, and whether the selected rule permits recovery on the actual record. A brief confined to “federal
common law applies” omits both the uniformity choice and the requirement of proved loss from delayed
notice. 318 U.S. at 366–370.
The holding does not establish general federal commercial law for every dispute involving a check or every
case involving the United States. The Court tied its reasoning to federal commercial paper issued through a
federal function, while acknowledging other settings where state law can appropriately be adopted. It also
expressly left open whether the express endorsement guaranty added anything beyond the warranty implied
upon presentment. 318 U.S. at 366–368 & n.3.
