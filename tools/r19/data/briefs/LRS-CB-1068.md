## Parties and requested relief

Massachusetts, other States, local governments, and private organizations challenged EPA's denial of a petition to regulate greenhouse-gas emissions from new motor vehicles. EPA defended the denial with the support of intervening States and industry associations. The original administrative petition had been filed by nineteen private organizations; the later judicial-review coalition included Massachusetts and other governmental petitioners. The Court needed only one petitioner with standing and based its decision on Massachusetts. 549 U.S. at 505 & nn.2-6, 510, 514, 518.

The petitioners sought lawful consideration of regulation under § 202(a)(1) of the Clean Air Act. They argued that EPA had authority over the specified gases and had unlawfully declined to exercise the statutory judgment that governs emissions standards. Their success in the Supreme Court removed the legal grounds on which EPA had denied the petition. It did not produce court-written standards or a judicial finding that a particular emissions limit was scientifically required. Id. at 505-506, 532-535.

## Statutory framework

Section 202(a)(1), 42 U.S.C. § 7521(a)(1), directs the Administrator to prescribe standards for emissions from new motor vehicles or engines when, in the Administrator's judgment, the relevant emissions cause or contribute to air pollution that may reasonably be anticipated to endanger public health or welfare. The dispute concerned both the breadth of the category subject to regulation and the discretion conveyed by the words referring to the Administrator's judgment. Id. at 506, 528-535.

The Act's definition of air pollutant, § 7602(g), covers any air pollution agent or combination of agents, including physical, chemical, biological, or radioactive substances or matter emitted into or entering ambient air. Its definition of welfare expressly includes effects on weather and climate. Section 7607(b)(1) provides judicial review of the relevant EPA action. These provisions gave the Court the statutory setting for authority, the legally permitted grounds of decision, and the procedural right material to standing. Id. at 506, 516-520, 528-529.

## Material facts and administrative proceedings

On October 20, 1999, nineteen organizations petitioned EPA to regulate carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, and hydrofluorocarbons from new motor vehicles. They relied on scientific assessments of warming and associated harms and on earlier EPA legal opinions accepting authority to regulate carbon dioxide. General Counsel Jonathan Cannon had taken that position in 1998, and his successor Gary Guzy reiterated it in 1999. Those prior legal views mattered to the Court's rejection of EPA's analogy to a different agency's longstanding disclaimer of tobacco jurisdiction. Id. at 510-511, 529-531.

EPA requested public comment in 2001 and received more than 50,000 comments. A National Research Council report, prepared at the White House's request, described accumulating greenhouse gases, rising temperatures, and uncertainties in predicting the magnitude and effects of future change. Both the agency and the litigants relied on aspects of that report. The dispute over scientific uncertainty concerned what decision EPA could lawfully make on the administrative record. Id. at 511-512, 533-535, 553-556.

EPA denied the petition on September 8, 2003, giving two independent grounds. First, it concluded that the Clean Air Act did not authorize mandatory regulation of greenhouse gases to address global climate change. It relied on the history of climate legislation, Congress's failure to enact a proposed emissions amendment, the economic and political significance of the issue, the Act's traditional application to local air pollution, and the Department of Transportation's fuel-economy responsibilities. Id. at 512-514.

Second, EPA stated that regulation would be unwise even if it possessed authority. It invoked scientific uncertainty, a preference for a coordinated rather than piecemeal response, voluntary programs and technological innovation, and the possibility that unilateral domestic regulation would impair efforts to obtain reductions from developing countries. This alternative ground required independent analysis: establishing statutory authority alone would not dispose of the case if EPA had lawfully declined to use it. Id. at 513-514, 532-535.

For standing, Massachusetts submitted evidence of its ownership of coastal land and the effects of rising sea levels. The majority relied on affidavits describing twentieth-century sea-level increases, existing coastal loss, and a greater future threat from inundation and storm surges. The dissent challenged whether the submissions adequately linked a concrete loss of Massachusetts land to the particular regulatory omission and to relief the Court could provide. Neither side's characterization should be substituted for the existence of that evidentiary disagreement. Id. at 521-526, 541-549.

## Procedural history

The D.C. Circuit denied the petition for review in a decision accompanied by three separate opinions. Judge Randolph assumed statutory authority without deciding it and concluded that EPA could consider the policy concerns it identified. He also avoided definitively resolving standing, reasoning that the standing and merits questions overlapped. Judge Sentelle considered the alleged injury insufficient for standing but accepted the contrary standing view as law of the case and joined the judgment on the merits as the disposition closest to his own position. Id. at 514-515.

Judge Tatel dissented. He considered Massachusetts' affidavits sufficient on injury, causation, and redressability, concluded that the statutory text authorized regulation, and rejected the policy grounds for refusing the statutory inquiry. The Supreme Court granted certiorari, addressed Article III before deciding the statutory questions, reversed the D.C. Circuit, and remanded for further proceedings. The three D.C. Circuit opinions are distinct from the two Supreme Court dissents. Id. at 515-516, 534-535.

## Questions presented

Did Massachusetts demonstrate a concrete injury fairly traceable to EPA's refusal and sufficiently redressable through judicial review, taking account of its state interests and statutory procedural right? Did § 202(a)(1), read with the definition of air pollutant, authorize regulation of greenhouse gases from new motor vehicles? Could EPA decline to make the endangerment judgment for the policy reasons given in its denial? A related threshold issue was whether denial of a rulemaking petition could be reviewed despite the usual presumption against reviewing individual nonenforcement decisions. Id. at 516-535.

## Rules and holdings

Massachusetts had Article III standing. Its ownership of threatened coastal property supplied a concrete interest. Its quasi-sovereign interests and congressionally recognized procedural right warranted special solicitude in the standing analysis. The contribution of domestic vehicle emissions to a global problem and the prospect of incremental risk reduction were sufficient; complete elimination of the harm was unnecessary. The Court did not confer standing on every concerned person or hold that a procedural entitlement dispenses with a concrete interest. Id. at 516-526.

The Clean Air Act's broad text gave EPA authority over the greenhouse gases at issue. The agency could not defeat that authority by relying on later legislative inaction, the importance of the subject, or overlapping fuel-economy regulation. Denial of the rulemaking petition was reviewable under a highly deferential standard. Id. at 527-532.

EPA's reasons for declining the statutory judgment had to relate to whether the relevant emissions cause or contribute to air pollution reasonably anticipated to endanger health or welfare. The agency could determine that the requisite endangerment was absent or explain why the information did not permit a reasoned judgment. The policy preferences it actually offered did not substitute for that statutory assessment. EPA retained discretion concerning a reasoned scientific judgment and the manner and timing of regulation within the Act's limits. Id. at 532-535.

## Actual judgment

The Court reversed the D.C. Circuit and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. It did not itself find endangerment or order immediate adoption of emissions standards. It also left open whether policy concerns could inform EPA's actions after an endangerment finding. EPA had to address the petition using the statutory criteria and could no longer rely on the rejected no-authority rationale or the unrelated policy reasons as sufficient grounds for its denial. Id. at 534-535.

## Majority reasoning

### A procedural right mattered because Massachusetts had a concrete interest to protect

Congress had authorized judicial review of the rejection of a rulemaking petition. A litigant asserting a procedural right protecting its concrete interests need not satisfy every ordinary requirement concerning the immediacy of injury and certainty of redress. There can be standing when lawful reconsideration has some possibility of reducing the injury, even though the agency retains discretion on the ultimate substantive outcome. The Court nevertheless insisted on a personal, concrete stake and expressly rejected suits based only on an abstract interest in correct administration. Id. at 516-518.

Only one petitioner needed standing, so the majority concentrated on Massachusetts rather than deciding the status of every organization and municipality. This choice limits the reach of the standing discussion. The statutory avenue of review did not independently establish that every member of the coalition could sue. Id. at 518.

### State status and the federal allocation of powers supported special solicitude

The majority invoked Georgia v. Tennessee Copper to explain a State's independent interest in its territory and environmental conditions. Massachusetts also owned substantial coastal property. On joining the Union, States surrendered powers to respond through war, international treaties, and some forms of regulation. Federal authority occupied that space, and Congress had directed EPA to protect health and welfare through the statutory program. The majority connected those quasi-sovereign interests with the procedural right to challenge EPA's denial. Id. at 518-520.

The opinion did not simply treat Massachusetts as the representative of all citizens dissatisfied with a federal policy. In responding to Massachusetts v. Mellon, it distinguished a suit to protect citizens from the operation of federal statutes from this effort to vindicate rights under an existing federal statute. Whether that distinction and the State's interests justified a different standing approach was a principal disagreement with Roberts. Id. at 520–521 n.17, 538–540.

### The injury was particularized despite being part of a widely shared problem

The majority relied on the State's ownership and loss of coastal land. The fact that climate-related risks affected many people did not make this alleged proprietary injury abstract. The affidavits described rising seas already affecting coastal property and further loss if the predicted trend continued. The Court treated those submissions as sufficiently concrete for the posture before it; it did not demand proof of the precise boundaries of every parcel that would be lost. Id. at 521-523 & n.21.

This reasoning separates two propositions often confused in describing the case. A concern shared with the public can still involve a concrete injury to a particular plaintiff. A plaintiff's strong concern, standing alone, does not establish that injury. The majority located the required personal stake in Massachusetts' affected territory and property. Id. at 522-523.

### Contribution and incremental mitigation could establish causation and redressability

EPA did not dispute the causal connection between human greenhouse-gas emissions and warming, but argued that the particular emissions subject to this petition made too small a contribution to establish standing. The majority rejected the premise that a regulatory omission cannot be challenged merely because addressing it would be an incremental response. Agencies ordinarily address large problems through partial measures. Id. at 523-525.

The record described the U.S. transportation sector as a substantial contributor, with approximately 1.7 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide emissions in 1999, more than six percent of worldwide carbon-dioxide emissions. Those figures supported the majority's rejection of the claim that the domestic contribution was insignificant. The dissent emphasized that § 202(a)(1)'s new-vehicle category and the reductions obtainable through particular standards would be smaller than the entire transportation-sector figure. Id. at 524-525, 543-545.

Redressability did not require EPA to reverse global warming by itself. A reduction that slowed or reduced the risk could count. Anticipated increases in foreign emissions did not eliminate the possible benefit from domestic reductions. The majority also observed that EPA's own support for voluntary reductions assumed some benefit from lowering emissions. It thus found a sufficient prospect that lawful reconsideration would lead to steps reducing Massachusetts' risk. Id. at 525-526.

### A rulemaking denial was distinguishable from routine nonenforcement

Heckler v. Chaney addressed refusals to institute enforcement. Here the petition sought rulemaking, and Congress had provided a review procedure. Rulemaking denials occur less frequently, are subject to formal procedures, and entail a public explanation that a court can evaluate for statutory consistency. Review remained highly deferential and narrow, but it was available. The Court therefore did not treat the agency's choice to do nothing as automatically falling within Chaney. Id. at 527-528.

### The statutory definition supplied authority without a climate-specific amendment

The majority read the definition of air pollutant as broad enough to encompass the four greenhouse gases. Carbon dioxide and the other gases were chemical or physical substances emitted into ambient air, and their role in producing the relevant pollution brought them within the text. The statute's breadth allowed it to reach developments not specifically anticipated when Congress enacted it. Breadth did not itself create an ambiguity delegating the authority question back to EPA. Id. at 528-529 & n.26, 532.

Later legislative attention to climate research did not withdraw previously conferred regulatory authority. Nor did Congress's failure to enact a proposed amendment prove that existing general language lacked the asserted coverage. The majority required a statutory basis for the claimed restriction rather than an inference from the absence of a later, more specific mandate. Id. at 529-530.

### Brown & Williamson and fuel-economy regulation did not compel a contrary reading

FDA v. Brown & Williamson involved a statutory scheme that, on the agency's asserted interpretation, would have required a tobacco ban despite a distinctive history of congressional protection of the tobacco market and agency disclaimers of jurisdiction. EPA regulation under § 202(a)(1) did not require a comparable ban. The Clean Air Act accommodated considerations such as technological development and time, and EPA's prior legal opinions had accepted greenhouse-gas authority. The majority therefore rejected the asserted analogy. Id. at 530-531.

The Department of Transportation's responsibility for fuel-economy standards addressed energy efficiency; EPA's statutory responsibility concerned emissions affecting health and welfare. Improving fuel economy might be one way to reduce emissions, but overlapping responsibilities did not make the two mandates legally incompatible. EPA had to carry out its own statute rather than infer that another agency's related authority displaced it. Id. at 531-532.

### The Administrator's judgment had to answer the question Congress assigned

The word judgment allowed EPA to evaluate whether the scientific and statutory criteria were satisfied. It did not authorize EPA to replace them with a preferred presidential policy, diplomatic strategy, or general preference for a different sequence of regulation. Once the agency responded to the petition, it had to give a reasoned explanation tied to the statute for making or declining to make the relevant determination. Id. at 532-533.

The majority did not deny that uncertainty can justify deferring a judgment. If uncertainty were sufficiently profound to prevent a reasoned determination about endangerment, EPA could explain that conclusion. But it considered EPA's actual invocation of residual uncertainty and other policy concerns inadequate to answer the statutory question. Because the agency had not supplied the required explanation, the proper course was remand rather than a judicial substitution of the scientific finding or the resulting standards. Id. at 533-535.

## Separate opinions

### Roberts, joined by Scalia, Thomas, and Alito: Article III was not satisfied

Roberts would have vacated the D.C. Circuit's judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss the petition for review for lack of jurisdiction. He did not merely propose affirmance of EPA on the merits. His disagreement concerned the judicial power to hear this suit, which had to be established regardless of the seriousness of the underlying environmental issue. Id. at 535-536, 549.

He rejected the majority's special solicitude. Tennessee Copper concerned the substantive and remedial significance of state interests, rather than relaxation of Article III's standing requirements. Massachusetts' proprietary interest in coastal land resembled an ordinary landowner's interest; reliance on a quasi-sovereign interest raised additional difficulties, including the restriction on a State suing the federal government as representative of its citizens. In his account, a general statutory review provision did not cure the constitutional deficiencies. Id. at 536-540.

Roberts also challenged the proof of existing or imminent loss. He considered the claims concerning already submerged state property too conclusory, and the future projections too dependent on long time horizons and uncertain assumptions to establish the necessary particular injury. He contrasted the projected sea-level change with the limitations of elevation measurements used to identify threatened land. These criticisms targeted the sufficiency of the submitted evidence for standing; they should not be recast as a holding that environmental harm can never be concrete. Id. at 541-543.

On causation and redressability, he traced the series of links between EPA's refusal, emissions from new U.S. vehicles, atmospheric concentrations, global temperature, sea level, and Massachusetts land loss. He stressed the difference between all transportation emissions and the narrower reductions EPA could require. The effect of foreign emissions, natural processes, and future conduct made the alleged benefit of the requested relief too speculative in his view. The majority's acceptance of a small contribution and possible incremental relief did not satisfy the ordinary burden he would apply. Id. at 543-547.

Roberts concluded that a federal court's jurisdiction should not expand because a policy problem is important or because political actors have not supplied the relief sought. He treated the standing requirement as part of the constitutional allocation of responsibility. The majority, by contrast, considered this a conventional statutory dispute brought by a State with a concrete stake and an authorized review right. Id. at 547-549; id. at 516-520.

### Scalia, joined by Roberts, Thomas, and Alito: additional statutory grounds favored EPA

Scalia joined Roberts on standing, but addressed the merits on the assumption that jurisdiction existed. He read § 202(a)(1)'s reference to the Administrator's judgment as making an endangerment determination a condition for mandatory regulation without imposing an unconditional duty to make that determination whenever a petition was filed. The agency could reasonably decide when to undertake that judgment and consider the policy and administrative factors surrounding it. Id. at 549-553.

Even under the majority's narrower account of permissible reasons, Scalia considered EPA's discussion of scientific uncertainty adequate. He reproduced the agency's account of unresolved questions concerning emissions, atmospheric responses, feedback effects, and health and welfare impacts. His objection was that EPA had already supplied the kind of explanation the majority purported to require; the disagreement was about its sufficiency, not the total absence of an agency explanation. Id. at 553-556.

Scalia independently disputed the definition of air pollutant. He distinguished the opening category, an air pollution agent, from the examples following including. A substance's satisfaction of the broad description of physical or chemical matter did not necessarily establish that it fell within the opening category. His illustrative point was that a phrase referring to American automobiles, including trucks and minivans, need not include foreign trucks merely because they are trucks. Id. at 556-558.

Because the Act did not separately define air pollution, Scalia would have deferred under the then-operative Chevron framework to EPA's interpretation distinguishing the asserted global greenhouse effect from the forms of ambient-air pollution ordinarily regulated under the Act. The majority rejected that restriction as inconsistent with the statutory breadth. Scalia's argument supplies a distinct textual and deference-based ground, in addition to his discretionary-timing and scientific-explanation arguments. Id. at 558-560; id. at 528-529 & n.26.

## Scope and casebook relationship

The two assigned excerpts place the same decision in administrative review and standing. Reading them together shows why a successful answer on statutory authority was insufficient without Article III jurisdiction, and why standing alone did not establish the legal error in EPA's denial. The full opinion supplies the detailed agency history, the three D.C. Circuit positions, the entire majority analysis, and both Supreme Court dissents. Principal assigned excerpts 1068–1077,1230–1238; available follow-up notes 1077–1079,1239–1243; 549 U.S. at 504–560.

The decision separates authority to regulate, the criteria governing an endangerment judgment, and the consequences of such a judgment. It also separates a sufficient prospect of redress for standing from an order guaranteeing the petitioners' preferred substantive result. This brief describes the 2007 decision. Later endangerment findings, regulatory developments, and changes in deference doctrine are separate matters and are not incorporated as holdings of this case. Id. at 518, 528-535.

## Complete opinion and footnote clarifications

The Court’s numbered majority notes run 1–29. Notes 1–6 identify the parties and groups; note 15 lists the nineteen administrative petitioners, which are not identical to the later judicial-review coalition. Note 7 traces the change from actual endangerment to reasonably anticipated endangerment. Notes 8–14 supply historical scientific and legislative background. These dates and scientific figures describe the record considered in 2007. They are not updated measurements or independent scientific findings in this brief. Id. at 505–510.

Notes 17 and 21, including their continuations, explain the majority’s answers to the standing dissent: asserting statutory rights differs from protecting citizens against the operation of federal statutes, and precise metes and bounds were not required to show existing and continuing land loss in this posture. Notes 18–23 supply hurricane, state-property, projected-loss, emissions and risk-reduction evidence. Note 24 continues onto 527 and defends SCRAP’s distinction between widely shared injury and an abstract public interest; it does not eliminate the need for injury. Notes 25–29 address statutory breadth, the air-pollution-agent objection, subsequent legislative history, research statutes and the ozone argument. Id. at 520–530.

The majority’s foreign-affairs discussion recognizes presidential authority but says it does not excuse refusal to execute domestic law. It identifies the State Department, rather than EPA, as Congress’s designated agency for the relevant foreign-policy role and notes that EPA had not shown consultation with it. This supports the rejection of EPA’s offered reasons for denying this petition; the Court expressly reserves how policy concerns may inform action after an endangerment finding. Id. at 534–535.

Roberts’s two notes distinguish an additional parens patriae interest from Article III’s minimum requirements and criticize SCRAP’s attenuated causal chain and imminence. His dissent disputes the proof connecting Massachusetts’s specific land loss to these particular new-vehicle standards, rather than adjudicating the existence or causes of global warming on the merits. Scalia’s two notes separately distinguish deferring the decision to exercise judgment from making the eventual regulatory decision, and illustrate the breadth problem he sees in the majority’s definition. His timing, scientific-explanation and definition/deference arguments are distinct alternative merits grounds. Id. at 540 n.1,548 n.2,551 n.1,558 n.2.
