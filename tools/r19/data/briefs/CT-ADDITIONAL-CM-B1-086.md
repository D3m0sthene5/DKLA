# Commerce & Industry Insurance Co. v. Bayer Corp.
433 Mass. 388, 742 N.E.2d 567 (2001) | Contracts

## Court and procedural posture
The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court decided the case on February 21, 2001, following argument on January 8. Justice Greaney wrote for a court comprising Greaney, Ireland, Spina, Cowin, and Sosman; the report contains no separate writing or disagreement. The civil action began on December 3, 1998. Superior Court Judge S. Jane Haggerty denied Bayer’s motion to compel arbitration and stay litigation, and the Supreme Judicial Court granted direct appellate review. The denial was immediately appealable under G.L. c. 251, § 18(a)(1). This appeal determined the dispute-resolution obligation, without adjudicating responsibility for the factory fire. (433 Mass. at 388–389 & n.2.)

## Facts and competing forms
Malden Mills manufactured textiles. An explosion and fire destroyed several buildings at its facility on December 11, 1995. Malden Mills and its property insurers, Commerce & Industry and Federal Insurance, sued Bayer and numerous other defendants. They alleged that static electrical discharge ignited nylon tow sold by Bayer, manufactured by a French entity, and used in producing flocked upholstery fabric. Their claims against Bayer alleged negligence and breaches of implied warranties of merchantability and fitness. The ignition mechanism and Bayer’s liability were allegations, rather than findings resolved on this appeal. (Id. at 389 & n.3.)

Malden Mills ordered nylon either by sending a purchase order or by telephoning and then sending its standard order. The reverse side required arbitration in New York or Boston, and under either AAA or General Arbitration Council of the Textile Industry rules, with those choices belonging to Malden Mills. Another provision declared the order the entire agreement regardless of the seller’s forms and permitted modification only in a writing signed by an authorized buyer representative. (Id. at 389–390.)

Bayer forwarded orders to the manufacturer, usually directing shipment straight to Malden Mills, and thereafter sent invoices. Capitalized language on the invoice made acceptance subject to a mutually executed contract or, absent one, Bayer’s prices and its own printed conditions. Paragraph fourteen disclaimed an acceptance or confirmation under § 2-207 and expressly conditioned acceptance on the buyer’s assent to additional or conflicting invoice terms. Those terms also disclaimed implied warranties and consequential-damages liability that the buyer’s orders provided. Bayer’s invoice said nothing about arbitration or any other method of resolving disputes. Malden Mills ordinarily paid within thirty days. (Id. at 390, 392–393 & n.8.)

After the fire, Bayer sought enforcement of the arbitration language that Malden Mills itself had printed. Malden Mills refused. None of the other defendants moved to compel arbitration. The trial judge held that conduct formed the sales contract, but the parties’ writings did not agree on arbitration; equitable estoppel did not supply the missing obligation. (Id. at 390–391 & n.4.)

## Issue, governing rules, and holding
When expressly conditional and inconsistent forms fail to form a written contract, does an arbitration provision appearing only in the buyer’s form become part of the contract subsequently established by delivery, acceptance, and payment? The court held that it does not on this record. G.L. c. 106, § 2-207(3) exclusively determines the terms of a contract formed by conduct: the terms on which the writings agree, together with supplementary terms supplied through other applicable Code provisions. Subsection (2)’s test for additional or different terms cannot be imported to preserve a term in a contract that the writings never formed. (Id. at 393–396.)

The opinion distinguishes three routes. Divergent forms can establish a contract under subsection (1) when the seller’s acceptance is not expressly conditional. An expressly conditional invoice instead requires affirmative acceptance of the counteroffer. If the forms do not establish a contract but shipment and acceptance recognize one, subsection (3) supplies the contract and its terms. Bayer conceded the third route governed: its conditional invoice was never affirmatively accepted, and Malden Mills’s purchase order also expressly restricted acceptance to its own terms. The court applies the Massachusetts text then in force, whose subsection (2) covered additional or different terms, unlike the uniform version’s reference to additional terms alone. (Id. at 391 n.5, 392–393.)

## Reasoning and rejected arguments
Bayer urged that arbitration caused no surprise or hardship and was not a material alteration. It relied on JOM’s language suggesting that subsection (3) implicitly incorporated subsection (2), and on official comment 6. The court rejected that approach because the route to formation determines the available term-selection rule. Subsection (2) governs terms in contracts formed through writings or confirmations of oral agreements; it cannot itself serve as another Code provision supplying terms where its conditions do not apply. Official comment 7 directly addresses contracts formed by conduct and points to subsection (3). Comment 6’s discussion of conflicting confirmation clauses did not establish agreement where one form contained arbitration and the other was simply silent about dispute resolution. (Id. at 394–396 & n.9.)

Subsection (3) can incorporate terms established by course of dealing, performance, or trade usage, but Bayer showed none supporting arbitration here. It conceded it had never previously arbitrated a dispute with Malden Mills. Its citation to Pervel involved a demonstrated custom of sending arbitration confirmations that buyers signed or retained without objection. Bayer offered no evidence of relevant industry custom. Its own failure specifically to object to the arbitration clause could not overcome the conditional invoices and the absence of an agreed arbitration term. (Id. at 395–397.)

Federal and state policies favoring arbitration presuppose an agreement to arbitrate. Interstate commerce brought the Federal Arbitration Act into consideration, but the parties’ forms did not establish a written contract containing arbitration, the conduct contract did not incorporate it, and no other agreement supplied it. Equitable estoppel likewise failed: Bayer did not establish an intention to remain bound to the buyer’s arbitration language absent a final written contract, or inequity from proceeding in court. Although arbitration can ordinarily be more efficient, that advantage was not established given this litigation’s multiple defendants and claims. (Id. at 397–398 & nn.10–11.)

## Disposition and assigned significance
The court affirmed the order denying Bayer’s motion to compel arbitration and stay litigation. It did not award fire-loss damages or dismiss the underlying claims. The buyer’s apparent reversal of position did not entitle the seller to revive a term when its own boilerplate helped prevent written agreement. (Id. at 398.)
