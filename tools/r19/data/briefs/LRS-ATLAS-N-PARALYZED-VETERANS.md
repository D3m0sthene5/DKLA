# Paralyzed Veterans of America v. D.C. Arena L.P.
117 F.3d 579 (D.C. Cir. 1997) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and assigned setting

The D.C. Circuit affirmed an order requiring most wheelchair locations in the proposed MCI Center to provide sightlines over standing spectators. Judge Silberman wrote for a panel with Chief Judge Edwards and Judge Sentelle; there was no separate writing. The Department of Justice’s technical-manual guidance was a permissible interpretation of its accessibility regulation, rather than an additional legislative rule requiring notice and comment. The panel also upheld the district judge’s remedial allowance for substantial compliance and rejected the plaintiffs’ demand that every wheelchair location have such a view. (580, 587–589.)

## Arena plans, statutory framework, and agency chronology

The owners were constructing a downtown Washington arena for basketball, hockey, concerts, and other events. Spectators could be expected to stand during especially exciting moments, potentially obstructing seated wheelchair users’ views. The ADA prohibited disability discrimination in public accommodations and required covered new facilities to be readily accessible and usable. Congress charged DOJ with regulations and standards, and DOJ’s Standard 4.33.3 required wheelchair areas integrated into the seating plan, choices of admission prices, comparable sightlines, companion seats, and multiple wheelchair locations in facilities exceeding 300 seats. (580–581.)

The Access Board had drafted the underlying guideline. Its January 1991 proposal recognized that general comparable-sightline language might not solve the standing-spectator problem and invited comments. DOJ proposed adopting the Board’s standards and directed comments to the Board. In July, the Board adopted the general language while saying that standing-spectator sightlines would be addressed in later recreational-facility guidelines. DOJ issued identical regulatory language that day, but did not adopt the Board’s explanation that the particular problem remained unresolved. This difference between the drafting bodies became central to the claimed change of interpretation. (581, 586–587.)

A midlevel DOJ official said in a 1992 speech to stadium operators that views over standing spectators were not required. By mid-1993, DOJ took the contrary position in its investigation of Olympic venues. In December 1994 it published a technical-manual supplement, without notice and comment, expressly interpreting comparable sightlines to require views over spectators expected to stand. A month later, the arena owners selected four seating configurations—one each for basketball and hockey and two for concerts. The plans supplied wheelchair seating exceeding one percent of total seats and various prices and locations, but only some wheelchair locations had standing-spectator views. The owners admittedly knew DOJ’s interpretation when making those choices. (581–582 & n.1.)

## District-court proceedings and parties’ arguments

Paralyzed Veterans and individual wheelchair users brought an ADA private action challenging integration, dispersal, and sightlines. DOJ declined repeated invitations to intervene, participated only as an amicus, and was not permitted to file a second amicus brief. The district court found integration adequate but dispersal inadequate. Only sightlines were appealed. It accepted DOJ’s manual interpretation but found substantial compliance sufficient, given DOJ’s uneven enforcement positions, practical competing requirements, and the owners’ good faith. Its approved revised plans provided standing-spectator views at 78 percent of wheelchair seats in one configuration and 85–88 percent in the other three. The later part of the appellate opinion summarizes the range as 75–88 percent; the brief follows the more specific configuration figures in the facts section. (582, 588.)

The owners argued that the regulatory language could not require views over standing spectators; alternatively, the court should interpret the ambiguous rule without deference; alternatively, DOJ had reversed an earlier authoritative interpretation or added a substantive requirement without notice and comment. They did not argue that DOJ’s construction conflicted with the ADA itself. The plaintiffs cross-appealed, arguing that the court should have deferred to DOJ’s asserted demand for universal compliance. (582–583, 588–589.)

## Text, ambiguity, and historical deference reasoning

The panel rejected the owners’ argument that comparable sightlines merely concerned distribution of wheelchair locations. Separate regulatory language already governed dispersal and prices, making an unobstructed view a natural additional meaning. Earlier private accessibility guidance had not established a settled narrower understanding that excluded temporary obstructions. The phrase nonetheless remained ambiguous about whether standing spectators, as opposed to permanent structural obstacles, were covered. DOJ’s reading fit the words. (583.)

Under the deference precedents applied in 1997, the court ordinarily sustained an agency’s interpretation of its regulation unless plainly erroneous or inconsistent with it. The panel recognized the concern that deference could encourage vague regulations, but concluded that APA procedures imposed a limit: an agency could not publish contentless generalities and supply the real law only through later informal interpretations. Standard 4.33.3 was sufficiently substantive, and its language reasonably alerted readers to the possibility of standing-spectator protection. Footnote 2 discussed the interests of regulatory beneficiaries as well as regulated parties; footnote 3 distinguished APA procedural constraints from constitutional nondelegation doctrine. (584–585 & nn.2–4.)

The Access Board’s role in drafting did not defeat deference to DOJ. The panel grounded deference primarily in the agency’s statutory responsibility for the regulation, rather than insider knowledge of its wording. Once DOJ promulgated the language as its own rule, DOJ administered it. Although DOJ lacked administrative adjudicatory authority and enforcement occurred in federal court, Congress had delegated rulemaking authority and specifically required technical-assistance manuals. That continuing administrative role distinguished a merely prosecuting agency and the multiple-agency situations cited by the owners. The private character of the lawsuit did not change this analysis. (585 & n.5.)

## Claimed reversal and interpretive-rule classification

The panel stated that an agency could not fundamentally revise a definitive interpretation of a substantive regulation without notice and comment. It distinguished the then-existing latitude to change statutory interpretations under Chevron from the APA’s requirements for amendments of agency rules. But that proposition did not invalidate the manual here because the owners failed to establish a prior authoritative DOJ interpretation to the contrary. The Board’s statement about future guidelines was not necessarily DOJ’s view, and DOJ could exceed the Board’s minimum standards. The official’s speech was not an authoritative departmental judgment; footnote 6 likewise discounted later testimony about earlier departmental intentions. The owners also could not establish detrimental reliance when they had notice of the manual before selecting the plans. (586–587 & n.6.)

Separately, the court asked whether the manual itself added a legislative rule. Agency labels and stated intentions were insufficient. The relevant inquiry examined whether the guidance carried independent force of law or instead explained a duty fairly encompassed by the existing regulation. Real practical consequences did not alone make an interpretation legislative. The closer the guidance was to the actual regulatory language, the more plausible its interpretive status; conversely, guidance supplying the real content of a highly general norm was more likely substantive. Here comparable sightlines already provided an arguable enforcement basis, and the manual’s interpretation was not sufficiently distinct or additive to require notice and comment. This classification analysis was distinct from the subsequently rejected interpretation-change doctrine. (587–588.)

## Cross-appeal and equitable remedy

The plaintiffs’ demand for one hundred percent compliance rested on an asserted DOJ position that the manual did not state. Other DOJ materials and the Olympic settlement referred to all or substantially all wheelchair locations. Footnote 7 explained the practical tension between sightlines and integration: placing wheelchair locations higher or at section boundaries might improve views while altering integration. (588–589 & n.7.)

The plaintiffs argued that a rejected second DOJ brief would have clarified that exceptions were allowed only for structural impracticality, technical infeasibility, or comparably obstructed views for standing users. The panel questioned whether a further litigation-only refinement deserved deference, but independently upheld the district court’s discretion to reject that second amicus submission. The government had declined intervention despite repeated invitations. Unlike direct review of agency action, this was a private equitable action in which the district judge had discretion to fashion relief within the regulation and manual’s latitude. The appellate amicus submission could not improve the plaintiffs’ posture. If DOJ wanted more uniform treatment, it had tools to clarify its manual. (589.)

## Result, later treatment, and source scope

The district-court judgment was affirmed on both appeal and cross-appeal. The owners lost their claim that no standing-spectator sightlines were required, and the plaintiffs lost their demand for universal coverage. The decision upheld this equitable order; it did not finally establish every ADA requirement for every arena. In 2015 Perez rejected the proposition that a significant change to a genuinely interpretive rule automatically required notice and comment, while reserving the general line between interpretive and legislative rules. That later holding limits how the 1997 reasoning should be used. (589; Perez, 575 U.S. at 96–97, 100–107.)
