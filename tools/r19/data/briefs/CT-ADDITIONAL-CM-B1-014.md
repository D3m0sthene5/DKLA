# Jennings v. Radio Station KSCS, 96.3 FM, Inc.
708 S.W.2d 60 (Tex. App.—Fort Worth 1986) | Contracts

## Court and procedural posture
Retired Justice Ashworth, sitting by assignment, delivered the Texas Court of Appeals' May 8, 1986 opinion. Steve Jennings, a prisoner proceeding without counsel, appealed a summary judgment rejecting his oral-contract claim against KSCS and its individual employees. The court reversed and remanded because the ground asserted in the motion did not establish that his pleaded facts were legally insufficient. The complete original reporter identifies Justices Burdock, Hughes, and assigned retired Justice Ashworth as the panel. It contains no judicial notes or separate writing; no numerical vote is supplied. (708 S.W.2d at 61–62.)

## Alleged promise and performance
Jennings alleged that radio was his only contact with the outside world and that he regularly listened to KSCS. The station repeatedly announced a guarantee of at least three songs consecutively, with a $25,000 payment if it failed. His petition complained that the station's five-song sequences consisted of three songs, a short commercial, and two songs; it also alleged that on specific occasions he contacted the station after fewer than three consecutive songs, but it refused payment. He alleged that he then stopped listening. These were allegations whose legal sufficiency was at issue, rather than findings that a qualifying commercial interruption occurred or that he had won a prize. (Id. at 61.)

The pleadings were imperfect: a sequence of three songs followed by a commercial and two more does not itself show failure to play at least three consecutively. The opinion nevertheless identifies a further allegation that Jennings contacted the station after particular failures and analyzes whether the pleaded listening induced by the promise could constitute consideration. It does not adjudicate the precise contest rules or resolve proof of a breach. (Id. at 61–62.)

## Proceedings and limited appellate review
The defendants filed special exceptions asserting failure to state a cause of action. Jennings filed a rebuttal but declined further amendment. The defendants then obtained summary judgment, and Jennings submitted no written response to the motion. His two appellate points generally attacked the judgment. Under the court's application of Texas Rule of Civil Procedure 166-A(c), however, absence of a response limited the appellate inquiry to whether the grounds expressly presented in the defendants' written motion could support judgment as a matter of law. His failure to respond did not convert an insufficient legal ground into an entitlement to judgment. (Id. at 61.)

The motion advanced one ground: even if true, the petition's facts disclosed no legally recognized duty to Jennings. On appeal the station elaborated that no enforceable agreement existed because no consideration flowed to it as offeror. The court therefore assessed that ground, rather than treating the case as an evidentiary contest about what was broadcast. (Id.)

## Consideration and application
The court stated that sufficient consideration could be a benefit to the promisor or loss or detriment to the promisee, and that action to one's detriment in reliance on a promise could bind the promisor. It inferred from Jennings's allegation that he stopped listening after nonpayment that he had listened because of the promised reward. He could have chosen another station but selected KSCS in the hope of obtaining payment if the station failed the guarantee. The station also benefited: the promotion attracted listeners hoping to win. The absence of a monetary payment for radio programming thus did not itself defeat consideration. (Id. at 61–62.)

This application concerns commercial inducement of the listener's attention and choice. The court did not hold that any existing listener automatically accepted any broadcast promise, nor did it award expectation damages simply because listening is inconvenient. It held that Jennings's particular petition sufficiently alleged a contract claim to require proceedings on the merits. The remedy at this stage was reversal of summary judgment and remand to the trial court, without a $25,000 award. (Id. at 62.)

## Later stage and accuracy of the historical result
The initial reversal did not mean that Jennings ultimately recovered. The complete later Texas Supreme Court opinion, Radio Station KSCS v. Jennings, 750 S.W.2d 760 (Tex. 1988), describes a second summary judgment and a second appeal, reported at 745 S.W.2d 97. The later dispute concerned Jennings's assertion that announcements of songs and singers amounted to paid commercial interruptions because record companies supplied records to the station. His affidavit did not explain how he personally knew the asserted business practices. The Supreme Court held it inadequate under the personal-knowledge requirement of Rule 166a(e), reversed the later appellate judgment, and affirmed the trial court's judgment for KSCS. Its opening paragraph characterizes the outcome as rendering judgment for KSCS; its final decretal paragraph specifies affirmance of the trial judgment. (750 S.W.2d at 760–762.)

That later per curiam opinion's four notes explain the narrower written contest rules, the exclusion of announcements such as station identification and public-service messages, the absence of the employee claims from Supreme Court review, an additional hearsay objection, and the related personal-knowledge evidence rule. The Supreme Court's expressed ground was evidentiary insufficiency, rather than repudiation of the initial listening-as-consideration analysis. The full intervening 1988 court-of-appeals opinion has not been separately read for this brief; its posture is stated only as the later Supreme Court describes it. (Id. at 761–762 & nn.1–4.)
