# Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall

**466 U.S. 408 (1984) · Supreme Court of the United States · April 24, 1984**

## A Peruvian crash and the company's Texas purchases

Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, commonly called Helicol, was a Colombian helicopter transportation company based in Bogotá. It served oil and construction companies in South America. On January 26, 1976, one of its helicopters crashed in Peru, killing four United States citizens employed on a pipeline project. Their survivors and representatives brought wrongful-death actions in Texas against Helicol, their employer’s consortium, and Bell Helicopter Company. Neither the decedents nor the plaintiffs were domiciled in Texas. 466 U.S. at 409–412 & n.5.

The employer, Consorcio, was a Peruvian consortium and alter ego of a joint venture headquartered in Houston. It contracted to build a pipeline for Peru’s state-owned oil company and needed helicopters to move personnel, equipment, and materials. In 1974, Helicol’s chief executive traveled to Houston at the venture’s request to discuss prices, availability, and operating arrangements. Helicol began performing before the contract was formally signed in Peru. That Spanish-language contract designated Lima as the parties’ residence, provided for Peruvian courts to hear contractual disputes, and directed payments into Helicol’s New York bank account. Id. at 410–411 & nn.1–3.

Helicol also had an ongoing purchasing relationship with Bell in Fort Worth. From 1970 through 1977, it bought helicopters comprising approximately 80 percent of its fleet, along with parts and accessories, for more than $4 million. It sent pilots to Texas for training and to ferry aircraft to South America, and sent management and maintenance personnel for familiarization and technical consultation. Payments from Consorcio exceeded $5 million and were drawn on a Houston bank, although Helicol received them in accounts outside Texas. Id. at 411.

The company had no Texas office, establishment, resident employees, property, records, shareholders, or designated service agent. It was not licensed to do business there, performed no Texas helicopter operations, and sold no product reaching Texas. It did not recruit employees there, solicit Texas business, or sign contracts there. Those absences framed whether the purchasing and negotiation contacts supported jurisdiction over the company generally. Id. at 411–412 & n.4.

## The Texas judgment and the question presented

Helicol specially appeared and sought dismissal for lack of personal jurisdiction. The trial court denied that request and, after a consolidated jury trial, entered a $1,141,200 judgment for the plaintiffs. The other defendants received directed verdicts on the plaintiffs’ claims; the consortium separately obtained a $70,000 cross-claim judgment against Helicol. The Texas intermediate appellate court reversed for want of jurisdiction. The Texas Supreme Court initially agreed, but withdrew its opinions on rehearing and ultimately sustained jurisdiction, with three justices dissenting. Id. at 412–413 & n.6.

Texas’s highest court interpreted its long-arm statute to reach the constitutional limit. The U.S. Supreme Court accepted that interpretation of state law and considered the due process question. The majority treated the parties as conceding that the wrongful-death claims neither arose out of nor related to Helicol’s Texas activities. It therefore addressed general jurisdiction over an unrelated claim, rather than deciding specific jurisdiction over a Texas-connected injury. Footnote 10 expressly reserved whether “arising out of” and “related to” describe different relationships and what connection would satisfy either. Justice Brennan disputed that reading of the plaintiffs’ position. Id. at 413–415 & nn.7–10.

## Holding: these contacts did not support general jurisdiction

Justice Blackmun’s eight-Justice opinion held that Helicol’s Texas activities were insufficient for general jurisdiction. It compared them with the concentrated Ohio corporate management in *Perkins*, where a displaced Philippine company’s president directed its limited wartime business from the forum. Helicol had no comparable Texas operation. The Court examined the negotiation trip, checks, purchases, and training, and rejected them as sufficient general business contacts. Id. at 414–419.

The single Houston negotiating trip was not continuous and systematic activity of the type involved in *Perkins*. Receiving payments drawn on a Houston bank added little. Helicol had not requested that bank or negotiated its use; ordinarily the payor chose where to bank. The unilateral choice of another party could not establish the defendant’s own forum relationship. The Court recognized that an unusual situation, such as concern about a bank’s financial health, could make the payee’s role different, but the record supplied no such circumstance. Id. at 416–417 & n.11.

The purchases and training were more regular, but the Court relied on *Rosenberg Bros. & Co. v. Curtis Brown Co.* for the proposition that purchases and purchase-related trips did not justify jurisdiction over unrelated claims. *International Shoe* had acknowledged that earlier decision. Helicol’s training was part of the package of purchased goods and services; sending personnel to learn to use the equipment did not turn the purchasing relationship into the broader corporate presence required here. Id. at 417–418.

The Court limited that purchasing rule to the general jurisdiction issue presented. Footnote 12 left open the continuing force of *Rosenberg* when an action arises out of or relates to the forum purchases. Nor did the Court adopt the plaintiffs’ proposed jurisdiction-by-necessity alternative. The plaintiffs had not established that Colombia or Peru could not provide a single forum for the defendants, and the Court would not make a far-reaching jurisdictional change on that incomplete record. Id. at 418 n.12, 419 n.13.

## Brennan's dissent: aggregate contacts and a claim-related alternative

Justice Brennan dissented alone. He considered the company's repeated purchases, training visits, technical consultations, and Houston negotiations sufficient purposeful commercial engagement to make Texas jurisdiction fair. He objected to relying on the older corporate-presence reasoning of *Rosenberg* without testing its compatibility with modern commerce and *International Shoe*. Nor did he regard *Perkins*’ particular circumstances as a mandatory minimum for every exercise of general jurisdiction. Id. at 419–424 & dissent nn.1–2.

Brennan separately argued that the actual wrongful-death claim was sufficiently related to Texas contacts to support specific jurisdiction. The Houston negotiations produced the transportation arrangement used on the pipeline, the helicopter involved was bought in Texas, and the allegedly negligent pilot was trained there. The jury had found negligent flying and lack of proper control to be proximate causes of the crash. In Brennan's analysis, the claim's failure formally to arise from negligent Texas training did not make the Texas activities unrelated to it. Id. at 424–426 & dissent nn.3–4.

He emphasized the difference between a claim formally arising from a forum activity and one significantly related to it. Insisting on the former alone could make constitutional jurisdiction depend upon state tort elements or the way the plaintiff worded a complaint. Brennan would have upheld specific jurisdiction on the significant relationship even though the physical accident occurred in Peru. The majority expressly declined to resolve those relationship questions rather than rejecting Brennan’s proposed standard as its holding. Id. at 425–428 & dissent n.5; id. at 415–416 n.10.

## Judgment and assigned context

The Court reversed the Texas Supreme Court, 8–1, because general jurisdiction over Helicol was constitutionally insufficient. This removed the jurisdictional foundation for the Texas judgment reviewed; the Court did not hold that the pilot had exercised due care or adjudicate a defense to the underlying wrongful-death liability. Id. at 419.
