# Public Citizen v. United States Department of Justice
491 U.S. 440 (1989) | Supreme Court of the United States | June 21, 1989 | LRS-SUP-0005D

## Facts and institutional setting

The dispute concerned the federal judicial nomination process, not an ABA committee exercising governmental decision-making power. Since 1952, the Department of Justice had sought confidential evaluations of prospective federal judges from the American Bar Association's Standing Committee on Federal Judiciary. The ABA was a private professional association; its fourteen-member committee was chosen by the association, received no federal money, and did not independently recommend people for nomination. The President retained the choice whether to nominate a candidate. 491 U.S. at 443-445.

For most covered lower-court nominations, a prospective nominee completed an ABA questionnaire. A committee representative examined writings, interviewed lawyers and judges and the candidate, and prepared an informal assessment. The chair communicated that assessment to the Justice Department. If the Department requested a formal evaluation, committee members considered a report and voted. The Department received the rating and information about the degree of agreement, but not the final report. After nomination, the rating became public at the Senate Judiciary Committee's request. Investigations, underlying reports, interviews, and committee deliberations remained confidential. Potential Supreme Court nominees ordinarily were not referred before nomination, although exceptions occurred. Id. at 444-446 & nn.1-3.

The Federal Advisory Committee Act, enacted in 1972, defined an advisory committee to include a committee established or utilized by the President or an agency to obtain advice or recommendations. Covered committees generally had to file a charter, announce meetings, keep minutes, allow public access subject to exemptions, and operate with a federal official authorized to attend and adjourn meetings. FACA also addressed balance, duration, administrative controls, and public reporting. Its opening findings sought to limit unnecessary committees and increase accountability. The question was whether this private ABA relationship fell within that scheme. Id. at 446-447, 451-452.

## Parties' positions and procedure

Washington Legal Foundation requested the identities of candidates under consideration and committee reports and meeting minutes. After refusal, it sued the Department in October 1986. An earlier suit against the ABA and committee had been dismissed because the Department was considered the proper defendant; the Court noted a separate appeal about suing a committee directly. Public Citizen intervened in the Department action. Both organizations sought a declaration that FACA covered the relationship and an injunction stopping the Department's use of the committee until compliance. Id. at 447-448 & nn.5-6.

The Department argued that the committee was outside FACA and that application would violate separation of powers. The district court, 691 F. Supp. 483 (D.D.C. 1988), accepted statutory coverage but dismissed because application would intrude on the President's Article II nomination authority. The challengers appealed directly to the Supreme Court. The ABA intervened for that appeal and also contested Article III standing: the claimed injury was allegedly a generalized grievance, and disclosure exemptions allegedly meant a favorable ruling would afford no real relief. The Supreme Court affirmed the dismissal on statutory grounds, leaving the majority's constitutional questions undecided. Id. at 448-451, 467.

The challengers emphasized the ordinary breadth of utilized, the longstanding institutional relationship, FACA's transparency purpose, and the General Services Administration's regulation treating certain preferred external advisers as utilized committees. The Department and ABA relied on the statute's institutional history and the burdens that coverage would impose on confidential presidential consultation. Those constitutional concerns were part of the majority's interpretive reasoning, rather than a majority holding that the application was unconstitutional.

## Issues and holdings

First, did the organizations have standing despite the public character of their concerns and possible disclosure exemptions? Yes. They sought specific information and access and had been denied it; relief could provide meaningful access and compliance with charter and notice requirements. Id. at 448-451.

Second, did FACA's reference to committees utilized by the Executive cover the Department's solicitation of this ABA committee's evaluations? No. Construed in light of the statute's purposes, predecessor regulatory scheme, legislative development, and serious constitutional difficulties, the definition did not cover this relationship. Id. at 451-467. The holding is about the identified relationship; it is not a universal exemption for every private adviser or every committee receiving no federal funds.

Third, was the application unconstitutional? The five-Justice majority did not decide that question. Three Justices concurring in the judgment would have found statutory coverage and invalidated the application under the Appointments Clause. All eight participating Justices agreed with the judgment of affirmance.

## Standing reasoning

The ABA's generalized-grievance argument confused an interest shared by many people with the absence of a particular injury. WLF had requested specified information and advance notice and received a refusal. As in records-request litigation, that denial was sufficiently distinct even if many others could make the same request. The Court did not require these plaintiffs to establish that a nominee was unqualified or that the selection process caused some additional personal harm. Id. at 449-450.

Possible exemptions did not eliminate redressability. The organizations conceded that many meetings and records might remain confidential, but FACA could still require a charter and notice and might disclose material about the committee's investigative, evaluative, and voting procedures. The ABA itself warned that FACA would meaningfully interfere with confidentiality, undermining its assertion that compliance could yield no meaningful access. The Department conceded standing to challenge at least some requirements. Because the coverage holding resolved the case, the Court did not decide the Department's provision-specific standing objection to other requirements. Id. at 450-451 & n.7.

## Majority's statutory reasoning

Justice Brennan acknowledged that the Department used the committee in the ordinary sense of utilized. But an unqualified definition could reach nearly any consultation with an outside group. FACA's intrusive management and disclosure rules would then apply to conversations with an American Legion post, a civil-rights organization consulted about appointments, or the President's political party. The majority considered those consequences strong evidence that Congress did not intend the dictionary's full breadth. It explicitly allowed examination of purpose and history when literal language produced an odd result, rather than requiring that a result be impossible or utterly absurd. Id. at 452-455 & nn.8-9.

The Court connected that approach to Holy Trinity's treatment of broad language in its legislative setting. It maintained that words remain the primary evidence, but their apparent clarity does not prohibit persuasive evidence about the meaning Congress intended. Its position was not that every policy objection permits rewriting a statute; the identified breadth and institutional context called for closer inquiry here. Id. at 454-455.

That inquiry traced executive efforts to regulate advisory committees before FACA, particularly Executive Order 11007. The order reached government-formed committees and outside committees used in the same manner as government-formed committees. Despite the ABA relationship's prominence, several administrations never treated it as covered. Its private origins, absence of federal funding, independence from the order's contemplated governmental management, and assistance with a constitutionally specified presidential function supported that understanding. FACA adopted many of the order's controls while adding presidential committees and stricter requirements. Id. at 455-459.

The majority read the House and Senate proposals and reports as focusing on committees established by government in an expansive sense, including quasi-public bodies' committees formed for agencies. Examples involving the National Academy of Sciences illustrated the perceived gap. The conference addition of utilized did not, in its view, silently extend regulation to every independently organized private group. The conference report's statement about committees established by or for agencies supported continuity with the narrower institutional meaning. The majority acknowledged the competing case for broad coverage and called the question close. Id. at 459-465.

The challengers' GSA argument was substantial but not controlling for the majority. Footnote 12 discussed the regulation's breadth, GSA's repeated omission of the ABA committee from annual lists, the regulation's adoption long after FACA, and the limited statutory authority supporting the definition. The majority viewed it as lacking the force of a binding legislative rule defining coverage. This is the Court's historical treatment of that regulation in this decision, not a statement of present administrative-law doctrine. Id. at 463-465 & n.12.

Constitutional avoidance supplied the deciding consideration. Applying government-management and public-access rules to confidential consultation during judicial nomination raised serious Article II and separation-of-powers questions; the ABA also raised First Amendment concerns. Where an alternative construction was fairly possible and supported by statutory history, the Court preferred it. The majority expressly recognized that avoidance cannot justify disingenuous evasion of enacted language. Here the competing statutory arguments and predecessor scheme made the alternative plausible. Id. at 465-467 & n.13.

## Kennedy's concurrence in the judgment

Justice Kennedy, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justice O'Connor, agreed that the district court's dismissal should stand but rejected the majority's method and statutory conclusion. The ordinary meaning of utilized plainly covered a recurring institutional arrangement supplying advice. He accepted a narrow absurdity exception for consequences Congress could not possibly have intended, but regarded the majority's examples as constitutional problems or disputed policy outcomes rather than genuine absurdities. Occasional casual consultation also need not equal the sustained relationship in this case. Id. at 467-473.

He criticized Holy Trinity's resort to legislative spirit as an invitation to substitute judicial preferences for enacted law, pointing particularly to that decision's religious-national characterization. He also disputed the majority's use of legislative history: FACA changed rather than merely copied executive practice; Congress generally legislates through categories without naming every committee; and the conference report's express purposes extended beyond expenditure of federal funds. He considered the GSA definition a reasonable, authorized, binding interpretation that directly described this committee's preferred, institutionalized advisory role. Id. at 473-480.

Kennedy agreed that constitutional avoidance requires a fairly possible construction, but thought the majority had exceeded that boundary. Otherwise, the possibility that an application was unconstitutional would circularly become a reason to pretend the statute never covered it. He therefore confronted the constitutional issue. Id. at 481-482.

In his view, the Appointments Clause explicitly assigns nomination of principal officers to the President and consent to the Senate. Congress has no corresponding role in selecting a nominee. Unlike cases involving an implied executive function where balancing may be appropriate, an express constitutional allocation has already fixed the balance. FACA's interference with the President's confidential investigation and consultation was enough to make this application invalid, even without an immediate threat to individual liberty. He expressly left First Amendment objections unaddressed at 472 n.1 and the separate treatment of inferior officers unaddressed at 483 n.3. He also reserved the broader proposition that every intrusion on an explicit constitutional distribution of power, regardless of the affected branch, precludes balancing. Id. at 482–489, especially 486 n.5 (Kennedy, J., concurring in the judgment).

## Judgment, alignment, and practical consequence

The Supreme Court affirmed the district court's judgment. Brennan wrote for White, Marshall, Blackmun, and Stevens. Kennedy concurred only in the judgment with Rehnquist and O'Connor; Scalia did not participate. There was no dissent. The organizations had standing but obtained no FACA compliance injunction. The majority left constitutional validity undecided, while the concurrence supplied a different ground for the same outcome. Id. at 442, 467, 489.

For statutory interpretation, the disagreement separates a contextual, purposive inquiry strengthened by avoidance from a stricter textual approach that reserves departure for genuine absurdity and then openly exercises constitutional review. An exam answer should identify which opinion supplies each proposition: the majority's statutory exclusion cannot be replaced with Kennedy's constitutional holding.
