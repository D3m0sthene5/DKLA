## Citation and opinion structure

Sierra Club v. Morton, 405 U.S. 727 (1972), No. 70-34. Supreme Court of the United States. Argued November 17, 1971; decided April 19, 1972. Justice Stewart delivered the Court's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justices White and Marshall. Justices Douglas, Brennan, and Blackmun each dissented. Justices Powell and Rehnquist did not participate. The judgment was therefore 4-3 among the seven participating Justices. Brennan expressly adopted the second alternative in Blackmun's dissent; he did not join every proposed disposition or argument in that opinion. 405 U.S. at 727, 741, 755-760.

## Parties and requested relief

The Sierra Club, a membership organization devoted to conservation, sued federal officials responsible for approving a proposed development in Mineral King Valley. Rogers C. B. Morton, Secretary of the Interior, was the named respondent in the Supreme Court. The controversy involved the Forest Service within the Department of Agriculture and approvals concerning a road and power line through Sequoia National Park within the Interior Department's responsibilities. The majority names Walt Disney Enterprises, Inc., as the selected developer (729); Douglas’s footnote uses Walt Disney Productions (743 n.5). The suit sought to restrain federal approvals. 405 U.S. at 728-731.

The Club sought declaratory and injunctive relief, including a preliminary injunction, on the theory that the federal officials' proposed approvals exceeded statutory authority and violated applicable administrative requirements. It claimed entitlement to sue through its longstanding conservation interest. It did not allege that the Club or its members used Mineral King or would be harmed in their activities or enjoyment of the area. That choice of standing theory, rather than the absence of an environmentally consequential dispute, controlled the majority's result. Id. at 730-731, 734-736.

## Material facts and underlying merits allegations

Mineral King was a largely undeveloped area in the Sierra Nevada within Sequoia National Forest, used for activities such as hiking, camping, and skiing. In 1965 the Forest Service solicited development proposals. Disney was selected from six applicants and received a three-year permit to conduct surveys and prepare plans. The Forest Service approved its master plan in January 1969. As described by the Court, the approximately $35 million project would accommodate 14,000 visitors daily and include hotels, restaurants, ski lifts, parking, and related facilities. Id. at 728-729.

The proposed authorization combined a thirty-year permit for an eighty-acre portion of the development with special-use permits for other facilities. Access required a new twenty-mile road, part of which would cross Sequoia National Park. A high-voltage power line would also cross park land. These details explained the involvement of multiple federal officials and the statutory objections to a project extending beyond a single resort permit. Id. at 729-730.

The Club challenged, among other things, the use of the permit arrangement to authorize a development exceeding the statutory acreage limitation; the road's asserted inconsistency with park purposes and restrictions; the absence of a public hearing allegedly required by agency regulations; and the authority to approve the power line. The Supreme Court identified these as allegations about the merits, not established violations. The Court's holding did not determine whether Disney or the officials had complied with those requirements. Id. at 730 n.2, 741.

The Club described its history, membership, and conservation purposes. Organized in 1892, it had approximately 78,000 members, including about 27,000 in the San Francisco Bay area. Its expertise and asserted interest in protecting national parks, refuges, and forests were undisputed for purposes of the case. What its papers lacked was an allegation connecting this proposed development to use of Mineral King or other individualized injury suffered by itself or its members. Institutional commitment and particular injury were separate questions under the majority's analysis. Id. at 735 & n.8, 739.

## Procedural history

The Club sued in the Northern District of California in June 1969. Following a two-day hearing, the district court granted a preliminary injunction. It accepted the Club's standing and concluded that the merits allegations raised sufficiently substantial questions to justify interim relief. The injunction was preliminary; the district court had not entered a final judgment establishing every alleged statutory violation. Id. at 730-731.

The Ninth Circuit reversed. It rejected standing based on the Club's special conservation interest and also addressed the requirements for preliminary relief, including irreparable injury and the merits showing. The Supreme Court granted review on standing and affirmed on that ground. It did not adopt a final merits judgment that the development was lawful or resolve each alternative reason the Ninth Circuit had discussed. Id. at 731, 741.

The standing omission was not simply an overlooked name in an otherwise adequate allegation of injury. The Club had chosen to rely on its organizational conservation interest and continued to defend that theory. Although an amicus referred to Club outings in Mineral King, the Club had not presented use of the area as the basis for its own standing in the lower proceedings. The Court noted the possibility of seeking leave to amend under Rule 15, but did not order that amendment be accepted or preserve the injunction pending it. Id. at 735 n.8, 736 & n.9.

## Questions presented

Under APA § 702, could a conservation organization obtain judicial review through its special interest in the affected environment without alleging injury to itself or its members? Could aesthetic or recreational harm qualify as injury in fact even though it was noneconomic? Did precedents permitting litigants to advance the public interest remove the need for a personal stake when entering court? The Court expressly left the separate zone-of-interests question unresolved because the required injury allegation was absent. Id. at 731-741, especially 733 n.5.

## Rule and holdings

An organization invoking APA review must show that it or its members are among those injured by the challenged action. Injury may consist of harm to aesthetic, environmental, or recreational interests; it need not involve economic loss. An organization can represent members with the requisite injury, and a litigant with standing may advance the broader public interest in seeking relief. A longstanding interest in conservation, however, does not itself establish that this plaintiff has suffered or will suffer injury. Id. at 734-740.

The Sierra Club's allegations did not establish the necessary injury. It failed to claim that its members used Mineral King or that the development would affect the activities or enjoyment of the Club or its members. The decision did not announce a general requirement that all environmental plaintiffs identify particular people by name. The defect here was the absence of an asserted connection between anyone represented by the Club and the threatened use or enjoyment of the area. Id. at 735-736 & nn.8-9.

## Actual judgment

The Supreme Court affirmed the Ninth Circuit's judgment. The Sierra Club lacked standing on the theory and allegations it had presented. The preliminary injunction was not reinstated, and the Supreme Court did not decide the legality of the development. Its observation that the Club could seek leave to amend was a reservation of a procedural possibility, rather than an order directing a favorable ruling on amendment or interim relief. Id. at 735 n.8, 741.

## Majority reasoning

### APA review still required a plaintiff with an affected interest

The Club relied on § 702's authorization for a person suffering legal wrong or adversely affected or aggrieved within the meaning of a relevant statute to obtain judicial review. The Court reviewed the movement away from a narrow legal-interest test, explaining that Data Processing had recognized injury in fact and an interest arguably within the relevant statutory zone. The Club's suit failed the injury component, so the Court did not decide whether it satisfied the zone component. Id. at 731-734 & n.5.

The opinion also acknowledged that Congress can enlarge the categories of interests that support judicial review. It found no basis in the general APA review provision for dispensing with injury to the person seeking review. The result therefore depended on the statutory avenue the Club invoked, not an assessment that a resort development could never present a judicial controversy. Id. at 732-734.

### The nature of the threatened harm was legally cognizable

The planned changes could reduce scenic, recreational, and environmental enjoyment. Such interests are important enough to qualify as injury in fact. The Court expressly rejected the proposition that economic injury is the only cognizable harm, and it did not deny injury merely because many people would share it. A person who used Mineral King could be affected by the proposed development even without owning property there or losing money. Id. at 734-735.

The relevant question was who would suffer that harm. The Club's allegations about the valley's ecology and aesthetics described an adverse change, but the general existence of that change did not establish an injury to the Club. The Court required the organization to connect the threatened harm to itself or its members. That connection distinguishes a judicially cognizable noneconomic injury from a plaintiff's abstract interest in a subject. Id. at 735-736.

### An organization could sue for injured members, but commitment alone was insufficient

The Court accepted that an organization whose members were injured could be an appropriate representative of those interests. It did not require each affected member to bring a separate action. Nor did it deny the Club's expertise, seriousness, or history. Those characteristics concerned the organization's qualifications to advocate, while standing concerned whether the litigant represented an actually affected interest. Id. at 739-740.

Allowing a special interest in a problem to supply standing would leave no principled stopping point at a large, established conservation organization. An individual with the same sincere interest could make the same claim. The majority considered such an expansion inconsistent with limiting courts to concrete controversies brought by affected parties. Its concern was the basis for judicial intervention, not the relative worth of the Club's conservation goals. Id. at 739-741.

### Public-interest arguments become available after standing is established

The Club relied on decisions permitting litigants to challenge agency conduct in the public interest even though the applicable statute did not give them a private right to win on the merits. The majority explained that those precedents still involved a litigant who had first demonstrated injury, such as a competitor facing economic loss. Once that entry requirement was met, the plaintiff could raise statutory and public-interest objections broader than its own commercial concern. Id. at 736-739.

The Court thus separated a litigant's stake in bringing suit from the grounds on which it may challenge the defendant's conduct and obtain equitable relief. It rejected the Club's suggestion that pleading a private injury would trap it into litigating only private interests. Footnote 15 expressly explained that injury in fact goes to standing and does not prevent an injured plaintiff from invoking the general public interest in support of equitable relief. Id. at 740 n.15.

### Amendment was possible, but the Court decided the theory actually presented

The Court would not infer use of the valley merely from the Club's name, membership, or conservation work. Nor did material mentioned by an amicus establish that the Club had alleged its own or its members' injury below. Its continued adherence to the special-interest theory left that deficiency unresolved. The majority recognized a possible Rule 15 request to amend, but affirmed the judgment before it rather than constructing a new injury allegation for the Club. Id. at 735-736 & nn.8-9.

This procedural consequence was important to the dissents. An organization might ultimately allege a qualifying injury while still losing interim protection during the period required to amend and obtain further relief. Blackmun's first proposed alternative responded to that concern by preserving the injunction conditionally. The majority did not order that course. Id. at 735 n.8, 755-757.

## Separate opinions

### Douglas: natural objects could be represented as legal parties

Douglas would have allowed litigation in the name of the threatened natural object itself. In his account, the case could properly be understood as Mineral King seeking protection through human representatives. Legal personality already extended to artificial entities such as corporations and, for particular admiralty purposes, ships. Recognizing a valley or river as a litigant would use a comparable legal device to ensure representation of an ecological community. Id. at 741-743.

The proposed representatives would be people with a meaningful relationship to the place, such as those who hiked, fished, camped, studied, or otherwise knew it. Douglas distinguished that relationship from simply reacting to environmental publicity. The object would represent the associated ecological unit, including forms of life unable to speak for themselves, while knowledgeable people would present its interests. His proposal therefore involved a different conception of the party whose interests were represented, rather than only an easier method of proving one member's recreational injury. Id. at 743-745, 750-752.

Douglas also expressed concern that agency relationships with regulated interests and the practical limits of congressional supervision could leave environmental interests underrepresented. Those were his institutional assessments, supported by authorities and examples in his dissent; they were not majority findings that the officials in this case were corrupt. He explicitly disclaimed the suggestion that judicial access required courts to assume the agencies' management functions. The relevant question was who could be heard, and the environmental claim might still lose on the merits. Id. at 745-752 & nn.6-9.

His appendix reproduced the Solicitor General's oral argument about separation of powers and the dangers of broadly expanding judicial intervention. The appendix is the government's argument quoted within a dissent, not an additional opinion of the Court. Douglas also stated that he shared Blackmun's views and would reverse. Id. at 741, 753-755.

### Blackmun, alternative one: preserve relief while requiring prompt amendment

Blackmun regarded the case as presenting substantial legal questions with a risk of irreversible environmental consequences before those questions could be resolved. He worried that affirmance would remove interim protection, make amendment uncertain or delayed, and allow construction to generate both physical changes and economic pressure against halting the project. These were reasons for the disposition he proposed, rather than findings that construction necessarily would occur in a particular way after the decision. Id. at 755-756.

His first alternative was to reverse the Ninth Circuit and approve the district court's standing ruling and preliminary injunction on condition that the Club promptly amend its complaint to satisfy the majority's injury requirements. Failure or refusal to amend would end that route. This proposal accepted the possibility of applying the majority's standing test but changed the interim procedural result. It was materially more protective than merely observing that leave to amend could be requested after affirmance. Id. at 756-757.

Blackmun emphasized the unresolved permit-acreage, park-road, power-line, refuge, and administrative-compliance questions. He considered those merits issues substantial enough to deserve adjudication, but did not decide them in a controlling opinion or find that each asserted statutory violation had been proved. Id. at 757.

### Blackmun, alternative two: recognize the organization's established environmental interest

Blackmun independently proposed expanding standing to allow a bona fide, established organization with relevant expertise and purposes to litigate environmental issues. That alternative would permit the Sierra Club to proceed on its organizational qualifications and interest rather than requiring the individualized-use amendment contemplated by his first alternative. He believed courts could impose practical limits based on the litigant's sincerity, knowledge, established status, and capacity to present a genuine controversy. Id. at 757-758.

His discussion of expected visitor traffic illustrated why he regarded the stakes as substantial. He also questioned whether local residents expecting economic benefits would necessarily serve as effective representatives of conservation concerns. These arguments supported his proposed expansion of access; they should not be restated as a majority rule that expert organizations automatically have standing or as factual findings about the motives of every local resident. Id. at 758-760.

### Brennan: specifically adopted Blackmun's second alternative

Brennan wrote a short separate dissent agreeing that the Sierra Club had standing for the reasons in Blackmun's alternative two. He would therefore have reached the merits. Because the Court did not address them, he recorded agreement that the merits questions were substantial rather than deciding their outcome. His stated basis was the broader organizational-standing approach, not merely a preference to permit amendment to establish members' use of the valley. Id. at 755.

## Scope and casebook relationship

The casebook excerpt presents the injury-in-fact dispute. The full opinion supplies the statutory allegations, the Club's deliberate choice of theory, the public-interest argument, the amendment reservation, and the differences among the three dissents. In particular, collapsing Blackmun's two alternatives into a single complaint about defective pleading misstates the disagreement Brennan actually adopted. Casebook pp. 1157-1162; 405 U.S. at 730 n.2, 735-740, 755-760.

The governing distinction is between the kind of injury and the plaintiff who suffers it. Environmental and aesthetic harms can qualify, but an organization relying on § 702 must connect such harm to itself or its members under the majority's rule. Once standing exists, the plaintiff can advance the public interest and litigate substantial statutory objections. The Court's affirmance resolved access on these pleadings; it neither approved the project nor held that only economic stakeholders could challenge it. Id. at 734-741.

### Assigned editorial note

The complete “Standing v. Cause of Action” note on 1161–1162 asks whether an association may assert interests beyond those held by particular members, and which interests it can represent on their behalf. It places the Club’s broader theory against the concern that citizen or taxpayer litigation could turn courts into forums for generalized grievances. The editors compare increasingly detailed injury, causal, and remedial inquiries with the usual elements of a cause of action, and note the later development of redressability. That comparison is editorial analysis: Sierra Club did not decide that standing and the merits are identical or adopt every later standing requirement in this opinion.
