# Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc.

**141 S. Ct. 1183 (2021), No. 18–956.** Supreme Court, April 5, 2021. **Civil Procedure — mixed questions, appellate review of jury verdicts, and the Seventh Amendment; copyright fair use.** Assigned account: CivPro, physical PDF pages 631–632, printed pages 1251–1252, with the ensuing editorial discussion continuing onto printed page 1253. Pinpoints below use each writing's separately numbered pages in the preserved official slip opinion.

## Facts and litigation history

Google acquired Android in 2005 and developed a platform for smartphone applications. Millions of programmers already knew the Java language and its system for calling prewritten functions. Google wanted those programmers to use their skills on Android. Licensing discussions with Sun Microsystems failed, apparently over restrictions including interoperability. Google then wrote its own platform and its own implementing code, but copied approximately 11,500 lines of declaring code and the associated organization for 37 Java API packages. Oracle acquired Sun in 2010 and sued Google. Breyer opinion 1–9.

The distinction between kinds of code explains the dispute. Implementing code tells the computer how to perform a task. A method call is the programmer's command requesting that task. Declaring code names and organizes the methods, classes, and packages, connecting familiar commands with the programs that carry them out. Google copied the interface through which programmers summoned tasks, while independently writing the implementations. The copied lines were roughly 0.4 percent of the 2.86 million lines in the Java API as a whole, but represented extensive copying of the declaring code within the selected packages. Id. at 3–8, 28–30.

The initial jury rejected Oracle's patent claims, found limited copyright infringement, and deadlocked on fair use. The district judge held the relevant declaring code and organization outside copyright protection. The Federal Circuit reversed the copyrightability ruling and remanded for a fair-use trial. The Supreme Court denied review at that earlier stage. After a weeklong trial on remand, a second jury found that Google had established fair use by a preponderance of the evidence. The Federal Circuit again reversed, resolving factual questions in Google's favor but holding the use legally unfair, and remanded for a damages trial. The Supreme Court granted review of both copyrightability and fair use. Id. at 9–11.

## Issues, judgment, and lineup

Google argued that the declaring code was an unprotectable method of operation and, alternatively, that copying it was fair use. It also argued that the jury's verdict should receive substantial-evidence review and that deciding fair use anew violated the Seventh Amendment. Oracle defended copyrightability and the Federal Circuit's conclusion that the commercial, verbatim copying was unfair.

The Court assumed copyrightability solely for argument and held Google's particular copying a fair use as a matter of law. It agreed with the Federal Circuit about the legal standard of review but disagreed about the substantive application. The judgment was **reversed and remanded for proceedings consistent with the opinion**. Breyer opinion 14–15, 18–21, 35–36.

Justice Breyer wrote for six Justices: Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh joined. Justice Thomas dissented, joined by Justice Alito. Justice Barrett did not participate. There was no separate concurrence. The official source includes two explanatory appendices to the Court's opinion and Thomas's complete nineteen-page dissent.

## Mixed questions and jury protection

Fair use combines factual matters with a legal conclusion. Courts should separate the parts and review each under the appropriate standard. Where a mixed question cannot be reduced further, the governing standard depends on whether its resolution predominantly involves factual or legal work. Here the ultimate determination predominantly involved legal work: applying judicial interpretations of the statute and elaborating principles that guide future cases. Underlying matters such as the extent of copying and effects on markets could still require jury findings. Breyer opinion 18–20.

The Court therefore rejected Google's demand to make the jury's final fair-use answer conclusive whenever substantial evidence supported it. De novo review of the legal conclusion did not authorize appellate judges to resolve contested subsidiary facts against the verdict. Indeed, the Court credited the Federal Circuit for leaving factual determinations with the jury, even though that court reached the wrong ultimate legal result. Id. at 19–20.

The Reexamination Clause did not prohibit that allocation because judicial determination of controlling law does not reexamine a jury-found fact. A court makes such determinations when deciding judgment as a matter of law. The Court also rejected a constitutional right to have the jury decide the ultimate fair-use defense. The eighteenth-century fair-abridgment and infringement decisions Google invoked involved different questions, while contemporary fair use had been described as equitable. The majority found no failure to preserve the substance of the common-law jury right existing in 1791. Id. at 20–21.

Thomas agreed that, under precedent, legal questions predominated and the court should construe disputed facts and inferences in Google's favor. He nevertheless would not definitively resolve the Seventh Amendment argument on the parties' limited presentation. His note 3 expresses that caution rather than a disagreement with the majority's basic mixed-question approach. Thomas dissent 8 & n.3.

## Why the Court found fair use

Section 107 directs consideration of purpose and character, the work's nature, the amount and substantiality taken, and market effects. These guide a contextual inquiry, not a mechanical vote among four independent boxes. Computer programs have functional features that can make conventional copyright categories difficult to apply. Fair use can preserve incentives for creation while preventing exclusive rights from stifling new expression. Assuming protection here did not exempt software from copyright's limiting doctrines. Breyer opinion 11–18.

**Nature of the work.** The Court started with the declaring code's position in a user interface. It was intertwined with uncopyrightable ideas about organizing tasks, familiar commands, and new implementing code Google had written. Its value also depended substantially on programmers' investment in learning the system. Although this was not a final holding that the interface was unprotectable, the characteristics placed it farther from copyright's core than much implementing code and favored fair use. Id. at 21–24.

**Purpose and character.** Google reused familiar commands in a different computing environment, enabling programmers to develop smartphone applications without abandoning acquired skills. New implementing code carried out the tasks on Android. The Court treated that reimplementation as transformative, despite acknowledging that both systems used the declarations to call functions. Android's commercial nature did not defeat the conclusion. Nor did the Court decide whether good faith generally belongs in every fair-use analysis: the other factors and the jury's resolution of contested evidence made that question nondeterminative here. Id. at 24–28.

**Amount and substantiality.** Viewed alone, 11,500 copied lines were extensive. Viewed within the full API, they were a small fraction. The appropriate comparison also depended on Google's legitimate purpose. Copying only the 170 lines necessary to use the Java language, as the Federal Circuit suggested, would not necessarily preserve programmers' knowledge of the familiar task-calling interface. Google copied what was needed for the new platform's use of those skills, rather than selecting the declarations for their intrinsic creative beauty. The amount was tied to the transformative purpose. Id. at 28–30.

**Market effects.** The jury could find that Android and Java SE operated in materially distinct markets and that Sun had not been positioned to succeed in the smartphone market even without Google's copying. The record also supported benefits to Sun from expanding the community of Java-trained programmers. The majority did not deny contrary evidence or Google's enormous revenues. It emphasized that the negotiations involved more than licensing these particular declarations and that defining every unauthorized use as a lost license risked circular reasoning. Id. at 30–33.

The source of the interface's value mattered as well. A substantial part came from third-party programmers' acquired skills. Enforcing control over the interface here could lock up those skills and restrict the creation of new programs, yielding returns attributable to users' future innovations rather than protecting the creator's expression alone. The Court weighed uncertain competitive losses, their source, and public creativity together, finding the fourth factor also favored Google. It expressly disclaimed overturning or modifying prior fair-use decisions involving other kinds of works. Id. at 33–36.

## Thomas's dissent

Thomas would first hold the declarations copyrightable. The statute protects instructions operating directly or indirectly; declaring and implementing code therefore both fit its text. The method-of-operation exclusion protects ideas rather than erasing protection for particular original expressions. Oracle could have organized its declarations in many ways, and Apple's and Microsoft's different interfaces demonstrated alternatives. In Thomas's view, skipping copyrightability let the majority artificially discount one protected kind of code throughout the analysis. Thomas dissent 4–11.

He accepted that software's functionality might make the nature factor favor Google, but regarded the other three factors as decisively favoring Oracle. The copied declarations attracted programmers and were the work's commercial heart. Moving the same functions to a new platform was derivative rather than transformative; simply enabling new products could otherwise justify almost any software piracy. He gave substantial weight to Google's commercial success and the licensing market. Id. at 11–18.

Thomas pointed to Amazon's 97.5-percent license discount and a sharp reduction in Samsung's payments as concrete competitive harm. Oracle need not have built its own smartphone to license its interface to someone who did. He disputed the majority's lock-in concerns, noting alternative interfaces, freely available declarations for programmers, and the limited Android versions actually involved. These are the dissent's assessment and criticisms, not unanimous factual or legal conclusions. He would measure substantiality against the declaring code copied rather than the whole software platform. Id. at 11–19, including nn.7–12.

The assigned account correctly separates the Court's agreement with the Federal Circuit's review framework from its disagreement over fair use. The ensuing textbook question and Professor Steinman's Rule 50 discussion, continuing onto printed page 1253, are editorial analysis rather than another holding of the Court. The decision establishes legal review of the ultimate fair-use question while preserving deference to underlying jury findings. It does not hold APIs categorically uncopyrightable or all commercial reuse permissible.
