# Figgie International, Inc. v. Destileria Serralles, Inc.
190 F.3d 252 (4th Cir. 1999) | Contracts | Trade usage, exclusive remedies, and essential purpose

## Failed labeling equipment and a disputed remedy page

Serralles bottled rum at a Puerto Rico plant. In June 1993 it contracted with Figgie for machinery capable of applying a clear label within a raised glass oval on a clear bottle of Cristal rum. Installation in April 1994 produced immediate problems. Figgie attempted repairs over several months, but performance remained unsatisfactory. In November the equipment was returned and Figgie refunded the purchase price. Serralles nevertheless sought compensation for alleged operating losses and delay in obtaining alternative machinery. Unable to resolve the dispute, Figgie sought a declaratory judgment that its remaining obligations were limited by the parties' remedies agreement. (190 F.3d at 254.)

Figgie asserted that its standard sales conditions made repair, replacement, or return with reimbursement the exclusive remedies and excluded consequential, incidental, contingent, and delay damages. But its original contract had been lost in a business reorganization. Serralles produced its copy, whose last page referred to general conditions printed on the reverse; that reverse was blank. Figgie claimed a copying error, while Serralles denied that the conditions ever accompanied the agreement. Figgie conceded at oral argument that their original inclusion presented a factual dispute. Its alternative ground was that industry usage supplied the same exclusive limitation regardless of that unresolved question. (Id. at 254–255.)

The district court granted Figgie summary judgment and denied Serralles partial summary judgment. The Fourth Circuit reviewed de novo, applying the summary-judgment requirement of no genuine dispute of material fact and viewing evidence favorably to the opposing party. It affirmed on trade usage and the continued effectiveness of the return-and-refund remedy, without finding that the missing written conditions definitely had been delivered. (Id. at 254–256, 259.)

## The commercial agreement included proved usage

The court applied South Carolina's historical UCC provisions. Section 36-2-719 allowed agreed limitations on remedies, made an explicitly exclusive remedy the sole remedy, restored ordinary Code remedies if the limitation failed of its essential purpose, and addressed unconscionable exclusions of consequential damages. The Code's definition of agreement included the factual bargain found through language and circumstances, including trade usage. Section 36-1-205 permitted usage to supplement or qualify express terms where reasonably consistent and defined usage through regularity sufficient to justify an expectation of observance. (Id. at 255–256.)

Figgie supplied affidavits from people with extensive bottle-labeling and packaging experience, stating that industry sellers limited buyers to repair, replacement, or return and excluded consequential damages. Serralles said it had not acquiesced but produced no evidence contradicting those affidavits. The unrebutted usage supplied the limitation. The holding therefore did not establish industry practice by appellate intuition or make every seller's assertion of custom conclusive; it rested on specific, uncontroverted summary-judgment evidence. (Id. at 256.)

Serralles argued that an implied usage could not satisfy section 36-2-719's requirement that exclusivity be expressly agreed. The court rejected that reading because the statutory agreement included trade usage, and the proved usage itself specified exclusivity. It agreed with other circuits permitting usage to add an exclusive remedy. It distinguished *Myrtle Beach Pipeline*, which required exclusivity to be explicit when construing a remedy set out in the writing but did not address a limitation supplied by usage. The absence of a proved written remedy page consequently did not leave the agreement remediless or necessarily open to every UCC remedy. (Id. at 256–257.)

## Unsuccessful repair did not eliminate the refund alternative

Serralles next argued that the limitation failed of its essential purpose. A commercially reasonable clause must give way when circumstances deprive a party of the substantial value of the bargain. A repair-only or repair-and-replacement remedy can fail when repair efforts do not succeed. But this agreement also allowed return and refund, which occurred. Figgie's initial election to attempt repair did not abandon its ability later to refund. A manufacturer naturally would try to achieve satisfactory operation before returning the price, especially for sophisticated commercial machinery. The record showed no failure of the refund alternative. (Id. at 257–258.)

The complete first footnote rejects a universal rule that refund always preserves an essential purpose. Latent defects discovered so late that repayment would be wholly inadequate under the circumstances could produce a different result. Serralles, by contrast, knew of the defects from installation and participated in efforts to fix them. The court thus distinguished failure to make the equipment work from failure of the agreed collection of remedies. (Id. at 258; n.1.)

## Repair assurances did not create a fresh unlimited undertaking

Serralles also relied on Figgie's later assurances of total commitment, responsibility for resolving problems, and intent to achieve satisfactory performance. It characterized them as new agreements, modifications, or waiver of the limitation. The panel found no reasonable evidence of either party's intent to change the contractual relationship or create another one. The communications occurred while Figgie pursued the contemplated repair remedy. None specified additional remedies if those attempts failed, and none showed an agreement to waive the existing limitation in exchange for permission to repair. (Id. at 258.)

The district court had additionally reasoned that even hypothetical new repair contracts would acquire the same limitation through trade usage. The second footnote expressly declines to decide that theory: because no new contracts were formed as a matter of law, the appellate holding did not establish that every subsequent promise automatically carries the original sales usage. (Id.; n.2.)

## Exact judgment and the buyer's cross-motion

The affirmance also covered denial of Serralles' partial-summary-judgment motion. Although defective performance when the equipment was returned was apparently undisputed, factual issues remained about the time reasonably allowed for correction and original inclusion of the standard conditions. More fundamentally, Figgie's successful independent trade-usage ground defeated the buyer's demand for broader recovery. The court did not award damages to Serralles or order additional factfinding on the missing-page dispute. (Id. at 259.)

Traxler wrote the published August 20, 1999 decision, joined by Widener and Diana Gribbon Motz, for a unanimous three-judge panel. The district court's judgment was affirmed, including Figgie's summary judgment and denial of Serralles' motion. (Id. at 253, 259.)
