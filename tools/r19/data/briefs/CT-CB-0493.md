# Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC
25 F.4th 613 (9th Cir. 2022) (amended opinion) | Contracts | Economic duress and undue influence

## Facts and procedural history

Dario Martinez-Gonzalez, a Mexican agricultural worker, supported his wife and family members and sought seasonal work paying substantially more than he could earn in Mexico. Elkhorn Packing, a labor contractor for D'Arrigo Brothers, accepted his application in 2016, helped obtain an H-2A visa, and transported him to Monterey County, California. He worked in lettuce fields and depended on employer-provided housing and transportation. He worked a season in Arizona between the two California seasons and quit during the 2017 California season. These are three successive harvesting seasons, rather than three California arbitration agreements. Amended slip op. 6–8 & n.2.

A few days after he began work, Elkhorn held an orientation at about 4 p.m. in his hotel's parking lot following a day's field labor. Roughly 150 workers stood in lines to sign employment packets; Martinez-Gonzalez waited approximately forty minutes according to the findings recounted in the dissent. Seated supervisors flipped through the packets, directed signatures, and urged workers to hurry for those behind them. The packets contained Spanish-language arbitration agreements. Martinez-Gonzalez signed without reading them, received no explanation or copy, and was not affirmatively told he could consult counsel. He did not ask for a copy, reading time, or consultation. The majority noted that nobody expressly told him that refusing arbitration would end his employment. The dissent emphasized the trial court's finding that he reasonably understood signing as necessary to keep his job and its rejection of employer testimony that signing was optional. Both propositions must be distinguished: absence of an express threat did not mean the trial court found a realistically voluntary choice. Slip op. 6–8, 27–30, 38–42.

In 2018 Martinez-Gonzalez brought federal minimum-wage and state wage, meal, rest, and privacy claims in state court on behalf of himself and similarly situated workers. After removal, the employers sought arbitration under the 2016 and 2017 agreements. Judge Edward Chen conducted a two-day bench trial and found economic duress and undue influence, holding the agreements unenforceable and denying the motion to compel. The Ninth Circuit reversed that interlocutory arbitration ruling and remanded; it did not resolve the wage claims. Slip op. 8, 26.

## Assigned stages, court, and scope of review

The two casebook captions refer to one amended panel decision, not separate decisions on economic duress and undue influence. The first selection begins at printed page 493 and the second at page 502; the latter expressly incorporates the earlier facts and portion of the opinion. This brief addresses both doctrines and Rawlinson's dissent together.

Bumatay wrote for himself and Siler, a Sixth Circuit judge sitting by designation; Rawlinson dissented. The original opinion filed November 3, 2021, at 17 F.4th 875 was amended February 14, 2022. The accompanying order denied panel and en banc rehearing: Bumatay and Siler favored denying panel rehearing, Bumatay voted to deny en banc rehearing and Siler recommended denial, while Rawlinson favored both. The full court was advised and no judge requested an en banc vote. That order does not constitute a substantive en banc opinion. Slip op. 1, 5.

FAA § 2 permits generally applicable defenses such as duress, fraud, and unconscionability. California law governed the defenses asserted here. Enforceability was reviewed de novo, underlying facts for clear error. The disagreement concerned both what California law demanded and whether the appellate majority properly deferred to the trial court's findings and credibility determinations. Slip op. 8, 26–27.

## Economic duress: rule and majority application

Economic duress requires a wrongful act sufficiently coercive to induce a reasonably prudent person lacking a reasonable alternative to agree, plus causation connecting the pressure to assent. Wrongfulness need not consist of a crime or tort; examples include false claims, bad-faith threats, withholding an acknowledged debt, or improper threats within Restatement § 176. The doctrine protects against exploitation but does not invalidate ordinary hard bargaining. The party seeking rescission bears the burden. Slip op. 9–11.

The majority held that neither wrongfulness nor lack of reasonable alternatives was established. The trial court found an unfavorable signing environment, but, in the majority's reading, did not find coercive purpose or bad faith. Holding an orientation at a convenient gathering place after workers arrived served a practical business function. The employer made no threat to withhold earned wages or comparable threat of economic disaster. Rich & Whillock's deliberate withholding of an undisputed debt from a subcontractor facing imminent bankruptcy therefore differed from this setting. The majority also questioned whether obtaining a conventional arbitration agreement, without lowering wages or releasing substantive claims, created the disproportionate loss of economic value contemplated by the cases. That discussion expresses skepticism in this record, rather than a categorical holding that arbitration agreements can never be obtained through duress. Slip op. 11–14 & n.3.

On alternatives, the majority relied on the absence of an express termination threat, the agreements' failure to describe signing as mandatory, and Martinez-Gonzalez's admission that he assumed it was mandatory. In its view a reasonably prudent person could ask whether signing was required. General need for wages and an adverse outcome that was feared but not certain did not eliminate reasonable alternatives. It also relied on the agreements' ten-day revocation period, finding no employer interference with revocation and applying the rule that failing to read a signed agreement ordinarily does not defeat enforcement. It did not simply tell him to find different housing or buy a return ticket; its specific proposed alternatives were inquiry and timely revocation. Slip op. 14–18 & nn.4–6.

Because those two elements failed, the majority expressly did not reach causation. It treated economic duress as a limited remedy whose prerequisites were absent while ordinary means of vindicating his substantive rights through arbitration remained available. Slip op. 18–19 & n.7.

## Undue influence: rule and majority application

California Civil Code § 1575 addresses abuse of confidence or authority to obtain unfair advantage, exploitation of weakness of mind, and grossly oppressive exploitation of necessities or distress. Odorizzi describes undue susceptibility and excessive pressure as balancing elements: greater vulnerability can make lesser pressure sufficient, and extraordinary pressure can overcome a person of ordinary capacity. The final inquiry is whether the person's will was overborne. The defense is factual and the party seeking rescission bears its burden. Slip op. 19–20.

The majority found no explicit finding or evidentiary basis for heightened susceptibility. Martinez-Gonzalez had a secondary-school education, could read Spanish, had worked in agriculture since childhood, supported his family before the job, and later voluntarily left it. Modest income and family obligations, without more, did not establish inability to exercise independent volition. Because heightened susceptibility was lacking, extraordinary force would be necessary. Slip op. 20–21.

Odorizzi's pressure indicators include unusual time and place, demands for immediate completion, emphasis on consequences of delay, multiple persuaders against one person, lack of independent advisers, and assertions that no time exists for consultation. They are contextual indicators, rather than an inflexible checklist. The majority accepted that the district court identified unfavorable circumstances but found them insufficient in context. The hotel parking lot conveniently assembled agricultural workers; waiting until their arrival was explained by business practicalities; tiredness and hunger did not establish oppressive conditions; and nobody prevented a request to read or consult counsel. Instructions to hurry accommodated the line, while general work-rule admonishments were not demands to accept arbitration. Slip op. 21–25.

Footnote 8 rejects merely counting satisfied factors and declines to decide an allegation that the documents were falsely described as Social Security paperwork: that issue was disputed and the district court made no finding of a false statement. Footnote 9 clarifies that Odorizzi's examples of bereavement, hospitalized pain, and an immediate late-night real-estate ultimatum are illustrative, not the complete universe of undue influence. The majority nonetheless used them to show the extraordinary force required in a person without heightened susceptibility. Slip op. 22–23 & nn.8–9. It held the contrary undue-influence finding clearly erroneous. Slip op. 25–26.

## Rawlinson's dissent

Rawlinson would uphold both defenses and affirm the denial of arbitration. Her principal objection was the majority's replacement of supported trial findings with its own evaluation. After observing witnesses, the district judge credited the workers and rejected employer testimony about optional signing. It found a tired, hungry worker far from home, dependent on employer housing and transportation, confronted with documents in a lengthy standing line and given no meaningful review opportunity or explanation. Under clear-error review, supported findings and credibility judgments warranted special deference. Slip op. 26–35.

She treated the combined environment as the wrongful act, rather than the mere request for arbitration. California duress can arise from lawful but coercive conduct; unequal economic power and the employer's awareness of family dependence mattered to the factual inquiry. The district court's finding of a coercive environment, in her view, captured coercive purpose even without those exact words. Nobody among thousands of workers had refused to sign, and supervisors described the documents as required or something workers were going to sign. Absence of an express threat did not make this atmosphere optional. Slip op. 35–40.

Rawlinson also rejected inquiry and revocation as meaningful alternatives on the credited record. The employer's practice prevented informed reading, provided no retained copy, and discouraged questions; a worker who did not know what he had signed could not realistically use an undisclosed revocation term. The majority and dissent expressly disputed what the findings established about reading opportunities and questions; those are rival readings of the record, not additional uncontested findings this brief attributes to both sides. Slip op. 40–43; compare majority nn.4, 6.

For undue influence she linked trial findings to five Odorizzi indicators: unusual time, unusual place, rushed completion, multiple supervisors, and absence of advisers. She did not identify findings for extreme consequences of delay or an express assertion that consultation time was unavailable. The majority's reliance on more dramatic illustrative cases did not exhaust the doctrine or justify normalizing the employer-created atmosphere. Each relevant trial finding had support, unlike the unsupported findings reversed in Myers. Either defense independently supported rescission. Slip op. 43–49. Her four notes discuss H-2A vulnerability, limits on alternate employment, her response to the majority's characterization of the disagreement, and replaceability of workers; they do not constitute findings by the majority.

## Holding, disposition, and source limits

The amended panel held that neither economic duress nor undue influence made these agreements unenforceable. It reversed and remanded specifically for the district court to decide whether the asserted claims fell within the agreements' scope. The court did not order a final disposition of the labor claims or itself settle scope. Slip op. 26.
