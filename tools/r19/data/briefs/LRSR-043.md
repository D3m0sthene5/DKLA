Immigration and Naturalization Service v. Chadha
Citation
Immigration and Naturalization Service v. Chadha, 462 U.S. 919 (1983). Supreme Court of the United
States, argued February 22, 1982, reargued December 7, 1982, decided June 23, 1983. Chief Justice Burger
wrote the opinion of the Court, joined by Justices Brennan, Marshall, Blackmun, Stevens, and O'Connor.
Justice Powell concurred in the judgment on a narrower ground. Justice White dissented. Justice Rehnquist
filed a separate dissent, joined by White, addressing severability. The result was 7-2; Burger's full reasoning
had six votes. The INS's appeal, No. 80-1832, was considered with the House's and Senate's petitions, Nos.
80-2170 and 80-2171. Full judicial opinion, including all separate opinions.
The case concerns a one-House legislative veto of an executive decision suspending an individual's
deportation. Statutory section numbers and descriptions of the immigration procedure below refer to the
provisions before the Court in 1983. Pinpoint references use the published U.S. Reports pagination.
Parties
Jagdish Rai Chadha was the person subject to deportation, the petitioner for review in the Ninth Circuit, and
an appellee or respondent in the Supreme Court proceedings. He challenged the constitutionality of the
House resolution that overturned the suspension of his deportation and sought to prevent the INS from
deporting him on that basis. 462 U.S. at 923-29.
The Immigration and Naturalization Service administered the immigration statute on behalf of the Attorney
General. Although the INS was the appellant in the principal Supreme Court case, it agreed with Chadha that
the legislative veto was unconstitutional. It nevertheless had entered and would enforce the deportation order
unless a court prevented it. The House of Representatives and the Senate defended the statutory veto
mechanism; they participated in the appellate litigation and separately sought Supreme Court review. This
alignment matters to both the procedural history and the Court's analysis of a genuine case or controversy. Id.
at 928-31, 939-40.
Material Facts
Chadha was of East Indian origin, was born in Kenya, and held a British passport. He lawfully entered the
United States in 1966 on a nonimmigrant student visa. His authorized stay expired on June 30, 1972, but he
remained. On October 11, 1973, the INS ordered him to show cause why he should not be deported for
overstaying. At a January 11, 1974, hearing, he conceded deportability and sought suspension of deportation
under § 244(a)(1) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, then codified at 8 U.S.C. § 1254(a)(1). Id. at
923-24.
Section 244(a)(1) authorized the Attorney General, in his discretion, to suspend deportation of an otherwise
deportable person who satisfied specified conditions. The person had to have been continuously physically
present in the United States for at least seven years, establish good moral character during the relevant period,
and show that deportation would result in extreme hardship to that person or to a spouse, parent, or child who
was a United States citizen or lawful permanent resident. Satisfying the statutory eligibility criteria permitted
the exercise of discretion; it did not by itself compel relief. Id. at 924-25 & n.1.
After proceedings resumed on February 7, 1974, an Immigration Judge considered Chadha's application,
including evidence bearing on his character and hardship. On June 25, 1974, the judge ordered his
deportation suspended. Section 244(c)(1) required the Attorney General to report each suspension to
Congress, including the pertinent facts, law, and reasons. The suspension was therefore part of a statutory

Section 244(c)(2) provided the challenged veto. During the congressional session when a suspension was
reported, or before the close of the next session, either the House or the Senate could adopt a resolution
stating that it did not favor the suspension. If either chamber did so, the Attorney General had to deport the
person or permit voluntary departure under the deportation order. If neither chamber acted within the
prescribed period, the Attorney General was to cancel the deportation proceedings. The suspension was thus
not yet a completed grant of permanent resident status when Congress considered Chadha's case. Id. at
925-26.
The suspension remained outstanding for approximately a year and a half. Congress's opportunity to veto it
ended with the close of the first session of the 94th Congress on December 19, 1975. On December 12,
Representative Eilberg, chair of the relevant House Judiciary subcommittee, introduced House Resolution
926, opposing permanent residence for six named individuals, including Chadha. The committee had
reviewed 340 suspension cases and concluded that these six did not satisfy the statutory requirements,
particularly the hardship requirement. Id. at 926-27.
On December 16, 1975, the resolution was discharged from further committee consideration and brought
before the House. It had not been printed or made available to other members before or at the vote. The
record disclosed no floor debate or recorded vote. The House adopted it on the basis of the committee's
recommendation. The resolution was not submitted to the Senate and was not presented to the President.
Those omissions were consistent with the statute's one-House procedure and became the basis of the
constitutional challenge. Id. at 926-28.
The House resolution changed the agency's course. The Immigration Judge reopened the proceeding to
implement it, although Chadha objected that § 244(c)(2) was unconstitutional. The judge concluded that he
lacked authority to invalidate an act of Congress and ordered Chadha deported on November 8, 1976. The
constitutional dispute thus arose from an operative deportation order against an identified individual, rather
than from an abstract disagreement between Congress and the Executive. Id. at 928.
Procedural History
Chadha appealed the deportation order to the Board of Immigration Appeals and renewed his constitutional
challenge. The Board dismissed the appeal because it likewise considered itself without power to declare an
act of Congress unconstitutional. Chadha then petitioned the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth
Circuit under § 106(a) of the Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1105a(a), which provided for review of final deportation orders.
There was no intervening federal district-court merits judgment. Id. at 928.
Before the Ninth Circuit, the INS agreed that the one-House veto was unconstitutional. The court invited the
House and Senate to submit briefs as amici curiae, meaning participants assisting the court without initially
occupying the same position as ordinary parties. The Ninth Circuit held that the House lacked constitutional
authority to require Chadha's deportation through this mechanism. It directed the Attorney General to refrain
from deporting Chadha on the basis of the House resolution. Its decision is reported at 634 F.2d 408 (9th Cir.
1980). Id. at 928-29.
The INS appealed to the Supreme Court under 28 U.S.C. § 1252, the then-applicable provision allowing
appeals in specified cases holding a federal statute unconstitutional. Both chambers disputed whether the INS
could appeal after prevailing on its legal argument. The House and Senate also sought review through their
own petitions for certiorari. The Court granted those petitions, postponed consideration of jurisdiction over

Before reaching the merits, the Court addressed appellate jurisdiction, severability, Chadha's standing,

Article III adverseness, and the political-question objection. These threshold matters occupied a substantial
portion of the opinion because the INS agreed with Chadha on the constitutional issue while remaining the
agency responsible for executing the challenged order. Id. at 929-44.
Issue
May Congress authorize either chamber, acting alone and without presenting its action to the President, to
overturn the Attorney General's suspension of an individual's deportation and thereby require deportation? Is
the House's action legislative in character, so that Article I requires passage by both chambers and
presentment, or may it operate as a reserved condition on the authority Congress previously delegated?
If the veto mechanism is unconstitutional, can it be severed from the suspension authority, leaving the
Attorney General's statutory power and the rest of the process in operation? Do the parties' alignment and the
availability of other possible immigration relief prevent the courts from deciding the constitutional question?
Rule and Holding
When Congress takes action that is legislative in purpose and effect, it must follow Article I's requirements of
bicameral passage and presentment to the President, unless the Constitution itself authorizes a different
procedure. The House's resolution was legislative because it changed the legal rights, duties, and relations of
Chadha and executive officials outside the Legislative Branch. An ordinary statute cannot authorize a
chamber to bypass the constitutional procedure for that later action. 462 U.S. at 944-59.
Section 244(c)(2)'s one-House veto was therefore unconstitutional. Congress could alter or revoke the
authority it had delegated, but it had to do so through constitutionally prescribed legislative action. The Court
severed the veto mechanism from the remaining suspension provisions, relying on the Act's severability
clause, statutory history, and the continuing workability of the remaining scheme. Id. at 931-35, 954-59.
The Court also held that judicial review was available. Chadha faced a concrete injury redressable by
invalidating the veto; possible alternative relief was speculative; the final deportation order depended on the
veto's validity; and the INS would enforce the order absent judicial relief. Congress's substantial authority
over immigration did not make the constitutionality of its chosen procedure a political question beyond
judicial review. Id. at 929-44.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the Ninth Circuit. The one-House veto was invalid and severable, so the order
preventing deportation on the basis of the House resolution remained effective. The decision preserved the
statutory suspension mechanism without the unconstitutional veto. It did not itself naturalize Chadha or
adjudicate every possible independent basis for immigration action against him. Id. at 934-35, 959.

Reasoning
The Court first explained why the INS could appeal despite agreeing with Chadha's constitutional argument.
The statutory requirements of § 1252 were satisfied: a federal court had held an act of Congress
unconstitutional in a civil proceeding to which a federal agency was a party. The INS was also sufficiently
aggrieved by the judgment because the judgment prevented it from carrying out a deportation order it
otherwise would execute. Its legal agreement with the Ninth Circuit did not erase the coercive effect of the
court's order on the agency. Id. at 929-31.
Severability was closely connected to whether Chadha could obtain effective relief. If invalidation of the veto
also destroyed the Attorney General's suspension authority, his constitutional victory might not produce the
relief he sought. Section 406 of the Act expressly provided that invalidity of a particular provision would not
affect the remainder. The Court treated that clause as creating a presumption that Congress did not intend the
whole scheme to depend on the veto's validity. The relevant question was whether Congress would have
wanted the valid remainder to operate without the invalid provision. Id. at 931-34.
The legislative history did not overcome that presumption. Congress had turned to administrative suspension
after experiencing the burden of providing individualized immigration relief through private bills. Although
Congress also wanted oversight, the Court found insufficient evidence that it would have chosen to resume
the full burden of private legislation rather than allow suspension without a one-House veto. The scheme
remained workable: the Attorney General could decide suspension applications, report grants to Congress,
and cancel proceedings after the statutory waiting period absent constitutionally enacted legislation to the
contrary. Reporting and the opportunity for ordinary legislation preserved a form of congressional oversight.
Id. at 932-35.
Chadha had standing because the deportation order threatened a concrete injury and a favorable decision
would remove the House resolution as the basis for it. The fact that his position also benefited the Executive
in an institutional dispute did not make his personal injury less real. An individual may challenge a structural
constitutional violation when that violation produces a judicially redressable injury to the individual. The
Court did not require the constitutional rule to protect only Chadha's private interests. Id. at 935-36.
The possibility of alternative immigration relief did not justify refusing to decide the case. Chadha had
married a United States citizen in August 1980, and the Refugee Act of 1980 potentially supplied another
avenue. But neither possibility established that he would receive relief. A person facing deportation could not
be denied review of the procedure producing the order on speculation that another application might
eventually succeed. The Court treated these arguments as reasons urged for avoiding the constitutional
question, and rejected them on the actual uncertainty of the proposed alternatives. Id. at 936-37.
The Ninth Circuit's statutory jurisdiction extended to this constitutional issue even though the House's vote
occurred outside the administrative hearing. The final order was entered to implement that vote; the validity
of the final order therefore depended on the validity of the veto. The grant of review over final deportation
orders encompassed matters on which those orders depended, rather than only questions the agency itself was
competent to decide during the hearing. Chadha directly attacked the order and sought relief inconsistent with
its execution. Id. at 937-39.
The dispute also satisfied Article III's case-or-controversy requirement. The INS would deport Chadha if the
challenged provision were upheld and would refrain if the judgment invalidating it stood. That gave the
court's decision concrete consequences notwithstanding the parties' agreement on legal doctrine. Congress's
participation supplied an active defense of the statute and removed any concern that the constitutional issue

lacked adversarial presentation. The Court distinguished the existence of a real controversy from the separate
practical interest in hearing fully developed arguments on both sides. Id. at 939-40.
The political-question objection failed because Congress's substantive authority over immigration did not
resolve the constitutionality of the means it used. Article I supplied judicially manageable standards for
evaluating enactment procedures. Accepting the claim that Congress's power over a subject made every
constitutional challenge to its legislation nonjusticiable would largely eliminate judicial review of federal
statutes. The Court therefore examined the method by which the House exercised power while leaving
Congress's broad substantive immigration authority intact. Id. at 940-44.
On the merits, the Court began with Article I, § 1 and § 7. Legislative power is vested in a Congress
consisting of a House and Senate. Bills must pass both chambers and be presented to the President, subject to
the Constitution's provisions governing approval, veto, and override. Section 7, clause 3 extends the
presentment requirement to qualifying orders, resolutions, and votes, preventing Congress from evading it
merely by changing the name of its action. Calling the measure a resolution therefore did not answer the
constitutional question. Id. at 944-46.
The Court explained the purposes of presentment and bicameralism through the constitutional design.
Presentment allows the President to protect the Executive from legislative encroachment and supplies an
additional check against improvident or oppressive measures. Bicameralism requires concurrence by
legislative bodies with different representative structures, encouraging deliberation and preventing one
chamber from exercising the entire legislative power. Those requirements protect individual liberty by
dispersing authority. The Court treated the prescribed procedure as binding even when a simpler arrangement
would be efficient or attractive to the political branches. Id. at 946-51.
The House resolution fell within those requirements because of its legal operation. It overruled the Attorney
General's suspension decision and imposed an obligation to deport Chadha. It thereby altered the legal rights,
duties, and relations of people and officials outside the Legislative Branch. Its individual focus did not
prevent it from being legislative for this purpose. The relevant inquiry examined the authority asserted and
the change the House purported to accomplish, rather than whether the measure had the appearance of a
generally applicable statute. Id. at 951-54.
The action it replaced confirmed its legislative character. Without the statutory veto provision, Congress
could seek to require a different outcome, if constitutionally permissible, only through legislation. A repeal or
amendment of an existing statutory authorization must follow Article I just as an original enactment must.
The original passage of § 244 did not supply advance compliance for every later congressional decision taken
under it. Congress's choice to delegate discretion and its later choice to override the resulting decision were
distinct exercises of power, each requiring the constitutional procedure applicable to that exercise. Id. at
953-55.
The Court also explained why this conclusion did not subject every executive implementation decision to
bicameralism and presentment. The Attorney General acted under a law already enacted through Article I and
remained confined by its substantive limits. Administrative action could be reviewed for conformity with that
law. Congress, by contrast, was seeking to exercise its own power to alter the result without passing a new
law. Once Congress delegated the suspension authority, it had to respect that allocation until it legislatively
altered or revoked it. Id. at 953-55 & n.16.
Express constitutional exceptions reinforced the rule. The Court identified the House's impeachment power,
the Senate's impeachment-trial power, and the Senate's advice-and-consent roles concerning appointments
and treaties as instances in which the Constitution specifically authorizes one chamber to act in a legally

The broad historical use of legislative vetoes did not save the device. Congress and Presidents had

part of the chosen structure of government, including when their observance made oversight less convenient.
Id. at 944-45, 958-59.
Separate Opinions
Justice Powell agreed that the House's action was unconstitutional but would have decided the case on a
narrower ground. The majority's reasoning threatened legislative veto provisions across many fields and
forms. Powell considered it unnecessary to reach that broad question because this particular veto represented
Congress's assumption of a function ordinarily assigned to courts or administrative tribunals: deciding
whether identified individuals satisfied existing legal criteria. Id. at 959-64 (Powell, J., concurring in the
judgment).
Powell focused on the House's stated reason for acting. The committee concluded that Chadha and five other
people did not meet the existing statutory hardship requirements. The House thus reviewed the application of
established law to particular people. Whether characterized as an original adjudication or appellate review of
the agency's decision, the action resembled adjudication rather than the prescription of a new general rule.
Powell regarded that exercise of power as an improper legislative intrusion into an adjudicative function. Id.
at 963-66.
The procedure exposed the individuals to precisely the danger the separation of powers was intended to
prevent. The House did not provide the ordinary protections associated with adjudication, including a
hearing, representation, and decision by an impartial tribunal subject to established rules. Political
accountability offers greater protection when Congress makes general policy than when a shifting legislative
majority disposes of the rights of a few identified people. Powell connected this concern to the broader
principle reflected in the Bill of Attainder Clause, while grounding his conclusion in separation of powers
rather than treating the resolution as a separately established bill-of-attainder violation. Id. at 960-67.
The majority disputed Powell's characterization and relied instead on Article I's legislative procedure. In
particular, it noted difficulties with describing the House as exercising appellate judicial review when the
favorable suspension decision had no ordinary aggrieved party seeking judicial reversal. Powell answered
that the relevant point was the nature and danger of the function the House assumed, whether or not this
particular administrative grant would otherwise have been appealed. His rationale therefore should be
distinguished from the Court's binding bicameralism-and-presentment rationale. Id. at 957 n.22, 965-66 &
n.8.
Justice White defended the legislative veto as an important accommodation between broad administrative
delegation and congressional responsibility. Congress could not realistically enact every administrative detail
itself, but it might be unwilling to transfer wide discretion without retaining a means to review its exercise.
The veto often formed part of the bargain under which Congress granted authority the Executive wanted.
Eliminating that mechanism could make delegation harder or leave agencies with discretion Congress had

White criticized the majority's broad definition of legislative action. Agency rules and adjudications also
change legal rights and obligations, often with major practical consequences, yet they need not themselves
pass both chambers and receive the President's signature. Existing doctrine permitted those acts because
Congress had enacted a statute authorizing them. White reasoned that the same initial enactment could
authorize a retained legislative check within the scope of the delegation. He found it difficult to justify
allowing agencies extensive legally binding discretion while prohibiting Congress from reserving a limited
power to prevent its exercise. Id. at 984-90.
White also identified a different baseline for deciding whether the veto changed the legal status quo. Under
the complete statute, the Attorney General's suspension was conditional. A permanent change in Chadha's
status required executive approval of suspension and the absence of an objection from either chamber during
the review period. In that arrangement, White saw functional agreement among the Executive, House, and
Senate before the change became final. A veto preserved the original deportable status, much as failure of a
private relief bill would do. Id. at 990-98.
This argument directly contested the majority's account of the legal effect of the House vote. The majority
treated the House as reversing an executive decision made under a valid statutory delegation, an alteration
requiring legislation. White treated the veto as the exercise of a condition built into the original grant, which
had itself been enacted through Article I. He considered the statute's combination of executive action and
legislative nonobjection consistent with the distribution of authority that bicameralism and presentment
protect. The majority required compliance with the specified constitutional procedure rather than this
asserted functional equivalent. Id. at 952-55, 990-98.
White further resisted the idea that the device necessarily aggrandized Congress at the Executive's expense.
Congress had used the veto as a reservation accompanying authority it otherwise might not grant, and the
check could operate within the limits of that grant. He favored examination of the particular arrangement's
operation and effects on the branches over a categorical rule invalidating the wider class of legislative vetoes.
His opinion included an extensive appendix describing statutory uses of the device, illustrating his concern
about the sweep of the decision. Id. at 974-84, 998-1013.
Justice Rehnquist, joined by White, dissented on severability. He accepted that a severability clause creates a
presumption, but considered Congress's actual design decisive. Congress had repeatedly refused to give the
Executive unconditional authority to grant this form of relief. The veto was an integral limitation on the
suspension power, and removing it expanded that power beyond what Congress had agreed to enact. A
general severability clause did not conclusively establish that Congress would have wanted this particular
delegation to operate without its accompanying check. Id. at 1013-16 (Rehnquist, J., dissenting).
Rehnquist accordingly would have reversed rather than preserved the suspension authority through
severance. His objection exposes the practical importance of the remedy: invalidating the veto while
retaining the delegation leaves the Executive with broader freedom from congressional control than the
enacted arrangement provided; invalidating the connected authorization can eliminate the relief Congress
made available. The majority and Rehnquist disagreed about which result Congress would have preferred if
the veto proved unconstitutional. White also endorsed this severability objection, in addition to his
disagreement on the merits. Id. at 1012-16.

Notes
The sequence of these cases illuminates two different constitutional limits. Whitman and Gundy concern what
choices Congress may assign to an administrator and how clearly Congress must constrain them. Chadha
concerns how Congress may intervene after granting authority. A delegation's validity does not establish that
Congress may retain a one-House mechanism for changing the resulting legal outcomes. The source of the
administrator's power and the procedure for later congressional action must each be examined. Whitman, 531
U.S. at 472-76; Gundy, Kagan plurality, slip op. at 4-6; Chadha, 462 U.S. at 953-56.
The actual holding invalidated a one-House resolution with binding legal consequences outside Congress.
The majority's reasoning also explains why adding a second chamber, while still omitting presentment,
would not satisfy Article I for legislative action. Conversely, a chamber's expression of disagreement without
binding external legal effect does not become the same device simply because it criticizes an agency
decision. The relevant questions are what the action legally does and what constitutional procedure governs
that exercise of power. 462 U.S. at 945-46, 952-56 & nn.20-21.
Severability must be analyzed separately from the constitutional merits. The Court's conclusion that the veto
violated Article I did not itself answer whether the suspension authority survived. The majority relied on the
express clause, congressional objectives, and the ability of the remainder to function. Rehnquist relied on the
centrality of the veto to Congress's willingness to delegate. The constitutional defect and the legislative-intent
inquiry led to distinct questions about the proper judgment and Chadha's practical relief. Id. at 931-35,
1013-16.
The different opinions also locate the protection of liberty in different institutional features. Burger
emphasized compliance with the Constitution's lawmaking procedure. Powell emphasized impartial
adjudication of individual rights. White emphasized preservation of a workable balance between delegation
and congressional control. These are the competing explanations actually advanced in the case. Powell
agreed with the result on his narrower ground, while White would have upheld the veto and also opposed its
severance. Id. at 946-59, 959-74, 984-1016.
