# Grease Monkey International, Inc. v. Godat
916 S.W.2d 257 (Mo. Ct. App. 1995) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Judge Pudlowski wrote the Missouri Court of Appeals, Eastern District, Division Four opinion dated December 5, 1995; Presiding Judge Ahrens and Judge Grimm concurred. Grease Monkey appealed denial of specific performance of a real-estate sale and an award of the buyer’s litigation expenses secured by a property lien. The court affirmed denial of specific performance and the fee award, but reversed the lien. It additionally awarded buyer Godat $9,798.71 for the appeal. Thus divisibility preserved the seller’s personal fee liability without establishing liability of a separate corporate property owner. (257, 259, 263.)

## Financing condition and unsuccessful extensions
The April 1992 agreement concerned property on Halls Ferry Road in St. Louis County. It made the sale contingent on a financing commitment by a specified deadline, otherwise declaring the contract null and void and requiring return of the deposit. The buyer could obtain conventional financing on terms satisfactory to him in his sole discretion. A separate clause entitled the prevailing party to litigation costs, including reasonable attorney’s fees, in any litigation between the parties. (259.)

Two extensions followed. The June 30 agreement preserved the financing condition while waiving the buyer’s other conditions, made the deposit nonrefundable, set July 9 for the financing commitment and July 15 for closing, and identified Boatman’s Bank as lender. After that bank denied the loan, Godat applied to Commerce Bank. On July 9 a Commerce vice president sent the seller’s agent a letter saying the bank approved a loan. He testified, however, that the letter was not a commitment: a commitment required an appraisal, which would affect the amount available. The buyer’s agent warned that a commitment could not be obtained by the deadline, and the seller’s own agent acknowledged that the letter was not customary commitment documentation. (259–260.)

Grease Monkey wrote that it regarded the letter as satisfying the condition; the buyer did not reply and continued pursuing financing. When closing did not occur, the seller offered an extension only on new terms: acknowledgment that the letter satisfied the contingency, another $5,000 nonrefundable deposit, and an increased purchase price. Godat refused. Grease Monkey sued for specific performance on September 14. The trial court found the financing condition unfulfilled and also found that Grease Monkey had not demonstrated an ownership interest sufficient to convey title. It awarded the buyer approximately $27,000 in litigation costs under the fee clause. (259–260.)

## Evidence supported failure of the condition
The appellate court reviewed the bench-trial judgment under the stated Murphy v. Carron standard and deferred to the trial court’s credibility determinations. The buyer’s uncertainty over the loan amount, the bank officer’s explanation, and the seller’s agent’s testimony supported the finding that no commitment had been obtained. The seller’s own later conduct strengthened it: an August 24 letter accused the buyer of failing the financing provision, while the seller’s demand for different terms suggested it did not regard the original agreement as still valid. The court treated that letter as an admission about nonfulfillment, not as acceptance of its legal assertion that the buyer was in default. (260–261.)

The seller also argued that the closing deadline existed for its benefit and had been waived. Its correspondence contradicted that position and sought a new bargain, so the court did not resolve the abstract beneficiary-of-condition question. Nor had the trial court refused performance because the contract was inherently uncertain or indefinite; it had found the expressly required contingency unsatisfied. The appellate court rejected an attack on the trial court’s adoption of the buyer’s proposed findings because the findings were consistent with the facts and specific enough for meaningful review. Adoption of a party’s draft was not itself reversible error. (260–261.)

## The litigation promise was independent of the sale
Failure of the sales condition did not erase the separate promise to pay litigation expenses. Divisibility depended primarily on intent, assessed through the agreement’s language and subject matter. Distinct promises capable of separate execution could operate as independent agreements even when made together. Performance of the sale did not depend on the litigation clause, and the promise concerning “any litigation” did not condition reimbursement on a favorable legal conclusion that the sale remained enforceable. The court therefore enforced the independent fee agreement while treating the sale agreement as void under its financing contingency. (261–262.)

The buyer’s failure to file a formal counterclaim did not defeat fees in this case. He had requested them in his written prayer and orally at trial, and had admitted the contract containing the clause. The seller itself sought fees under the same provision, supplying ample notice of the basis for the request and an opportunity to respond. The court relied on the procedural rule permitting oral motions with adequate notice and opportunity to be heard; it did not announce that unnotified fee requests invariably succeed. (261.)

## The lien improperly reached a separate corporation
The recorded owner was Grease Monkey Income Limited Partnership, 1987-1. The seller asserted that those assets had been rolled into Grease Monkey Holding Company, its parent, and claimed ownership through its status as a wholly owned subsidiary with the same officers and directors. Those assertions did not eliminate corporate separateness. A parent was not ordinarily liable for its subsidiary’s acts; treating them as one required evidence of the domination and control necessary to pierce the veil, not a presumption from ownership or shared management. (260, 262.)

The parent was not even before the court. A lien on its alleged property effectively imposed the subsidiary’s debt on it without an opportunity to be heard and without showing that its shareholders’ interests were represented. The court therefore reversed that lien while leaving the fee obligation against the litigating seller intact. (262–263.)

## Specific performance and final judgment
A party seeking specific performance had to remain ready, willing, and able to perform. The buyer’s willingness to complete the original transaction did not establish the seller’s entitlement. Grease Monkey refused an extension and tried to obtain a better deal, then sued on the old terms only when the buyer rejected the new proposal. The court regarded an order of specific performance in those circumstances as inequitable. Its final judgment affirmed in part, reversed only the lien, and granted the buyer’s appellate-fee motion for $9,798.71. (262–263.)

It is important to retain that partial reversal rather than describing the judgment as an unqualified affirmance.
