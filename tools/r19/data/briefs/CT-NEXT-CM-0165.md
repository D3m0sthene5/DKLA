# Stees v. Leonard
20 Minn. 494, 20 Gil. 448 (Minn. Apr. 1874) | Contracts

## Facts and the bargain
John A. and Washington M. Stees engaged Charles Leonard and Monroe and Romaine Sheire to erect and complete a three-story business building on their St. Paul property. The August 18, 1868 agreement was under seal, incorporated detailed plans and specifications prepared by M. Sheire & Bro., and set a $6,735 price payable in installments as construction progressed. Except for painting, completion was due January 1, 1869. The specifications required foundation walls two feet thick with specified projecting footings; they did not otherwise prescribe the foundation’s character. The builders undertook to complete the building, rather than merely supply specified labor. (20 Minn. 495–497.)

The first building reached three stories and collapsed November 1, 1868. The builders tried again in 1869; the second structure likewise collapsed, on August 1. They then abandoned construction. They maintained that their materials and workmanship complied with the specifications and that concealed quicksand, which became unable to support the structure when saturated, caused both failures. The owners instead alleged negligent construction and inferior materials. Their demand was $5,214.80 plus interest: $3,745.80 previously paid, $1,000 for loss of use, and $469 for damage to an adjoining house and property inside it. This is the pleaded demand, not a separately identified amount of the jury’s verdict. (Id. 496–499.)

## Litigation and competing positions
A Ramsey County jury found for the owners. The builders moved for a new trial on a bill of exceptions and appealed the denial. Their objections concerned exclusion of evidence supporting several related defenses: that specification compliance discharged their duty; that the owners selected the site and therefore owed a suitable foundation; that the owners promised before execution to drain the land if necessary; and that later promises and drainage activity excused the builders from doing that work themselves. (Id. 499–503.)

The builders argued that supplying adequate soil was analogous to the owner’s duty to supply adequate materials, and that the foundation specifications fixed the extent of their undertaking. They characterized the prior drainage promise as collateral to the written contract. For the subsequent promises, they relied on consideration, modification by performance, estoppel, and liability for negligently performing even a gratuitous undertaking. The owners objected that the evidence contradicted the written bargain or the defenses actually pleaded. The appeal therefore involved both substantive allocation of construction risk and the procedural limits on the evidence offered to shift it.

## Issues, rules, and holdings
The court held that latent soil difficulties did not excuse an absolute promise to erect and complete an otherwise possible building. Its historical formulation required performance of a positive express undertaking despite unforeseen hindrance or hardship, subject to prevention by an act of God, the law, or the other contracting party; difficulty short of absolute impossibility was insufficient. The contractor could qualify the undertaking in the agreement. The court’s examples also show that the phrase “act of God” cannot be treated as a categorical excuse for every destructive natural event: an unfinished building’s destruction could still leave rebuilding possible and within the assumed duty. (Id. 503–506.)

Compliance with the stated footings did not complete the broader contractual obligation. Stronger foundations or drainage, if necessary to erect the building, remained the builders’ responsibility. The prior oral drainage promise concerned that very obligation and could not vary the later written contract. The later offers of proof were also properly rejected, because the facts offered were not the defense pleaded. (Id. 507–511.)

## Reasoning
The court used construction cases involving accidental fire, lightning, wind, and concealed soft soil to establish the distinction between destroying work already performed and rendering completion impossible. The parties had allocated the task of delivering a finished building to the builders. Unexpected expense or the need to redo work did not transfer that task to the owners. Stees’s specifications accordingly identified features of the promised result without eliminating the builders’ duty to take other measures necessary for completion. (Id. 504–508.)

The site-selection argument failed on the pleadings. Although the contract described the location imperfectly, the complaint identified the owners’ parcel, and the answer expressly admitted that the builders contracted to construct on that parcel and began performance there. Their admissions removed the factual premise that the owners could later choose some different, unsuitable site. The drainage subject was equally integral: because drainage was necessary to complete construction, a prior oral promise allocating it differently was not a collateral matter omitted from the bargain. (Id. 506–508.)

The omitted latter portion of the assigned opinion supplies an important qualification. The court recognized an American exception under which subsequent oral arrangements could modify a sealed contract if executed or acted upon so that enforcing the original undertaking would be inequitable. It did not decide whether the offered drainage arrangement had consideration or whether reliance or performance could create an estoppel. Those questions were unnecessary because of the mismatch between pleading and proof. (Id. 508–509.)

The answer alleged that the owners promised to drain but wholly failed to do so. It alleged no consideration or performance supporting an estoppel; a naked promise alone did not release the builders’ obligation. At trial, however, the builders offered a materially different account: the owners initially did drain the land, the builders consequently refrained from drainage, and the owners later stopped immediately before collapse. That theory might have supplied a defense if properly pleaded—the court expressly declined to rule it insufficient—but it contradicted the allegation of total nonperformance. Each offer was tendered as a whole, so the inclusion of inadmissible material permitted rejection of the whole offer. The objection that the evidence was incompetent, irrelevant, and immaterial was sufficiently specific in this setting, because defense counsel knew the answer’s contents. (Id. 509–511.)

## Judgment, authorship, and limits
Justice Young delivered the court’s opinion. The order denying a new trial was affirmed; the owners’ jury result therefore remained in place. The preserved report contains no separate judicial writing, numbered judicial footnote, or express tally of participating justices. The case should not be described as deciding that every subsequent oral construction modification is ineffective: its final ground was the defense’s pleading defect.

The assigned principal passage occupies textbook PDF 66–69, printed 1074–1077, and ends with affirmance before the Notes on the final page. The editors abridge the subsequent-promise analysis into a bracketed account; the full report restores the pleading-versus-proof distinction and the reserved question of a potentially sufficient defense. The surrounding discussion places Stees in the mistake chapter, but the opinion itself applies absolute undertaking and evidence rules rather than a developed modern mutual-mistake test. Later design-specification doctrine discussed in the textbook Notes is separate editorial material and is not this 1874 holding.
