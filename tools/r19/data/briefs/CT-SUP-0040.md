# TeeVee Toons, Inc. v. Gerhard Schubert GmbH
2006 WL 2463537 (S.D.N.Y.), No. 00 Civ. 5189 (RCC) | Contracts | CISG interpretation, merger clauses, and partial summary judgment

## The failed Biobox manufacturing system

Steve Gottlieb, founder and president of record company TeeVee Toons (TVT), invented and patented a biodegradable cardboard package for audio and video cassettes called the Biobox. In 1994 he sought a company capable of developing a mass-production system. He chose German manufacturer Gerhard Schubert GmbH, which marketed in the United States through its exclusive agent, Rodico. TVT and Schubert entered into a written quotation contract in February 1995. Delays extended the project by nearly two years. When the completed system arrived at TVT's contracted production facility, Cinram in Richmond, Indiana, in late August 1997, serious speed and quality failures persisted. TVT began notifying Schubert of those defects by October 1997. (Full opinion §§ I, II.C.2.a.1; assigned PDF pp. 40–42.)

TVT and its affiliate Steve Gottlieb, Inc. (SGI), sued in July 2000. Their claims sought recovery for the machine, repair and support expenses, facility costs, project administration, and lost profits, alongside common-law fraud and negligence. A prior motion to dismiss was denied in 2002. This memorandum and order decided Schubert's later summary-judgment motion; it did not review a verdict after trial. Judge Richard Casey granted the motion in part and denied it in part. (Full opinion §§ I–II.)

## The disputed fine print and the CISG framework

The United States and Germany were CISG Contracting States, and the parties' places of business were in those countries. The court therefore applied the Convention to the sale under Article 1(1)(a). None of the enumerated exclusions applied. Although design and installation involved services, manufacturing the system was the preponderant obligation; Article 3 did not remove the transaction from the Convention. Neither party had expressly opted out. The court separately applied New York law to the common-law tort claims. (Full opinion §§ II.C.1, II.D.1.)

TVT invoked Article 35's protections for ordinary and particular purposes and conformity with a sample or model, and Article 36's guarantee provisions. The record left no genuine dispute that the machine failed to produce at the proper speed and quality. Notification after delivery was reasonable under Article 39 on these manufacturing facts; the court's footnote distinguished foreign cases finding much shorter notice periods unreasonable. Those conclusions did not themselves establish TVT's final right to recovery because the contract might have varied the Convention's protections. (Full opinion §§ II.C.2–3; assigned PDF pp. 41–42.)

The attached Terms and Conditions limited warranties and remedies. They included a six-month defective-materials/workmanship replacement warranty, excluded other express or implied warranties, disclaimed merchantability and fitness, and asserted that the quotation and incorporated writings exhausted the agreement. Schubert argued that these provisions defeated or limited TVT's claims, including a cap at the quoted $862,160 machinery price. TVT responded that, during negotiations between the February 3 quotation and February 13 acceptance, Rodico's agent Neuber expressly told Gottlieb that the fine print did not apply to this particular deal. Neuber admitted telling TVT not to worry about or overemphasize it but denied saying to ignore it. (Assigned PDF pp. 42–45, including nn. 2–4.)

## Why the merger clause did not end the inquiry

Article 11 permits proof of a sales contract without a writing. Article 8 requires interpretation according to a party's intent when the other knew or could not have been unaware of it; otherwise the standard is the understanding of a reasonable person of the same kind in the circumstances. Article 8(3) directs attention to all relevant circumstances, including negotiations, practices, usages, and later conduct. The Convention consequently lacks the domestic parol-evidence exclusion on which a conclusive-writing argument would depend. The court had to consider negotiations even when they contradicted the written fine print. (Assigned PDF p. 42.)

The merger clause made the issue more difficult: could an oral understanding exclude boilerplate that itself said prior oral understandings were extinguished? The court regarded this specific interaction as apparently novel in American CISG cases. MCC-Marble supplied relevant Article 8 reasoning but had not resolved this merger-clause question. Judge Casey consulted CISG Advisory Council Opinion No. 3, which treated a merger clause's evidentiary effect as dependent on the parties' actual intention. The advisory material was interpretive assistance, rather than a judicial decision or a binding amendment to the Convention. (Assigned PDF p. 43 and n. 2.)

The court reasoned that the clause would be operative only if both parties shared the intention to be bound by it. TVT's evidence was not enough to conclusively establish exclusion, but it was enough to create a genuine question about whether Neuber could not have been unaware of Gottlieb's interpretation. At trial the factfinder would examine the agents' subjective intentions when TVT accepted the offer. The court therefore did not hold that oral statements automatically trump every CISG merger clause or that these disclaimers were conclusively unenforceable. It held that their incorporation could not be determined against TVT on this summary-judgment record. (Assigned PDF pp. 43–44.)

If the parties intended the writing and merger clause to govern, Article 6 allowed contractual variation and the disclaimers would undermine the CISG warranty claims. If they lacked the relevant shared intent, those provisions would drop out and Article 35 protections would remain available. The full opinion similarly explained that a valid disclaimer could defeat the pleaded Article 36 claim even though the contract contained a substantively similar replacement guarantee: TVT had sued on the Convention's warranty rather than the contractual warranty. (Assigned PDF p. 44; full opinion § II.C.3 and its explanatory footnote.)

## Full-opinion rulings beyond the assigned merger-clause excerpt

SGI was dismissed from the action for lack of standing. It had been incorporated in 1997, after the 1995 agreement and the relevant inducements. It was neither a contracting party nor an intended beneficiary known or contemplated by Schubert. It could not assert TVT's contract rights or claim reliance on statements made before its existence. Alleged later service contracts appeared neither in the complaint nor the supporting record. The court also declined a late request to substitute Gottlieb individually: that would not make him a party to the quotation contract, would reopen completed discovery, and was unnecessary given TVT's continued participation and his ownership of it. These were the court's historical standing rulings, not a claim that every contemporary third-party contract objection is jurisdictional. (Full opinion § II.B.)

For TVT, Articles 45 and 74 permitted damages for loss, including lost profits, limited by foreseeability at formation and computation with sufficient certainty. Mere fixed costs that would have been incurred with a working machine were not loss caused by breach. The court rejected recovery of Cinram facility/service expenses because the record supplied no adequate basis to compute relevant amounts. It also rejected costs of replacing the facility: the need was neither established nor shown foreseeable. Summary judgment was granted to Schubert on those two measures under all three CISG theories. (Full opinion §§ II.C.2.a.2.i, II.C.2.b, II.C.3.)

Four other measures survived: the machine price; labor and services attributable to defects; increased project-administration expense; and foreseeable lost profits from sales and licensing. The amount and allocation of service charges were disputed, including whether some invoices had been paid at all. Administrative estimates could permit sufficiently certain computation without mathematical precision. Evidence of actual customer orders supported at least some lost profits even though the orders themselves were not in the record; the court drew the favorable inference required on this motion. Surviving a damages challenge did not establish a fixed award, allow all projected profits, or eliminate the unresolved disclaimer issue. (Full opinion § II.C.2.a.2.ii.)

The fraud claim was not automatically duplicative of contract because TVT alleged precontract misrepresentations of present capabilities. It nevertheless failed for lack of justifiable reliance under New York law. TVT had retained a certified packaging consultant, Kelsey, who engaged in technical discussions, received specifications, and recommended the manufacturer. Its president's personal lack of packaging expertise did not excuse failure to use information accessible through that consultant. The negligence claim also failed: it sought economic loss from defective goods and contractual nonperformance without a sufficiently alleged duty independent of the contract. (Full opinion §§ II.D.2–3.)

## Exact order and consequence

Schubert obtained summary judgment against SGI on all claims, against TVT on fraud and negligence, and against TVT's CISG claims insofar as they sought Cinram facility/service or replacement-facility damages. Summary judgment was denied on TVT's remaining CISG claims and their four other damages measures. The court scheduled a conference to arrange trial. It neither granted TVT summary judgment nor awarded damages nor finally determined that the boilerplate was excluded. This is a single district judge's memorandum and order, with no separate opinion. (Full opinion § III.)
