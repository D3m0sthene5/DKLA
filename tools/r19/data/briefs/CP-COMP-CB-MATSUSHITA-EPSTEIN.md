# Matsushita Electric Industrial Co., Ltd. v. Epstein

**516 U.S. 367 (1996)** — Supreme Court of the United States, February 27, 1996; argued November 27, 1995; No. 94-1809. Justice Thomas wrote for seven Justices. Stevens joined Parts I, II–A, and II–C but neither Part II–B nor the judgment. Stevens and Ginsburg wrote separately; Stevens joined Ginsburg throughout, and Souter joined her Part II–B. Assigned: CivPro physical PDF page 690, printed 1372–1373, Note 2.

## Issue, posture, and result

Could a federal court refuse full faith and credit to a Delaware judgment approving a shareholder class settlement solely because its release included securities claims within exclusive federal jurisdiction? **No.** Section 1738 generally required the judgment to receive the effect it would have in Delaware. The Court concluded that Delaware would treat this settlement as preclusive and that section 27 of the Securities Exchange Act did not create an implied exception. It **reversed and remanded** the Ninth Circuit. This was a decision about the effect of a settlement judgment, rather than a ruling that Matsushita had or had not violated the securities rules. The Court expressly reserved the respondents' constitutional adequacy-of-representation challenge. (369–370 & n.1, 375–387, especially 379 n.5.)

## Transaction and parallel litigation

Matsushita's 1990 tender offer sought MCA, a Delaware corporation. Shareholders sued MCA's directors in Delaware for alleged fiduciary breaches concerning shareholder value. Their amended state complaint also alleged waste through exposure to federal securities liability and Matsushita's conspiracy and assistance in the fiduciary breaches. These were pleaded as state causes of action, including the waste claim premised on potential federal liability. (369–370, 382 n.7.)

Separately, shareholders sued Matsushita in a California federal court, alleging violations of SEC Rules 10b-13 and 14d-10 in the acquisition. The federal court denied class certification, granted Matsushita summary judgment, and dismissed the case on April 15, 1992. The plaintiffs appealed. Federal jurisdiction over the Exchange Act claims was exclusive under section 27, but that exclusivity did not deprive Delaware of jurisdiction over its pending state suit. (370–371, 381–382.)

The Chancery Court rejected an initial settlement in April 1991. It offered shareholders no money, proposed eliminating a poison pill with little practical benefit after the acquisition, sought substantial counsel fees, and lacked an opt-out right. Negotiations later produced a second settlement with a $2 million fund, a release encompassing the pending federal case, and an opportunity to opt out. After deductions, the fund would yield only a few cents per share. The court found the monetary benefit meager and barely adequate but approved it; it reduced the requested attorney fee to $250,000. Suspicion of collusion was reported, but collusion was not found proved. (371–372; Ginsburg, 390–393, 399 & n.10.)

Notice was mailed twice and published in the Wall Street Journal. The respondents did not opt out, object, or appear at the approval hearing. Delaware's Supreme Court affirmed the approval judgment. The federal appeal nevertheless continued, and the Ninth Circuit refused to treat the settlement as a bar: it limited state settlement preclusion to claims that could have been extinguished through issue preclusion from an adjudication of the state claims. That court also ruled on the federal securities claims. The Supreme Court reviewed the preclusion question; it did not resolve whether the securities rules supplied the asserted private rights of action. (372–374, 370 n.1; Ginsburg, 389–394.)

## Section 1738 and the two steps

A judgment incorporating a class settlement was the product of a judicial proceeding under section 1738. Settlement did not make the judgment categorically exempt. Nor did the exclusively federal character of released claims by itself supply an exemption. Federal courts could not replace the statute with their own preferred preclusion test. (373–375.)

Marrese supplied the sequence: **first determine the effect of the judgment under the rendering State's law; then, if that law would bar the action, ask whether the relevant federal statute creates an exception to section 1738.** Ordinary rules concerning claims that a court lacked power to hear still mattered at the state-law step, but the effect of a judicially approved settlement release required examination of Delaware's settlement law. (375–376.)

The Court read Delaware decisions, especially Nottingham Partners and the later Union Square arbitration case, to permit settlements releasing related claims that could not themselves have been adjudicated in the Chancery Court. The settlement could embrace claims sharing the factual predicate of the settled litigation. Delaware could not directly command dismissal of the federal lawsuit; its judgment could nonetheless operate as a collateral bar when the federal court applied section 1738. Here the release expressly identified the pending California action, and the respondents had remained in the settlement class. (376–379.)

This reasoning should not be reduced to a universal rule that any transaction-wide release binds every possible absent claimant. Ginsburg objected that the Court had blended Delaware's identical-factual-predicate standard with a broader same-transaction test. Her criticism was part of the disagreement over deciding Delaware law in the first instance. (395 n.4.)

## Exclusive federal adjudication did not prohibit a settlement release

The second step required an implied partial repeal of section 1738 because section 27 contained no express exception. The Court applied the demanding requirement of irreconcilable conflict between the federal statutes. Section 27 reserved adjudication of Exchange Act duties to federal courts, but it did not prohibit parties from releasing such claims in a state settlement of a suit properly within state jurisdiction. Evaluating the claims' potential value to assess settlement fairness did not amount to adjudicating their merits. (380–383.)

Uniform interpretation and federal expertise remained compatible with that distinction. The state court approved a settlement rather than authoritatively construing and enforcing securities liabilities. Other Exchange Act provisions contemplated overlapping state and federal remedies and prohibited double recovery, without displacing section 1738. Related precedents on state findings and arbitration also refuted an unconditional right to litigate every exclusively federal claim to judgment in a federal forum. (383–386.)

The jurisdictional exception was inapplicable because Delaware actually had jurisdiction over the underlying state suit and the parties. An exception for a court that lacked power over the action it entertained was different from the argument that Delaware could not adjudicate each released federal claim. Note 8 distinguished Kalb, where a federal statute prohibited the very state proceeding. (386–387 & n.8.)

## What remained open and the separate opinions

**The Court did not decide that the settlement satisfied due process.** Its note 5 acknowledged the inadequate-representation argument and declined to address it because it fell outside the question presented and was not the basis of the decision below. Note 6 separately reserved the respondents' pure-contract argument about failure to opt out, while explaining that section 1738 would require adherence to a State's contract-based approach if that was its law. (379–380 nn.5–6.)

Stevens agreed that section 27 did not impliedly repeal section 1738, but he wanted the lower federal court to decide Delaware preclusion law first. He also emphasized that the Ninth Circuit could consider whether adequacy of representation had been fully and fairly litigated in Delaware. (387.)

Ginsburg agreed that remand was appropriate but opposed the Court's first-instance resolution of Delaware law. Her Part II–B, joined by Stevens and Souter, stressed the continuing constitutional requirement of adequate representation of absent class members. Notice and an opt-out opportunity did not replace that requirement, and a rendering court's approval could not automatically determine every future court's recognition obligation. (388–389, 393–397.)

She described the respondents' arguments that the state representatives might not have tendered shares, might therefore lack the federal claims they released, and might have undervalued those claims for a modest fund coupled with substantial counsel fees. She also discussed the demand for explicit class findings and the contested burden of proof. **She did not find those allegations established.** Both the respondents' adequacy challenge and Matsushita's argument that adequacy had already been fully and fairly litigated remained for remand. Her final note also identified Delaware's Rule 60 counterpart as an avenue for seeking relief. (397–399 & nn.6–11.)

## Assignment comparison and sources

The textbook correctly identifies the settlement-release holding under section 1738. Its short description of Ginsburg's due-process concern should be read as a concern about **adequate representation in binding absent class members**, rather than an absolute entitlement to a separate federal adjudication. The majority reserved that constitutional question; it did not reject the challenge on its merits. The note omits Stevens's separate opinion and Souter's limited joinder.
