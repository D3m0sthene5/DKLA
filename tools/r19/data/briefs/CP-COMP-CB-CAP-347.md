# Castano v. American Tobacco Co.

**84 F.3d 734 (5th Cir. 1996) — United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, May 23, 1996.** Judge Jerry E. Smith wrote for Judges Smith, Duhé, and DeMoss; no separate opinion is reported. Civil Procedure: nationwide damages classes, predominance, superiority, state-law variation, and a proposed multistage trial. Assigned account: CivPro, physical PDF 429, printed 830. This brief addresses the 1996 interlocutory certification appeal, not subsequent tobacco settlements.

## The proposed class and allegations

The plaintiffs sued tobacco companies and the Tobacco Institute on a then-novel theory that nicotine addiction itself was a compensable injury. They alleged that defendants concealed nicotine's addictive nature and manipulated nicotine levels to sustain dependence. Their nationwide class encompassed nicotine-dependent people who had bought and smoked defendants' cigarettes, their estates and representatives, and relatives or significant others claiming as heirs or survivors. Claims extended back to 1943. Current smokers were among the representatives; Dianne Castano sued for her deceased husband. The court treated these as allegations, not proven concealment, manipulation, or causation. **737–738 & nn.1–3.**

The complaint asserted nine causes of action encompassing fraud, negligent misrepresentation, emotional-distress and negligence theories, consumer-protection laws, express and implied warranties, strict liability, and Louisiana redhibition. Plaintiffs sought compensatory and punitive damages, fees, and several forms of equitable relief, including notice about addiction, restitution or disgorgement, and medical monitoring. Individual class membership itself required proof of dependence; plaintiffs conceded that addiction would have to be proved for each member. **737–738 & nn.4–6.**

## The proposed trial and the actual certification order

Plaintiffs suggested a four-phase process. A class jury would first decide common matters of defendants' conduct and asserted core liability. Sample cases would then establish compensatory amounts and a punitive-to-compensatory ratio. Individual damages would follow through claims procedures, verification, and assertion of defenses; the court would ultimately apply the punitive ratio and assess reasonableness. Much remained unresolved about personal-injury trials and how individualized evidence would be handled. The district court did not adopt the entire proposal, though its certification order incorporated important elements. **738 & nn.7–8.**

The district court found Rule 23(a) satisfied but declined Rule 23(b)(2) certification of equitable relief, including medical monitoring. Plaintiffs did not cross-appeal that denial. Under Rule 23(b)(3), it divided issues into core liability; injury, causation, reliance, and defenses; compensatory damages; and punitive damages. It conditionally certified core liability and punitive damages, using Rule 23(c)(4) to separate issues. It rejected class treatment of the other categories because of their individualized character. **738–740 & nn.9–10.**

The court reasoned that resolving defendants' conduct once would significantly advance individual claims, relying on earlier asbestos decisions. It deferred detailed state-law and reliance analysis and read *Eisen* as forbidding inquiry beyond the pleadings into the claims' merits. Its punitive-damages approach also relied on a vacated panel opinion in *Watson*, which the Fifth Circuit explained had no precedential weight. Defendants obtained interlocutory review under §1292(b), before Rule 23(f)'s later adoption. **737, 739–741 & nn.11–12.**

## Governing rule and the two certification errors

Certification required a rigorous examination of Rule 23, with plaintiffs bearing the burden. Broad discretion did not permit bypassing the rule's framework. The appellate court identified two independent errors: failure to analyze how state-law variations affected predominance and superiority, and failure to consider how the underlying claims actually would be tried. It also found that this immature tort did not satisfy superiority at the time. **740–741.**

Conditional certification did not postpone the initial obligation to establish the requirements. Its purpose was to preserve flexibility when later developments exposed unforeseen problems, not to approve a class first and decide predominance afterward. **741.**

## State law required a workable analysis

A nationwide class involved differing substantive rules, not merely different geographic locations. The court needed to identify the law governing the claims and assess whether variations could overwhelm otherwise common issues. Plaintiffs could not discharge their burden through assurances that state rules were essentially alike. Other cases had sustained multistate classes after extensive analysis grouped legal variations into manageable patterns; the principle was not that different state law always defeats certification. **741–743 & nn.14–16.**

Here plaintiffs' materials included limited surveys, a proposed jury form, and citations to other certifications. They did not explain meaningfully how this class would accommodate differences. Note 15 illustrates problems involving reliance, disclosure duties, strict liability, assumption of risk, comparative fault, and emotional-distress limitations. Smoking histories, knowledge, duration, and reasons for beginning also differed. Yet that note carefully acknowledges that common issues might predominate notwithstanding those differences: the point was the district court's failure to undertake the necessary inquiry, not a completed appellate choice-of-law analysis establishing every state's governing rule. **742–743 n.15.**

The reliance on *Jenkins* did not supply the missing manageability assessment. That asbestos proceeding involved 893 cases, one state's law, and trial in one district. Castano involved nationwide claims, numerous theories and defendants, and more than fifty years of alleged conduct. Comparing this proposed class with millions of hypothetical separate trials meant little while its actual management problems remained unexamined. **743–744.**

## Predominance required understanding the trial

The district court misread *Eisen* and *Miller*. Those decisions did not authorize certification based on the perceived strength of plaintiffs' merits or denial because they were unlikely to win. But that restriction was different from refusing to examine facts and law needed to decide Rule 23. A court must understand the claims, defenses, and likely proof to compare common and individualized matters. **744–745 & n.17.**

The court assumed that common conduct was significant simply because it would appear in every case. That reasoning confused existence of a common question with predominance. Whether proving defendants' alleged conduct would actually dominate a trial depended on the roles of reliance, addiction, causation, defenses, and other individual issues. In the fraud claims, postponing whether individual reliance was required risked completing a class trial only to discover the class should never have been certified. **745 & nn.18–20.**

Note 21 also rejected manufacturing predominance by isolating a common issue under Rule 23(c)(4). The Fifth Circuit read that subdivision as permitting separation of common issues only after the cause of action as a whole satisfied Rule 23(b)(3)'s predominance requirement. That is the court's express historical interpretation; this brief does not represent it as a subsequently verified nationwide consensus about issue classes. **745–746 n.21.**

## Why the court independently rejected superiority

The court considered class treatment especially risky for an untested addiction-as-injury theory without a useful record of individual trials. It worried that nationwide aggregation could amplify claims and impose extraordinary settlement pressure through an industry-threatening verdict. It did not categorically declare all mass-tort classes forbidden; its footnotes discuss both successful and unsuccessful classes and the importance of the particular controversy. **746–748 & nn.22–26.**

The prospect of a court-system crisis was speculative. Potential claimants would not necessarily sue, and there was reason to believe individual suits could be economically feasible because damages might be substantial, punitive damages were available in many jurisdictions, and some claims allowed fees. Prior tobacco plaintiffs' difficulties did not by themselves justify class treatment. Individual trials, coordinated discovery, or other management could help develop experience with the new theory without this nationwide certification. **747–749 & nn.24–27.**

Nor were efficiency gains assured. Evidence about defendants' conduct might need repetition in individual reliance or comparative-fault trials. Individual cases could narrow viable theories, produce authoritative state-law decisions, and reveal whether common issues were genuinely significant. Premature certification risked years of litigation followed by decertification when an assumed uniform issue proved individualized. **749–750 & nn.28–29.**

The proposed division between a class jury deciding defendants' conduct and later juries deciding comparative negligence raised a Seventh Amendment risk. Comparative fault required evaluating both sides' conduct; a later jury might revisit a finding made by the first. Bifurcation was permissible only when issues were sufficiently separable to avoid reexamination. The court treated the substantial risk here as another reason superiority was lacking, not as a ban on every bifurcated proceeding. **750–751 & nn.30–31.**

Plaintiffs' urgency argument did not overcome these problems. The medical-monitoring class they invoked had already been denied without appeal, and the proposed class included survivor claims. Choice-of-law proceedings, discovery, class trial, appeals, and then individual trials could prolong rather than shorten recovery. **751 & nn.32–33.**

## Judgment and limits

The panel **reversed and remanded with instructions to dismiss the class complaint**. It concluded that the identified procedural errors could not presently be cured by another certification attempt because the claims' novelty independently defeated superiority. The judgment did not award damages or adjudicate every individual's tobacco claim. **752.**

Note 13 expressly left additional challenges undecided, including the claimed due-process defect in a punitive ratio, categorical Erie and Article III objections, and defendants' alternative predominance argument. The opinion's detailed procedural and superiority analysis should not be converted into rulings on those reserved grounds. **741 n.13.**

The assignment highlights state-law diversity and immature-tort concerns, then describes later state settlements as editorial history. That later account is not evidence of what the Fifth Circuit ordered in 1996 and is not treated here as a separately verified modern settlement analysis.
