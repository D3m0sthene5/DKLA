# Burlington Northern Railroad Co. v. Ford

**504 U.S. 648 (1992) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 12, 1992.** Justice Souter wrote for a unanimous Court; there were no separate opinions. The assigned account is CivPro, physical PDF pages 221–222, printed page 400, Note 4.

## Facts and procedure

William Ford and Thomas Johnson were Burlington Northern employees who alleged injuries sustained while working at the railroad's premises in Sheridan, Wyoming. Each brought a Federal Employers' Liability Act (FELA) action in the state trial court for Yellowstone County, Montana. Burlington was incorporated in Delaware and had its principal place of business in Fort Worth, Texas. It claimed, however, that its principal place of business within Montana was in Hill County and moved to transfer both cases there. The trial court denied those motions. This was a venue dispute, not a judgment determining the employees' injuries or the railroad's liability. (649.)

Montana generally placed trial in a county where a defendant resided when the action began. If no defendant resided in the State, the plaintiff could designate any county. State decisions treated a corporation as resident in Montana for venue purposes only if Montana was its State of incorporation; a Montana corporation resided in the county of its principal place of business. Together these rules ordinarily confined suits against domestic corporations to one county while permitting suits against foreign corporations in any of Montana's 56 counties. (650–651.)

Burlington took interlocutory appeals. The Montana Supreme Court consolidated them and affirmed. It found rational-basis review satisfied, at least in these FELA cases, by the federal policy of facilitating injured workers' recovery. It also observed that the employees could have sued Burlington in federal court in Yellowstone County. The United States Supreme Court granted certiorari and affirmed on a different rationale. The sole judicial footnote explains that the state decision was final for §1257(a) review notwithstanding the cases' interlocutory posture. (650 & n.*.)

## Issue and governing standard

Did Montana deny equal protection by giving a corporation incorporated elsewhere fewer venue protections than a Montana corporation, even when the foreign corporation maintained a significant Montana place of business? No. The rules neither deprived Burlington of a fundamental right nor classified along suspect lines such as race or religion. They therefore survived if they rationally furthered legitimate state ends. The Court found a rational connection to balancing plaintiffs' forum choices against defendants' litigation convenience. (651–654.)

## How the Court evaluated the distinction

Venue rules adjust competing interests in the location of litigation. A place attractive to one litigant may inconvenience another, and balancing those interests is a legitimate state concern. A State could give plaintiffs considerable weight by allowing them to sue in any county, or could temper that freedom when a reasonable assessment of defendants' interests justified doing so. Equal protection did not require Montana to select the one arrangement the Court considered most convenient. (651–652.)

Montana could reasonably protect a resident defendant from the tactical disadvantage of a trial far from its residence. It could also view the difference between Montana counties as less significant for a nonresident already compelled to litigate far from home. Burlington did not seriously challenge the any-county rule for individual nonresidents or foreign corporations without a fixed Montana business location. Its narrower objection was that its Montana office should receive the same treatment a domestic corporation's principal office would receive. (652.)

The Court rejected that objection because a corporation's home office could reasonably matter more to litigation convenience than a regional office. Montana could assume that foreign corporations were unlikely to have their principal offices in Montana and that its domestic corporations would probably maintain headquarters there. On this record, those assumptions were not irrational. Montana could consequently decide that only the convenience of litigating near the corporate home office outweighed the plaintiff's choice. These were permissible legislative assumptions, not factual findings that every foreign corporation necessarily had headquarters elsewhere. (652–653.)

Using the location of a corporation's actual principal place of business would have implemented that policy more precisely than using incorporation. But rational-basis review tolerates imperfect categories. Incorporation could be more readily verified, and Montana could reasonably avoid collateral litigation over headquarters at the cost of some imprecision. Burlington had not shown that the resulting underinclusion or overinclusion was so extreme as to become irrational. In addition, Burlington's own headquarters were outside Montana, so a rule based directly on corporate domicile would not give it the protection it sought. (653–654.)

## Burlington's precedent argument

Burlington relied on Power Manufacturing Co. v. Saunders, which had invalidated an Arkansas venue distinction. There, domestic corporations could be sued where they maintained business locations, while foreign corporations authorized to do business in Arkansas could be sued in any county. Because the foreign-corporation category was limited to those authorized to operate in Arkansas, most affected corporations probably had an Arkansas business location. (654.)

Montana's broader foreign-corporation category was different: most corporations subject to its any-county rule probably did not have their principal place of business in Montana. The Arkansas distinction was therefore less precisely fitted to the relevant convenience considerations. The Court distinguished Saunders while expressly assuming, rather than deciding, that it remained good law. Burlington's failure to overturn the Montana rule did not amount to overruling that earlier decision. (654.)

## Judgment and assigned application

The Court **affirmed the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court**, sustaining the challenged venue distinction against Burlington's equal-protection claim. The decision left the underlying FELA merits for further proceedings. (654.) The holding concerned corporate incorporation and venue under rational-basis review; it did not approve distinctions based on race or religion. The textbook's questions about other classifications are questions, not additional holdings. Its quotation accurately illustrates the balance of interests but omits the fuller explanation of the home-office rationale and the Saunders distinction. (Assigned printed 400; actual 651–654.)
