# United States v. Morgan (summary order)
412 F. App'x 357 (2d Cir. 2011) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Judgment and the actual criminal posture
On March 4, 2011, the Second Circuit affirmed Richard Morgan's oxycodone-conspiracy conviction and sentence in a summary order. The panel comprised Katzmann, Raggi, and Lohier; the order is unsigned and contains no separate writing. Its sole footnote explains that the consolidated appeal by David Miller involved an Anders motion decided separately. This order addresses Morgan alone. (358 & n. 1; 361.)

Morgan had pleaded guilty under a cooperation agreement to conspiracy to distribute and possess with intent to distribute oxycodone, under 21 U.S.C. §§ 846 and 841(b)(1)(C). Judge Platt sentenced him on May 22, 2009, principally to 168 months' imprisonment. Morgan challenged procedural and substantive reasonableness and the government's withdrawal of its substantial-assistance motion under Guidelines § 5K1.1. While awaiting sentencing, he had been arrested on a warrant charging excessive retail purchases of pseudoephedrine, conduct the government treated as a violation of his cooperation agreement. The summary order does not affirm a separate pseudoephedrine conviction. (358–360.)

## The statutory-text issue assigned by Katzmann
The arrest affidavit described a pattern of purchases from pharmacies beginning in January 2008 and 24.48 grams of decongestant tablets purchased at six CVS pharmacies in January 2009. Morgan admitted the purchases but argued that § 844 targeted manufacture of methamphetamine, whereas he was addicted to pseudoephedrine and consumed the tablets himself. Section 844 prohibited knowingly or intentionally purchasing at retail, within a thirty-day period, more than nine grams of pseudoephedrine base in a scheduled listed chemical product. (359–360.)

The panel rejected a personal-use exemption absent from the text. Statutory construction ordinarily ends with an unambiguous provision; the claimed legislative aim did not erase the conduct covered by the enacted words. Moreover, § 841(c)(2) separately addressed possession for manufacturing methamphetamine, undercutting an attempt to import that purpose into § 844. To the extent the sentencing court understood the admitted purchases to violate § 844, that understanding was not error. This was a sentencing/cooperation issue within the oxycodone case, not adjudication of every element of a newly charged purchase offense through a separate trial or plea. (359–360.)

The conduct was relevant even apart from criminality. Morgan had earlier claimed sobriety by the time of his 2007 arrest; his later dependence contradicted that presentation. The warrant affidavit described purchasing patterns consistent with “smurfing,” or structuring transactions to evade detection. Conduct while on bail awaiting sentencing undermined the asserted rehabilitation and supported deterrence concerns. The panel found no impropriety in considering the cooperation violation and withdrawal of the assistance motion. (360.)

## The remaining sentencing issues
Review was for abuse of discretion, distinguishing procedural defects from the substantive range of permissible sentences. The court rejected Morgan's assertion that the district judge failed to calculate the Guidelines: the presentence report contained the calculation, participants repeatedly referred to it, the prosecutor expressly stated the 168–210-month range, and the judge recognized that the imposed sentence was its bottom. The district court expressly understood the availability of a non-Guidelines sentence. Its statements that it considered the statutory sentencing factors were unrebutted; it was not required to recite each factor robotically. (358–359.)

The sentence's explanation was terse but adequate in context. The judge discussed the difficulties of drug sentencing, the parties' submissions, Morgan's work as a physician, and the cooperation violation after ruling on presentence-report objections and hearing arguments for a lower sentence. A bottom-of-range sentence did not demand a full opinion when that context demonstrated consideration and a reasoned basis. (360–361.)

Substantively, the circuit did not apply a presumption that Guidelines sentences are reasonable, but reserved reversal for exceptional decisions outside the permissible range. Morgan's sale of hundreds of oxycodone prescriptions, facilitating distribution of tens of thousands of pills, and his conduct on bail made 168 months neither shockingly high nor legally unsupported. Finally, the cooperation agreement released the government from an obligation to seek a departure if it determined that he had committed or attempted additional crimes. The new arrest on a supported warrant supplied a good-faith basis; Morgan did not rebut the government's explanation or show bad faith. The panel rejected the remaining arguments and affirmed. (361.)
