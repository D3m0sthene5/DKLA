# Virtue (Vertue) v. Bird
3 Keble 766–767, 84 Eng. Rep. 1000; 1 Ventris 310, 86 Eng. Rep. 200 (K.B.; recovered reports under 29 Charles II, assigned as 1678) | Contracts

## Report, court, and procedural result
The recovered Keble report captions the case Vertue and Bird and places it under Trinity Term, the twenty-ninth year of Charles II, expressly headed 1677, following Friday, June 15. The term lists Chief Justice Rainsford and Justices Twisden, Wild, and Jones. The ruling is attributed to the court collectively, without individual opinions or a numerical vote. Its precise reported result is judgment stayed after a verdict, not an appellate affirmance or a quantified damages reduction. The textbook identifies Virtue v. Bird as a 1678 King’s Bench case and also cites a Ventris report. The independent reviewer subsequently recovered and read that complete parallel entry. Both reports end with judgment stayed, but they describe the cargo and place differently; those variants are preserved below. (84 Eng. Rep. 1000; 86 Eng. Rep. 200.)

## The agreement and claimed consequential loss
The plaintiff brought an action on an agreement to carry corn from a location identified as A. to Ipswich, Suffolk, and deliver it at a place the defendant would appoint. The report says no time was stated. The plaintiff averred that he carried the corn on a particular day and notified the defendant, who failed to appoint a delivery place. He alleged that this omission caused four of his horses to become diseased and die. Those are the report’s allegations underlying the claim; it does not provide a separately reconstructed evidentiary record, a carriage price, or a valuation of the horses. (1000.)

After a verdict and the staying of judgment, Holt asked the court to permit judgment. He argued that the defendant had caused nonperformance of the agreement and should answer for the resulting consequential damage. The reporter thus preserves a causal argument for recovery: the carrier had reached Ipswich, but the other party’s failure to specify where to unload prevented completion and exposed the horses to harm. (1000.)

## Reason for withholding judgment
The court rejected that argument because the plaintiff could have taken the horses out of the cart or put the corn down somewhere in Ipswich. It treated those available protective actions as a reason not to allow recovery for the claimed horse loss caused by remaining in that situation. The omission to appoint a place therefore did not make the defendant answerable for every ensuing loss the plaintiff could have prevented. The report ends by repeating that judgment was stayed. It gives no award or new damages calculation. (1000.)

The modern lesson drawn by the textbook is avoidability: a promisee cannot recover a loss that reasonable protective action could have avoided. The old report supports that specific comparison between the defendant’s omission and the plaintiff’s available response. It does not itself formulate Restatement section 350, a general mitigation-expense rule, or a comprehensive test for the reasonableness of cover. Those appear in the textbook’s later explanatory discussion and should not be attributed to this seventeenth-century court.

## The recovered Ventris report and the assigned account
The complete parallel entry, also captioned Vertue versus Bird, appears on 86 English Reports 200 under the heading Easter Term, the twenty-ninth year of Charles II. It expressly cross-references 3 Keble 766. Ventris describes carriage of the defendant’s timber to the defendant’s house, rather than Keble’s corn to Ipswich. The plaintiff declared that the defendant delayed six hours in specifying where to deliver; his horses, hot from hauling timber and standing in the open air, died soon afterward. The report does not specify four horses. These are pleading allegations, not a modern reconstruction of adjudicated medical causation. (1 Ventris 310, 86 Eng. Rep. 200.)

After a not-guilty plea and a plaintiff’s verdict, Ventris moved in arrest of judgment. He argued that letting the horses stand was the plaintiff’s folly and that the defendant was not bound, under penalty of an action of this kind, to receive the timber or appoint a place. On refusal, the carrier could recover the contracted carriage compensation after doing his part, but could not maintain this action for failure to appoint the place. The report says that all the court agreed to stay judgment. Its account therefore adds a cause-of-action argument and a distinction between carriage compensation and the claimed loss; it should not be flattened into a modern statutory mitigation holding. Ventris attributes the quoted folly explanation to the motion, while expressly reporting the court’s collective agreement in the result. (200.)

The complete assigned paragraph on C&M physical PDF page 336, printed page 993, combines a goods-to-Ipswich description, the six-hour and heat allegations, the folly explanation, and Keble’s removal-or-unloading alternatives. The now recovered reports verify those particulars separately, while retaining their different corn/timber, Ipswich/house, and procedural presentations. The textbook’s general avoidability principle and Restatement section 350 discussion are later explanatory material, not words of either seventeenth-century report.

The date difference remains explicit. Keble’s original term heading gives 1677; Ventris places the entry under the twenty-ninth regnal year, in an Easter rather than Trinity sequence. The assignment gives 1678. No exact day or reconciliation of those report variants is invented.
