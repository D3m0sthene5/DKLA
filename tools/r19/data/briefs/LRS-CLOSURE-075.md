# Interstate Commerce Commission v. Cincinnati, New Orleans and Texas Pacific Railway Co.

167 U.S. 479 (1897) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, posture, and answer

Justice Brewer delivered the Court’s opinion on May 24, 1897, answering a question certified by the Sixth Circuit. The question was whether the ICC possessed jurisdictional authority to make the specified future-rate order, assuming regular antecedent procedures. The Court answered no. Justice Harlan’s dissent is recorded, but the report contains no separately written dissent or judicial footnotes. The decision resolves agency authority, rather than deciding the economically reasonable rates for the routes involved. (479–483, 493, 512.)

## Freight dispute and proceedings

Freight bureaus in Cincinnati and Chicago challenged rates charged by a group of rail and steamship carriers for transportation to southern destinations. On May 29, 1894, after complaints, answers, a hearing, and investigation, the ICC found the challenged rates unreasonable and unjust. Its order required carriers, beginning July 10, to cease collecting more than specified amounts per hundred pounds across six freight classes for shipments to eight named destinations. It also required appropriate relationships between those rates and rates to other southern points. This was an affirmative prescription of future maximum charges, not merely a finding that past charges violated the Act. (479–482.)

The carriers did not comply. The ICC sued in the federal circuit court for the Southern District of Ohio to compel obedience. That court dismissed the bill. On appeal, the Sixth Circuit certified the agency-power question to the Supreme Court while assuming the procedural regularity of the preceding proceedings. The Court was not reviewing a freestanding rate schedule Congress itself had enacted. (482–483.)

## Arguments and statutory alternatives

The ICC argued that the Act’s command of just and reasonable charges, combined with authority to execute and enforce the Act, necessarily authorized determination and enforcement of a reasonable replacement rate. Its counsel distinguished adjudicating a concrete complaint from legislating a general tariff. An order merely telling a carrier to be reasonable, counsel argued, would offer little practical relief; sections governing investigation, reparation, and judicial enforcement should supply an effective remedy. The reproduced arguments also alleged discriminatory advantages for eastern traffic, but those allegations did not become a Supreme Court determination of the contested rates’ economic reasonableness. (483–493.)

The Court identified three possible legislative choices: Congress could prescribe rates itself, delegate prospective ratemaking, or leave initial rate setting with carriers subject to legal prohibitions and supervision. It held that this statute adopted the third course. Its earlier decision at 162 U.S. 184 had rejected an express or necessarily implied rate-prescribing power; because the issue had been vigorously challenged, Brewer reconsidered it in detail. (493–495.)

## Retrospective supervision versus prospective ratemaking

The Court treated inquiry into whether already charged rates were reasonable as a judicial function and prescribing future rates as a legislative function. Given the breadth of railroad operations, invested property, freight traffic, and differing commercial conditions, prospective ratemaking was a major power requiring clear statutory authorization. The Court compared numerous state enactments expressly allowing commissions to fix, revise, or establish tariffs. It also examined statutes conferring narrower supervision and recommendation powers. These examples showed familiar legislative language available to Congress when it intended a rate-setting delegation. The federal Act did not use it. (495–501.)

The Court rejected an inference from the general duty to enforce reasonable-rate obligations. Authority to execute and enforce law was not itself authority to create a future tariff. Courts had long enforced common-law reasonableness obligations without thereby acquiring general ratemaking authority; the President’s duty to execute laws likewise did not imply authority to set carriers’ rates. The point was statutory allocation, not a holding that Congress could never delegate prospective ratemaking. (500–502.)

## Section 6’s affirmative structure

Section 6 reinforced that conclusion. It required carriers to publish and file their established schedules, permitted increases and reductions subject to specified notice, regulated charges inconsistent with published tariffs, and addressed joint rates. The ICC could prescribe schedule forms and publicity measures, but the Act did not require its approval of the tariff itself. The Court reasoned that recognition of carriers’ authority to establish and change rates was inconsistent with an agency-created permanent rate enforced through a judicial command. (502–506.)

The Court therefore relied on both missing express language and affirmative statutory structure. It did not infer lack of power merely because the subject was commercially important. Its summary connects the significance of prospective ratemaking, the familiar express language of delegations, the limited enforcement grant, and section 6’s preservation of carrier rate setting. (505–506.)

## Why complaint adjudication did not change the result

The ICC’s narrower argument—that it could identify an unreasonable existing rate, find a reasonable substitute after hearing, and then enforce that substitute prospectively—still reached the same undelegated result. The statute authorized own-motion investigations as well as complaints, so describing ratemaking as complaint resolution would not meaningfully confine the claimed power. Procedures for investigation and enforcement could not become a substantive grant absent elsewhere in the Act. (508–510.)

The Nebraska comparison did not control because its supervisory statute conferred broader powers. Nor could the Commission create its own jurisdiction through contemporaneous statutory construction. An early Commission decision had itself disclaimed general ratemaking authority. Finally, the Court rejected the distinction between permissible maximum rates and impermissible absolute or minimum rates: Congress had granted none of these forms of prospective rate prescription. (507–512.)

## Duties the Commission retained and exact disposition

The Court expressly preserved important ICC responsibilities: investigating carrier management, requiring information, enforcing nondiscrimination and long/short-haul provisions, preventing rebates and undue preferences, and ensuring tariff publicity. Enforcing those obligations could promote reasonable and equal rates without authorizing the Commission to legislate a tariff. The decision did not render the agency powerless over rate-related misconduct. (506–507.)

The certified question was answered in the negative. The Court did not itself affirm or reverse a circuit appellate judgment; it supplied the controlling answer to the certified authority question. Harlan dissented without a published explanation. (512.)
