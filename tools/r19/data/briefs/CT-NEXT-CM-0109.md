# Bloor v. Falstaff Brewing Corp.
601 F.2d 609 (2d Cir. 1979) | Contracts

## Acquisition and promised sales effort

Falstaff bought Ballantine’s brewing labels, trademarks, receivables, distribution system, and related assets, but not its brewery, under a March 31, 1972 contract. It paid $4 million and promised fifty cents per barrel sold for six years, ending March 31, 1978. The agreement required best efforts to promote and maintain a high sales volume. A separate clause required $1.1 million for each remaining year, including fractions, if Falstaff substantially discontinued distribution during the royalty period, subject to a governmental-restraining-order exception. The volume covenant protected part of the purchase price; the discontinuance clause supplied a distinct trigger and remedy. 601 F.2d at 610–611.

Ballantine’s sales had declined before the acquisition. Its previous owner had spent approximately $1 million annually on advertising and increased sales while losing $15.5 million in thirty-three months. Falstaff initially continued those advertising and distribution practices but claimed another $22 million in losses through June 1975. Facing payroll and debt problems, Falstaff came under Paul Kalmanovitz’s control after cash advances, guarantees, preferred shares, and a voting trust. He cut advertising to $115,000, closed four of six retail depots, and concentrated on making beer available at the brewery rather than servicing small outlets. He also ended unlawful promotional practices, which the best-efforts obligation did not require continuing. Id. at 611–612 & nn.1–4.

Ballantine’s sales fell much faster than relevant brewing-industry comparisons after 1974. Meanwhile Falstaff recovered financially, with $8.7 million in 1976 net income and substantial growth in working capital and cash. Trustee Bloor sued for breach of best efforts and substantial discontinuance. After trial, Judge Brieant awarded damages on the former theory but rejected the latter. Falstaff appealed liability and damages; Bloor cross-appealed the damages measure and denial of the discontinuance remedy. New York law controlled by agreement. Id. at 610–613.

## Financial limits without abandoning the covenant

Falstaff argued that the trial judge had imposed an absolute obligation to maintain sales even at ruinous losses. The appellate court rejected that interpretation of the actual decision. Best efforts did not require bankruptcy or substantial losses solely to promote Ballantine. But once the insolvency threat had passed, the disproportionate sales collapse required exploration of commercially reasonable steps that could arrest it without financial disaster. Falstaff’s general profit-maximization policy did not discharge a brand-specific promise to maintain high volume. Id. at 612–614 & nn.6–8.

The court considered Feld’s good-faith treatment of an output contract and acknowledged that New York’s best-efforts cases were not perfectly clear. Their reasoning nevertheless required attention to the promisor’s capabilities and actual performance, rather than treating disappointing profitability as a universal excuse. Footnote 8 corrects the trial court’s suggestion that Falstaff had ordinary borrowing capacity during its earlier crisis: Kalmanovitz’s financing involved acquisition of control, not a conventional commercial loan. That correction did not undermine liability for the later failure of effort. Id. at 613–614 & nn.7–8.

## Evidence of breach and burden

Findings supporting breach included closure of the North Bergen depot that served small stores and bars, replacement distributors serving fewer accounts, selection of a New York distributor with a competing brand, failure to pursue Guinness-Harp’s distribution offer, abandonment of sales goals, and incentives to favor royalty-free Falstaff labels. The appellate court did reject one subsidiary inference: greater Falstaff advertising and temporary lower prices in Texas and Missouri had explanations tied to territory and a particular competitive promotion. That error did not erase the larger pattern. Id. at 614.

Treating Ballantine at least as well as Falstaff’s own products was not necessarily enough. Falstaff could sacrifice its own brands’ volume for profit, but the acquired brands carried a royalty-dependent promise to Ballantine. Even without express best efforts, the price arrangement implied good-faith effort toward substantial sales; the express covenant added a high-volume objective. Bloor did not have to identify every feasible promotional step once he established the permissible inference that Falstaff simply disregarded volume to improve overall profits. The burden then shifted to Falstaff to show that nothing significant could have been done without financially disastrous consequences. Id. at 614–615.

## Lost royalties and the separate discontinuance clause

Damages required a stable foundation for estimating royalties that performance would have generated, rather than mathematical certainty. The trial court compared the decline of Ballantine with a composite of Rheingold and Schaefer, similarly priced beers sold principally in the Northeast. Falstaff correctly identified one distortion caused by a production interruption in the comparison’s base year; omitted late-period sales offset it to some degree. The judge also deducted estimated royalties attributable to ending unlawful sales practices. The available evidence supported the estimate despite residual uncertainty. A sufficiently specific objection about Schaefer’s losses had not been presented. Bloor’s preference for all brewers outside the fifteen largest did not make the closer brand comparison erroneous. Id. at 615.

Best-efforts breach did not equal substantial discontinuance. Distribution in this industry normally used independent wholesalers; leaving the retail-truck model therefore did not itself activate the liquidated remedy, particularly when the number of wholesalers carrying the brand increased nationwide. Much of the 63.12 percent sales decline reflected smaller brewers’ general loss of market share. The 518,899 barrels sold in 1977 remained substantial. The court affirmed denial of the separate discontinuance claim while upholding the lost-royalty award for inadequate efforts. Id. at 615–616.
