# Koken v. Black & Veatch Construction, Inc.
426 F.3d 39 (1st Cir. 2005) | Contracts

## Decision and assigned setting

The First Circuit affirmed summary judgment for the alleged manufacturer and distributor of a fire blanket. The plaintiffs had not supplied evidence establishing that inadequate warnings caused the loss or that the blanket failed the reasonable expectations of ordinary users. Judge Timothy Dyk, sitting by designation from the Federal Circuit, wrote for a panel also including Judges Selya and Howard; no separate opinion appears. The decision was issued October 14, 2005. The casebook selects the merchantability discussion, while the full opinion also resolves warning theories, expert evidence, discovery sanctions, and an attempted late supplementation of the record. Locators below use the numbered paragraphs of the preserved OpenJurist judicial reproduction, rather than unverified reporter pagination.

## What happened and why the claims arose

Androscoggin Energy owned a Maine construction project insured by Reliance Insurance. Black & Veatch was the general contractor; Redco and O'Connor were subcontractors. On May 17, 1999, Perry Austin, a Redco welder with twenty-six years' experience, stood on a ladder on a plywood platform over a generator and used a torch to cut a steel lifting lug. A fire blanket covered the platform. Molten slag fell onto it, melted through it, and started a fire. A fire watch supplied a chemical extinguisher, and Austin extinguished the fire. The fire itself did no damage; corrosive extinguisher chemicals damaged the generator, producing an estimated $9 million in repair and delay costs. Those costs were asserted losses, not a damages award. (Paragraphs 1–7.)

The blanket was lost afterward, and its manufacturer remained disputed. The plaintiffs alleged that Auburn Manufacturing made it and Inpro distributed it. Three rolls of Auburn's 1000-degree, medium-duty blankets had been delivered to the project. The rolls lacked attached ratings or warnings. Catalog and invoice descriptions included the temperature rating, but the record did not establish that Austin received those descriptions. Auburn also sold light- and heavy-duty blankets. Its literature distinguished their permitted applications and warned against using medium-duty blankets for horizontal capture of concentrated spatter or red-hot cut pieces. Austin did not know blankets had ratings and said he did not expect this blanket to melt. (Paragraphs 8–9.)

Reliance and Black & Veatch pursued product-liability and warranty claims against Auburn and Inpro, which asserted defenses and contribution claims involving each other and the subcontractors. The appeals depended on there being a triable claim against a supplier. The district court granted the suppliers summary judgment, later refused Black & Veatch's request to supplement the summary-judgment record, and declined monetary discovery sanctions. The appellate court considered the evidence favorably to the parties asserting supplier liability, without treating their product-identification allegations as established findings. (Paragraphs 4–10; notes 1–2.)

## Merchantability: ordinary purpose and objective expectations

Maine's UCC implied merchantability when merchants sold goods of the relevant kind. Auburn and Inpro did not dispute that they were merchants of fire blankets. Merchantability required fitness for the ordinary purposes of those goods. This inquiry first requires identifying customary uses; a purchaser cannot turn every application it chooses into an ordinary purpose merely by making that use. The plaintiffs ordinarily bear that burden. Here the record did not clearly establish whether the horizontal torch-cutting application was an ordinary use. But the suppliers had represented below that they were not claiming the wrong blanket was used for Austin's described application. Their apparent waiver required the court to proceed to fitness rather than decide against the plaintiffs solely on ordinary-purpose grounds. (Paragraphs 44–47.)

The governing fitness standard concerned reasonable expectations of an ordinary user or purchaser, rather than Austin's personal expectations. Black & Veatch argued that his surprise at melting itself showed that the blanket failed to perform as expected. That inference omitted the objective component. A product does not breach merchantability whenever a particular individual misunderstands its capabilities. The court used an earlier ladder case to illustrate the evidentiary difference: an expert's disapproval of an industry standard, unsupported by trade customs, field literature, or reasonable consumer expectations, does not establish that the product falls below an objective standard. It did not declare industry practice conclusive or require an expert in every warranty case. (Paragraphs 48–50.)

Here no evidence established that ordinary users reasonably expected a blanket to prevent this type of melting. Foreman Paul Gagnon expected sparks and small fires to be contained, thought the blanket performed as anticipated, and said burn-through holes were not uncommon. Austin's subjective surprise could not fill the evidentiary gap. Summary judgment on merchantability therefore stood. Maine allowed the warranty claim without contractual privity, treating implied merchantability as a tort for this purpose, so absence of a direct sale to these plaintiffs was not the reason they lost. The court also noted that Black & Veatch's cursory four-line warranty argument could itself justify rejecting its claim, but supplied the merits analysis independently. (Paragraphs 44–52; note 9.)

## Warning claims and the evidence needed for causation

Under Maine negligence and statutory strict liability, the plaintiffs had to establish a duty to warn, inadequacy of a warning, and proximate causation. The court disagreed with the district court's broad suggestion that experienced welders' knowledge of fire risks eliminated a duty to warn about any particular blanket limitation. Knowing a general hazard does not establish knowledge of a product's particular, less obvious risk. The court predicted Maine's acceptance of the sophisticated-user doctrine as a corollary of obvious danger; it did not report an existing explicit state supreme court adoption. (Paragraphs 11–20.)

Nevertheless, each proposed warning failed on the actual record. A vertical-use-only warning concerned light-duty blankets, whereas the alleged blanket was medium duty. The proposed prohibition on cutting operations relied on expert Robert Waite, whose opinion on that point was excluded because he articulated no supporting methodology. His experience qualified him to discuss the danger of molten slag on a 1000-degree blanket, but did not automatically validate his broader prohibition. The panel found no abuse of discretion and rejected the claim that the challenge had been raised without notice. (Paragraphs 21–30.)

A temperature-rating warning might have been owed to ultimate users despite catalog and invoice descriptions. The court reserved whether Maine would adopt a learned-intermediary exception and whether it applied here. Even assuming inadequate warning, Austin said he would have consulted his foreman; no evidence showed what the foreman would then have done differently, or what ordinary welders understood the rating to require. A jury would have had to speculate that a label would prevent this operation and loss. The duty to disclose a blanket's otherwise unknown dangers did not itself require advising users of an available 3000-degree alternative. (Paragraphs 31–36; notes 4–5.)

For the concentrated-spatter warning, the court accepted for argument that the supplier had a duty and failed to provide it. Maine's presumption that a warning would be read and heeded still could not establish that the operation involved the particular prohibited condition. The record described molten slag, without equating it with concentrated spatter or red-hot cut pieces; Auburn's materials themselves distinguished slag from concentrated spatter. The purchasing agent's unwillingness knowingly to order the wrong product also did not show that this warning would have changed his order. The presumption could connect a properly identified hazard to compliance, but could not prove that the missing warning addressed the hazard actually encountered. (Paragraphs 37–43; notes 7–8.)

## Other rulings and exact result

The panel also upheld refusal of discovery sanctions. Auburn argued that missing expert-damages documents increased its review costs, but the district court found that it eventually received the material and could not determine whether difficulties reflected deliberate obstruction or a discovery stay Auburn had requested. An expert's belief that documents would have been found if produced did not establish clear error without evidence of an exhaustive search or comparable support. (Paragraphs 53–56.)

Black & Veatch's proposed supplementation came after the magistrate judge's recommended disposition. The district court reasonably concluded that the supposedly new matters had already been subsumed in the record and arguments and that reopening would undo the completed proceedings. The panel found no abuse of discretion; it also found no error in treating the identified deposition portion as irrelevant. The court affirmed the judgment and left other issues unnecessary to the result unresolved, including whether a bailee could recover under the particular strict-liability statute. This was a final appellate affirmance of supplier summary judgment and related procedural rulings, not a finding that every fire blanket is fit for every cutting operation. (Paragraphs 57–58; notes 3 and 10.)
