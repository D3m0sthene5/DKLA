# R.E. Davis Chemical Corp. v. Diasonics, Inc. (post-remand appeal)
924 F.2d 709 (7th Cir. 1991) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
On February 8, 1991, the Seventh Circuit affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded a damages judgment following the earlier appeal reported at 826 F.2d 678. Judge Cudahy wrote for a panel including Cummings and Easterbrook, with no separate opinion or recorded division. The court upheld Diasonics’ lost-volume status and accepted accounting methodology, but required further consideration of the research-grant and upgrade provisions. Consequently, the decision did not finally affirm every component of the $153,050 net judgment. (709–710, 714.)

## Transaction and litigation sequence
Davis agreed on February 23, 1984, to buy a .35-tesla nuclear magnetic resonance instrument from Diasonics for $1.5 million. Full payment would entitle Davis to a $225,000 research grant based on an approved development program. The agreement also offered an upgrade to a high-field/spectroscopy system by June 1, 1985, approximately fifteen months after scheduled delivery, for an additional $700,000. Davis paid a $300,000 deposit but failed to take delivery, breaching the agreement. Diasonics resold the MRI to a third party for the same contract price and refused to return the deposit. Footnote 1 explains that Diasonics was incorporated in California when suit began, though incorporated in Delaware by this appeal; its principal business location remained California. (710 & n. 1.)

Davis sued for its down payment under UCC § 2-718(2), and Diasonics counterclaimed for lost profit under § 2-708(2). The district court originally granted Davis summary judgment on the theory that a lost-volume seller could receive only resale-based damages under § 2-706. In the 1987 appeal, the Seventh Circuit predicted that Illinois would permit lost-profit recovery and remanded for proof that Diasonics had capacity for both sales, that the additional sale would have been profitable, and that the second sale probably would have occurred without Davis’s breach. The present brief addresses the distinct 1991 application after that remand. (710–711.)

After a three-day bench trial, the district court found lost profit of $453,050 and entered judgment for $153,050 after crediting the retained deposit. It refused a research-grant deduction because stated conditions had not been fulfilled and excluded evidence of losses Diasonics allegedly would have incurred on the upgrade. Davis challenged lost-volume status, the profit accounting, the $225,000 grant treatment, and the excluded upgrade evidence. (710–713 & nn. 4–6.)

## Lost-volume proof and identification of the resale
Lost volume means that an ordinary contract-market difference or resale accounting fails to compensate a seller capable of making the original and additional sales. The prior decision required capacity, profitability, and a probable additional sale. Davis argued that Diasonics could not qualify without tracing the particular MRI it had ordered to an identified resale buyer. The panel rejected that rigid demand on this record. The generic MRI units were interchangeable and not assigned to particular buyers until shortly before delivery. Failure to trace that individual unit did not itself disprove a lost sale. (711–712 & n. 3.)

Capacity to manufacture one more MRI was undisputed. Diasonics’ controller testified that its sales force pursued every possible lead and qualified customer, rather than selecting only particular prospects in advance. Its status as a young company seeking business in a competitive market independently corroborated that testimony. Davis offered no contrary proof regarding additional capacity or comprehensive solicitation. The district court’s finding that another sale probably would have occurred absent the breach was therefore not clearly erroneous. The holding applied evidence to the three-part test; it did not abolish the probability requirement or treat every manufacturer with spare capacity as automatically a lost-volume seller. (711–712 & nn. 2–3.)

Davis also attacked average rather than actual costs, discrepancies among prior figures and financial statements, omitted expenses, included revenues, and figures involving another MRI type. Damages required reasonable certainty, not mathematical precision. The district judge had evaluated both sides’ accountants and credited Diasonics’ figures. Its factual accounting determination survived clear-error review even if an appellate court might have weighed the competing evidence differently. (712.)

## Research grant: genuine exchange or disguised rebate
The grant question required more than noting that Davis had not paid the full price or obtained approval of a development program. Those unmet conditions explained the district court’s initial treatment but did not establish the economic nature of the promised $225,000. If the research would genuinely benefit Diasonics, the grant could represent an independent exchange for valuable consideration, rather than an avoided sales expense or automatic reduction of price. If both parties understood that practically worthless information would qualify for a routinely payable rebate, however, damages had to reflect that $225,000 reduction. Breach did not authorize Diasonics to recover more than its bargain. (712 & n. 4.)

The trial judge had mentioned both the usefulness of research and Diasonics’ customary discounts in its competitive market. The explanation was too ambiguous to show whether the grant had been classified on that evidence or simply rejected because of unfulfilled conditions. The appellate court remanded for the required characterization; it did not itself find the research worthless or order an automatic $225,000 credit. (712 & n. 4.)

## Upgrade option and avoided expenses
Illinois ordinarily required strict adherence to conditions for exercising an option. But the label “option” could conceal an integral element of the original transaction. If exercise was substantially likely, expenses Diasonics avoided by not supplying the upgrade could affect the lost-profit calculation. Davis had some evidence supporting that characterization: it designed the MRI building for the more powerful system, and Diasonics initially sought profits from Davis’s failure to upgrade. Those facts warranted consideration; they did not conclusively establish exercise or the amount of a resulting loss. (712–713 & nn. 5–6.)

The court used other contract classifications, including stock arrangements and nominal-price purchase options in supposed leases, to illustrate examining substance rather than terminology. It adopted a practical threshold: the evidence must show a substantial likelihood of exercise and persuasively establish the option’s value before it enters damages. The nonexhaustive remand factors were the option’s importance to the original bargain, its value relative to the additional $700,000 payment, and the original MRI’s value relative to its $1.5 million price. An inflated original price and relatively nominal upgrade charge might make exercise the only economically rational course; a charge roughly equal to added value might leave exercise genuinely uncertain. (713–714.)

## Disposition and assigned-source distinction
The final instruction was further consideration of research-grant and upgrade evidence. Lost-volume status and the accepted accounting approach stood, but the judgment was affirmed only in part, reversed in part, and remanded. This protects the seller’s lost bargain while accounting for expenses or benefits it would have furnished under performance; lost-volume recovery is not permission to disregard economically integrated obligations. (714.)
