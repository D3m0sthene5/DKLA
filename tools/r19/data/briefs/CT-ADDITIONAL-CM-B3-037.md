# State v. Strickland
42 Md. App. 357, 400 A.2d 451 (1979) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and disposition

Maryland’s Court of Special Appeals decided this case on April 19, 1979. Chief Judge Gilbert wrote for a panel with Judges Thompson and Wilner; the complete report contains no separate writing. The State appealed an order returning $2,500 in bribe money to James Linwood Strickland after his conviction. The appellate court denied Strickland’s motion to dismiss the appeal, reversed the return order, charged appellate costs to him, and directed that the mandate issue forthwith. It did not review or reverse his unappealed bribery conviction and four-year sentence. (42 Md. App. at 357–359, 364.)

## Facts and chronology

Strickland sought to induce District Court Judge Sylvania W. Woods to show leniency toward a friend with a pending case. Woods promptly contacted the State’s Attorney. The opinion describes an apparent request that the judge cooperate with the investigation until payment occurred. Strickland delivered $2,500 to Woods, who immediately handed it to agents of the State’s Attorney. This was an attempted judicial bribe detected through cooperation with law enforcement, not an adjudicated instance of a judge corruptly keeping the money or agreeing to grant leniency. (Id. at 358.)

Strickland was indicted, pleaded guilty, and received a four-year prison term. When the guilty plea was accepted on November 17, 1977, the State offered the cash as an exhibit, which the court admitted. Judgment was entered March 1, 1978, and Strickland did not appeal. Almost three months after trial he moved for sentence modification or reduction, return of property, and other relief. Following a hearing, the circuit court ordered return of the money on August 10, 1978. The State appealed the property-return portion. Footnote 2 observes that placing the money into evidence was unnecessary and doubts that this particular appeal would have arisen had the State not done so. That observation does not convert the property order into an appeal from the conviction. (Id. at 358–359 & n. 2.)

## Why the State could appeal

Strickland argued that the order arose within a criminal case and therefore fell under the restricted statutory authority for State criminal appeals. Footnote 3 reproduces the relevant provision allowing specified appeals from dismissal of charging instruments and from a failure to impose a mandatory statutory sentence. Neither ground described this dispute. A sentence-reduction motion itself was discretionary and not appealable. The court nevertheless distinguished the nature of the property claim from its filing location. (Id. at 359 & n. 3.)

The demand sought a determination of ownership or entitlement to possession of personal property. Earlier authority had treated a similar post-conviction petition as the equivalent of replevin or an action determining title. The intervening jurisdictional statute assigned replevin to the District Court; if entertained in the circuit court holding the money, this petition was instead in the nature of trespass or trover to determine title. Either way, the relevant proceeding was civil. Introducing the money as criminal-trial evidence and filing the request under the criminal docket did not alter that character. The court consequently held that the State could appeal and denied the motion to dismiss. (Id. at 359–360.)

## Exhibit withdrawal and the forfeiture objection

Strickland relied on Rule 1217(f)(2), which directed the clerk to notify counsel of impending exhibit disposal and provided an opportunity to request withdrawal. He asserted that the record disclosed neither compliance with that notice procedure nor a State request to withdraw the exhibit. The court read withdrawal to mean taking back what one had supplied. Ordinarily the party placing an exhibit in evidence could request its withdrawal; another party could not use the rule to recover it absent a showing of ownership. Strickland had not supplied the exhibit to the court—the State had—and the rule did not itself restore his title to the cash. (Id. at 360–362.)

The hearing judge had viewed State retention as an unconstitutional forfeiture of estate resulting from conviction under Article 27 of Maryland’s Declaration of Rights. The appellate court rejected that premise. In its analysis, Strickland freely relinquished possession and title when he delivered the money to Woods, despite his testimony that he did not intend a gift. Woods received it as the State’s agent, so title passed to the State. Because Strickland no longer owned an interest in the money, retaining it did not forfeit his estate. This was the court’s stated ownership rationale, not a general authorization to confiscate every defendant’s property used as evidence. (Id. at 362.)

## Illegality, failed consideration, and public policy

The court also rejected an attempt to recover the money by treating the transaction as an unperformed bargain. Agreements to commit a crime are illegal and void, and courts do not award damages for their breach. Even assuming for argument that Woods had breached such an agreement or that the consideration had wholly failed, Strickland could not obtain judicial help to recover payment made for an illegal act. The hypothetical did not establish an enforceable promise by the cooperating judge. The authorities discussed at 362–363 reinforced that a briber cannot obtain recovery simply because the expected corruption never occurred. (Id. at 362–363.)

An Attorney General opinion stating a general rule of returning property to the person from whom it was taken did not establish a contrary right. Its list of seven statutory exceptions did not purport to be exhaustive, and its opening qualification recognized that other law could govern. Public policy independently forbade courts from assisting a person who voluntarily paid to corrupt judicial action. The court emphasized the damage that judicially ordered refunds of bribes would inflict on public confidence. Its express rule was that, absent a statute directing return of bribe money, the briber could not recover it whether the attempted corruption succeeded or failed. The return order was therefore reversed. (Id. at 363–364 & n. 4.)
