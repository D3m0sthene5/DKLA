# Milicic v. The Basketball Marketing Co., Inc.
857 A.2d 689 (Pa. Super. Ct. 2004) | Contracts

## Decision and posture
Pennsylvania’s Superior Court affirmed an order granting Darko Milicic a preliminary injunction against The Basketball Marketing Company, doing business as AND 1. The injunction restrained interference with his efforts to obtain endorsement contracts after he disaffirmed an agreement made during minority. Judge McCaffery wrote for a panel with Judges Hudock and Popovich; no separate writing appears. The decision was filed August 25, 2004. Its inquiry was whether apparently reasonable grounds supported interim relief, rather than a final adjudication of the declaratory action or tort damages. (689, 691, 693 ¶4, 697 ¶¶18–19.)

## Agreement, disaffirmance, and competing endorsements
The company marketed basketball apparel and related products from Pennsylvania. Milicic, a Serbian basketball player, signed its endorsement agreement on June 15, 2002, when sixteen. The company would supply money and products in exchange for endorsements. Two amendments changed compensation and payment recipients but retained the other provisions, including Pennsylvania choice of law. Initially little known in the United States, Milicic became a prominent 2003 NBA prospect and ultimately the Detroit Pistons’ second overall draft pick. His improved market position made a more lucrative endorsement possible. (691–692 ¶2.)

In June 2003 the parties discussed a buyout and agreed he could approach other companies to estimate another agreement’s value for those negotiations. His buyout proposal was rejected. Shortly after reaching eighteen he sent an unequivocal disaffirmance letter and began returning the money and products he had received, or their equivalent value. The reproduced trial-court passages contain inconsistent dates for the letter and birthday intervals; the material conclusion was that the disaffirmance occurred within a reasonable time after majority. The appellate court did not resolve the internal chronological discrepancy. (692 ¶2; 694 ¶12.)

AND 1 refused to treat the letter as ending the agreement. On July 11 it wrote to Adidas and Reebok, asserting both a contractual dispute and an agreement that remained valid and enforceable for several years, and requested communications with Milicic. Adidas stopped advanced negotiations, preventing execution of a nearly completed endorsement agreement. Milicic sued for temporary, preliminary, and declaratory relief. After a temporary restraining order, a hearing, and briefing, the trial court issued the preliminary injunction that the company appealed. (692 ¶2.)

## Governing review and preserved issues
The appellate court did not independently decide the ultimate merits. It examined whether the record supplied apparently reasonable grounds for the chancellor’s action and would intervene only if grounds were plainly absent or the governing law palpably erroneous or misapplied. The company presented five issues concerning actionable conduct, injunction prerequisites, supposed premature merits findings, commercial speech, and the choice-of-law clause. (692–693 ¶¶3–4.)

The objection to unspecified improper findings had not been properly raised and developed below and was waived. The commercial-speech contention likewise had received inadequate development below, including only a footnote. The court additionally rejected the company’s reliance on Central Hudson: that advertising decision did not govern whether its conduct concerning an allegedly valid contract was actionable. The challenge to applying Pennsylvania law was omitted from the Rule 1925(b) concise statement and also waived. Therefore the case does not establish a general merits rule about enforcing a choice-of-law clause after disaffirming a contract. (693–694 ¶¶5–11 & n.1.)

## Minority and the preliminary-injunction requirements
The court endorsed the trial court’s reasoning that all four injunction prerequisites were satisfied: a strong likelihood of success, immediate irreparable injury not adequately compensable in money, greater injury from denial than grant, and restoration of the status quo. Pennsylvania law generally made a minor’s contract voidable, except for necessities, if disaffirmed within a reasonable time after majority. The letter supplied more than a reasonable probability of success in the declaratory action. That likelihood supported interim relief; it was not a separately entered final declaratory judgment. (694–696 ¶¶12–13 & n.2.)

Milicic’s agent explained the short period after the NBA draft in which publicity and excitement could be converted into endorsement opportunities. Blocking a principal endorsement could also impair secondary deals. The loss could be estimated only conjecturally, rather than through an accurate pecuniary measure, so eventual damages were inadequate. The trial court weighed the competing injuries in light of minority’s protective policy: the experienced company had produced no evidence of counsel advising Milicic and had not obtained a guardian to protect the minor’s interests. The trial court’s description of a child in a foreign country signing the company’s document was reproduced as part of that balancing analysis. The public interest included protecting minors from immature mistakes and adult overbearance. Enjoining further interference would return the parties to their position before the challenged letters. (695–696 ¶12.)

## Actionable interference
AND 1 maintained that its letters merely communicated publicly known litigation and could not be wrongful regardless of whether the contract was valid. The court refused to sever the communication from the underlying contractual dispute. To justify preliminary restraint, conduct had to breach a statutory or common-law duty. The likely effectiveness of disaffirmance was therefore relevant to whether AND 1 could claim justification for telling negotiating competitors that its contract remained enforceable. Footnote 3 explained that the contractual-validity question necessarily informed the interference question. (696 ¶¶14–16 & n.3.)

The court identified the elements of intentional interference with prospective contractual relations: a prospective relationship, purposeful action intended to prevent it, lack of privilege or justification, and actual legal damage. Adidas negotiations were active, the letters were deliberately aimed at stopping them, and the affidavit of Adidas’s general counsel showed that they did stop the deal. Coupled with Milicic’s likely success in invalidating the endorsement agreement, these facts supported a potential interference claim and apparently reasonable grounds for restraint. The court sustained the injunction without fixing tort damages. (696–697 ¶¶16–18 & n.4.)

## Exact result and reading scope
The order granting the preliminary injunction was affirmed. No final damages award or final declaration of contract invalidity was entered by this appellate disposition. The assigned accounts describe disaffirmance and the return of benefits; the complete opinion supplies the crucial preliminary posture and preservation limits.
