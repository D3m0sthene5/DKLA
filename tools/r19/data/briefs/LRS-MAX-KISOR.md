# Kisor v. Wilkie

**Course:** Legislation, Regulation & the State  
**Citation:** 588 U.S. 558 (2019)  
**Court and date:** Supreme Court of the United States, June 26, 2019

## Case orientation

The Court declined to overrule Auer and Seminole Rock deference to agency interpretations of their own regulations. A five-Justice majority reinforced demanding prerequisites: genuine ambiguity after exhausting interpretive tools, a reasonable reading, and an authoritative, expertise-based, fair and considered agency judgment. The Court vacated and remanded because the Federal Circuit had too quickly found ambiguity and assumed deference followed. It did not decide whether Kisor was entitled to retroactive disability benefits. All nine Justices agreed with the judgment, although four would have formally rejected Auer. (Kagan slip opinion at 11–19, 25–29.)

## Facts and proceedings

Kisor, a Vietnam veteran, applied for disability benefits in 1982, alleging PTSD related to Operation Harvest Moon. The VA psychiatrist acknowledged his participation in that battle but did not diagnose PTSD; benefits were denied. Kisor reopened the claim in 2006 with a new psychiatric report and additional service records. The VA granted benefits from the reopening date, rather than his original application.

A VA regulation allowed reconsideration involving relevant official service department records not previously considered. The Board of Veterans' Appeals, acting through a single administrative judge, interpreted relevant to mean records bearing on the basis of the earlier denial. The new service records confirmed combat participation, which had not been disputed, rather than addressing the missing PTSD diagnosis. Kisor argued that records could be relevant to another benefits criterion even if not previously disputed. (Kagan at 2–3.)

The Veterans Court affirmed. The Federal Circuit found both readings plausible and deferred to the Board under Auer because its interpretation was not plainly erroneous or inconsistent with the regulatory framework. The Supreme Court granted review to consider overruling Auer and Seminole Rock. It did not grant review to choose a definitive construction of relevant or finally adjudicate the benefit award.

## Threshold one: genuinely ambiguous regulation

A court must first use the traditional tools of interpretation, including text, structure, history, and purpose. Difficulty on an initial reading, technical language, or the existence of competing party interpretations does not itself establish ambiguity. If those tools yield one reasonable meaning, the court must enforce it. Deference cannot permit an agency to create a new regulation under the guise of interpretation. (Kagan, majority Part II–B, at 13–14.)

## Threshold two: reasonable reading

Even when genuine ambiguity remains, the agency's construction must fall within the zone of permissible readings identified through that interpretive work. The familiar plainly-erroneous-or-inconsistent formulation does not authorize unlimited agency choice or a more indulgent reasonableness test than the statutory-deference regime then described by Chevron. Agencies can fail this requirement. (At 14.)

## Further inquiry: authority, expertise, and considered judgment

Reasonableness alone is insufficient. The court independently examines the character and context of the interpretation. The Court identified important markers rather than an exhaustive rigid test. (At 15–18.)

First, the interpretation must be an authoritative or official agency position. It need not personally come from the agency head: appropriate staff memoranda can qualify when issued by actors and through vehicles understood to establish authoritative policy. An informal employee statement or an interpretation expressly disclaimed as authoritative does not automatically receive controlling weight. (At 15–16.)

Second, the interpretation must implicate substantive agency expertise. Technical matters often qualify, but apparently ordinary issues can also involve specialized policy judgments. Conversely, a matter within courts' familiar competence or another agency's responsibilities may not. An agency gains no special interpretive authority merely by parroting statutory text in a regulation. (At 16–17, note 5.)

Third, the reading must reflect fair and considered judgment. Convenient litigation positions and post hoc rationalizations ordinarily do not qualify. New interpretations cannot unfairly surprise regulated parties, whether by changing a prior construction or imposing retroactive liability for longstanding conduct previously unaddressed. The Court described deference to positions first offered in briefs as generally inappropriate, but expressly preserved exceptions such as Auer's court-requested amicus interpretation by a nonparty agency. It did not hold that every interpretation appearing in litigation is necessarily disqualified. (At 17–18, note 6.)

If controlling deference is unwarranted, an agency explanation may still receive weight proportional to its persuasive force under Skidmore. Auer deference and persuasion remain different legal concepts. (At 12; Roberts at 1–2.)

## Why the Court preserved Auer

Five Justices relied on stare decisis. Auer and Seminole Rock belonged to a lengthy line of decisions applied throughout administrative law. Overruling could destabilize settled regulatory constructions, and Congress could alter the governing statutes. Mere disagreement with the doctrine's merits or reasoning did not supply the special justification needed to overrule it. Kisor did not establish unworkability or intervening legal changes; expansion of the administrative state supported strengthening limits rather than discarding the doctrine. (Kagan, majority Part III–B, at 25–28.)

The Court's refusal to overrule the precedents did not endorse every previous reflexive application. Its clarified framework required meaningful judicial work before deference.

## Four-Justice rationale: delegation and the APA

Only four Justices joined Kagan's broader explanation in Parts II–A and III–A. She understood Auer as a rebuttable presumption that Congress generally wanted agencies to resolve ambiguities in the rules they promulgated. Agencies could reconstruct the rule's original purposes, draw on expertise, make policy judgments under presidential supervision, and promote uniform application. (Kagan plurality at 4–11.)

Kagan also defended compatibility with APA § 706, reasoning that its command to determine meaning did not specify a review standard and that reasonableness review still decided a legal question. She understood the APA to preserve preexisting review practices. As to § 553, interpretation did not independently acquire legislative force merely because a court deferred: the underlying legislative rule supplied the enforcement authority, while courts retained power to reject incorrect or unqualified interpretations. (Plurality at 19–23.)

She disputed the claimed incentive to draft strategically vague rules, emphasizing a lack of demonstrated examples and countervailing pressures toward clarity. She also rejected separation-of-powers objections, viewing the limited doctrine as preserving judicial review and combined agency functions as exercises of executive authority. Those merits defenses did not obtain Roberts's fifth vote and should not be labeled a five-Justice APA or constitutional holding. (Plurality at 23–25.)

## Applying the framework to Kisor

The Federal Circuit needed to redo its analysis. Its observation that both parties offered seemingly reasonable readings did not exhaust the tools for determining whether relevant was genuinely ambiguous. If ambiguity remained, the court also had to assess whether a single Board member's nonprecedential ruling reflected the agency's considered, authoritative position. The Solicitor General had raised that concern in argument; the Supreme Court left it for consideration rather than holding categorically that all Board decisions fail the authority requirement. (Kagan majority at 28–29.)

## Separate writings

Roberts agreed to retain Auer on stare decisis grounds and joined the limits and remand. He emphasized practical overlap between situations warranting deference and situations in which an agency's thorough, expert, consistent explanation would persuade a court. He expressly recognized a conceptual difference between binding deference and persuasive weight. He also distinguished interpretation of regulations from interpretation of statutes and said Kisor did not decide Chevron's fate. (Roberts at 1–2.)

Gorsuch agreed with vacatur and remand but would abolish Auer. He traced its origins to an unexplained wartime dictum and disputed Kagan's historical account. In his view, APA § 706 required independent judicial interpretation, while controlling informal interpretations circumvented § 553's rulemaking procedures. He argued that Article III and impartial adjudication required a court's own legal judgment, rather than preference for a potentially interested government litigant. His note 47 explained that Kisor's own review statute, § 7292(d)(1), modeled the APA's legal-review commands; the case itself did not directly arise under the APA. (Gorsuch at 1–29, all corresponding notes.)

In Parts IV–V, joined by Thomas and Kavanaugh, Gorsuch further rejected private authorial intention as a substitute for public meaning, defended persuasive agency expertise without compulsory deference, and disputed the claimed benefits for uniformity. He questioned methodological stare decisis, distinguished particular regulatory holdings from an abstract interpretive default, and argued ordinary overruling considerations favored abandoning Auer. Alito joined Parts I–III, not these additional parts. Gorsuch preferred Skidmore and independent judgment to the newly complicated deference framework. (Gorsuch at 29–42; notes through 119.)

Kavanaugh, joined by Alito, also would retire Auer. He expected rigorous use of interpretive tools to resolve most cases without deference. He distinguished judicial interpretation from reasonable agency policy choices expressly allowed by open-ended regulatory terms, viewing the latter as an issue of State Farm review. Like Roberts, he said statutory deference was a separate question. These were judgment concurrences, not dissents from vacatur and remand. (Kavanaugh at 1–2.)

## Judgment and exam use

The judgment was vacated and remanded unanimously. Five Justices retained Auer and adopted the limits; four would overrule it. Kisor's entitlement and the regulation's definitive meaning remained unresolved.

Work through genuine ambiguity, permissible meaning, official authority, expertise, and fair considered judgment. An agency cannot receive controlling weight merely by identifying two plausible readings. Distinguish a change in a binding rule from interpretation within its permissible scope, and controlling deference from respect for a persuasive explanation. This historical 2019 brief does not treat Kisor as deciding subsequent statutory-deference disputes.
