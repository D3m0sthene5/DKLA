# Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge Co.
35 F.2d 301 (4th Cir. 1929) | Contracts

## Facts and litigation
Rockingham County’s five-member board awarded Luten a bridge-building contract on January 7, 1924, by a three-to-two division. Public controversy followed. Pruitt, one of the supporters, submitted his resignation to the superior-court clerk on February 11. The county offered evidence that the clerk accepted it before Pruitt attempted to withdraw it and appointed Hampton as successor. Hampton took the oath and thereafter regularly conducted county business with Martin and Barber; Pruitt and the other two original supporters attended no further board meetings. These details came before the appellate court through affidavits and offers of proof, much of which the trial court excluded, rather than an appellate evidentiary trial. 35 F.2d at 302–304.

At a regularly advertised February 21 meeting, the functioning board declared the contract invalid and instructed Luten to proceed no further. It also abandoned the road for which the bridge would serve as a connecting link. The county sent the resolution to Luten. Further resolutions in March and April warned that construction would occur at Luten’s risk and disavowed a member’s private encouragement to continue; a September resolution refused payment of bridge bills. When the first notice issued, labor and materials on site amounted to about $1,900. Luten nevertheless continued building and sued for $18,301.07 allegedly earned through November 3. Id. at 303.

Three original commissioners then met privately with an attorney and filed an answer admitting the complaint, without a lawfully convened board meeting or consultation with the other commissioners. After a new board took office, the county sought to strike that answer and filed its own defense. The trial judge treated the commissioners’ answer as the county’s admission, admitted it in evidence, excluded the county’s proffered resignation and cancellation evidence, and directed a verdict for Luten’s full claim. The county appealed. Although it initially contested validity, on appeal it admitted execution, validity, and its own breach; the ultimate dispute was the permissible recovery. Id. at 303–304.

## Issues
The complete opinion addressed three connected questions: whether the privately filed answer bound the county; whether the functioning board’s notices were county action; and whether Luten could recover for continued construction after receiving an effective repudiation. The assigned excerpt concentrates on the third, but the first two determined whether the repudiation evidence could properly reduce recovery. Id. at 304.

## Governmental authority and the excluded evidence
A county board exercises its corporate powers as a body in a legally convened meeting. Even assuming the three original commissioners still constituted a numerical majority, their private conference did not meet the statutory requirements for a regular, special, or called meeting. Their answer was unauthorized if offered as the county’s pleading and improper if offered as their personal answer to an official-capacity claim. It should have been stricken and could not constitute evidence of a county admission. Id. at 304–305.

The proffered resignation evidence was relevant. Acceptance by the proper authority would terminate Pruitt’s ability to withdraw the resignation and permit appointment of Hampton; the three acting members would then constitute a quorum. The court expressed this reasoning conditionally on the offered facts being true. Independently, Hampton’s appointment by the proper appointing official, oath, and unchallenged exercise of the office established the basis for treating him as a de facto commissioner. Acts at lawful meetings were valid for the county and persons dealing with it despite a possible defect in appointment. This was not a contest between two simultaneously functioning rival boards. The county’s resolutions and notices therefore could not be excluded on the ground that the acting commissioners lacked authority. Id. at 305–307.

## Avoidable loss after repudiation
The county had no unilateral entitlement to rescind the valid bargain without compensation. Its notice was a breach, not a lawful release from all liability. Nevertheless, once Luten received the repudiation, it could not continue constructing a bridge the county no longer wanted and charge the resulting avoidable expenditure to the county. The abandoned connecting road left the bridge useless to the county. Luten’s compensable interest lay in its loss from being stopped, including the anticipated profit; earning that profit did not require completing unwanted work after notice. Id. at 307–308.

The court applied the American rule against increasing damages through continued performance after absolute repudiation. It distinguished authorities allowing an immediate anticipatory-breach suit: permission to sue before the performance date did not authorize avoidable later construction. Its illustrations and cited painting and manufacturing cases supported the same distinction between compensation for breach and reimbursement of costs that need not have been incurred. Id. at 307–308.

The measure on proof of duly given notice was compensation for labor and materials expended and expenses incurred in partial performance before repudiation, plus the profit the contract would have produced. This did not confine Luten to $1,900, a figure concerning work and materials at the first notice, or eliminate its lost profit. It also did not entitle Luten to the $18,301.07 claimed for continued work. The opinion used the language of a duty to mitigate; in this litigation its consequence was a limitation on recoverable damages. Id. at 308–309.

## Result and source scope
Parker wrote for a panel with District Judges McClintic and Soper; no separate writing or numbered judicial footnote appears in the complete report. The court reversed and remanded for a new trial because the unauthorized answer should have been stricken, the county’s evidence should have been admitted, and the directed verdict for the full claim was erroneous. It did not finally calculate the county’s liability. Id. at 302, 309.
