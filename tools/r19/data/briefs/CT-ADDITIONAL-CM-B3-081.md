# Sethness-Greenleaf, Inc. v. Green River Corporation
65 F.3d 64 (7th Cir. 1995) | Contracts

## Decision and review posture
The Seventh Circuit affirmed the contract judgment allowing Sethness-Greenleaf to reclaim the Green River soda trademarks and secret formulas after the buyer failed to pay following written notice and a 30-day cure opportunity. Judge Easterbrook wrote the August 21, 1995 opinion for a panel with Judge Kanne and visiting District Judge Allen Sharp. The report contains no separate writing; its sole starred note identifies Sharp's designation. The appeal concerned a contract judgment certified under Rule 54(b), while calculation of Lanham Act damages remained in the district court. It therefore did not finish every aspect of the litigation. (65 F.3d 64–66 & designation note, 68.)

## The financed acquisition and accumulating concentrate debt
In 1985, Daniel Meyers and Cornell Wing arranged for their Green River Corporation to acquire Green River's trademarks, secret formulas, and other business assets from Sethness-Greenleaf for $75,000. The seller would continue producing concentrate, which GRC would buy. Each paid gallon generated a credit, initially $3 and later $5, against the acquisition price. Until full payment, the trademarks and formulas remained in escrow as security. The deal closed in June 1985, but GRC was behind by July 1986. In June 1988 it issued notes for approximately $26,000 to address unpaid balances; that additional credit did not solve the problem. (Id. at 65.)

Every shipment carried an invoice requiring payment within ten days, which GRC routinely disregarded. During the next fourteen months the balance grew beyond $60,000, and most of the $75,000 acquisition price remained unpaid. On August 3, 1989, Sethness-Greenleaf demanded full payment within thirty days and warned that failure would trigger escrow default and repossession of the protected assets. GRC failed to pay. It admitted owing roughly $60,000 for supplied product, but disputed the seller's right to demand payment then and retake the escrow items. Its November payment offer was conditioned on dismissal of the resulting suit and was rejected. (Id. at 65–66.)

## Trademark dispute and the judgment under review
Unable to obtain further concentrate on acceptable terms, GRC began selling its own formulation under the Green River label, claiming ownership of the trademarks. Customers noticed the change. A prior appellate decision upheld preliminary injunctive relief and identified the remaining liability question as whether the parties had orally agreed to allow payment to be delayed as long as it was. On return, the district court found no such oral agreement on summary judgment, held that nonpayment following demand entitled the seller to reclaim the soda rights, and enjoined imitation sales. The certified contract judgment produced this appeal; statutory damages remained unresolved. Those earlier proceedings are described as this opinion's procedural history, not separately adjudicated stages covered by this brief. (Id. at 66.)

## Why the invoices supplied binding payment terms
GRC first argued that fourteen months of arrears disproved agreement to the invoices' ten-day deadline. Applying Illinois's UCC § 2-207, the court explained that merchants could contract through exchanged forms without individually negotiating each standard term. Additional terms in a written confirmation became part of the merchant agreement unless acceptance was expressly limited, the addition materially altered the bargain, or timely objection occurred. Both sides were admittedly merchants, GRC's orders supplied no payment timing, and it received the invoices without objection. The ten-day term was not surprising or materially altering: the Code's default rule, § 2-310(a), called for payment on delivery and was less generous. GRC's failure to comply did not show that the obligation never existed. (Id. at 66.)

## No objective agreement for indefinite credit
Meyers's affidavit initially asserted that the seller's president promised credit while sales grew and no payment until profitability. At his deposition he retracted those assertions and acknowledged that the parties had not really discussed the subject. His private wish or expectation could not establish the objective communications required for contractual obligation, so the district court properly disregarded that testimony as proof of the supposed agreement. (Id. at 66–67.)

GRC alternatively relied on course of dealing or performance. The court acknowledged that conduct could clarify an incomplete or ambiguous bargain, but the documents here cut the other way: GRC had received over 200 invoices and did not deny knowing their terms. Its first responses to the demand contested the amount and whether unpaid product obligations counted as escrow default; the long-term-credit theory appeared only after the litigation was well underway. That history did not demonstrate a communicated agreement displacing the invoices. (Id. at 67.)

## Lenience, waiver, notice, and the cure period
The court reasoned that tolerating late payment did not oblige a seller to forbear indefinitely. Treating patience as that undertaking would encourage immediate supply cutoffs and deprive buyers generally of lenience. But the court did not declare that conduct could never waive a particular default or require notice before renewed strict enforcement. It recognized those possibilities and distinguished them from intentional abandonment of the right to payment. (Id. at 67–68.)

Nothing showed that Sethness-Greenleaf relinquished that right. Even if its initial conduct encouraged complacency, it waited until GRC had notice that further delay would not be accepted and allowed another thirty days. Escrow § 6(b) specifically entitled GRC to written notice of past-due obligations and a thirty-day cure period. It received both and had not bargained for a longer grace period. The court therefore affirmed the judgment enforcing the consequences of its nonpayment. (Id. at 68.)
