# Hoover Motor Express Co. v. Clements Paper Co.

193 Tenn. 6, 241 S.W.2d 851 (Tenn. July 27, 1951) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B1-066

The Tennessee Supreme Court unanimously reversed the decrees sustaining Clements Paper Company's claim arising from an unconsummated real-estate purchase. Hoover's January 13 communication had withdrawn its November 19 offer before Clements attempted written acceptance on January 20. No formula expressly announcing revocation was necessary when the communication brought home to the offeree that the offeror no longer consented to the proposed transaction. Justice Tomlinson wrote; the report says all concur and contains no separate writing. The cause was remanded for a conforming decree, with all costs in all courts against Clements. (193 Tenn. at 8, 12–14.)

## An authorized acceptance withheld to seek better terms

On November 19, 1949, Hoover delivered a written offer concerning its purchase of real estate from Clements. No consideration was paid for the offer. Clements authorized its vice-president, Williams, to accept in December, but he withheld acceptance in the hope of securing a substantially better bargain. He wanted Clements to retain an easement through the property and tried repeatedly to contact Hoover about that idea. Crucially, he never actually communicated the proposed changes or additions. The court therefore did not treat a communicated counteroffer as the event terminating the original offer. (Id. at 8, 10.)

On January 13, 1950, Williams reached Hoover by telephone and said Clements was ready to proceed and that he wished to discuss the transaction. Hoover replied that he did not know whether they were ready, they had not decided, and they might not want to proceed. Williams's testimony included more than that one hesitant statement: Hoover said he did not think the company would proceed, indicated that it had other plans, declined positively to commit, and said he would call January 17. He never made that later call. Williams testified that he was shocked to hear that they did not plan to go through with it, because he had already made his own plans and obtained an elevation. Clements's complaint similarly described this as its first information or intimation that Hoover would not or might not carry out the agreement or offer. (Id. at 10–12.)

Clements nevertheless sent a January 20 written acceptance, closing with a demand that Hoover comply with its part of the agreement. Clements asserted no acceptance before that date. The court regarded the wording as illuminating Williams's understanding that something had already occurred that might cause Hoover to refuse performance. His earlier authority and private intention to accept if a better bargain could not be secured had not themselves exercised the power of acceptance. (Id. at 9–10.)

## The concurrent findings did not permit ordinary reweighing

When Hoover refused to proceed, Clements sued for specific performance or alternatively damages. The chancellor sustained its bill, finding that Hoover had breached the parties' written contract, and the Court of Appeals agreed. A reference to the master was ordered to ascertain damages. Thus the lower courts had not already fixed a final monetary damages amount. Hoover obtained certiorari, arguing both that there was no concurrent controlling finding and that any such finding lacked support under the applicable law. (Id. at 8.)

The Supreme Court rejected the first argument. The chancellor's finding of an existing contract implicitly found that the offer had not been withdrawn before acceptance; the Court of Appeals made that finding expressly. These were concurrent findings on the controlling fact question. The reviewing court was bound by them, and by reasonable inferences from supporting evidence, if any material evidence sustained them. It could not conduct de novo fact review. Instead, it examined the evidence most favorable to Clements to determine whether that evidence could reasonably support a continuing offer. (Id. at 8–10.)

That standard made Clements's own witness decisive. The court did not need to choose between competing versions of the telephone conversation: even Williams's account could not reasonably support the finding that Hoover's original commitment continued. The legal error was treating withdrawal as ineffective without an explicit verbal retraction. Application of the correct rule left the concurrent finding without material evidentiary support. (Id. at 10, 12–14.)

## A withdrawal may be communicated by implication

The parties agreed that an offer unsupported by consideration must be accepted before withdrawal in order to form a contract. The court explained that the offer must still exist when acceptance occurs. It found no prior Tennessee decision resolving this precise issue and turned to the general rule stated in the authorities before it: express notice in prescribed words is unnecessary. Knowledge of conduct inconsistent with the continuing offer can terminate the power of acceptance. (Id. at 8–9, 12.)

The First Restatement of Contracts § 41, quoted in the opinion, included a communication received before acceptance that states or implies that the offeror no longer intends to contract. The quoted agency principle likewise treated manifested cessation of consent as withdrawal. The court also invoked Coleman v. Applegarth's quotation from Dickinson v. Dodds rejecting any requirement that the offeror actually say that the offer is withdrawn. Those authorities supplied a manifestation-and-knowledge rule, not a requirement of an undisclosed final internal decision. (Id. at 12–13.)

Applying that rule, the court held that Williams learned on January 13 that Hoover no longer consented to the transaction. Williams's own descriptions of the other plans, refusal to commit, and shock supported the meaning conveyed. An indication that Hoover might later communicate again did not preserve the existing offer after this withdrawal. Because there was no continuing offer on January 20, the written acceptance could not form the alleged contract. (Id. at 11–14.)

The court expressed concern about disagreeing with two capable lower courts but attributed their result, and Clements's position, to the mistaken insistence on express withdrawal language. It reversed both decrees and remanded for entry of a decree consistent with the absence of a contract, allocating all court costs to Clements. It did not grant specific performance, affirm a damages award, or hold that every expression of uncertainty necessarily revokes an offer in every context. The holding concerns this received communication and this complete evidentiary record. (Id. at 14.)
