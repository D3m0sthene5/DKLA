# Estate of Nelson v. Rice
198 Ariz. 563, 12 P.3d 238 (Ct. App. 2000) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and disposition
The Arizona Court of Appeals, Division Two, Department B, decided the case on October 31, 2000. Chief Judge Espinosa wrote, with Presiding Judge Howard and Judge Druke concurring. The court affirmed summary judgment for Carl and Anne Rice against the Estate of Martha Nelson’s claims to rescind or reform the sale of two paintings for mutual mistake or unconscionability. It also denied the Rices’ appellate attorney-fee request in its discretion. The opinion views the evidence and reasonable inferences favorably to the estate as the party opposing summary judgment; its factual recital is not a jury verdict on disputed allegations. (198 Ariz. at 563–565, 568, ¶¶ 1–2, 16.)

## Sale, attribution, and later proceedings
After Martha Nelson’s death in February 1996, representatives Edward Franz and Kenneth Newman employed Judith McKenzie-Larson to appraise estate property for a public sale. She told them she did not appraise fine art and that another appraiser would be needed if she found any. She reported none, and the representatives relied on her silence and appraisal when setting prices. They also employed an expert for the estate’s Indian art. Newman later testified that he was not concerned about McKenzie-Larson’s lack of fine-art expertise because he thought nothing except the house and Indian collection had significant value. (Id. at 565–566, ¶¶ 2, 10.)

Carl Rice attended in response to a newspaper advertisement and paid the asking price of $60 for two oil paintings. He had some experience buying and selling art, but was not an educated buyer, had never made more than $55 on a single work, and had previously bought pieces that proved forgeries or works by less popular artists. The low price and professional administration led him to assume these were not originals. He liked one painting’s subject and the other’s frame. Back home, he compared the signatures with an artists’ signature book and thought they resembled Martin Johnson Heade’s. The Rices sent photographs to Christie’s, which authenticated Magnolia Blossoms on Blue Velvet and Cherokee Roses and sold them on consignment. The auction price was $1,072,000; after the buyer’s premium and commission, the Rices realized $911,780. Those figures are distinct from the $60 estate-sale price. (Id. at 565, ¶ 3.)

The representatives learned about the sale in February 1997 and first sued the appraiser, believing her responsible for the loss. They settled that November because she lacked assets to pay damages. During 1997 the Rices paid $337,000 in income taxes on the profit, bought a home, created a family trust, and spent some proceeds on living expenses. In January 1998 the estate sued the Rices. On cross-motions for summary judgment, it argued that both sides had treated the paintings as relatively valueless decorations; the Rices asserted risk allocation, laches, and the absence of grounds for unconscionability. The trial court found a mistake about value but allocated its risk to the estate, rejected unconscionability, and granted the Rices’ cross-motion. It denied the estate’s new-trial motion. (Id. at 565, ¶¶ 4–5.)

## Governing standards and the requested remedies
The appellate court reviewed genuine factual disputes and application of law de novo. Summary judgment was appropriate when the opposing evidence had so little probative force under the applicable burden that reasonable jurors could not agree with that party. Rescission for mutual mistake required clear and convincing evidence, a mistake about a basic assumption, and a material effect on the agreed exchange. Even then, the adversely affected party could not rescind for a mistake whose risk it bore under Restatement sections 152 and 154. (Id. at 565–566, ¶¶ 6–8.)

Reformation had a separate defect. It corrects a written instrument that fails to express an agreement the parties actually made; it does not impose an agreement they never made. The estate had not shown an instrument inaccurately expressing the agreed price. Discovering that the paintings were more valuable did not support rewriting the sale into a different bargain. The court’s first note expressly denied reformation on these facts. (Id. at 566, ¶ 7 & n. 1.)

## Conscious ignorance and alternative risk allocation
Section 154(b) allocates risk to a party aware that it has only limited knowledge of relevant facts but treating that knowledge as sufficient. The estate disputed the relevance of a Restatement illustration concerning valuable minerals discovered on land sold as farmland. Even assuming that example differed, comment c’s conscious-ignorance principle applied. The estate knew it possessed framed art outside the Indian collection and knew its appraiser lacked fine-art qualifications, yet depended on her to decide whether qualified help was needed. It chose to proceed without such help. That conduct allocated to the estate the risk that the paintings were valuable. (Id. at 566–567, ¶¶ 8–10.)

The estate invoked Renner, where a shared error about water sufficient for growing jojoba supported rescission notwithstanding an incomplete investigation. The court distinguished it because the risk in that case had not been allocated to either party; here the estate’s conscious treatment of limited information as sufficient supplied that allocation. The appellate court consequently did not need to resolve the remainder of the estate’s mutual-mistake arguments. In particular, its reasoning should not be restated as a merits decision that no mistake whatsoever had occurred. (Id. at 567, ¶ 11 & n. 2.)

The court also invoked section 154(c), permitting a reasonable judicial allocation of risk. The estate had ample opportunity to identify what it sold, ignored the possibility of valuable paintings, and sought relief only after the buyers’ efforts revealed their worth. Considering the transaction’s purpose and ordinary bargaining behavior, the court deemed allocation to the estate reasonable. An additional reply argument that negligence should not defeat relief without bad faith or gross negligence was waived because the estate had not raised it in its opening brief. The court did not decide that proposition on its merits. (Id. at 567, ¶ 12 & n. 3.)

## Unconscionability
Unconscionability was a legal question reviewed de novo. The court distinguished defects in the bargaining process from substantive unfairness in the terms and assessed the bargain as of formation, not with hindsight about its outcome. The estate asserted substantive unconscionability, including the extraordinary price disparity. But it had dictated the sale prices, there had been no negotiation, and Carl had paid exactly what it asked. An unexpectedly bad bargain was insufficient on these facts to establish oppressive terms and justify undoing the transaction. The court agreed with the trial judge that the result might appear unconscionable to the estate after discovery without making the original terms unconscionable. (Id. at 567–568, ¶¶ 13–15.)
