# Grigerik v. Sharpe — Connecticut Appellate Court stage
45 Conn. App. 775, 699 A.2d 189 (1997) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
On July 22, 1997, the Connecticut Appellate Court reversed the plaintiff's jury judgment on contract and negligence. It ordered a new trial on contract and judgment for defendants on negligence. Judge Shea wrote; Judges Landau and Heiman concurred. This intermediate decision is recounted in the introduction to the assigned 1998 Supreme Court opinion. Its beneficiary-intent and limitation rulings must remain distinct from that later review. (775–777, 789–794, 798; C&M printed 1190.)

## Transaction and jury findings
Edward Lang owned undeveloped Killingworth land within a watershed area. Grigerik offered $9,000 as is, or $16,000 if Lang obtained approval as a building lot. Lang agreed, and a sanitarian told them an engineer needed to prepare a drainage plan. Lang retained engineer Gary Sharpe and his firm for the site plan, septic design, and soil testing. Evidence supported a finding that he told Sharpe the work was needed for a sale to a buyer, although Sharpe denied receiving that information. The opinion recounts facts the jury reasonably could have found, rather than erasing that testimony conflict. (777–778 & n. 2; 789.)

The work was completed in October 1985 and the wetlands commission approved the plan. Grigerik bought the property November 19 for $16,000. A new sanitarian denied his subsequent building-permit application because of soil suitability and doubts about the original testing. March 1987 tests required a curtain drain; after installation, further tests still showed the land unsuitable. State-supervised testing in 1989 again concluded minimum standards could not be met. Earlier plan approval did not establish entitlement to a later building permit. (778–779.)

Grigerik served Sharpe in September 1989 and joined the corporation in 1991. A jury found for him. The original report inconsistently prints the award including interest as $44,024 at 779 and $44,074 at 790; both scans confirm the discrepancy. Note 1 explains that individual-directed counts were withdrawn before submission, although the verdict on the remaining counts was returned against both defendants. The opinion calls the submitted counts contract and negligence for convenience. (777 n. 1; 779, 790.)

## Beneficiary instruction and holding
The oral engineering contract was between Lang and the defendants, so Grigerik needed beneficiary status to enforce it. The trial court allowed recovery if he was an intended, contemplated, or foreseeable beneficiary. Jurors answered no to intended and contemplated, but yes to foreseeable. Defendants invoked Connecticut authorities requiring the parties' intent to create a direct obligation to the third person and sought judgment from those negative answers. (779–780.)

The Appellate Court rejected both foreseeability alone and defendants' shared-conscious-intent formulation. It examined the first Restatement's donee, creditor, and incidental categories and the second's intended/incidental distinction. If Lang retained the engineers to fulfill his commitment to convey an approved building lot, the buyer could be a creditor beneficiary under the former formulation and intended beneficiary under § 302(1)(b). Subsection (b) included nonmonetary performance of the promisee's obligation; subsection (a)'s monetary-debt language did not exclude this arrangement. (780–782 & nn. 3–4.)

Earlier Connecticut cases spoke of both parties' intent to create a direct obligation, meaning an enforceable right rather than necessarily direct delivery of services. The court read Stowe v. Smith as changing that mutual-intent requirement in line with Williston, Corbin, and the Restatement. It focused on the paying promisee's purpose. The promisor need not consciously intend a beneficiary's later suit, and the beneficiary need not be identified when the contract is made. Intent was objectively derived from language, acts, and circumstances rather than speculation about unspoken mental processes. This states the 1997 court's interpretation, without certifying that it survived the later Supreme Court stage. (783–787 & n. 5.)

Foreseeability was insufficient because it could describe an incidental beneficiary without a contract right. That useful tort-duty concept could not itself establish a contractual relationship. The defective charge might also have influenced the negative interrogatory answers: jurors told all three categories sufficed could select the easier foreseeable category without resolving disputed testimony. Most importantly, the charge omitted the relevance of Lang's purpose in fulfilling his obligation to the buyer. The answers thus did not warrant final defense judgment. A new contract trial under a proper instruction was required. (787–789 & n. 6.)

## Negligence limitations and repose
Negligence received a different disposition. Former § 52-584 required action within two years of discovery and no more than three years from the act or omission. The engineers finished in October 1985; this action began in September 1989, and Grigerik had reason to suspect deficiencies when his 1986 permit application was denied. He invoked § 52-584a's seven-year period following substantial completion of an improvement by architects or professional engineers. (790–791 & nn. 7–8.)

The court treated that statute as additional repose protection leaving shorter applicable limitations in place, not a general extension of negligence periods. It also rejected classifying an unbuilt septic design as the contemplated improvement merely because a correct plan enhanced land value. Designs can precede construction and use by years; starting the statutory period at each designer's completion could undermine the opportunity to discover defects manifesting after use. The negligence claim was therefore barred, requiring judgment for defendants rather than retrial. (791–794 & nn. 8–9.)

## Trial rulings and disposition
The court did not reach whether late amendments to the negligence specifications were improperly allowed, because that claim was barred. The contract allegation of failed or improper percolation tests remained broad enough to permit relevant proof at retrial. Admission of Hart's revised expert view was within discretion: he changed it after hearing new testimony, and inconsistent deposition answers could be used to challenge credibility. Sanitarian Block's substantial training, certification, experience, and design work supported admission despite his lack of an engineering degree or professional license, which affected weight in these circumstances. (794–797.)

The final mandate reversed both submitted counts, directed judgment for defendants on negligence, and ordered a new contract trial. No damages were newly awarded.
