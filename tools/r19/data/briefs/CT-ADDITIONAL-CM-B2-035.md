# Borelli v. Brusseau
12 Cal. App. 4th 647, 16 Cal. Rptr. 2d 16 (1993) | Contracts

## Court, issue, and pleading posture

The California Court of Appeal, First District, Division Four decided the appeal on January 19, 1993. Justice Perley wrote the majority opinion, joined by Presiding Justice Anderson. Justice Poché dissented in a complete separate opinion. Hildegard Borelli appealed dismissal of her complaint after a demurrer was sustained without leave to amend. She sought specific performance of her deceased husband Michael’s alleged promise to leave property to her in return for personally caring for him at home after a stroke. The issue was whether those services supplied consideration or were already required by her marital duty of support, and whether enforcing the bargain offended public policy. The court accepted properly pleaded material facts for demurrer purposes, while refusing to treat assertions in the briefs beyond the complaint as established facts. (647, 649–650, 655.)

## Alleged chronology and bargain

The couple executed an antenuptial contract on April 24, 1980 and married the next day. Michael was hospitalized for heart problems in March 1983, February 1984, and January 1987. Concerned about his health and longevity, he told Hildegard that he intended to leave her specified property and benefits: an interest in a Sacramento lot, a life estate in a Hawaiian condominium, a quarter interest in Borelli Meat Co., remaining bank cash, education expenses for his stepdaughter Monique Lee, his interest in a Kensington residence and its furniture, a partnership interest, and health insurance for Hildegard and Monique. These earlier statements supply background; the alleged exchange occurred later. (650.)

Michael suffered a stroke in August 1988. During hospitalization and rehabilitation he repeatedly said that he disliked being away from home and did not want to enter a nursing home, although staying home required round-the-clock care and modifications to the house. In about October, he allegedly promised to leave Hildegard the listed property, including a one-hundred-percent interest in the Sacramento property, in exchange for her caring for him at home throughout his illness. That would avoid the rest-home or convalescent-hospital placement his doctors recommended. The complaint invoked the confidential marital relationship and alleged full performance by Hildegard. Michael died on January 25, 1989. His will instead left her $100,000 and his interest in the residence they owned as joint tenants, while the bulk of the estate passed to his daughter, Brusseau, the executor and respondent. These allegations were not tested at a trial. (650–651.)

The existence of the antenuptial agreement was pleaded, but its text was not in the record. Poché inferred in footnote 2 that it preserved substantial separate property; that inference was not an adjudicated construction of the missing instrument. Neither opinion established the value of every promised item or found that the alleged oral exchange had actually occurred. (659 n.2.)

## Majority: support as a personal pre-existing duty

The majority began with the public interest in marriage as a legal status rather than an ordinary commercial relation. It examined Civil Code provisions requiring spouses to support each other, assume mutual respect and fidelity, and provide support while living together, alongside provisions permitting interspousal transactions respecting property. For the majority, the property-contracting permissions did not let spouses bargain away the personal obligations attached to marital status. These are the statutory provisions as quoted in this 1993 opinion, not a representation that their numbering or language remains current. (651–652.)

Estate of Sonnicksen and Brooks v. Brooks treated nursing-type care of an ill spouse as an existing marital obligation. A compensation promise for that care therefore lacked new consideration and was contrary to public policy. Hildegard argued that those precedents embodied outdated assumptions about women and denied equal protection because husbands could fulfill support duties financially while wives had to nurse personally without pay. The majority rejected that characterization, reasoning that the support obligation operated reciprocally and that later decisions applied it to both spouses. (652–654.)

Its supporting authorities addressed consortium, welfare assistance, and related aspects of marriage. Consortium included companionship, affection, comfort, and physical help in maintaining the home, not merely income. The majority also noted that Watkins v. Watkins allowed recovery for homemaking performed before marriage but did not extend that recovery to support furnished during marriage. Vincent v. State of California addressed equal treatment of spouses under assistance programs and regarded an existing marital duty as a constitutionally sound basis for denying payment in the relevant circumstances. These supporting cases were not themselves rulings on Hildegard’s alleged contract. (653–654.)

The majority concluded that support included more than physical tasks that could be assigned to a hired worker. Sympathy, companionship, and affection made it a personal duty, comparable in its nondelegability to fidelity and mutual respect. Consequently, Hildegard’s personal performance of the alleged care supplied no new consideration. The court preserved rights arising from marriage, such as community-property rights, while denying separate compensation for performance of this support obligation. It did not hold that spouses were categorically unable to make enforceable property contracts with each other. (654.)

## Majority’s policy answer to the dissent

The majority acknowledged that a longstanding rule need not remain merely because of its age. Nevertheless, it regarded the duty as gender neutral and found a continuing potential for fraudulent assertions of compensation agreements whenever personal care was rendered. It did not claim that fraud alone fully justified the rule or find that Hildegard herself had committed fraud. It also rejected the dissent’s suggestion that refusal to enforce might encourage spouses to leave and negotiate their return. In its view, sickbed bargaining was antithetical to the statutory institution of marriage, whose personal mutual support could not be reduced to arm’s-length price negotiations. (654–655.)

Thus the rejection of this bargain rested on both the absence of new consideration under the majority’s conception of marital support and public policy. The conclusion was reached on demurrer despite the allegation that the wife actually performed the promised care. Performance alone did not cure the asserted pre-existing-duty defect. (649, 654–655.)

## Poché’s dissent: personal nursing exceeds the duty to ensure care

Poché accepted that spouses owe support and medical care but disputed a requirement that each personally perform all nursing services. He traced the older precedents’ language to coverture and a historical division under which a husband provided money and a wife furnished domestic labor. Modern reciprocal duties, married women’s employment, and changed household roles undermined the premise that personal nursing was the sole way to discharge support. His point was not that marriage created no support obligation, but that arranging professional assistance, nonprofessional help, or care by relatives could satisfy it. Agreeing to personal care at home therefore could supply something additional of legal value. (655–657, 659–660.)

The dissent further relied on statutory freedom to transact regarding property and the retreat of interspousal immunity from tort and contract litigation. Footnote 1 addressed the related abandonment of parental immunity. In Poché’s view, enforcement would allow competent spouses to allocate responsibilities without depriving them of contracting powers available to strangers. The husband could have purchased the same personal services from someone else; the marriage certificate should not eliminate the consideration in the wife’s choice to supply them herself. (657–660 & n.1.)

He rejected a categorical fraud concern because courts could hear evidence and decide whether a particular contract existed or was fraudulently induced. Fraud was not alleged as a proved fact at this demurrer stage. Footnote 2 also declined to resolve possible statute-of-frauds and transmutation difficulties in light of the complaint’s allegation that the defendants were estopped to invoke those provisions. That was the dissent’s treatment of an additional pleading issue, not a majority holding that an oral transfer satisfied every statutory requirement. (659 & n.2.)

Finally, Poché contrasted the decision with enforceable reconciliation agreements following separation. He inferred from the allegation that Hildegard forwent independent living that she might otherwise have left; footnote 3 identifies that inference. The majority called it speculation. The dissent thought it perverse to make prior separation improve spouses’ ability to bargain for the very conduct that would preserve their marriage. He acknowledged the unsettling prospect of negotiations at a sickbed but rejected coerced altruism as a substitute for a demonstrated legal policy. He would have allowed Hildegard an opportunity to try to enforce the alleged agreement, rather than treating it as invalid at the pleading threshold. (660–662 & n.3.)

## Exact disposition and reported later proceedings

By a two-to-one vote, the court affirmed the judgment of dismissal and awarded costs to respondents. It neither ordered transfer of the promised property nor awarded compensation for nursing or found that Hildegard’s allegations were false. The reporter records denial of rehearing on February 17, 1993 and denial of Supreme Court review on April 1, with Justice Kennard voting to grant review. Those later procedural notations do not constitute a Supreme Court merits opinion approving every aspect of the majority’s reasoning. (655, 662.)

Its description of a $100,000 bequest omits the husband’s joint-tenancy-residence interest that the actual complaint also describes.
