# Raila v. United States
355 F.3d 118 (2d Cir. 2004) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, judgment, and assigned passage

The Second Circuit decided No. 03–6057 on January 14, 2004, following argument on November 6, 2003. Judge Katzmann wrote for the unanimous panel of Judges Cardamone, Sotomayor, and Katzmann; there were no separate writings. It reversed the District of Connecticut’s dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. 119, 123; reproduced opinion ¶¶ 3–5, 27.

## Alleged events and proceedings

Lenore and Whitton Raila lived in Norwalk, Connecticut. Their complaint alleged that on the afternoon of November 26, 2001, Lenore exited the front door and slipped on a package a Postal Service employee had left immediately below the doorstep. She alleged a hip fracture, forehead abrasions, shock, numbness and tingling in her hands, and severe physical and emotional suffering. Whitton alleged loss of consortium. They attributed the injuries to negligent placement of the package and failure to warn the occupants of its dangerous location. Those facts were accepted as alleged for purposes of the dismissal, not established by trial findings. 119; ¶¶ 7–8.

The Railas sued the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act on September 11, 2002. The Government moved under Rule 12(b)(1), asserting that 28 U.S.C. § 2680(b), the postal matter exception, preserved immunity and deprived the district court of jurisdiction. Judge Covello granted that motion on February 26, 2003. He relied on the ordinary dictionary meaning of transmission as conveying something to another place and on district decisions treating transmission as extending from postal deposit through delivery, including negligent package placement. 119–120; ¶¶ 5, 8–10.

The panel reviewed both the jurisdictional dismissal and statutory interpretation de novo. It accepted the complaint’s allegations and favorable reasonable inferences and recited the then-applicable no-set-of-facts formulation for dismissal. 119–120; ¶¶ 7, 13. That procedural statement should be understood as the court’s historical formulation in this 2004 opinion.

## Statute and issue

The FTCA broadly waived sovereign immunity for specified injuries caused by government employees acting within the scope of employment, where a private person would be liable under the law of the place of the conduct. But § 2680(b) excepted claims arising from loss, miscarriage, or negligent transmission of letters or postal matter. The issue was whether negligent transmission encompassed a package’s placement causing personal injury outside the mail itself, or instead concerned failed or damaged delivery and resulting consequential injury. 119–120; ¶¶ 9–13.

The Government’s position treated the negligent act as part of conveying the package and therefore immune. The plaintiffs’ position distinguished harm involving the mail’s delivery or condition from a common-law injury caused by a postal employee during delivery. The panel accepted the latter interpretation and held that the exception did not bar these claims. 120–123; ¶¶ 13–24.

## Text, associated words, and statutory context

The panel began with statutory language and recognized that clear text would end the inquiry. It evaluated ambiguity in light of the entire scheme. Transmission alone could plausibly describe almost everything a postal worker did, because transmission was the Service’s business. That possibility did not make it the intended scope of this particular exception. 120; ¶¶ 14–15.

Applying noscitur a sociis, the court read negligent transmission alongside loss and miscarriage. The associated terms concerned damage to or delay of the postal material and consequential harm. Reading the final term consistently with them preserved immunity for those postal mishaps while allowing an injury such as slipping on a dangerously placed package. 120–121; ¶ 15.

The Court’s discussion in Kosak supplied further context. Other FTCA exceptions used broader language covering activities of the Tennessee Valley Authority or Panama Canal Company, or claims respecting customs and tax collection and detention. Section 2680(b) instead specified particular postal wrongs. Its narrower form was consistent with an important FTCA purpose: permitting recovery for accidents caused by postal employees driving vehicles. The Government conceded that such accidents were not barred. The panel found no logical basis for immunizing the employee’s negligent package placement while permitting the vehicle claim. 121–122; ¶¶ 16–18 & nn.1–3. Those notes reproduce the comparator statutory provisions; they do not resolve a separate customs claim in Raila.

## Legislative history and consequences

The legislative history was limited, but the court found it useful. A 1942 report on an earlier bill said that common-law torts, including government employees’ negligent automobile collisions, were to remain covered. At a 1940 hearing, Justice Department representative Abraham Holtzoff explained that registration could protect people sending postal matter and that unrestricted suits for mail loss or delay would be intolerable. The panel understood that explanation as directed to harm to the mail rather than every common-law injury caused during postal work. Its discussion of Birnbaum likewise distinguished an injury against which registering the parcel could not protect the plaintiff. 122; ¶¶ 19–20.

The Government’s reading also produced distinctions the panel regarded as absurd. If a delivery driver simultaneously threw a package into one pedestrian and drove the truck into another, it would immunize the flying-package injury while permitting the vehicle claim. If two postal customers slipped on separate puddles, one created by a negligently broken parcel and one by a negligent janitor, it would immunize one injury but not the other. These were interpretive hypotheticals, not additional accidents proved in this litigation. 122–123; ¶¶ 21–23.

The Government agreed at argument with those immunity consequences. Footnote 4 preserves an important qualification to the second hypothetical: dropping a package does not necessarily constitute negligence, but the Government conceded the different treatment if the employee was negligent in doing so. Thus the court assumed negligence for comparing the statutory consequences; it did not impose strict liability for every dropped parcel. 123 n.4; ¶ 31 in the reproduction.

## Holding, disposition, and limits

The postal matter exception did not bar the Railas’ claims. The panel reversed the order dismissing the complaint and remanded. It did not determine breach, causation, damages, exhaustion, or ultimate liability under Connecticut law. 123; ¶¶ 24, 27.

The court declined to resolve the Government’s prediction that the interpretation would produce substantial litigation. Its task was to interpret the statutory words consistently with Congress’s intent, not select an immunity policy by projecting caseload. Congress remained able to amend the statute. 123; ¶ 25.
