# National Maritime Union of America v. Commander, Military Sealift Command

**824 F.2d 1228 (D.C. Cir. 1987) — United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit — July 31, 1987 — No. 86-5210.** Judge Bork wrote for the panel of Judges Bork, Starr, and D.H. Ginsburg. There were no separate opinions. The assigned discussion and quotations appear in the November continuation at physical pages 131–132, printed pages 1188–1189.

Internal disagreement among a union's members did not by itself defeat associational standing. Nevertheless, that did not mean the unions could proceed with every claim or win the suit. Their attack on a missing wage determination lacked a sufficiently traceable and redressable injury. Their separate administrative-appeal and employment-preference claims failed on the merits. The court therefore affirmed judgment for the defendants despite accepting the unions' position on the conflict-of-interests question emphasized in the assignment.

## Procurement and competing employment interests

The Navy's Military Sealift Command operated twelve government-owned oceanographic ships using permanent and temporary civil-service mariners. Under procedures implementing OMB Circular A-76, it compared continued government operation with private bids. The initial solicitation omitted Service Contract Act wage requirements. Marine Transport Lines received the tentative award, and affected employees and their representatives had an opportunity to appeal administratively. The administrative appeals were denied. **1230.**

The government then concluded that the Act applied, rescinded the tentative award, and requested new offers and a revised government cost estimate. Marine Transport unsuccessfully challenged that course in a separate district-court suit. The amended solicitation required the successful contractor to pay wages and benefits determined by the Labor Department, retroactively from the award date. The Department lacked a database permitting a timely wage schedule, so neither the solicitation nor the contract contained the actual schedule. Lavino Shipping Company became the lowest bidder and won. **1230–1231 & n.1.**

The Command refused another administrative appeal because the appealable issues could and should have been raised after the original tentative award. It also limited contractual first-refusal employment rights to displaced permanent mariners. In a tight labor market, it considered those career employees particularly difficult to place and did not want temporary employees competing with them for the protected opportunities. **1230–1231.**

Three plaintiff unions represented civil-service mariners and Marine Transport employees, but not Lavino employees. They wanted performance enjoined, the Lavino award vacated, and a new solicitation after the wage determination. Alternatively, they sought an administrative appeal and first-refusal rights for temporary mariners. Thus, one demand might benefit temporary members at the expense of permanent members represented by the same organizations. Other unions intervened for the defendants. **1231 & nn.2–3.**

## Procedure and the association question

On cross-motions for summary judgment, the District Court dismissed for lack of standing. It concluded that the unions were outside the Service Contract Act's zone because they did not represent the successful contractor's employees. It also regarded Circular A-76 as an internal management instrument without protected private interests. The unions appealed. **1231.**

The circuit applied the three Hunt conditions: individual member standing, germaneness to organizational purposes, and no requirement of individual participation in the claim or requested relief. Germaneness was satisfied because the unions sought employment opportunities for members. The court considered whether conflicting interests defeated representative standing, without needing to classify that objection as a separate fourth condition or an aspect of Hunt's third requirement. **1231–1232 & n.5.**

The first-refusal demand presented a genuine conflict: expanding temporary workers' opportunities could reduce permanent workers' opportunities. Bork read *UAW v. Brock* as suggesting that such conflicts should not automatically destroy associational standing, while acknowledging that the Supreme Court's treatment was not entirely clear. He also supplied an independent justification. Associations routinely resolve internal policy disputes through their own procedures; accepting the benefits of association often involves accepting a collective position unfavorable to some members. Mere disagreement did not establish improper representation. **1232–1234.**

The court did not declare dissenting members bound individually or decide every possible safeguard. It discussed intervention, organizational procedural challenges, and a possible duty-of-fair-representation problem where the union's exclusive bargaining authority was implicated. That duty was not implicated by these claims. Nor did the holding depend on an assumption that later individual litigation would always escape preclusion; note 9 expressly declined to rely on that possibility. **1233–1234 & n.9.**

The resulting association holding was conditional: the unions could represent some members despite conflicting interests **if those members themselves had standing**. The court next examined the individual-standing requirement rather than treating the association ruling as the end of the case. **1234.**

## Service Contract Act claim

The Act protected prevailing wages for employees of government contractors, not government employees' wages or losing bidders' employment opportunities. Its legal duties ran to the successful contractor's employees. The unions therefore did not assert invasion of a statutory wage right belonging to the members they represented. Their own potential interest in organizing Lavino employees did not help: voiding the Lavino contract would undermine that claimed organizational opportunity. **1231–1232 n.4, 1234–1235 & n.10.**

The unions' lost jobs and job opportunities were real economic injuries. The difficulty was connecting them to the particular alleged defect—the absence of a specified wage schedule—and showing likely relief through a new bidding process. The contract already required retroactive payment of whatever prevailing wages the Department determined. Nothing established that inserting a schedule would change the winner. Marine Transport was not a party, might not bid again, and had supplied no account of how its wage assumptions would change. The government's own operating-cost estimate did not turn on the Act's wage requirements. Independent decisions and uncertain wage calculations therefore made the causal and remedial chain speculative. **1235–1236 & n.11.**

The court distinguished a disappointed bidder's challenge. A bidder could assert its own right to a fair procurement process, which a lawful new bidding process would remedy even without a guaranteed contract award. Its participation could also clarify its readiness to bid and cost assumptions. But the bidder's right belonged to Marine Transport, not these unions. Notes 12–13 preserved limits concerning bidders outside active consideration and differences between requests for rebidding and requests for direct award. **1236–1238 & nn.12–13.**

The court rejected the wage claim for Article III causation and redressability reasons. It expressly did **not** decide the Service Contract Act zone-of-interests issue on which the District Court had relied. **1238 n.14, 1240.**

## Administrative appeal and first refusal

The court accepted Article III standing to assert the allegedly denied appeal and first-refusal rights: direct denial of those rights was traceable to the Command, and orders granting them would redress it. It assumed, without deciding, the remaining zone, cause-of-action, and justiciability questions because both claims failed substantively even under the less demanding of the proposed review standards. The District Court's undisputed-fact findings on the cross-motions supplied a record for that merits disposition. **1238–1239.**

The regulations required an administrative appeal procedure, but did not require a second futile appeal. The permissible issues concerned the choice to contract out, not the relative merits of competing private bids or management decisions. The government cost estimate and gap between public and private costs had not materially changed. The unions had already had incentive and opportunity to challenge contracting out after the first award. Denying another appeal was reasonable. **1239.**

The first-refusal provisions referred to displaced employees, and a Navy handbook described temporary employees as eligible. The court distinguished eligibility from an unconditional entitlement and deferred to the Command's interpretation. Protecting permanent career employees against added competition in a tight market was also a reasonable policy basis. The court did not order equal first-refusal rights for temporary employees. **1239–1240.**

## Judgment and assignment

The court **affirmed judgment for defendants**. Its association ruling removed the objection based solely on member conflicts; it did not supply the missing individual standing for the wage claim or overcome the merits defects of the other claims. The assigned quotations accurately emphasize internal organizational decisionmaking and possible protections for dissenters, but omit the later analysis and unfavorable disposition. Reading them as an overall union victory would misstate the actual judgment.

This brief describes the 1987 decision, including its then-current standing and regulatory-deference reasoning. It does not independently verify that every procedural doctrine or procurement regulation discussed remains unchanged today.
