# Citizens to Preserve Overton Park, Inc. v. Volpe

**Course:** Legislation and the Regulatory State
**ID:** LRS-CB-0884

## Citation

Citizens to Preserve Overton Park, Inc. v. Volpe, 401 U.S. 402 (1971), decided March 2, 1971. Justice Marshall wrote the Court's opinion, announced by Justice Stewart. Assigned excerpt: casebook pp. 884–889, with all four accompanying editorial notes through p. 895, November first scan, PDF pp. 103–114. Full judicial opinion: [Justia reproduction](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/401/402/); [original U.S. Reports scan](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep401/usrep401402/usrep401402.pdf). The Court's opinion appears at pp. 404–421; Justice Black's separate opinion and Justice Blackmun's statement appear at pp. 421–423.

## Parties

The petitioners were private citizens and local and national conservation organizations challenging federal approval of a highway through Overton Park in Memphis. Respondent John Volpe was the Secretary of Transportation, whose department had authorized federal funding for the project. The state and city had participated in planning and land acquisition, but the statutory decision at issue belonged to the federal Secretary. The petitioners sought judicial review of that approval, including the consideration of alternate routes and measures to reduce damage to the park. 401 U.S. at 406–409.

## Material Facts

Overton Park comprised 342 acres near central Memphis, including recreational facilities, a zoo, and approximately 170 acres of forest. The planned segment of Interstate 40 was a six-lane expressway that would take twenty-six acres and separate the zoo from the remainder of the park. Most of the roadway would be depressed, with an exception where it crossed a creek. The park route had received federal approval at earlier stages before Congress enacted the park-protection provisions governing the challenged final decisions. Id. at 406–407.

Section 4(f) of the Department of Transportation Act and § 138 of the Federal-Aid Highway Act prohibited approval of a federally funded project using public parkland unless there was no feasible and prudent alternative and the project included all possible planning to minimize harm. After those requirements became applicable, the Secretary announced concurrence with local officials on the route in April 1968. Tennessee acquired the park right-of-way in 1969, and final approval of the route and design followed that November. Neither announcement contained the Secretary's factual findings or an explanation of why alternative routes and harm-reduction measures were insufficient. Id. at 405, 407–408.

The petitioners asserted that routes north or south of the park were feasible and prudent. They also proposed tunneling or drainage methods that might reduce the damage if the park route were used. They disputed whether the Secretary had made an independent judgment rather than merely accepted local views. The government defended the approval through affidavits prepared for the litigation; petitioners supplied contrary affidavits and sought to depose a former Federal Highway Administrator. These were competing assertions, not findings that the Supreme Court resolved in favor of either proposed route or design. Id. at 408–409 & nn.17–20.

## Procedural History

The suit began in the District of Columbia and was transferred, at the Secretary's request, to the Western District of Tennessee. The District Court granted summary judgment for the Secretary. It considered formal findings unnecessary, refused the requested deposition as an intrusion into an administrator's mental processes, and treated the affidavits as insufficient to show that the Secretary exceeded his broad authority. The Sixth Circuit affirmed. Id. at 406, 409 & n.7.

The Supreme Court stayed construction and granted expedited review. It agreed that no applicable statute required formal findings for this decision, but rejected the lower courts' treatment of the Secretary's discretion and their exclusive reliance on litigation affidavits. Because the full administrative record was not before the reviewing courts, the Supreme Court required further review in the District Court under the proper statutory standards. Id. at 406, 409–420.

## Issue

Was the Secretary's approval insulated from review as action committed to agency discretion, or did the park-protection statutes supply legal standards a court could apply? If review was available, which APA standard governed this informal approval, and could the courts sustain it on litigation affidavits without examining the whole administrative record? The associated procedural question was whether formal findings were mandatory and, if not, what explanation could be obtained to make judicial review effective. Id. at 410–420.

## Rule and Holding

The approval was reviewable. The exception in APA § 701(a)(2) for action committed to agency discretion is narrow and did not apply because the statutes supplied meaningful limits: parkland could be used only when no feasible and prudent alternative existed and all possible harm-minimizing planning had occurred. Ordinary cost savings and avoidance of displacement did not permit unrestricted balancing against preservation. Alternatives had to present unusual problems or costs or disruption of extraordinary magnitude to justify use of the park on prudence grounds. Id. at 410–413.

The applicable inquiry required review of statutory authority, arbitrary or capricious choice, and required procedures. It called for careful examination of the relevant facts without substituting judicial judgment for the Secretary's. Neither substantial-evidence review under § 706(2)(E) nor trial de novo under § 706(2)(F) applied to this approval. Formal findings were not required in these circumstances, but litigation affidavits were an inadequate substitute for the full record considered by the agency. Id. at 413–420.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court reversed and remanded for the District Court to review the Secretary's decision on the full administrative record before him when he acted. The District Court could obtain a necessary explanation through appropriate testimony or findings and was to assess later explanations critically. The Supreme Court did not decide that a particular alternative route was feasible and prudent, order a tunnel, or finally prohibit the highway. Its majority also did not require immediate remand to the Secretary for a new hearing; that was the remedial position taken in Justice Black's separate opinion. Id. at 420–422.

## Reasoning

1. The statutes contained law a court could apply. Congress had not prohibited review. The relevant provisions used a prohibition with defined exceptions rather than conferring wholly open-ended authority to choose a route. The words concerning feasible and prudent alternatives and planning to reduce harm therefore supplied standards for testing the Secretary's exercise of discretion. The existence of some discretion did not itself trigger § 701(a)(2). Id. at 410–411.

2. Feasibility concerned engineering; prudence did not authorize equal-weight balancing of all considerations. The government conceded that an alternative's feasibility turned on sound engineering. It argued for broad balancing under prudence, including cost and community disruption. The Court reasoned that those ordinary considerations usually favored already publicly owned parkland, where construction would avoid displacement of homes and businesses. If they routinely outweighed preservation, the protective statute would accomplish little. Congress had instead made park preservation paramount, while permitting use in genuinely unusual circumstances. Id. at 411–413.

3. The Secretary had to exercise independent judgment. The federal statutory duty could not be fulfilled simply by reproducing a local planning preference. The Court's discussion and legislative-history footnote treated the Secretary as responsible for looking beyond information provided by state and local officials. Whether he actually performed that task remained a factual issue for proper review; the opinion did not resolve the conflicting affidavits itself. Id. at 408–409, 412 n.28.

4. The type of agency action determined the relevant review provisions. This was approval of funding and a particular route, rather than rulemaking or a decision based on an adjudicatory hearing record required by statute. The local public hearing served to inform the community and obtain views, rather than to create the exclusive evidentiary record for the agency's decision. The two circumstances the Court identified for § 706(2)(F) de novo factfinding likewise were absent. The court still had to perform an adequate inquiry under the other applicable parts of § 706. Id. at 413–415.

5. The court had to examine authority, reasoning, and procedure separately. First, it had to identify the narrow range of choices Congress allowed and determine whether the Secretary reasonably could place the facts within that range. Second, it had to examine whether the choice addressed the relevant factors and reflected a clear error of judgment. Third, it had to determine compliance with applicable procedures. A presumption of regularity did not eliminate any of those inquiries. At the same time, searching review did not authorize the judge to select the preferred highway route. Id. at 415–417.

6. The lack of formal findings was not independently dispositive. Neither park-protection statute required the findings the petitioners demanded, and the APA's formal-findings provisions did not apply to this action. The decision itself was unambiguous: the Secretary had approved a particular route and design. The issue was whether it was adequately supported and explained for review. Thus the majority declined to invalidate the action simply because it lacked a formal document labeled findings. Id. at 417.

7. A later departmental order did not require the Supreme Court to bypass available record review. DOT Order 5610.1 required formal findings but was issued after the challenged approval. Petitioners invoked the principle of applying the law in effect at appellate decision. The Court considered the changed circumstances, including land acquisition and clearing, and the availability of an administrative record permitting prompt review. It therefore declined to order an automatic remand to the Secretary solely to prepare formal findings. The District Court nevertheless could use findings if necessary to obtain an adequate explanation. Id. at 417–420 & nn.31–33.

8. Litigation affidavits could not replace the administrative record. The affidavits were prepared as defenses in court and did not constitute the whole record that had informed the approval. Reviewing those statements alone risked evaluating a reconstructed justification instead of the administrative action itself. The lower courts therefore had not performed the review the APA required. The proper starting point was the full contemporaneous record. Id. at 419–420.

9. Supplemental explanation had to illuminate the decision actually made. The Court recognized that the existing documents might fail to disclose which factors the Secretary considered or how he interpreted them. It allowed the District Court to obtain testimony from participating officials if necessary, while emphasizing that inquiries into mental processes ordinarily should be avoided. When contemporaneous findings exist, a strong showing of bad faith or improper behavior is required before such inquiry. Here there were no formal findings, so explanation might be indispensable. The majority did not order an unrestricted deposition of every official. Id. at 420.

10. The remedy preserved critical review of retrospective accounts. The Secretary might instead prepare findings supplying the missing explanation. The Court acknowledged that this would have a post hoc element and instructed the District Court to view it critically. It left the choice of an effective and expeditious explanatory method to that court. This qualification prevents reading the opinion as either categorically prohibiting later explanation or freely permitting entirely new grounds to replace those underlying the decision. Id. at 420–421.

## Separate Opinions

Justice Black, joined by Justice Brennan, agreed that the Court of Appeals should be reversed but disagreed with sending the matter to the District Court for record review. He believed the Secretary had not performed the statutory duty at all and wanted the entire matter returned to the Secretary for the hearing, findings, and policy determinations required to implement Congress's protection of public parks. Whether those findings were called formal or informal seemed immaterial to him. His conclusion that the Secretary had already failed his duty was stronger than the majority's decision to require adequate judicial examination of what occurred. Id. at 421–422 (Black, J., separate opinion).

Justice Blackmun fully joined the majority opinion and judgment. In a separate statement, he emphasized that the project reflected more than a decade of planning under successive officials and that the 1966 and 1968 statutes imposed new requirements on a substantially developed undertaking. That history, in his view, explained the sparse record without excusing the need for the review ordered by the Court. Id. at 422–423.

Justice Douglas did not participate. The six-Justice majority included Blackmun; Black and Brennan agreed with reversal but favored a different remand. Id. at 421–423.

## Notes

The assigned excerpt omits the Supreme Court’s separate opinions and compresses the later DOT-order discussion. Those matters above come from the full opinion. All four following notes at casebook pp. 889–895 are editorial material; their later litigation, comparisons and critiques are distinguished from this appeal’s judgment.

1. Reviewability (889): the editors draw attention to judicial review of informal action without a specially prescribed agency procedure or an express statute authorizing review of that particular decision. They link the presumption of reviewability and the narrow committed-discretion exception to later treatment.

2. Informal records and subsequent history (889–891): the editors discuss how judicial review encourages agencies to assemble records and administrative routines. They describe further litigation after this remand, including the Secretary’s refusal to approve the park route and the later Sixth Circuit rejection of a requirement to name an alternative route before refusing approval. Their criticism of fitting intergovernmental planning into a rationalized record is editorial analysis; the later decisions retain their own source-review statuses.

3. Chenery I (891–892): the editors distinguish review of reasons the agency actually adopted from judicial substitution of a different policy rationale. Overton Park’s allowance of a critically assessed later explanation illuminates the original decision; it does not license counsel to invent grounds the agency never adopted. The separate Chenery decisions retain their own independent audits.

4. Bad faith and Department of Commerce v. New York (892–895): the editors discuss the 2019 census litigation, the enlarged record and discovery, and differing voting coalitions on reasonableness and the contrived stated rationale. This is a later application, not a holding in the 1971 case. The editors emphasize that not every motive must be disclosed and question the likely reach of that later decision; its own brief supplies its complete judicial account and audit status.

Keep reviewability under § 701 separate from the standard and record of review under § 706. A statute can provide standards sufficient for judicial review even though the agency retains discretion in applying them. The reviewing court then examines the actual decision within those constraints. The term plenary review in the remand does not transform the proceeding into a trial de novo on the preferable highway route. Id. at 410–420.

The opinion's compressed statement about substantial-evidence review at p. 414 should be read with the text of § 706(2)(E), reproduced across pp. 413–414 in its footnote 30: that provision addresses proceedings under §§ 556–557 or otherwise reviewed on the record of a statutorily provided hearing. Do not turn the opinion's reference to rulemaking into an unqualified statement that every § 553 notice-and-comment rule receives § 706(2)(E) review. The actual holding here is that this route approval did not. This brief describes the historical statutes and decision rather than certifying present highway-approval requirements.
