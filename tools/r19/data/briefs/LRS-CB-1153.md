## Parties

The petitioners were the Association of Data Processing Service Organizations and another petitioner, Data Systems, Inc., engaged in selling data-processing services. They challenged a ruling by the Comptroller of the Currency permitting national banks to supply certain data-processing services. The respondents included the Comptroller and American National Bank & Trust Company, a national bank entering the petitioners' field of business. The processors sought judicial review of the ruling on the ground that the Comptroller had authorized competition forbidden by federal banking law. 397 U.S. at 151-152.

Their claimed stake was economic. They were prospective competitors of an institution receiving the benefit of the challenged regulatory interpretation. That relationship mattered both to whether they alleged an actual injury and to whether the banking statutes arguably protected or regulated the interests they asserted. Whether those statutes ultimately prohibited the bank's services was a further question that the Supreme Court did not resolve. Id. at 151-156, 157-158.

## Material facts and statutory setting

The Comptroller's 1966 ruling permitted national banks, as an incident to banking services, to make data-processing services available to other banks and to bank customers. The processors alleged that the ruling opened their market to national banks and would cause them economic injury. Their complaint also supplied a particular example: American National Bank was performing or preparing to perform services for two customers with whom Data Systems had previously agreed or negotiated to provide data processing. This allegation connected the challenged ruling to identifiable business competition rather than only disagreement with banking policy. Id. at 151-152.

Two statutes framed the controversy. The National Bank Act gave national banks specified banking powers and incidental powers necessary to conduct banking. Section 4 of the Bank Service Corporation Act of 1962 restricted a bank service corporation to providing bank services for banks. The processors contended that the statutory restrictions on banking-related enterprises also prevented the Comptroller from authorizing the national bank's commercial data-processing activity. The Court considered the statutes' implications for access to review while leaving their ultimate application to the bank's activities undecided. Id. at 154-156, 157-158.

The distinction between a national bank and a bank service corporation was therefore significant. Section 4 did not straightforwardly name every institution whose conduct was challenged. The Court asked whether its text and legislative background arguably placed competitors within a protected or regulated class. It did not first require the processors to establish that the provision conclusively prohibited this national bank from serving these customers. Id. at 155-156.

## Procedural history

The processors sued to challenge the Comptroller's ruling. The District Court dismissed for lack of standing. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, applying an approach under which a competitor needed a recognized legal interest, such as an interest arising from a charter or contract, statutory protection against competition, or congressional authorization to represent the public interest. The lower courts' threshold analysis thus depended heavily on whether the processors could establish a legal entitlement to protection from the competition. Id. at 151-153.

The Supreme Court reviewed that dismissal, not a judgment resolving the legality of the banking services after a merits hearing. It separated the existence of a concrete stake from the existence of an ultimately enforceable substantive entitlement. It then examined whether the processors' interests were arguably within the relevant statutes' zone and whether Congress had foreclosed review. The Court held that the processors could proceed and reversed the judgment. Id. at 152-158.

On remand, the substantive banking-law questions remained for adjudication. The Supreme Court expressly declined to decide whether the National Bank Act or the Bank Service Corporation Act actually prohibited the Comptroller's ruling. Its conclusion that the petitioners could obtain a hearing did not establish liability, invalidate the ruling on the merits, or award compensation for lost customers. Id. at 157-158.

## Questions presented

Did competing data-processing businesses allege the injury necessary for Article III standing when a federal ruling authorized national-bank competition, including competition for two customers whom one petitioner had already approached? Did APA § 702 require them to prove an existing legal right to be free from that competition, or was it enough that their interests were arguably within the zone protected or regulated by the relevant banking statutes? Finally, did the statutory scheme preclude judicial review despite their qualifying interest? Id. at 151-157.

## Rule and holding

The Court identified injury in fact, economic or otherwise, as the initial standing inquiry. It rejected the legal-interest test as a threshold requirement because the existence of the substantive legal interest asserted ordinarily belongs to the merits. The petitioners' alleged competitive losses, supported by the particular two-customer allegation, supplied injury in fact. Id. at 152-153.

The Court additionally asked whether the interest sought to be protected was arguably within the zone of interests protected or regulated by the statute or constitutional guarantee involved. Under the Court's then-current account of APA § 702, the processors satisfied that inquiry. The Bank Service Corporation Act and its legislative setting furnished sufficient indications that competitors' interests were within the relevant zone; conclusively proving the banking-law violation was unnecessary at this stage. Id. at 153-156.

The Court also found no congressional intent to preclude judicial review under APA § 701(a). Neither the National Bank Act nor the Bank Service Corporation Act justified denying these injured competitors access to review. These holdings allowed litigation of the merits without deciding them. Id. at 156-158.

## Actual judgment

The Supreme Court reversed the Eighth Circuit's judgment and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. The petitioners obtained access to a merits hearing. The Court left open whether either banking statute actually prohibited the Comptroller's ruling. 397 U.S. at 157-158.

## Reasoning of the Court

### 1. A substantive legal entitlement cannot serve as the entire gateway inquiry
The Court began with the lower courts' legal-interest formulation. Asking whether the processors possessed a
legally protected entitlement against competition risked deciding the controversy's substance under the label
of standing. The processors' claim was precisely that the governing statutes limited the Comptroller's
authority to permit this activity. Requiring them to establish that proposition before obtaining judicial review
would conflate the right to present their claim with the right to prevail on it. Id. at 152-153.

The Court's alternative began with the plaintiff's actual stake. Economic loss is a familiar concrete injury, and the allegation about two customers illustrated how the ruling could affect Data Systems' business. The Court did not require a common-law monopoly or a contractual right against the bank as a prerequisite to recognizing that competitive harm. The question whether the law protected the business from the harm remained distinct. Id. at 152.

### 2. The Court retained a statutory connection through the zone-of-interests inquiry
Article III injury did not exhaust the majority's analysis. APA § 702 referred both to persons suffering a legal
wrong and to persons adversely affected or aggrieved within the meaning of a relevant statute. The Court
understood the latter language to reach beyond persons who could already prove a traditionally recognized
legal interest. It nevertheless required an arguable relationship between the interest asserted and the statutory
protection or regulation invoked. Id. at 153-154.

The word "arguably" matters to the operation of this test. The Court could recognize competitors as appropriate challengers without deciding the disputed scope of the bank's statutory powers. The threshold statutory inquiry was sufficient to permit examination of that merits question. It did not guarantee a successful answer to it. Id. at 154-158.

### 3. Injury need not be economic, although these petitioners alleged economic harm
The Court explained that interests supporting standing could include aesthetic, conservational, and
recreational concerns. That discussion broadened the categories of cognizable interests beyond financial loss.
In this case, however, the Court relied on commercial competition. The opinion did not decide that
dedication to conservation, by itself and without injury to the plaintiff, always supplies standing. The
casebook's later placement of Sierra Club is useful for seeing the separate importance of identifying who
suffers the harm. Id. at 153-154; casebook pp. 1156-1157.

### 4. The banking provisions supplied an arguable connection to competitors
The Court examined § 4's limitation on bank service corporations and the legislative concern with banks
entering nonbanking commercial fields. The relationship between those restrictions and the processors'
competitive interests was sufficient for the threshold inquiry. An important limit is that the Court did not
definitively hold, in deciding standing, that every restriction on a bank service corporation directly governed
every national bank. That was part of the disputed substantive problem. 397 U.S. at 155-156, 157-158.

The Court also considered earlier competitor-standing decisions, including Hardin v. Kentucky Utilities Co. and Chicago v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co. It treated them as evidence that a challenger need not establish a conventional private right before contesting administrative action affecting a statutorily relevant interest. Its discussion of Arnold Tours likewise rejected an approach that would make access to review depend on already proving the disputed statutory protection. Id. at 154-156.

### 5. Standing and statutory preclusion were separate questions
Even a person with a sufficient injury and statutory connection might face a statute precluding review. The
Court therefore separately applied APA § 701(a), which addresses statutory preclusion and action committed
to agency discretion. It read the APA's judicial-review provisions generously and found nothing in these
banking enactments demonstrating an intent to foreclose review by the processors. Id. at 156-157.

The absence of an express competitor-review provision did not itself defeat the action. The Court's approach allowed review under the APA when the challenger had the required injury and statutory relationship and the governing scheme did not exclude review. This analysis concerns whether a court may consider the claim; the court must still apply the substantive banking law after the threshold has been crossed. Id. at 156-158.

### 6. The disposition preserved the distinction the opinion developed
The Court ended by expressly withholding a decision on whether the ruling violated the National Bank Act
or the Bank Service Corporation Act. That reservation was the procedural consequence of its reasoning. The
lower courts had stopped the case at standing, so the processors were entitled to have their substantive
challenge heard rather than to receive a Supreme Court merits victory by implication. Id. at 157-158.

## Separate opinion: Brennan, joined by White

### Agreement on the result; disagreement with the added standing test

Brennan's joint opinion applied to both Data Processing and Barlow. He agreed that the plaintiffs in both cases should obtain review but objected to placing the zone-of-interests inquiry inside standing. In his account, injury in fact supplied the essential standing inquiry. Whether Congress permitted this plaintiff to challenge the agency and whether the plaintiff's claimed legal interest was substantively protected belonged to two further, largely distinct inquiries. 397 U.S. at 167-172 (Brennan, J., concurring in the result and dissenting).

### Three inquiries with different functions

Standing identified a person with an actual stake sufficient for an adversarial judicial controversy. Reviewability asked whether Congress had foreclosed judicial examination of the action altogether or at the instance of the particular plaintiff. The merits asked whether the asserted legal interest was protected and whether the agency had violated that protection. Brennan did not deny the relevance of statutory text or legislative purpose. He disputed where those considerations belonged and how much a plaintiff had to establish at each stage. Id. at 168-176.

Where no statute expressly granted review, indications that the plaintiff's class was a statutory beneficiary could support reviewability. Given the APA's presumption favoring review, Brennan considered slight indications sufficient for that purpose. Stronger proof might be necessary to win on the merits. Thus an injured competitor could be entitled to a judicial hearing and nevertheless lose after the court fully construed the banking statute. Id. at 173-176.

### Why Brennan considered the majority's formulation problematic

Brennan asked what the plaintiff had to show to enter the statutory "zone": a broadly stated competitive interest, a particular legally protected interest, an allegation, or evidence approaching a merits demonstration. If the new test demanded the last of these, it threatened to reintroduce the legal-interest barrier the majority had rejected. If it merely determined whether Congress allowed this injured person to obtain review, Brennan thought it should be addressed openly as reviewability rather than as standing. Id. at 176-178.

His objection was therefore substantial and methodological. The majority required injury plus the arguable statutory-zone connection as part of its standing analysis. Brennan and White accepted access to review but sought to keep injury, statutory reviewability, and substantive entitlement analytically separate. Their agreement with reversal did not make the majority's additional test unanimous. Id. at 167-178.

## Study notes and limits

A useful application sequence is to identify the concrete harm, identify the statute invoked, ask the threshold statutory and reviewability questions actually used by the opinion, and then reserve the asserted statutory violation for the merits. Applied here, the two-customer allegation explains injury; the banking restrictions explain the arguable statutory connection; the absence of preclusion permits review; and the lawful scope of the bank's services remains undecided. This sequence is an analytical synthesis of the opinion, not an additional holding. 397 U.S. at 151-158.

### Assigned editorial notes

The first note (1156) contrasts the decision with the editors’ account of the earlier legal-wrong and special statutory-review routes. It identifies injury in fact and an arguable statutory zone as the new route actually used here. That historical assessment belongs to the editors; it does not change the Court’s reservation of the banking-law merits.

The second note (1156–1157) questions the opinion’s claim to statutory interpretation and expansion of standing. It observes that the precise injury-in-fact and zone-of-interests formulations do not appear in the APA and offers an alternative reading of § 702 that could ground both requirements in its adversely affected or aggrieved language, without necessarily invoking Article III. It also questions whether importing beneficiary or direct-injury requirements could restrict access in cases under specific review statutes. Its competing interpretation and speculation about historical development are editorial analysis, not Douglas’s holding or Brennan’s separate opinion.
