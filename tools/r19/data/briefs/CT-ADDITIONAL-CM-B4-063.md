# Cutter v. Powell
(1795) 6 T.R. 320; 101 E.R. 573 (K.B.) | Contracts

## Court, decision, and source locators

The Court of King’s Bench decided the case on June 9, 1795. Lord Kenyon, Chief Justice, and Justices Ashhurst, Grose, and Lawrence each explained why the express bargain defeated recovery on the stated facts. The report concludes with a postea to the defendant, qualified by an opportunity to supply further information about commercial usage before the end of the term; the case was not mentioned again. The citations below use the verified printed pages of the preserved Smith reprint, 1212–1219, rather than inventing unverified parallel-report page breaks. (Smith reprint 1212–1219; BAILII export judicial lines 104–147.)

## Facts and chronology

At Kingston, Jamaica, on July 31, 1793, the defendant, master of the Governor Parry, delivered a written promise to Thomas Cutter. Ten days after the ship arrived at Liverpool, he would pay Cutter thirty guineas, provided Cutter proceeded, continued, and did his duty as second mate from Jamaica to Liverpool. Cutter boarded on July 31, and the vessel sailed on August 2. He performed his duty until his death on September 20, before the ship reached Liverpool on October 9. The report treats the performed service and death as stated facts; it does not find desertion, misconduct, or fault by Cutter. (1212.)

The usual wage of a second mate hired by the month for such an outward-and-home voyage was £4 per month. Sailors hired for the run from Jamaica to England ordinarily received a gross sum, and the usual voyage lasted approximately eight weeks. Thus the thirty-guinea promise—£31 10s.—was substantially larger than the approximately £8 that two months’ service at the monthly rate would yield. The comparison concerned the ordinary rate for the entire voyage; it was not a calculation of damages awarded for Cutter’s actual service. (1212–1213, 1215, 1217.)

## Procedure and the question presented

Cutter’s administratrix sued in assumpsit for work and labor performed by the intestate. The defendant pleaded the general issue. At Lancaster, the jury returned a verdict for the plaintiff for £31 10s., subject to the King’s Bench’s opinion on the stated case. The legal question was whether the estate could recover compensation for completed service although the express condition requiring service through the voyage had not occurred. The court’s eventual disposition favored the defendant, rather than affirming the plaintiff’s conditional verdict. (1212, 1219.)

The case had been argued in the preceding term by J. Haywood for the plaintiff. The court postponed decision to investigate commercial usage. On renewed argument, Chambre represented the plaintiff and Wood the defendant. Recent examples showed merchants sometimes paying all wages and sometimes a proportionate amount under similar circumstances, but no fixed and settled usage was established. That evidentiary uncertainty matters because several judges expressly allowed that a proven usage might affect the bargain’s construction. (1213, 1216–1219.)

## The parties’ arguments

The administratrix sought a proportionate recovery on a quantum meruit. She distinguished the rule connecting sailors’ wages to earned freight: the ship had completed its voyage, and the owners had earned freight. She also distinguished forfeiture for desertion, since Cutter had died without wrongdoing. The impressed-seaman authority, Wiggins v. Ingleton, supported compensation despite interrupted service. Her counsel characterized the note as security for the promised sum rather than a comprehensive agreement excluding other compensation. The condition, on that view, ensured good conduct and discouraged desertion, and ought to be liberally construed. Edwards v. Child supported recovery for an outward voyage despite capture on the return. (1213–1214 and authority notes.)

Alternatively, counsel conceded that the condition might bar an action for the thirty guineas, but argued that the written promise covered only the specified event of completed service. Because that event had not happened, a different implied undertaking could compensate the labor actually supplied. That argument attempted to preserve restitution without enforcing the unfulfilled express promise. (1214–1215.)

The defendant answered that an express agreement excluded an inconsistent implied bargain. This agreement was entire and indivisible: the larger lump sum was promised only if Cutter continued his duty throughout the voyage. Cutter had elected the chance of earning substantially more than ordinary monthly wages, and his representative remained bound by that election. Counsel distinguished ordinary yearly hiring, where an executor might recover proportionately after death, from an agreement promising an unusually large sum expressly conditional on full service. Neither the owner’s receipt of freight nor Cutter’s blameless death satisfied that condition. Counsel invoked maritime loss and rent after a house burned as examples of innocent contracting parties bearing losses under their bargains. Kenyon interrupted the rent illustration to qualify it by describing equitable relief where a tenant surrendered the lease; the Smith editor’s additional note discusses later authorities and is not a new Cutter holding. (1215–1216 and notes.)

## Governing rule and application

Kenyon confined his analysis to the particular contract and stated facts, expressly avoiding a general determination about every marine contract. When parties had expressly agreed on compensation, the law did not imply an inconsistent obligation. Here the nearly fourfold difference between the thirty-guinea promise and ordinary two-month wages supported an all-or-nothing bargain. Kenyon described the arrangement as a kind of insurance: Cutter would obtain the larger sum if he performed the entire duty, and nothing under that bargain if he did not. This was an interpretation of the allocation of compensation risk, not a finding that Cutter purchased a separate insurance policy. (1216–1217.)

Kenyon’s reasoning remained sensitive to usage. The recently reported Liverpool examples were insufficient to establish one, but he stated that a universal commercial understanding construing these notes differently would cause him to abandon his present construction. The opinion therefore should not be converted into a rule that trade usage could never qualify the literal condition. (1216–1217.)

Ashhurst likewise treated full performance as a condition precedent to payment. The estate could not abandon the express agreement merely because an implied agreement would yield a better result. Cutter was not to blame for his failure to complete the duty, but lack of fault did not itself fulfill the condition or establish a right to compensation contrary to the bargain. (1217–1218.)

## The other judicial explanations

Grose considered both the particular stipulation and possible general maritime rules. He had examined the laws of Oleron, consulted merchant practice, and considered Chandler v. Greaves, where a sailor put ashore with a broken leg recovered full wages and the court refused a new trial. Those examples made him cautious: he did not know Chandler’s precise grounds, and his own inquiry into practice was not sufficiently satisfactory to support reliance. Nevertheless, this agreement’s large conditional compensation supported the conclusion that Cutter was entitled either to thirty guineas for the entire duty or to nothing under the promise. Grose suggested further inquiry into whether these commonly used notes had previously been construed. (1218–1219 and Chandler authority note.)

Lawrence invoked an entire-service agreement reported in Salkeld, involving a £100 annual promise and an unsuccessful £75 claim for three quarters’ service. He distinguished ordinary hired service, understood to permit proportionate wages even if service ended before the full year. A proven usage allowing proportionate compensation for a mate in Cutter’s position could similarly support the plaintiff. But absent that usage, the express entire contract controlled. Lawrence also suggested that the impressed-seaman example might be exceptional because the king’s officers commonly supplied a replacement, preserving service for the shipowner. He did not decide a universal rule for every interruption by public authority. (1219.)

## Exact result and significance for the assignment

All four judicial explanations favored the defendant on the contract and evidence before the court. The reported postea went to the defendant unless additional information about usage was supplied before term ended; no further mention occurred. The estate therefore recovered neither the promised thirty guineas nor a proportionate quantum meruit in this proceeding. The conditional procedural formulation and the judges’ openness to established usage are part of the result, not exceptions fabricated from later doctrine. (1219.)
