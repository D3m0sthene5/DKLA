# Biestek v. Berryhill
587 U.S. 97 (2019) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and assigned setting

The Supreme Court rejected a categorical rule that a vocational expert’s refusal to disclose underlying data on request always prevents the expert’s testimony from constituting substantial evidence. It affirmed the Sixth Circuit, while expressly declining to decide whether the particular administrative record adequately supported the denial of Michael Biestek’s benefits without that categorical rule. Justice Kagan wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas, Breyer, Alito, and Kavanaugh. Justice Sotomayor dissented separately; Justice Gorsuch dissented with Justice Ginsburg. The assigned material is editorial note 3 on printed page 920, physical PDF page 140 of the November first volume. It accurately emphasizes both the case-by-case standard and the majority’s limited resolution. Locators below refer to each separately numbered writing in the preserved April 1, 2019 slip opinion. (Majority 1, 11; syllabus 2.)

## Facts and administrative chronology

Biestek had worked as a carpenter and general construction laborer. Degenerative disc disease, Hepatitis C, and depression caused him to stop working, and he claimed eligibility for disability benefits beginning in October 2009. The Social Security Administration’s administrative law judge had to determine which other jobs he could perform despite his limitations and whether those jobs existed in significant numbers in the national economy. The hearing was adjudicative but informal and nonadversarial; governing law permitted evidence that would be inadmissible in court. Vocational experts supplied relevant employment knowledge from public sources, employer contacts, and their own placement experience. (Majority 2–3.)

At the July 21, 2015 hearing, vocational expert Erin O’Callaghan identified sedentary, relatively unskilled bench-assembler and sorter work compatible with the ALJ’s description of Biestek’s limitations, education, and work history. She testified that there were 240,000 assembler jobs and 120,000 sorter jobs nationally. Her background included five years as an SSA vocational expert and more than ten years counseling people with disabilities about work opportunities. These credentials were part of the majority’s factual account; they did not establish that every asserted employment figure was necessarily correct. (Majority 3–4.)

On cross-examination, counsel asked where the figures came from. O’Callaghan identified Bureau of Labor Statistics information and her own labor-market surveys. Asked to produce the private surveys, she invoked client confidentiality. Counsel proposed redacting names, but the ALJ intervened and said he would not require production in any form. Counsel did not continue questioning about the assembler and sorter figures. The resulting decision awarded benefits beginning in May 2013, when Biestek turned fifty and his advancing age adversely affected his employment prospects, but denied benefits for October 2009 through May 2013. The ALJ relied on O’Callaghan’s testimony to find that he could have adjusted to other work during that earlier period. This was a partial award, rather than a denial of the entire application. (Majority 4.)

## Judicial proceedings and the competing arguments

Biestek challenged the earlier-period denial in federal district court, arguing that the refusal to provide supporting data necessarily deprived O’Callaghan’s testimony of substantial evidentiary value. The district court rejected the argument, and the Sixth Circuit affirmed at 880 F.3d 778 (2018). The Sixth Circuit declined to adopt the Seventh Circuit’s rule concerning requested but undisclosed vocational data. The Supreme Court took the case to address that proposed categorical disqualification. (Majority 4–6.)

Biestek distinguished his proposed substantive sufficiency rule from a freestanding procedural entitlement to discovery. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26 required disclosures from experts in court, but the SSA’s informal hearing framework did not import that requirement. His contention instead concerned whether evidence already admitted had sufficient quality and quantity to sustain a finding. His rule depended on a request and refusal: an expert’s testimony without supporting data might suffice when nobody asked for the data, but could not suffice after a request was refused. He expressly accepted that this distinction did not arise from waiver of the substantial-evidence standard. (Majority 6–7 & n.1.)

The agency’s position, accepted by the majority, was that neither the absence of data nor a refusal to disclose it had an invariant effect on evidentiary sufficiency. Qualifications, explanations, methods, reasons for withholding, other record evidence, and the opportunity to question the expert could make the same kind of testimony adequate in one record and inadequate in another. The dissenters disputed both the majority’s characterization of the question presented and its decision to leave this record’s sufficiency unresolved. (Majority 7–11; Sotomayor 1–3; Gorsuch 6–8.)

## Governing standard and majority reasoning

Under 42 U.S.C. § 405(g), supported agency fact findings are conclusive. Substantial evidence requires more than a mere scintilla and asks whether a reasonable mind could accept the evidence as adequate to support the conclusion. The Court described the threshold as not high, but it remained an inquiry into the existing administrative record. Admissibility under informal hearing rules did not itself answer whether evidence satisfied this substantive threshold. (Majority 5–7.)

The majority began with common ground: testimony could sometimes suffice without underlying survey records. Its hypothetical expert had excellent credentials, a history of sound testimony, a reasoned account of representative employer surveys and accepted national extrapolation, cogent answers to questions, and no contradictory record evidence. Such testimony could readily be adequate even without production. Disclosure would improve reliability and constituted a best practice; the SSA’s own handbook instructed experts to have relevant materials available and be able to explain their sources and reasoning. The fuller instruction about explanations appears in Sotomayor’s quotation of the handbook. The Court did not turn that guidance into an absolute production condition for evidentiary sufficiency. A less qualified expert’s unsupported or contradicted testimony could fail the standard. (Majority 7–8 & n.2; Sotomayor 3 & sole note.)

Adding a refusal did not logically make every adequate record inadequate. An expert might have a legitimate confidentiality concern or face burdens associated with producing or redacting large amounts of material. An ALJ might independently have grounds to find the testimony trustworthy. Withholding could support an adverse inference, especially when the expert’s explanation was weak, no other reason supported confidence in the figures, or the data could easily have been supplied. But that inference was not invariably required. Conversely, an absence of a request did not immunize unreliable testimony: withholding could count against an expert even without a demand. The record as a whole, rather than the procedural fact of a request alone, remained decisive. (Majority 8–10.)

The Court similarly rejected an automatic cross-examination objection. Without underlying files, counsel could still inquire into sources, methods, and the derivation of the figures. Such questioning could reveal defects, and obstacles to effective examination could reduce the testimony’s weight. Yet absence of the files did not always prevent useful testing or destroy all indicia of reliability. The same limits on questioning existed when data were absent without an express refusal, a situation in which Biestek conceded that testimony might suffice. (Majority 10–11.)

The resulting rule requires assessment of all features of the expert’s testimony and the rest of the administrative record, with deference to the ALJ who observed the hearing. It permits an insufficiency finding when refusal combines with other shortcomings; it also permits a sufficiency finding when the record supplies adequate grounds for reliance. The majority deliberately stopped there. In its view, the petition and presentation sought the categorical rule, rather than a separate ruling on this record’s sufficiency under the proper standard. Footnote 1 answers the dissenters on that scope question. (Majority 6 n.1, 11.)

## Separate dissents

Sotomayor agreed with Gorsuch that the question presented included whether the actual record was sufficient. She stressed that the agency bore the burden of establishing work opportunities once the pertinent impairments were established, and the ALJ had a duty to develop the nonadversarial record. Instead, the ALJ interrupted the effort to obtain redacted surveys. The expert disclosed neither whom she surveyed, how many surveys she conducted, what information she gathered, nor a meaningful methodological explanation. Taken with the missing surveys, this conclusory testimony was inadequate. Sotomayor did not rule out reliance on undisclosed data in every case: a legitimate production obstacle and a fuller oral explanation could support a different result. Her footnote invoked the agency handbook’s expectations concerning available materials and explanations. (Sotomayor 1–3 & sole note.)

Gorsuch, joined by Ginsburg, compared the evidence to other forms of conclusory, speculative, false, or unsupported evidence that cannot sustain a fact finding. He argued that the public statistical source did not supply the specific figures at issue; the expert’s private information was therefore essential, yet neither produced nor meaningfully explained. A refusal to produce readily available stronger evidence supported an inference that the asserted conclusion lacked support. The majority’s hypothetical reliable experts did not describe the record actually before the Court. (Gorsuch 1–6.)

Gorsuch also rejected the majority’s account of the petition. A question about whether testimony can suffice after refusal allowed an answer identifying circumstances in which it could or could not. He would resolve the narrower category presented by an expert’s bare conclusion, requested but withheld support, and no demonstrated production obstacle. He understood the majority to leave lower courts free to find comparable records insufficient, but faulted it for withholding concrete guidance and relief in this case. His final discussion connected the substantial-evidence requirement to protection against arbitrary decisions grounded in undisclosed information. These are dissenting conclusions about this record, not majority findings that the record was insufficient. (Gorsuch 6–9.)

## Disposition and significance

The Court affirmed the Sixth Circuit’s judgment. It issued no production order and did not independently uphold the vocational estimates as sufficient under a case-specific application of the standard. The practical doctrinal distinction is between rejecting automatic evidentiary disqualification and endorsing the adequacy of a particular evidentiary record. The opinion accomplished the former and reserved the latter. Both the majority and Sotomayor’s dissent allowed that sufficiently explained expert testimony might qualify without disclosed source files; their disagreement included the proper treatment of the actual hearing and the scope of the question presented. (Majority 11; Sotomayor 3; Gorsuch 7–9.)
