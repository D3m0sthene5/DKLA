# Buckley v. Valeo
424 U.S. 1 (1976) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and statutory setting

Congress amended the Federal Election Campaign Act in 1974 after experience with large campaign contributions and their potential to buy political influence. The law combined contribution limits, expenditure limits, disclosure, public financing of presidential campaigns, and an independent Federal Election Commission. The challengers included Senator James Buckley, presidential candidate Eugene McCarthy, contributors, political parties, and advocacy organizations. Their different interests supplied standing to contest the provisions that restricted their campaign activities or subjected them to the Commission's authority. These were pre-enforcement constitutional challenges to the statutory system, rather than an appeal from a particular campaign-finance penalty. 424 U.S. at 7–13.

The principal contribution ceilings were $1,000 from a person to a candidate for an election, $5,000 from a qualifying political committee, and $25,000 in total annual contributions from an individual. The statute separately limited independent spending concerning a clearly identified candidate to $1,000 annually; a candidate's spending from personal or controlled family resources to amounts varying by office; and total campaign expenditures. Committees had to maintain identifying records for contributions exceeding $10 and publicly report identifying information for contributors exceeding $100. Other persons making certain contributions or expenditures exceeding $100 also had reporting obligations. These amounts describe the statute adjudicated in 1976. Id. at 7–8, 23–24, 35–39, 51–55, 62–64.

For the assigned appointments issue, the critical institutional fact was the Commission's composition. Its eight members included six voting Commissioners: two selected by the President pro tempore of the Senate on the majority and minority leaders' recommendations, two similarly selected by the Speaker of the House, and two selected by the President. All six required confirmation by both Houses of Congress, and each appointing authority had to select members from different parties. The Secretary of the Senate and Clerk of the House were nonvoting ex officio members. Commissioners served statutory terms and were not subject to another branch's day-to-day direction. Id. at 113, 126 n.162, 161–164.

The Commission did substantially more than collect reports. It could investigate, issue subpoenas, audit, promulgate regulations, formulate policy, issue advisory opinions protecting good-faith reliance, determine eligibility for public campaign funds, and seek repayment of overpayments. It had primary civil enforcement responsibility and could bring suits itself; certain criminal-law violations could trigger civil proceedings by the Attorney General at its request. It also had statutory authority, after proceedings, to disqualify a candidate who failed to file a required report. These powers made the nature of the institution, rather than its label, decisive. Id. at 109–113.

## Litigation, arguments, and questions presented

The litigation used the Act's special procedure for district-court certification of constitutional questions to the D.C. Circuit sitting en banc, followed by direct Supreme Court appeal. After a remand to develop findings and identify the issues, the Court of Appeals substantially upheld the challenged provisions. A three-judge District Court separately upheld the public-financing provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. Both judgments reached the Supreme Court. One reporting provision, § 437a, had been invalidated below and was not appealed; the Supreme Court's broad discussion of disclosure therefore does not reverse that unchallenged ruling. Id. at 8–12 & nn.6–7, 61 n.70.

On appointments, the challengers argued that Congress could create the Commission and specify its duties but could not appoint the officials executing and administering federal law. The Commission answered that the Constitution permitted congressional appointment of officials performing appropriate legislative functions; Congress's broad election-regulation authority and the Necessary and Proper Clause supposedly justified the arrangement. Its defenders also emphasized the danger of putting election enforcement entirely in presidential hands when an incumbent President might be a candidate. Id. at 118–119, 131–135.

The Court of Appeals had treated much of the appointments challenge as premature because particular powers had not yet been exercised. The Supreme Court found the challenge ripe. The Commission had begun rulemaking, additional statutory operations were approaching, and the certified questions placed its impending exercise of authority before the Court. Plaintiffs with concrete interests could challenge the structure of an agency that would determine their rights. This conclusion addressed timing without dispensing with Article III injury. Id. at 113–118.

The other branches of the case asked whether contribution and expenditure limits impermissibly restricted political expression and association; whether disclosure was overbroad or vague, especially for minor parties; and whether public campaign funding exceeded congressional power or discriminated against minor and new parties. Those holdings belong to the same judgment, although the assigned casebook principally reproduces the Commission issue.

## Appointments holding and governing rule

An appointee who exercises significant authority under federal law is an officer subject to the Appointments Clause. Principal officers require presidential nomination and Senate advice and consent. Congress may permit inferior officers to be appointed by the President alone, a court of law, or a department head. Congress and its presiding officers are not additional appointing authorities for these officers. The Commission's voting members were at least inferior officers; the Court did not need to settle a more precise principal-versus-inferior classification because their selection failed even the permissible methods for inferior officers. Id. at 124–128, 132.

None of the six voting members had been appointed constitutionally. The President's two selections were subject to House confirmation as well as Senate confirmation, and the other four originated with congressional officers who were neither courts nor department heads. Congress's express authority to choose its own legislative officers did not give those officers an implied power to select officials administering public law. The Court preserved Congress's capacity to appoint personnel performing appropriate legislative functions. Id. at 126–128.

Footnote 162 distinguishes officers from employees, describing employees as subordinate lesser functionaries. The Commissioners' statutory terms, independence, and significant responsibilities removed them from any plausible employee category. Buckley supplies a substantial-authority principle, but does not resolve every borderline federal position or hold that all federal workers require Article II appointment. Id. at 126 & n.162.

## Appointments reasoning and allocation of powers

The Court read the Clause within the Constitution's larger separation of powers. The branches are interdependent rather than completely isolated, but Congress cannot enlarge its legislative role by appointing those charged with enforcing its statutes. The convention history supported the allocation of appointments to the President with Senate participation: proposals assigning appointments to the Senate evolved into the final constitutional arrangement, and a proposed open-ended legislative alternative was rejected. Text, history, and the danger of congressional aggrandizement supported the same conclusion. Id. at 120–132.

Broad authority over a subject does not erase structural limits. Congress's power to regulate federal elections was no stronger, for this purpose, than its plenary authority in other legislative fields. Nor could the Necessary and Proper Clause justify a selection method forbidden by the Appointments Clause. The practical wish to insulate election enforcement from an incumbent President could not alter the constitutional allocation; congressional appointees might themselves be responsive to incumbent legislators. Id. at 132–135.

The Court also distinguished appointment from removal and daily supervision. Humphrey's Executor and Wiener permitted independence from presidential removal or direction in the settings those cases addressed, but the officers involved were selected through constitutionally prescribed appointments. Quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial functions therefore do not place an administrative agency outside the Appointments Clause. Buckley does not hold that every properly appointed administrator must be removable at presidential will. Id. at 133, 135–137, 140–141.

The consequence depended on the particular powers. Gathering, receiving, investigating, and disseminating information comparable to work Congress could assign a legislative committee remained permissible. The existing Commission could perform those functions. Civil enforcement litigation was different: vindicating public rights through judicial relief executes the law rather than merely assisting legislation. Rulemaking, consequential advisory opinions, and determinations of eligibility for funds or office likewise performed significant duties in administering public law, even when resembling legislation or adjudication. Those powers could be exercised only by properly appointed officers. Id. at 137–141.

The Court assumed for argument that a properly appointed agency might receive the statutory powers; it did not adjudicate the independent validity of every power. It specifically reserved whether candidate disqualification would itself violate Article I and declined to decide the separate challenge to Congress's statutory power to disapprove Commission regulations. Invalid appointment sufficed to decide the issue before it. Id. at 137 n.175, 140 n.176.

## Campaign-finance holdings in the full decision

The Court upheld the contribution ceilings. A contribution conveys support and association, while its conversion into further political communication generally involves the recipient's speech. The ceilings consequently imposed less direct restraint than expenditure limits. Preventing actual or apparent quid pro quo corruption was sufficiently important, and the restrictions were closely drawn to avoid unnecessary burdens on association. Bribery laws reached only the more blatant abuses, and disclosure alone did not remove the risks. The record did not show that the $1,000 ceiling prevented effective campaigns or invidiously disadvantaged challengers and minor parties. The higher ceiling for established political committees discouraged circumvention by persons simply labeling themselves committees; the annual aggregate ceiling also served an anticircumvention rationale. Id. at 19–38.

By contrast, expenditure limits directly reduced the quantity and reach of political expression. The $1,000 independent-spending restriction was first narrowed to express advocacy of election or defeat to avoid vagueness, but was unconstitutional even as narrowed. That narrowing weakened its claimed effectiveness against influence-buying, while genuinely independent spending lacked the prearrangement that gave coordinated spending its contribution-like character. The Court treated authorized or coordinated expenditures as contributions subject to the surviving limits. Equalizing speakers' financial influence did not justify suppressing political communication. Id. at 39–51 & nn.52–53.

The Court invalidated limits on a candidate's use of personal resources because they directly restricted the candidate's communication and did not prevent outside corruption; using personal money could reduce dependence on donors. It also invalidated overall campaign expenditure ceilings. Contribution limits and disclosure addressed the asserted corruption danger, and Congress could not decide how much political debate was wasteful or excessive. Acceptance of public financing could, however, be conditioned on a candidate's voluntary agreement to spending limits. Id. at 51–59 & nn.57, 59, 65–66.

The disclosure and reporting provisions survived exacting scrutiny as construed. They informed voters about financial support, exposed potential corruption, and enabled enforcement of contribution limits. The Court rejected a blanket minor-party exemption on this record, while allowing an exemption upon a reasonable probability that disclosure would expose supporters to threats, harassment, or reprisals. Proof could concern harassment of members or an organization and, for a new party, similar organizations or individuals. Independent reporting was narrowed to campaign-connected contributions and spending expressly advocating a clearly identified candidate's election or defeat. The Court also construed political committees as candidate-controlled organizations or organizations whose major purpose was candidate nomination or election. It sustained the low statutory thresholds on the record, without deciding whether the nonpublic $10–$100 contribution records could constitutionally be made public. Id. at 60–85, particularly 64–68, 74, 78–84.

Public presidential campaign financing survived the facial attack. Congress could spend to reduce dependence on large private contributions and facilitate electoral discussion. The tax-return checkoff determined the appropriation's amount rather than giving each taxpayer control over a subsidy recipient. The funding scheme distinguished major parties with at least 25% of the preceding presidential vote, minor parties with at least 5% but less than 25%, and new parties. Congress could require demonstrated support and avoid subsidizing hopeless candidacies. The Court considered denial of a subsidy less restrictive than exclusion from the ballot, noted that candidates could still raise private funds, and relied on the spending ceilings voluntarily accepted by publicly funded competitors. Primary matching requirements encouraged geographically dispersed small contributions. Public financing remained severable from the compulsory expenditure ceilings. The Court left possible later proof of discriminatory operation, and questions concerning unaffiliated independent candidates, unresolved. Id. at 85–109 & nn.118, 122, 131.

## Judgment, participation, and separate opinions

In No. 75-436, the Court affirmed in part and reversed in part the D.C. Circuit's judgment; in No. 75-437, it affirmed the District Court's public-financing judgment. The mandate issued forthwith except for a stay, not exceeding thirty days, of the judgment affecting the Commission's powers. The Court accorded de facto validity to past Commission actions and temporarily allowed continued functioning, giving Congress time to reconstitute the agency or provide valid enforcement arrangements. This remedial treatment was omitted from the assigned principal excerpt. Id. at 142–144.

Eight Justices participated; Stevens took no part. All eight accepted the appointments defect and the answers restricting the Commission's powers. The opinion was per curiam, with Brennan, Stewart, and Powell joining throughout. Other Justices joined different portions, so the case does not have one vote margin for every holding. The expenditure ceilings for independent and overall campaign spending fell over White's dissent; the personal-resource ceiling fell over the dissents of White and Marshall. Contribution restrictions survived over Burger's and Blackmun's objections. The general-election funding treatment challenged by minor parties survived over Burger's and Rehnquist's objections. Id. at 5, 257, 266–267, 286, 290–294.

Burger agreed that the expenditure limits and the Commission's appointments were invalid. He rejected the contribution/expenditure distinction, regarded the low disclosure thresholds as inadequately justified, and opposed public financing as an intrusion into political activity that could entrench major parties. He also thought the Court's piecemeal invalidation destroyed an integrated statutory arrangement despite the severability clause. Unlike the other participating Justices, he opposed blanket de facto validation of unidentified past Commission acts and would leave individual challenges to the District Court. Id. at 235–257, particularly 241–246, 250–255.

White would uphold all three categories of expenditure limits, crediting Congress's experience with corruption, circumvention, escalating costs, campaign debts, and barriers created by personal wealth. He viewed regulation of money as producing permissible incidental effects on speech. He agreed with the appointments defect, de facto treatment, and stay, but qualified the rulemaking answer: although the Court left congressional disapproval unresolved, he would sustain that procedure for a properly appointed agency because a regulation took effect through congressional inaction rather than a new legislative enactment. He separately reserved the validity of candidate disqualification. Id. at 257–286 & n.1.

Marshall joined everything except invalidation of the personal-resource ceiling. He treated that ceiling as limiting a candidate's contribution to the campaign rather than total campaign spending: the candidate could still raise money from others. Especially with outside contribution ceilings in place, limiting personal spending promoted equal access and prevented wealth from gaining an initial advantage others could not overcome. Id. at 286–290 & nn.1–2. Blackmun rejected a principled constitutional distinction between contributions and expenditures and would invalidate the challenged contribution restrictions as well. Id. at 290. Rehnquist accepted Congress's public-financing power but regarded the general-election formula as unjustifiably preferring the established major parties; optional subsidies did not share the necessity that supported state ballot-management rules. Id. at 290–294.
