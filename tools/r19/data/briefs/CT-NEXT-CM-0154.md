# Parker v. Twentieth Century-Fox Film Corp.
3 Cal. 3d 176, 474 P.2d 689 (1970) | Contracts

## Contract, substitute offer, and procedure
Shirley MacLaine Parker contracted in August 1965 to play the female lead in Fox’s proposed musical Bloomer Girl. The agreement guaranteed $750,000 for fourteen weeks beginning May 23, 1966, expressed as $53,571.42 weekly. Fox decided not to make the film and notified Parker on April 4 that it would not fulfill its obligations. In the same letter, it offered her the lead in Big Country, Big Man, a dramatic western to be filmed in Australia. Bloomer Girl was to be produced in California and would use her dancing as well as acting talents. Fox offered the same compensation and thirty-one of the original agreement’s thirty-four numbered provisions, but removed or impaired approval rights concerning the director, dance director, and screenplay. Parker had one week to accept and did not do so. 3 Cal. 3d at 179–180 & nn. 1–2.

Parker sued for compensation due and, alternatively, breach damages. Fox admitted the agreement’s validity, Parker’s compliance and readiness to perform, and its own anticipatory repudiation. Its sole defense was that rejecting Big Country unreasonably failed to mitigate her loss. Parker obtained summary judgment for $750,000 plus interest. Fox appealed. Although Parker had not started performing Bloomer Girl, the parties stipulated for the motion that she had been discharged, allowing application of wrongful-discharge damage principles. Id. at 180–182 & n. 4.

## Issue and governing principles
The issue was whether Fox’s offer established a triable mitigation defense or whether its different and inferior character could be decided as a matter of law. Normally an employee recovers agreed compensation less earnings the employer affirmatively proves were received or could reasonably have been received from other employment. Before hypothetical earnings from an unaccepted opportunity may reduce recovery, however, the employer must establish that the work was comparable or substantially similar. Different or inferior work need not be accepted. Id. at 181–182.

The majority distinguished reasonable efforts to find comparable employment from an employee’s entitlement to reject different or inferior work. Fox raised no issue concerning Parker’s general search for employment; its theory depended exclusively on the specific replacement offer. Once that offer was legally different or inferior, the majority did not require a further balancing of whether rejection was reasonable. Id. at 182–183 & n. 5.

Summary judgment required the moving evidence to support judgment and the opposing evidence to present no genuine factual issue; the court could not weigh disputed facts. Affidavits for the moving party were strictly construed, and doubts favored trial. Admissions in an adversary’s pleadings could establish facts, but a party’s own conclusory pleading could not replace adequate evidence. These rules governed the actual procedural ruling, rather than a verdict after a trial on the merits. Id. at 181.

## Majority application and contractual details
The majority held that the musical lead in Los Angeles and the straight dramatic western lead in an Australian opal-mine story were different kinds of employment. Independently, eliminating contractual approval rights made the substitute inferior. Fox’s letter removed dance-director approval because the replacement was not a musical. It also replaced the original director and screenplay approvals with consultation subject to Fox’s final decision if the parties disagreed. Equal pay therefore did not establish equal employment. Id. at 180 & n. 2, 183–184.

Fox’s opposition declarations characterized the new work as comparable and not inferior. The majority treated those assertions as conclusions concerning undisputed terms rather than facts generating a triable issue. It considered neither expertise nor judicial notice necessary to recognize the loss of contractual rights. The report thus supports summary judgment on the identified record, not a categorical holding that every contested employment-comparability question can be resolved without trial. Id. at 183–184.

The original agreement also stated that Fox need not use Parker’s services and that its obligation was to pay the guaranteed compensation, subject to the agreement’s terms. Parker argued that this and other grounds excused mitigation altogether. Because the substitute offer could not support Fox’s defense, the majority expressly did not decide that separate argument. The guaranteed-pay clause should not be described as an adjudicated waiver of mitigation. Id. at 179–180 & n. 1, 184.

## Sullivan’s dissent
Acting Chief Justice Sullivan agreed that an employee need not accept a different or inferior kind of work, but disputed the majority’s use of that rule. In his view, differences between films did not establish materially different kinds of employment: both were starring roles, and the significance of genre, location, and approval rights required factual appraisal. He regarded the reasonable importance of a lost provision, weighed against the alternative offer’s advantages, as the pertinent inquiry. A per se rule that any omitted right makes work inferior threatened to displace mitigation almost entirely. Id. at 184–188, 190–191.

Sullivan examined the actual declarations in greater detail. Parker’s evidence did not explain the practical importance of the approval terms, while Fox’s production executive described her experience in dramatic and western roles, asserted no reputational harm, and denied injury from losing approvals. Sullivan also criticized the trial court’s use of judicial notice to determine entertainment-industry practices without the opportunity to be heard required by Evidence Code § 455. He would have reversed for a trial on comparability, rather than held that Parker necessarily had to accept the role. His dissent allowed that summary judgment might be proper in another case with manifestly dissimilar work and adequately developed declarations. Id. at 188–191 & dissent nn. 6–8.

## Disposition, votes, and sources
Burke wrote the majority, joined by McComb, Peters, Tobriner, and assigned Justices Kaus and Roth; Sullivan dissented. The judgment for $750,000 plus interest was affirmed, a six-to-one division shown by the report’s named participation. Rehearing was denied October 28, 1970; Mosk did not participate in that denial and Sullivan favored granting it. The assignment asterisk accompanying Kaus and Roth explains their Judicial Council service. Id. at 184, 191.
