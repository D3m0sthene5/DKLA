# EarthInfo, Inc. v. Hydrosphere Resource Consultants, Inc.
900 P.2d 113 (Colo. 1995) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
The Colorado Supreme Court, sitting en banc, decided the case on June 30, 1995. Justice Scott wrote the opinion; the complete report contains no separate writing or numerical voting lineup. On review of an unpublished affirmance of a rescission and restitution judgment, the court upheld the availability of profit disgorgement in these circumstances but rejected an award that failed to distinguish the parties’ contributions to those profits. It affirmed in part, reversed in part, and returned the matter to the court of appeals with directions to remand for recalculation and a new restitution order. (113, 115, 121.)

## The software relationship and royalty dispute
Between 1986 and 1988, Hydrosphere contracted with US West to develop CD-ROM products making public hydrological and meteorological data accessible to users. Five Hydrodata products included disks, access software, and a user manual. US West received ownership, copyrights, and patents, while Hydrosphere remained responsible for technical support. US West undertook to create manuals, package and market the products, and pay fixed hourly development fees plus royalties based on net sales for inventive product ideas. The payments were quarterly. (115–116 & n. 1.)

EarthInfo acquired US West’s interest on February 10, 1989, agreeing to pay $60,432. A separate agreement with Hydrosphere assumed US West’s obligations, including royalties on the developed products. EarthInfo paid royalties through June 30, 1990. A disagreement then arose about whether a new derivative product also carried a royalty obligation. EarthInfo maintained that the contracts did not address derivatives. On October 30, when third-quarter royalties came due, it announced that it would withhold those and future royalties pending clarification. It continued paying development fees, including $19,000 after June 30. After strained negotiations, Hydrosphere notified EarthInfo on December 12 that it was rescinding the contracts. (116 & nn. 2–3.)

Following a March 1992 trial, the trial court accepted EarthInfo’s position about the derivative product: royalties were not owed on its sales. But that did not excuse withholding royalties on the other products. The court found the unilateral suspension a substantial breach. At a subsequent hearing both parties sought rescission and restitution. The court found damages inadequate because the parties’ deep disputes made a renewed productive relationship unrealistic. It chose June 30, 1990, as the rescission date. The Supreme Court expressly declined to decide whether that date was correct because the issue was not before it. (116 & n. 4; 118 & n. 7.)

## The judgment under review
The trial court required EarthInfo to return tangible property and related promotional and proprietary material. It also ordered repayment of $265,204.91 in net profits from June 30, 1990, through its order. It credited EarthInfo with the $60,432 acquisition amount and $19,000 in subsequent development fees, producing a $185,772.91 judgment for Hydrosphere. The acquisition debt had in fact later been negotiated down to $18,172.80, a fact the opinion records without making it the resolution of the issues accepted for review. (116–117 & n. 3.)

EarthInfo challenged the property return and the award of all net profits. Its June 1992 bankruptcy subsequently ended its pursuit of the returned property. The court of appeals affirmed the trial court’s discretionary use of net profits; Judge Rothenberg dissented on that issue. The Supreme Court reviewed the availability of disgorgement and the failure to apportion the resulting profits. The appellate dissent described here is part of the procedural history, not a separate writing reproduced in the Supreme Court report. (117 & n. 5.)

## Why disgorgement was permissible
The court recognized a tension between compensating the promisee’s lost expectation and preventing a breaching party from retaining gains produced by wrongdoing. An expectation award might leave some gains with the breacher when those gains exceeded the promisee’s loss. The court adopted neither a universal rule allowing that outcome nor a universal rule requiring disgorgement. Instead, the trial court was to decide the availability of profits as restitution case by case. (117–118 & n. 6.)

Rescission may be warranted for a substantial breach causing irreparable injury where damages are inadequate, difficult, or impossible to assess; parties can also rescind by mutual consent or conduct. Those considerations supported the remedy here: the breach was substantial, damages could not realistically repair the ongoing hostile relationship, and both parties sought rescission. Rescission ordinarily requires restoration of benefits on both sides and a practical equitable return to the status quo. Restitution measures the defendant’s gain rather than the plaintiff’s loss, so its amount can exceed expectation damages in an appropriate case. The court’s watch-theft illustration explains that distinction; it is not a characterization of the parties’ transaction as theft. (118–119 & nn. 7–10.)

Profit recovery is extraordinary. A mere contract breach generally does not make every resulting benefit recoverable, and some profit almost always derives from the defendant’s own investment or effort. Relevant considerations include the seriousness of the wrongdoing, the connection between the wrongdoing and the profit, and the feasibility of separating contributions. Intentional or substantial wrongdoing, or the absence of another way to measure enrichment, can support the remedy. Here EarthInfo’s conscious and substantial repudiation of its existing royalty obligations, difficult-to-assess damages, and mutual rescission justified disgorging profits attributable to Hydrosphere’s contribution. The absence of royalties on derivatives did not legitimize withholding royalties on the original products. (119–120.)

## Why the profits still had to be apportioned
The court distinguished deducting legitimate business expenses from identifying each party’s contribution to the remaining net profit. A defendant usually has the best information about its expenses and can properly be required to prove them. But EarthInfo’s work also helped create the profit itself: it supplied marketing, packaging, enhancements, manuals, trademarks, promotional materials, and licensee lists. Its use of Hydrosphere’s software did not make the entire resulting profit Hydrosphere’s contribution. Even wrongful conduct does not justify confiscating the defendant’s own investment and enterprise. (120–121.)

The trial court needed a reasonable approximation or informed estimate of the parties’ respective contributions. The seriousness of wrongdoing and risk borne by the plaintiff could affect both the burden and the required precision. If contributions were inseparable or untraceable, profits ordinarily should not be awarded unless the defendant was a very serious wrongdoer. For this case, the court expressly placed the burden of establishing sufficient facts for fair apportionment on the **plaintiff**, Hydrosphere. This is distinct from a defendant’s burden to substantiate deductible expenses. The existing findings did not establish the relative contributions or whether they could be separated, so the court ordered further proceedings rather than fixing a final share or amount. (120–121.)

## Assigned account and reading scope
The full C&M editorial account at physical PDF 334, printed 991, correctly emphasizes that restitution must account for benefits on both sides, but it states that EarthInfo bore the burden of proving the parties’ relative contributions. The complete original page 121 instead assigns that burden to the plaintiff in this case. The report also concerns profits from the selected rescission date forward, rather than an undifferentiated award of profits for the entire contractual relationship. This brief follows those actual qualifications.
