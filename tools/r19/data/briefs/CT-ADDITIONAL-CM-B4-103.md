# Scher Enterprises, Inc. v. Bronco Wine Company
178 F. Supp. 2d 780 (E.D. Mich. 2001) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

District Judge Cohn issued this memorandum and order on December 11, 2001, in the Eastern District of Michigan. The order denied Bronco’s motion for summary judgment because the evidence created a genuine factual dispute over whether it unreasonably withheld consent to a distributor’s business transfer under the Michigan wine-distribution statute. It did not establish final liability, determine a damages amount, or enter a final judgment awarding recovery to Scher Enterprises, formerly Viviano Wine Importers. There were no separate writings. (780–781, 787–789.)

## Business transaction and claimed loss

Viviano distributed several of California-based Bronco’s wine brands in Michigan. On July 26, 2000, J. Lewis Cooper Company signed a letter of intent to buy Viviano’s business. The companies executed an asset-purchase agreement on September 29, amended October 5, with a scheduled October 17 closing. The agreement addressed supplier relationships, but its supplier schedule was not supplied to the court; the court presumed it identified Bronco. (781–782.)

Viviano and Cooper met Bronco’s chief financial officer, Fred Franzia, in California on October 12. Viviano asserted that Bronco then expressed no concerns. It said it delivered written notice and an approval request on October 16. Bronco’s October 25 refusal cited insufficient notice and Cooper’s distribution of Gallo wines, Bronco’s largest competitor. Bronco said its national policy avoided distributors carrying its chief competitors where alternatives existed and that it did not want its brands subordinated to another line. Cooper protested the following day and offered further explanation of its capabilities. Bronco instead selected L & L as its Michigan distributor. Viviano alleged that exclusion of the Bronco business forced a reduced-price sale to Cooper for $1.65 million and sued on November 2. The court expressly noted that the difference between the originally offered and actual prices was unclear; $1.65 million was a sale price, not an awarded damage figure. (782–783; 781 n. 2.)

Viviano claimed both unreasonable refusal to consent and improper withholding of wine orders placed before the sale but undelivered when Bronco learned of the transfer. The present motion principally addressed consent and the proposed buyer’s qualifications. (781, 789.)

## Governing statute and summary-judgment question

The court’s operative analysis applied M.C.L. § 436.1305(15), forbidding withholding consent where the proposed transferee met the supplier’s material and reasonable qualifications and standards. The statute required written notice, prohibited unreasonable delay, and declared unapproved transfers null and void. It defined reasonable qualifications by the average standard of the supplier’s criteria for wholesalers entering or renewing agreements during the twenty-four months before the proposed transfer; covered business transfers included sales, assignments, stock or asset transfers, mergers, consolidation, and dissolution. Its broader purposes included stable competition and a viable three-tier distribution system. (783–784.)

At the hearing, Bronco supplied two apparently legitimate reasons: Cooper distributed competing Gallo wine and also distributed spirits. The court invited supplemental evidence on the materiality and reasonableness of those qualifications and Cooper’s compliance. On summary judgment, it could not choose between conflicting evidence as a factfinder; reasonable inferences were drawn against the moving supplier. The issue was whether the record permitted a genuine disagreement, not whether any supplier preference was automatically invalid. (783–785.)

## Competing wine evidence

Scher identified twenty-eight Bronco distributors also carrying Gallo and three more learned of through contacts. He said he had not known of the asserted anti-Gallo policy before the refusal and noted that L & L distributed other competing premium wines. Bronco did not deny the identified overlap. Franzia explained that many distributors came with the acquired Napa Ridge brand, resulted from later distributor mergers, or served markets without another delivery option. He said all thirty-one were outside Michigan and represented only 7.5 percent of 413 national distributors, which he characterized as 92.5 percent consistency with its policy. He also identified two terminated relationships after those distributors began handling Gallo. (785–786.)

L & L’s president said it did not itself distribute Gallo but used nineteen out-state jobbers, ten of whom did. He emphasized the lack of direct Bronco agreements with those jobbers and L & L’s ability to terminate for good cause or add another distributor in their territory. That network was one reason Bronco chose L & L. The distinctions supported Bronco’s position but did not conclusively remove the factual question, particularly given Bronco’s knowledge that its replacement network also carried Gallo. (786–787.)

The opinion also described affidavits from an attorney and a beverage-industry expert criticizing the policy and discussing separate sales divisions. Note 3 qualified their status: neither was listed as a witness or deposed, and their opinions were not the record evidence the court had directed Viviano to identify. The court focused on the actual evidence concerning Bronco’s stated business reasons rather than treating those critiques as an established expert finding that the policy was unreasonable. (785 n. 3.)

## Spirits evidence and why trial remained necessary

Scher identified twenty-two Bronco distributors outside Michigan handling spirits. He emphasized Michigan’s state-controlled spirits system, the limited functions performed by Cooper as an authorized distribution agent, the small portion of Cooper’s portfolio devoted to spirits, and its separate spirits sales force. Franzia again distinguished inherited distributors and other markets, including New Hampshire’s legal requirements. He said Michigan spirits distribution concentrated substantial leverage in a few authorized agents, including a consortium involving Cooper, and maintained that Bronco selected the distributor best meeting its market-specific requirements. (786–787.)

These competing explanations left factual questions about the supposedly national policies, exceptions, choice of L & L, and whether Cooper actually failed material and reasonable standards. The existence of other Gallo and spirits distributors did not itself establish final statutory liability, just as Bronco’s percentage and market explanations did not compel judgment in its favor. Because the court was deciding a motion rather than trying those facts, it denied summary judgment. (787, 789.)

## Authorities, unadjudicated delivery claim, and limits

Bronco cited Sally Beauty’s UCC treatment of a competitor-controlled transferee, but the court found it factually and legally inapplicable to this statutory dispute. A private arbitration upholding a beer supplier’s consolidation policy also did not establish entitlement to summary judgment here. The opinion instead discussed a prior Sixth Circuit decision affirming a supplier’s victory after a full bench trial, emphasizing that good faith, reasonableness, good cause, and notice could require factual determination. That earlier supplier victory was not the outcome of the present motion. (787–789.)

Note 4 explains the similar but distinct wine and beer statutes and discusses the meaning of material and reasonable qualifications, including a prior vagueness analysis. Note 5’s discussion of unpublished authority expressly identifies Anastasoff as vacated as moot; the brief does not treat it as an intact binding rule or certify modern citation practices. The introduction’s beer-subsection citation should not obscure the wine provision reproduced and applied in the analysis. (783–784, 787–788 & nn. 4–5.)

The court did not need to decide Viviano’s argument that the motion neglected the pending-deliveries claim. It characterized that claim as weakly pleaded and Bronco’s response as cursory, observing that Bronco had not seriously sought summary judgment on it. Neither dismissal of that claim nor a finding that withholding deliveries was unlawful was entered in this order. The operative result was denial of Bronco’s motion, leaving the identified dispute for further proceedings. (789.)
