# Dames & Moore v. Regan
453 U.S. 654 (1981) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision, alignment, and procedural posture

On July 2, 1981, the Supreme Court affirmed the Central District of California's dismissal of Dames & Moore's challenge to executive measures implementing the agreement that secured release of the American hostages in Iran. The Court granted certiorari before the Ninth Circuit entered judgment and ordered its mandate to issue immediately. The validity of the President's actions was distinguished from compensation that an affected claimant might later establish. (453 U.S. 654, 659–662, 666–668, 688–690.)

Justice Rehnquist wrote the opinion, joined throughout by Chief Justice Burger and Justices Brennan, Stewart, White, Marshall, and Blackmun. Justice Powell joined except footnote 6, which rejected a compensable property interest in these attachments; he separately concurred in part and dissented in part. Justice Stevens joined except Part V, which addressed the availability of a compensation action, and concurred in part. All nine thus accepted the authority to nullify the attachments, transfer the assets, and suspend the covered claims; eight joined the attachment-compensation ruling and a different eight joined Part V. The separate opinions did not dissent from the judgment affirming dismissal of this challenge. (Id. 658, 674 n.6, 690–691.)

Two distinct legal questions drove the reasoning. The International Emergency Economic Powers Act expressly authorized action against attached Iranian property. It did not itself authorize suspension of the underlying in personam claims. That second action rested on related legislation, a longstanding practice of executive claims settlement accepted by Congress, and the particular foreign-policy settlement. The Court repeatedly declined to announce general rules for other situations or a plenary presidential power to settle claims. (Id. 660–662, 669–679, 686–688.)

## Contract claim, asset freeze, and conditional attachments

Dames & Moore's subsidiary had contracted with the Atomic Energy Organization of Iran to conduct site studies for a proposed nuclear power plant. The organization terminated the contract for convenience on June 30, 1979. The subsidiary assigned its interest to Dames & Moore, which asserted that $3,436,694.30 plus interest remained due for previously performed work. The contract contemplated conciliation followed, if necessary, by litigation in Iran; Dames & Moore alleged that the organization continually postponed the requested settlement meeting. These details identified the commercial claim, without resolving its ultimate treatment by the later tribunal. (Id. 664 & n.4.)

After seizure of the American Embassy on November 4, 1979, President Carter declared a national emergency on November 14 and blocked Iranian government, instrumentality, controlled-entity, and central-bank property within United States jurisdiction. The declaration's validity was not challenged. Treasury regulations initially made attachments and other judicial processes against the blocked property void unless licensed. They expressly permitted amendment or revocation of licenses. A November 26 general license allowed specified litigation without entry of a judgment; December 19 clarification allowed prejudgment attachment. (Id. 662–664 & nn.1–3.)

On December 19, Dames & Moore sued Iran, the Atomic Energy Organization, and several Iranian banks in federal district court. The court ordered attachments, and property of some bank defendants was attached to secure any eventual judgment. The timing was central to the later property ruling: these interests arose after the freeze, through licenses whose continuing operation was expressly conditional. The Court did not treat them as unconditional preexisting liens that the government subsequently appropriated. (Id. 663–664, 672–674 & n.6.)

## The international agreement and separate litigation stages

The January 19, 1981 agreement, embodied in two Algerian declarations, provided for arbitration before an Iran–United States Claims Tribunal, with final and binding awards enforceable under national law. The United States undertook to terminate covered domestic litigation and attachments and to transfer Iranian assets by July 19. One billion dollars would fund a security account supporting payment of awards. The Court's factual account dates the hostages' release January 20, the day after the agreement; its syllabus compresses release into January 19. (Id. 664–665.)

Carter's January 19 orders revoked licenses concerning Iranian funds, nullified non-Iranian interests acquired after the freeze, and directed transfers through the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. On February 24, President Reagan ratified those measures and suspended claims that could be presented to the tribunal, giving them no legal effect in pending United States litigation. Suspension would end if the tribunal lacked jurisdiction. Claims would be discharged when an award was paid or the tribunal determined that no recovery was due. This conditional mechanism differed from abolishing every American claimant's access to every court. (Id. 665–666.)

Meanwhile, Dames & Moore obtained summary judgment against Iran and the Atomic Energy Organization, rather than the banks, and sought execution in Washington state court. The federal district court later stayed execution pending the defendants' appeal, vacated prejudgment attachments, and stayed proceedings against the banks in light of the orders. Separately, on April 28, Dames & Moore sued the United States and Treasury Secretary for declaratory and injunctive relief against implementation of the settlement, asserting statutory, constitutional, and property objections. On May 28, the District Court denied a preliminary injunction and dismissed that complaint for failure to state a claim. (Id. 666–667.)

The District Court nevertheless entered an injunction pending appeal against transferring property subject to Dames & Moore's judicial liens. Other federal courts had reached differing conclusions concerning the settlement. With a July 19 international performance deadline approaching, the Supreme Court granted certiorari before judgment in the Ninth Circuit, heard argument June 24, and decided this executive-action challenge July 2. It was not deciding the merits of the contractor's underlying payment claim or reviewing a Ninth Circuit merits judgment. (Id. 660, 667–668.)

## Youngstown as a framework, rather than a mechanical classification

The Court used Justice Jackson's Youngstown analysis: express or implied congressional authorization strengthens presidential authority; congressional silence may produce a zone of uncertainty requiring attention to the circumstances; and opposition places presidential power at its lowest ebb. Rehnquist also warned that the categories were simplified groupings. Actual measures occupy a spectrum between express authorization and prohibition, especially when Congress cannot foresee the details of an international crisis. That qualification led the Court to examine legislative context and practice, rather than treating absence of an express claims-suspension statute as either conclusive invalidity or complete freedom from congressional control. (Id. 668–669.)

## Statutory authority over the attached assets

IEEPA § 1702(a)(1)(B) empowered the President to compel transfers and nullify exercises of rights in property in which a foreign country or national had an interest, subject to United States jurisdiction. The statutory language covered revocation of the attachments and the directed asset transfers. Dames & Moore argued that legislative history limited peacetime powers to maintaining or ending a freeze, because IEEPA omitted the Trading With the Enemy Act's power to vest title in the government. The Court refused to erase the operative transfer and nullification language. Absence of a power to take title did not eliminate a power to direct the foreign assets' disposition in the manner used here. (Id. 669–672 & n.5.)

The claimant's reliance on the attachment licenses also failed. Both the freeze regime and licenses expressly subordinated attachments to further presidential action. Allowing individual creditors to convert conditional attachments into permanent restraints would defeat the purpose of preserving frozen assets for negotiation of the emergency. The Court described those assets as a bargaining resource the President could retain and deploy under the statutory grant. This action fell within Youngstown's strongest category because Congress specifically authorized it. (Id. 672–674.)

Footnote 6 separately rejected Dames & Moore's attachment-based compensation theory. The claimant argued that only the license to obtain future attachments was revocable, while completed attachments created property rights. The Court read the regulatory scheme to make these attachments themselves contingent, and concluded that the claimant had acquired no property interest of the sort supporting compensation. Powell disagreed with resolving that distinct issue categorically; his objection did not deny the President's authority to nullify the attachments. (Id. 674 n.6, 690–691.)

## Suspension of claims: statutory limits and congressional acceptance

An underlying claim existed apart from any attachment. An in personam suit sought liability and damages without focusing on particular Iranian property within United States jurisdiction. IEEPA's property provisions therefore did not authorize its suspension. The Court also declined to read the 1868 Hostage Act as specific suspension authority. Although that Act broadly permitted necessary and proper nonwar measures to obtain an unjustly imprisoned citizen's release, its history concerned foreign governments denying naturalized Americans' citizenship and repatriating them; the Iranian hostages had been seized because they were American. The history did not clearly authorize sacrificing private litigation in this different setting. (Id. 675–677 & n.7.)

Both statutes nevertheless informed the inquiry. They demonstrated Congress's willingness to give the President substantial discretion over foreign emergencies and unjust imprisonment. Related broad grants, without contrary legislative intent, could indicate acceptance of measures on presidential responsibility even when they did not specifically authorize the particular step. The Court conditioned that inference on the additional history of congressional acquiescence in this kind of action; it did not rest on statutory silence alone. (Id. 677–679.)

The United States had long settled nationals' claims against foreign governments through executive agreements, sometimes renouncing claims without the owners' consent in return for lump sums or arbitration. The Court identified early practice and at least ten settlements since 1952. Crucially, Congress had enacted the International Claims Settlement Act of 1949 to distribute a Yugoslav settlement and establish machinery for future agreements, then repeatedly amended it to support settlements involving other countries. Preparatory legislation concerning East Germany and Vietnam and IEEPA's legislative history further recognized executive settlement authority. Congressional oversight legislation required transmission of significant executive agreements without subjecting them all to advance approval. (Id. 679–682 & nn.8–10.)

Pink and related decisions recognized a presidential role in removing claims obstacles to relations between nations. Dames & Moore argued that earlier practice lost significance after the 1952 move away from absolute sovereign immunity, because claimants now could sue without diplomatic assistance. The post-1952 settlements answered that objection. It also argued that the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act of 1976 depoliticized commercial litigation and displaced presidential control. The Court understood FSIA as removing the sovereign-immunity barrier and withdrawing the Executive's power to make binding immunity determinations, rather than prohibiting consensual international claims settlements. Congress had rejected separate proposals to restrict executive agreements and later supported settlements in IEEPA's history. (Id. 682–686 & n.11.)

Suspension likewise did not divest federal courts of jurisdiction. Claims outside the tribunal's jurisdiction could revive. The President's congressionally accepted settlement altered the substantive law that courts applied, rather than removing judicial authority to hear an otherwise unchanged claim. The distinction answered the Article III objection presented here, without granting an unrestricted executive power to dictate judgments. (Id. 684–686.)

## The particular settlement and compensation remedy

The tribunal provided an alternative capable of meaningful relief, strengthening the analogy to traditional settlements. The Court did not guarantee full recovery or adopt uncritically the government's prediction that every claimant would do better there. Statutory authority to transfer assets did not itself depend on supplying another forum, but access to the tribunal meant that suspension exchanged domestic litigation for a genuine settlement mechanism. Congress had held hearings without disapproving the agreement, and a Senate committee described the tribunal as vitally important. This contrasted with Congress's express intervention against a proposed Czechoslovak settlement. (Id. 686–688 & nn.12–13.)

The resulting holding was deliberately limited: where settling claims was a necessary incident to resolving a major foreign-policy dispute and Congress had acquiesced, the President possessed the authority used here. The Court did not recognize plenary authority over claims, even those against foreign governments. (Id. 688.)

Whether suspension of Dames & Moore's underlying claim amounted to a compensable taking was not ripe; both sides conceded that point. But availability of compensation if a taking eventually occurred was ripe because adequate provision had to exist at the time of a taking. The Court held that an appropriate Tucker Act action could proceed in the Court of Claims and that the treaty exception posed no jurisdictional obstacle. It neither awarded compensation nor decided whether this claimant had suffered a taking, and footnote 14 expressly preserved that question. This reserved claim-based issue differs from footnote 6's rejection of the attachment-based property theory. (Id. 688–690 & n.14.)

## Separate opinions and assigned scope

Stevens thought a taking resulting from requiring this claimant to proceed in another forum was so remote that the Court should not address the Court of Claims' jurisdiction. He joined the rest of the opinion, including footnote 6. Powell would preserve both attachment and underlying-claim compensation theories for case-specific Court of Claims litigation. His first footnote questioned whether making attachments conditional could itself take a right otherwise available and whether revocable licenses necessarily made acquired liens revocable. He emphasized that national foreign-policy burdens could not simply be imposed on a few private claimants without compensation, while agreeing with the settlement authority and the availability of an appropriate compensation action. (Id. 690–691 & Powell nn.1–2.)
