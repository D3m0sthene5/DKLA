Parties
George Eldridge, the plaintiff and Supreme Court respondent, received Social Security disability-insurance
benefits and sought to prevent their termination before an evidentiary hearing. The petitioner was the federal
Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare, responsible for the challenged benefit-administration procedures.
State-agency participation in assessing disability did not change the Court’s identification of the constitutional
question as one under the Fifth Amendment concerning a federal statutory benefit program. 424 U.S. at
323–326, 332–333.
Eldridge’s immediate demand concerned continued payment while the agency reviewed his eligibility. He did
not ask the Court to make the ultimate medical determination itself. The Secretary defended the existing
sequence of written pretermination procedures, later evidentiary hearing, and judicial review, and also argued
that Eldridge’s failure to exhaust administrative remedies prevented the district court from hearing the case. Id.
at 324–332.
Material Facts
Eldridge began receiving disability benefits in June 1968. His original disabilities involved chronic anxiety and
back strain; diabetes was later identified. In March 1972, the monitoring state agency sent him a questionnaire
about his condition and treatment. He reported no improvement and identified medical sources. After obtaining
reports from his physician and a psychiatric consultant, the agency tentatively concluded that his disability had
ceased in May 1972. Id. at 323–324 & n.2.
The agency’s letter gave reasons and offered time to submit additional information. Its medical account
indicated controlled diabetes, no sufficiently restrictive back limitations, and no emotional condition
preventing all suitable work. Eldridge responded that he had spinal arthritis rather than a strained back and that
the existing record established continuing disability. The agency reaffirmed cessation; the SSA accepted that
determination and notified him in July that benefits would end after that month. The notice explained his right
to request reconsideration within six months. Id. at 324–325 & n.2.
The program provided insurance benefits according to statutory disability and insured-status requirements,
rather than a separate means test establishing financial need. A worker had to show a medically determinable
impairment preventing substantial gainful work, considering such matters as age, education, and work
experience. The Court reviewed termination based on a determination of medical recovery, expressly leaving
aside the different procedures used when termination rested on a return to work. Id. at 335–337 & nn.1, 14–15.
Before termination, the recipient could learn the proposed reasons and evidence, respond, and submit
additional material. Medical-source reports were written. The full opinion qualifies its general description of

Procedural History
Eldridge did not request administrative reconsideration. He sued in the Western District of Virginia for
reinstatement pending a hearing and challenged the procedures as unconstitutional. The district court ordered
reinstatement during the litigation and ultimately held that an evidentiary hearing was required before
termination. It relied on Goldberg and considered the recipient’s interest and the potential for disputed medical
and nonmedical evidence sufficient to require comparable protection. The Fourth Circuit affirmed the
injunction on the district court’s reasoning. Id. at 324–326 & nn.3–6.
The Secretary sought Supreme Court review, arguing both lack of jurisdiction under the Social Security Act’s
review provisions and constitutional adequacy. The Supreme Court held that the district court had jurisdiction
over Eldridge’s collateral constitutional claim, despite his nonexhaustion, but reversed on the due process
merits. The jurisdictional ruling therefore favored Eldridge while the judgment concerning the requested
pretermination evidentiary hearing favored the Secretary. Id. at 326–332, 349.
Issue
Could the district court consider Eldridge’s claim to a pretermination hearing under 42 U.S.C. § 405(g) when
he had presented a continuing-benefits claim but had not completed administrative review? On the merits, did
the Fifth Amendment require an evidentiary hearing before stopping disability-insurance benefits for medical
recovery, notwithstanding existing notice and written-response procedures and the availability of a later
hearing with retroactive relief? Id. at 323, 326–335.
Rule and Holding
Section 405(g)’s final-decision requirement includes a nonwaivable requirement to present a benefits claim and
a waivable requirement to exhaust the prescribed review process. Eldridge satisfied presentment through his
questionnaire and response contesting cessation. His constitutional claim was collateral to substantive
eligibility, and he colorably alleged harm that retroactive payments could not fully repair. In those
circumstances, the denial was sufficiently final for judicial review of that constitutional claim despite
nonexhaustion. Id. at 328–332.
To determine required procedural safeguards, the Court identified three considerations: the private interest
affected; the risk of error under existing procedures and the probable value of additional or substitute
protections; and the governmental interest, including the relevant function and fiscal and administrative
burdens. Applying them, the Court held that the described procedures complied with due process without a
pretermination evidentiary hearing. It did not deny the protected property interest or eliminate notice, an
opportunity to respond, the later hearing, or judicial review. Id. at 332–335, 347–349.

Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Fourth Circuit’s judgment sustaining the pretermination-hearing injunction. It
decided that the challenged procedural system was constitutionally adequate; it did not decide whether
Eldridge remained disabled, award him a fixed amount, or independently adjudicate every termination decision
under the program. The holding concerns the timing and character of required process in the setting reviewed.
Id. at 336–337, 349.
Reasoning
The Court first addressed the statutory route into federal court. Section 405(h), as construed in Salfi, foreclosed
reliance on ordinary federal-question jurisdiction to review the benefits denial. Section 405(g) supplied review
of a final decision after a hearing. Reading that requirement as an indivisible command to complete every
agency step would have barred Eldridge, but the Court separated claim presentment from exhaustion. Without
presentment there could be no agency decision at all; the exhaustion component served a different function and
could yield in appropriate circumstances. Id. at 326–330.
Eldridge had presented the essential benefits claim even though he did not separately present his constitutional
demand for a hearing. His written assertions that he remained disabled asked the agency to continue benefits,
and the agency denied that request. The constitutional issue also differed from the merits of eligibility: a later
favorable benefits determination could not answer whether the Constitution required an earlier hearing. The
Court considered his alleged uncompensable interim harm sufficiently plausible to justify prompt review. That
conclusion established jurisdiction; it did not prejudge the ultimate strength of his due process claim. Id. at
329–332.
On the merits, the Secretary accepted that continued receipt of the benefits was a protected statutory property
interest. The Court therefore moved to what process was due. It treated due process as requiring a meaningful
opportunity to meet the case for deprivation, with procedures adapted to context rather than a trial-type hearing
before every governmental action. Eldridge himself accepted the overall review process if payments continued
until the evidentiary hearing; the disagreement concerned the point at which the initial cutoff could occur. Id. at
332–335.
The private-interest analysis focused on interruption pending review because retroactive payments were
available following a successful challenge. The majority distinguished Goldberg’s means-tested welfare
recipients, whose eligibility established financial need at the margin of subsistence, from disability-insurance
recipients, whose eligibility did not turn on the availability of other resources. That distinction was a judgment
about the general program population. The majority acknowledged modest family resources, inability to obtain
temporary work, and lengthy review delays; it did not find that Eldridge personally could easily replace the lost
income. Id. at 339–343.
The majority considered potential private resources and other assistance relevant to the severity of interim
deprivation. Its footnote addressed the objection that the same disability definition governed Supplemental
Security Income, so a cessation determination could affect that alternative as well. It referred to state or local
assistance, food stamps, and the separate procedures for existing SSI recipients. These were reasons the Court
gave in assessing the historical system, rather than a finding that all terminated workers actually received
substitute benefits. Id. at 341–343 & n.27.
The next inquiry concerned the kind of error a hearing could prevent. The majority characterized disability
determinations as relatively focused on medical reports, examinations, and documented functional restrictions.
In its account, disagreement among professionals often did not present the same credibility problem as welfare

That analysis did not reduce disability to diagnosis alone. The opinion expressly acknowledged that age,
education, work history, and available work also entered the statutory determination. The majority believed
those matters could generally be presented effectively in writing, alongside medical material. A brief that says
the Court found disability claims exclusively medical would omit this qualification and overstate the basis for
dispensing with the earlier oral hearing. Id. at 344–345 & n.28.
The adequacy of existing safeguards was essential to the result. The questionnaire identified relevant
information; medical sources could supply reports; the recipient received proposed reasons and an evidence
summary; a representative could inspect the file; and the recipient could submit contrary evidence and
arguments. These steps allowed a response directed to the agency’s actual concerns before payments stopped.
The Court upheld a structured opportunity to contest a proposed decision, not termination without prior
communication. Id. at 337–338, 345–346 & n.18.
Amici relied on substantial reversal rates to argue that the initial process was unreliable. The majority
considered such evidence relevant but not conclusive. The parties used different denominators, and later review
could include new evidence or new examinations rather than simply detecting an error on an unchanged record.
The Court consequently declined to equate every appellate reversal with an error that an earlier trial-type
hearing would have prevented. It did not establish that reversal statistics can never illuminate procedural
fairness. Id. at 346–347 & n.29.
On the governmental side, the majority considered the costs of additional hearings, continued payments to
persons ultimately found ineligible, and administrative resources diverted from the program’s other work. It
did not accept a precise cost estimate as dispositive or treat financial expense alone as sufficient to defeat a
safeguard. Its conclusion rested on those burdens together with the assessed private interest and relatively
limited expected contribution of an earlier evidentiary hearing. Id. at 347–348.
Finally, the Court resisted automatically transplanting judicial trial procedures into administrative decisions. It
gave weight to the responsible administrators’ judgments while conducting its own constitutional assessment.
The pretermination response opportunities, the later evidentiary hearing, and subsequent judicial review
together supplied a meaningful chance to present the claim in the system before it. The later hearing’s existence
therefore mattered even though it could not, by itself, settle every question about the adequacy of an earlier
deprivation. Id. at 348–349.
Separate Opinions
Justice Brennan, joined by Marshall, would have required an evidentiary hearing before termination, agreeing
with both lower courts and invoking his earlier Richardson v. Wright dissent and Goldberg. His short opinion
directly attacked the majority’s characterization of the recipient’s likely hardship. He regarded the supposed
limited nature of deprivation as speculative and considered Congress’s provision of disability benefits without
a separate means test an acknowledgment of need that the Court should not discount. Id. at 349–350.
Brennan pointed to the reported foreclosure of Eldridge’s home and repossession of family furniture following
termination, citing statements at oral argument. He also rejected the availability of other assistance as an
answer to the loss of the particular benefit. Those facts are attributed here to the dissent’s account, rather than
presented as additional findings made by the majority. The disagreement concerned both the practical

Notes
The analysis has a threshold and a design stage. A protected property interest was accepted; the three-factor
inquiry determined the safeguards necessary to protect it. The case should therefore not be summarized as
holding that disability benefits are discretionary gratuities or that recipients have no right to process. It also
concerns an evidentiary hearing of the Goldberg type, defined in the opinion’s footnote, rather than every
possible form of pretermination opportunity to be heard. Id. at 325 n.4, 332–335.
The jurisdictional and merits conclusions can coexist. A colorable claim of harm from delay justified hearing
the constitutional question before administrative exhaustion; after considering that question, the Court found
the existing procedures adequate. Losing on the merits did not retroactively eliminate the court’s authority to
decide the claim. Id. at 330–332, 349.
The assigned notes question the majority’s emphasis on accuracy and its characterization of disability
determinations. Those are critiques of the reasoning, not additional holdings. This brief preserves the
majority’s actual qualifications and Brennan’s objection so the competing accounts can be evaluated against
the decision’s facts. The administrative time estimates, medical-file restrictions, and benefit rules described
above are those discussed in 1976 and are not representations of current program administration. Casebook p.
465; 424 U.S. at 338 n.18, 341–348, 350.
