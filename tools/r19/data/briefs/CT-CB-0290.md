Northrop Corp. v. Litronic Industries
29 F.3d 1173 (7th Cir. 1994)
Citation, court, and assigned scope
The Seventh Circuit decided Northrop on July 18, 1994. Chief Judge Posner wrote the
principal opinion; Judge James C. Hill sat by designation from the Eleventh Circuit, and Judge
Ripple wrote separately. The assigned excerpt, printed pages 290–292, centers on conflicting
form terms. The full decision also addresses Northrop's separate appeal concerning other
boards, the formation sequence, waiver, and Ripple's objection to institutional commentary
on state law. Paragraph citations use the linked OpenJurist transcription's numbering, not
official court paragraph numbers.
Parties and requested relief
Northrop, a defense manufacturer, bought electronic boards from Litronic for use in weapon
systems. It sued to recover amounts paid for allegedly defective boards of two different
specifications. Litronic defended the claim concerning No. 1714 boards on the ground that its
ninety-day warranty had expired. Northrop also sought recovery for another group of boards
that the factfinder determined it had not returned. The outcome of one dispute did not
automatically control the other. Opinion ¶¶ 4, 6–10.
Material facts and formation sequence
In 1987 Northrop requested bids for customized No. 1714 printed wire boards. Its request
stated that any purchase would use a Northrop purchase order whose terms would override
inconsistent seller terms. Litronic offered four boards at $19,000 each with delivery in six
weeks. Its form contained a ninety-day warranty in lieu of other warranties and a clause
giving its terms priority over the buyer's unless separately negotiated. Id. ¶ 4.
Northrop purchasing officer Lynch telephoned Litronic's representative, Lair, and accepted
up to Lynch's $24,999 authority, indicating that a formal order for all four boards would
follow. Litronic began manufacturing. A month later Northrop sent a “turn on” letter
authorizing all four boards. The formal order may not have been mailed until several months
after the telephone conversation. Northrop's familiar form contained a warranty without a
time limit and requested the seller's written acknowledgment. Litronic never signed and
returned that acknowledgment, and Northrop did not pursue the omission. Id. ¶¶ 4–5.
Litronic delivered the first three boards in July 1988, more than a year after the initial
conversation. Northrop did not claim the manufacturing delay itself was culpable. Testing
took months. Northrop returned the three boards in December and January as defective; the
fourth board had not arrived. Litronic invoked its ninety-day limit, while Northrop pointed to
its own unlimited warranty. Id. ¶ 6.
The parties treated the warranty dispute as also controlling the permissible time for rejection
of nonconforming boards. The appellate court accepted that framing for these appeals,
although it noted that rejection ordinarily raises the reasonable-time question under §
2-602(1). The court did not purport to collapse every warranty claim into rejection of goods.
Id. ¶ 7.

Procedural history
A magistrate judge awarded Northrop $58,000 for the delivered No. 1714 boards, as the
reported opinion states, but denied recovery for the separate boards that Northrop failed to
return. Both sides appealed. The $58,000 is the judgment reported by the court; it should not
be silently recalculated from the earlier $19,000 quotation. Id. ¶ 8.
The parties agreed that Illinois substantive law governed. Illinois courts had not selected
among the competing interpretations of “different” terms in § 2-207. The federal court
therefore had to predict Illinois law rather than choose its own preferred rule as a
freestanding federal commercial law. It reviewed the finding that Northrop had not returned
the other boards for clear error and found none. Id. ¶¶ 3, 9–10.
Issues
Did Northrop's oral acceptance and reference to its forthcoming form create a contract while
communicating a warranty inconsistent with Litronic's offer? Under the Illinois rule the
federal court predicted, do conflicting terms drop out, do the offeror's terms prevail, or are
different terms treated like additional terms under subsection (2)? Did the seller's priority
clause or the missing signed acknowledgment change that result? Separately, could
Northrop recover for boards it did not return by invoking its statutory security interest
without accounting for their disposition?
Rules and holdings
The court concluded that a contract existed and that Northrop's acceptance contained
different warranty terms without being expressly conditioned on Litronic's agreement to
them. It predicted that Illinois would adopt the majority knockout approach: the conflicting
ninety-day and unlimited warranty provisions drop out, leaving a reasonable period supplied
through the Code. Northrop did not obtain the unlimited warranty on its own form merely
because Litronic's limit failed. Id. ¶¶ 11–18.
Northrop's separate appeal failed because a buyer retaining rejected goods under a security
interest must account for them. It cannot recover its payment while leaving open the
possibility that it sold, reused, or otherwise benefited from the goods without crediting that
benefit. Physical return is not invariably necessary, but a failure to account was fatal on this
record. Id. ¶¶ 9–10.
Actual judgment
The judgment was affirmed on both appeals. Northrop retained its $58,000 recovery for the
No. 1714 boards, and the denial of recovery for the other boards also stood. The court did not
award both contractual warranty alternatives or remand for Northrop to explain the missing
boards. Id. ¶¶ 8–10, 20.

Reasoning
The acceptance was different without being expressly conditional
Lynch's reference to the forthcoming Northrop form mattered because both parties knew, or
should have known from prior dealings, that it contained a different warranty. This was more
than silently accepting the seller's form and later privately wishing for another term. Yet
Lynch did not expressly make agreement contingent on Litronic's acceptance of Northrop's
warranty. The distinction preserved a contract under § 2-207(1) while leaving the terms
question open. Id. ¶¶ 11–13.
Any condition concerning approval by Lynch's superiors had been satisfied and therefore did
not resolve the warranty issue. The subsequent manufacture, shipment, and payment also
confirmed that the parties had acted on an agreement. The court did not require Litronic's
unused acknowledgment line to become the controlling fact after the parties proceeded
without it. Id. ¶¶ 11–13, 19.
The statutory wording permits several plausible routes
Subsection (1) expressly mentions terms that are additional or different; subsection (2)
speaks of additional terms. The court laid out three responses to this mismatch. The knockout
view removes conflicting terms from both forms and uses a neutral Code rule. The
offeror-wins view excludes the offeree's conflicting terms and retains the offer's terms. A
third approach treats different terms like additional terms, so materiality determines
whether the offeree's variation enters the contract. Id. ¶¶ 1–2, 14.
Posner favored the third approach as a policy and drafting interpretation. In this transaction
it would have preserved Litronic's ninety-day limit because an unlimited warranty materially
differed. That preference was not the holding. A brief that quotes the preferred approach and
announces it as Northrop's rule reverses the reasoning that actually produced the judgment.
Id. ¶¶ 2, 14–16.
The state-law prediction favored the majority knockout approach
Illinois had followed majority approaches in other UCC disputes, and uniform interpretation
was an express Code objective. Those considerations supported predicting adoption of the
prevalent knockout rule even though Posner thought a minority alternative more sensible. A
neutral replacement term also reflected the parties' conduct: both wanted a transaction,
while neither clearly accepted the other's inconsistent boilerplate. Id. ¶¶ 15–16.
Litronic stressed its smaller size and unwillingness to assume extensive warranty duties. The
court found that point insufficient to infer that the larger buyer had accepted a particularly
short limit for technically complex boards. More importantly, the knockout result did not
award Northrop its own indefinite warranty; it required only a reasonable duration, and the
complicated testing supported the six-month rejection period found reasonable below. Id. ¶¶
14, 18.

The priority clause issue was waived, and conduct could lead to the same result
The court observed that an offeror can expressly limit acceptance to the terms of its offer.
Litronic's document did contain a priority clause, but Litronic did not argue that it
conditioned contract formation on Northrop's acceptance of the ninety-day warranty. The
court treated that argument as waived. It did not adjudicate the clause's effectiveness on a
fully preserved record. Id. ¶ 15.
The opinion also considered subsection (3): parties whose writings fail to form a contract may
nevertheless form one through performance, with agreed terms and Code supplements
replacing discrepancies. That alternative reading of the dealings would lead to the same
result. The court therefore had no need to decide it separately. This discussion explains why
carefully conditional forms do not guarantee a party's preferred terms once it proceeds with
performance anyway. Id. ¶ 17.
Retaining collateral requires accounting, even where return is not mandatory
For the other boards, § 2-711(3) allowed an appropriate security interest after payment. The
buyer could retain or resell goods under the applicable rules, and worthless goods might
sometimes be discarded. But Northrop did not establish what had happened to these boards.
Its principal assertion that they had been returned was disbelieved; its alternative legal
argument against a return requirement did not explain their use or proceeds. Id. ¶¶ 9–10.
The defect was the possibility of double recovery. A buyer that recoups its loss by resale or
advantageous use cannot also recover the full payment without accounting. The court
mentioned Illinois's “mend the hold” doctrine, restricting changes of litigation position, but
did not need to resolve that doctrine's scope or compatibility with federal procedure because
Northrop lost even assuming its alternative argument was available. Id. ¶ 10.
Separate opinion: Judge Ripple
Ripple joined the judgment and the prediction that Illinois would follow the majority
approach. He expressly declined to endorse an institutional opinion about which
interpretation was best. State courts have constitutional authority to shape state
jurisprudence; a federal diversity court should make its best prediction and avoid presenting
its own policy preference as an institutional position on that law. Ripple welcomed individual
judges' participation in scholarly and law-reform debate, while distinguishing that role from
adjudication. Id. ¶¶ 21–23.
This was a concurrence, not a dissent from the knockout result. It sharpens the Erie lesson:
the rule the federal court regards as best and the rule it predicts the state would adopt need
not coincide.
Study use and limits
The case combines a formation question, an unsettled state-law interpretation, waiver, and a
distinct remedial claim. The principal opinion's references to §§ 2-309 and 2-601 accompany
its discussion of a reasonable replacement period, while paragraph 7 specifically identifies §
2-602(1) as the rejection-timing provision. Keep the warranty framing accepted for this
appeal separate from a general account of those statutes. The brief reports the 1994
state-law prediction and does not certify the current law of every jurisdiction.
