# Rivet v. Regions Bank of Louisiana

**522 U.S. 470 (1998) — Supreme Court of the United States — February 24, 1998.** Justice Ginsburg wrote for a unanimous Court. The assigned Civil Procedure account is Note 3, printed page 1274, physical PDF page 643, following the *Moitie* account. The decision distinguishes a federal defense that could defeat a state claim from a federal claim that supports removal.

## Property transactions and the competing claims

In 1983, a partnership mortgaged its interest in the Louisiana equivalent of a leasehold estate in New Orleans to a predecessor of Regions Bank. In 1984 it granted a second mortgage to Mary Anna Rivet, Minna Ree Winer, Edmond G. Miranne, and Edmond G. Miranne, Jr., to secure additional borrowing. The partnership later entered bankruptcy. The report uses “Regions” collectively for the bank and its predecessors, First Federal Bank and Secor Bank. 522 U.S. at 472 & n.1.

The trustee sought permission to sell the leasehold free of claims. Bankruptcy Court orders in June and August 1986 authorized the sale and approved the bank's purchase as the sole auction bidder. The court directed cancellation of all liens, mortgages, and encumbrances, including both mortgages. Nevertheless, the second mortgage remained inscribed on the parish's mortgage rolls. In 1993 the bank bought the underlying land from Walter L. Brown, Jr., and Perry S. Brown and then sold the entire property to Fountainbleau Storage Associates. Id. at 472–473.

On December 29, 1994, the second mortgagees sued in Louisiana state court. They alleged that the 1993 transfers violated Louisiana law by failing to satisfy their superior mortgage rights. They sought recognition and enforcement of their mortgage or, alternatively, damages. The bank and other defendants maintained that the earlier Bankruptcy Court orders had already extinguished the rights on which the suit depended. That position raised a potentially dispositive defense; it did not itself establish that the plaintiffs were asserting a federal cause of action. Id. at 473.

## Removal, lower-court proceedings, and the question

The defendants removed to the Eastern District of Louisiana on a federal-question theory. The plaintiffs moved to remand, while the defendants sought summary judgment. Relying on the Fifth Circuit's *Carpenter* decision, the District Court held that a prior federal judgment's preclusive effect supplied a proper basis for removal. It granted the bank and Fountainbleau summary judgment on preclusion grounds and separately granted the Browns summary judgment for failure to state a claim against them. Id.

The Fifth Circuit affirmed. Its view was that a defendant could remove a state cause of action completely precluded by a prior federal judgment on a federal question. It understood that proposition to follow from footnote 2 of *Federated Department Stores, Inc. v. Moitie*. Judge Jones dissented, distinguishing the allegedly federal claims recast in *Moitie* from these exclusively state-law mortgage claims. The Supreme Court granted review to resolve disagreement among the circuits. Id. at 473–474 & n.2.

The question was whether a defendant's assertion that an earlier federal judgment precluded the entire suit could support federal-question removal. The Court answered **no**. Preclusion remains a defense, even if the prior judgment is federal and even if the defense would dispose of the entire action. Id. at 472, 476–478.

## The well-pleaded-complaint rule and its corollary

The historical removal statute allowed removal of a civil action within the district court's original jurisdiction. For federal-question jurisdiction, the federal issue must appear as part of the plaintiff's properly pleaded claim. A defense is not part of that claim. A federal defense therefore cannot support removal even when the complaint anticipates it or the parties agree that the defense is the only real issue. Id. at 474–475.

The rule does not let a plaintiff conceal a necessary federal claim through artful pleading. Complete preemption illustrates that corollary: when federal law completely preempts a state-law claim, the purported state claim is federal from its inception. It is then removable because federal law supplies the claim's actual character. Ordinary federal preemption, by contrast, is generally a defense. Id. at 475–476.

The defendants sought to treat complete claim preclusion like complete preemption. The two doctrines differ in what they do to the plaintiff's cause of action. A completely preemptive federal statute transforms the purported state claim into a federal claim. A prior judgment's preclusive effect instead extinguishes the claim; it does not substitute a federal cause of action for the state one. Preclusion is an affirmative defense under Rule 8(c), not a means of rewriting the complaint. This mortgage dispute involved Louisiana parties and depended exclusively on Louisiana law. Nothing about the Bankruptcy Court defense changed that character. Id. at 476–477.

## What Moitie's footnote did—and did not—establish

*Moitie* principally concerned an exception to claim preclusion after parties declined to appeal adverse judgments while other litigants successfully appealed. Its removal footnote accepted, in that particular setting, the lower courts' determination that at least some refiled claims were essentially federal claims artfully cast in state-law terms. The Court had not closely elaborated a separate preclusion-based removal rule. Id. at 477–478.

Lower courts had placed more weight on that brief comment than it could bear. *Rivet* clarified that *Moitie* did not create a preclusion exception to the rule barring removal on the basis of a federal defense. Whether the bank's prior judgments were ultimately preclusive was a matter for the state proceedings, subject to the Supreme Court's ultimate review. The decision separated the proper forum from the merits of that defense. Id. at 478.

## Exact judgment and limits

The Court **reversed the Fifth Circuit and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion**. There was no separate writing or dissent. It did not decide that the second mortgage remained enforceable, invalidate the Bankruptcy Court orders, award damages, or resolve whether the plaintiffs could state a claim against the Browns. Its holding was that the asserted federal preclusion defense furnished no basis for removal under the federal-question provision. Id. at 473, 478.

Footnote 3 identifies another possible protective mechanism: under the Anti-Injunction Act's relitigation exception, a federal court may enjoin state proceedings when necessary to protect or effectuate its judgments. The Court did not issue such an injunction here. That mechanism is distinct from recognizing original federal-question jurisdiction over a state-law complaint merely because a federal judgment may defeat it. Id. at 478 n.3.
