# Sirkin v. Fourteenth Street Store
124 App. Div. 384, 108 N.Y.S. 830 (1st Dep’t 1908) | Contracts

## Court and precise result
The New York Supreme Court, Appellate Division, First Department decided this case February 14, 1908. Justice Laughlin wrote the majority opinion, joined by Justices McLaughlin and Clarke. Justice Scott dissented, joined by Presiding Justice Patterson. The three-to-two court reversed the Appellate Term’s determination and the seller’s judgment and ordered a new trial, with costs to the appellant to abide the event, excluding disbursements for printing its reply brief. It held that the buyer must be permitted to prove its pleaded commercial-bribery defense. If established, that defense would prevent the seller from enforcing the resulting sales contracts. The appellate decision was not a final finding that the alleged bribe had been proved. (124 App. Div. at 384–387, 394–396.)

## Transaction, defense, and lower courts
Samuel Sirkin sued the Fourteenth Street Store, a New York department-store corporation, for $1,555.81, the purchase price of hosiery and wrappers sold and delivered. The store’s separate defense alleged that, without its knowledge or consent, Sirkin promised its purchasing agent five percent of the price of goods the agent ordered. It further alleged that the orders resulted from that arrangement and that Sirkin paid the agent $75 after delivery, solely in return for placing those orders. The store characterized the bribe and sales as parts of an illegal transaction. (Id. at 385–386.)

At trial Sirkin proved orders from the agent, delivery, and nonpayment. He then moved for judgment on the evidence and pleadings, arguing that the separate defense was legally insufficient. The store objected and requested permission to prove its allegations. The City Court instead directed a verdict for the price plus interest. It reasoned that although the agreement to pay the agent was contrary to public policy, the buyer could not retain the goods without paying. The Appellate Term affirmed. The First Department’s review therefore concerned the legal sufficiency of an excluded defense, requiring the majority to assume what the store could prove rather than resolve disputed evidence against it. (Id. at 385–387.)

## Why the bribe and sales could not be separated
The majority treated the alleged promise of a percentage of every order as an inducement to place the orders and as consideration for doing so. It could form one corrupt agreement governing all orders or separate corrupt arrangements governing each. The orders need not have been placed simultaneously with the bribe promise: later orders given pursuant to that promise remained connected to it. The court refused to isolate a formally ordinary sales agreement from the unlawful method by which the seller allegedly obtained it. (Id. at 387–388, 393–394.)

Section 384r of the Penal Code, enacted in 1905, criminalized specified secret gifts, promises, and commissions intended to influence an agent’s conduct in the principal’s business, as well as receipt of prohibited commissions. Its text is reproduced in the report. It provided a fine between $10 and $500, or such a fine and imprisonment for up to one year. The legislature did not expressly state that the resulting sales contract was void. The majority nevertheless treated the statute as an authoritative declaration and extension of public policy against corrupting agents, supporting refusal to enforce contracts procured through the prohibited conduct. The bribe promise itself was also unenforceable under preexisting public-policy principles. (Id. at 387–389.)

Denying only the agent’s commission or the amount added to a price would inadequately deter the practice. The principal had purchased faithful service from its agent; a secret payment tempted that agent to overpay, accept inferior goods, or advance personal interests. The seller argued that the charged price had not been shown to exceed market value. The majority found that objection insufficient because the injury to loyalty and public policy did not depend on proving a particular overcharge. It also refused to enforce merely the seller’s net share after subtracting the bribe: that division would itself require the court to give effect to the illegal arrangement. (Id. at 389–391.)

## Retention, ratification, and proof of illegality
An agent receiving secret money would be accountable to the principal, but the majority regarded that remedy as inadequate because secrecy made detection and proof difficult. It distinguished commissions paid with the principal’s knowledge and consent. It also rejected the argument that the store’s only options were rescission, return of the goods, or a damages counterclaim, and that retaining the goods ratified the purchase. Ordinary fraud can permit a private election to affirm or rescind; this transaction implicated a public policy that forbade ratification as well as its formation. (Id. at 391.)

The seller could not avoid the defense by presenting an apparently valid prima facie sales case without exposing the bribe. Illegality established through the defendant’s evidence or surrounding circumstances also could bar recovery. The majority’s purpose was to withhold judicial aid from an unlawful transaction, not to protect only parties who otherwise would deserve a windfall. It therefore accepted the consequence that a buyer might retain delivered goods without a judicially enforceable price obligation if the pleaded defense was proved. Its final direction remained a new trial, rather than a factual adjudication of the bribe or a judgment ordering disgorgement by the agent. (Id. at 392–394, 396.)

## Scott’s dissent
Scott, joined by Patterson, would affirm the seller’s judgment. He emphasized that the buyer had the goods or their proceeds, had tendered neither back, and alleged no resulting financial loss. In his view the illegal agreement between seller and agent was distinct from the lawful, completed sale between seller and buyer. The agent could not enforce a criminal promise, but the seller could establish the sale without relying on that promise. (Id. at 394–395.)

The secret commission made the purchase voidable for fraud, allowing rejection, return, or a damages counterclaim upon the appropriate showing. The criminal statute prescribed penalties without adding forfeiture of the price of an otherwise valid sale. Scott thought imposing that additional consequence was a legislative decision, not a judicial means of deterring misconduct. He also refused to presume damages absent allegations of them. The majority and dissent thus differed over the connection between the contracts and the court’s role in deriving civil unenforceability from the statutory public policy. (Id. at 395–396.)

## Assigned account and reading scope
C&M printed page 761 describes commercial bribery, the public-policy bar, and the dissent’s objection to an additional penalty. That short account’s statement that recovery was denied must be understood with the actual new-trial posture: the store first had to establish its excluded defense. Page 762 returns to the agent’s accountability and separately criticizes the majority’s broad licensing analogy. The opinion did invoke recovery restrictions on unlicensed services or sales; the textbook distinguishes revenue measures from licensing intended to protect public welfare. That editorial qualification is not itself a holding adjudicating a licensing dispute in Sirkin. (Id. at 388–389, 391.)
