# Arnstein v. Porter

**154 F.2d 464 (2d Cir. 1946) · United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit · February 11, 1946**  
**Civil Procedure · Summary judgment, credibility, and copyright infringement**

## Facts and procedure

Composer Ira B. Arnstein sued Cole Porter for musical copyright infringement, seeking damages and demanding a jury. Arnstein contended that Porter had obtained access to and copied his compositions. Porter denied access and copying. Both parties had been deposed, and recordings of the disputed music were submitted. The district judge found portions of Arnstein’s account, including assertions involving intermediaries or “stooges,” implausible and accepted Porter’s denial. He granted summary judgment. 468–470.

The record was not confined to those improbable assertions. More than a million copies of one Arnstein composition had been sold; other pieces had been sold, distributed to radio stations, bandleaders, or publishers, or publicly performed. The appellate majority regarded that circulation as circumstantial evidence from which access could be inferred if a jury rejected Porter’s denial. Porter also challenged Arnstein’s copyright ownership, the inclusion of uncopyrighted material and song titles, and the allegedly vexatious nature of the action in light of previous unsuccessful suits. 469–470; 474–475.

The appeal therefore concerned both whether a jury should assess the disputed infringement claim and whether particular components could properly remain in the case. It did not determine that Porter had plagiarized Arnstein, nor did it award damages. The appellate court itself listened to the recordings but disagreed internally about what they showed. 469; 472–475; 475–478.

## Two distinct copyright questions

Judge Jerome Frank’s majority opinion, joined by Judge Learned Hand, separated proof of copying from proof of unlawful appropriation. Copying might be established by an admission or circumstantial evidence, usually access coupled with sufficient similarity. Where access was not otherwise shown, similarities had to be striking enough to exclude independent creation. Technical analysis and expert testimony could help decide whether copying occurred. 468–469.

Even proven copying did not automatically establish infringement. The second question was whether Porter had taken enough of what appealed to the ordinary audience to appropriate Arnstein’s protected work unlawfully. For this popular music, the relevant reaction was that of ordinary listeners, rather than a trained musician’s judgment of artistic quality. Expert analysis could not substitute for that audience inquiry. The opinion nevertheless permitted testimony, including appropriately directed musical-expert testimony, to assist the jury in understanding listeners’ reactions; the expert’s refined taste was not controlling. 472–473 & nn.18–25.

These were separate factual inquiries, although exceptionally striking similarities could prove both. The court found some similarity in the recordings, insufficient by itself to infer copying but sufficient to become probative if access were established. It also could not conclude that the resemblance was necessarily too trivial to support unlawful appropriation. Thus the failure of similarity alone to establish copying did not end a case containing potentially relevant access evidence. 469; 472–473.

## Why the majority required a trial

Frank applied the circuit’s then-expressed caution against summary judgment where there was even the slightest factual doubt. Here the district judge had resolved credibility on depositions without hearing the parties testify. However unlikely parts of Arnstein’s account appeared, the majority thought the circulation of his works and the unresolved credibility of Porter’s denial presented a genuine issue. It would not substitute its own assessment of a witness for the jury’s. 468–470.

Frank emphasized that Arnstein’s prior deposition examination of Porter did not necessarily exhaust the value of trial. Live cross-examination might elicit admissions, and the jury could assess demeanor while Porter answered. Historical Rules 26 and 43 ordinarily favored oral testimony in court when the witness was available. The majority rejected assuming, in advance, that exceptional circumstances would permit Porter’s deposition to replace his trial testimony. Its discussion of those rules describes the provisions and dispute before the court in 1946, not their current numbering or every modern ground for using a deposition. 470–472 & nn.8–17g.

The majority did not forbid all summary judgments in copyright suits. It gave examples where documentary proof or an unmistakable absence of similarity would make trial futile. It also acknowledged that admissions could remove the necessary factual dispute. Its conclusion was that this record did not demonstrate the sort of certainty needed to deny trial when credibility and matters within the defendant’s knowledge remained central. The majority could not then say that a directed verdict for Porter at the end of a trial would necessarily be proper. 470–473 & nn.15–16, 22–23.

Frank also distinguished deciding a summary-judgment appeal from reviewing findings after a trial. Earlier infringement decisions sustaining defendants’ victories often rested on trial findings supported by evidence. The majority’s appendix compared such cases with a plaintiff’s successful claim based on striking similarity, explaining why those outcomes did not authorize the appellate court to resolve the present facts itself. That appendix is part of the opinion’s reasoning, rather than separate background material. 473–475 and appendix.

## Claims excluded or restored

The court did not send every component to trial unchanged. Claims concerning the uncopyrighted “Twilight Waltz” and “Duet” from “Song of David,” and the titles “What Is Love” and “Night and Day,” were to be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. A title was not copyrightable, and the facts did not permit joining those nonfederal claims with the copyright infringement action. This was the opinion’s historical jurisdictional treatment of those claims. 474 & nn.30–31.

The claim involving “A Mother’s Prayer” stood differently. Arnstein had assigned that copyright but alleged an oral agreement that it would revert upon the assignee’s death, which had occurred. Porter invoked the parol-evidence rule and an earlier adverse ruling in a suit against someone else. The majority held that no party in the assignee’s chain of title was before the court, so that rule did not bar proof here; the previous adjudication did not resolve this action. 474.

Nor could previous lost suits simply establish that the present case was vexatious. Absent the prerequisites for res judicata, each action had to be assessed on its own merits, while remaining subject to precedent. Frank distinguished possible remedies for a genuinely vexatious lawsuit from prejudging this one because its plaintiff had sued unsuccessfully before. 474–475 & nn.33–34.

## Clark’s dissent

Judge Charles E. Clark would have sustained dismissal. He heard no significant musical resemblance and believed intellectual comparison of the scores should complement, rather than be displaced by, listening. Arnstein’s claims largely identified short sequences of commonplace notes, often with different rhythms. Clark’s example compared Arnstein’s “A Modern Messiah” with Porter’s “Don’t Fence Me In”: different meters and only one shared sequence of five consecutive notes, with different values. He thought such fragments supplied no legally adequate basis for infringement. 475–478 & nn.1–2.

Clark also considered access insufficiently supported. Discovery had produced Porter’s categorical denial and no substantial countervailing evidence; cross-examination should not be expected to create an entire case from nothing. He disputed the majority’s restrictive prediction about when Porter could use his deposition at trial. In Clark’s view, the rule’s distance and absence provisions required application to the actual circumstances, not an advance restriction based on residence. 478 & nn.3–4.

His procedural objection was broader. Rule 56 contained neither a copyright exception nor a general presumption against summary judgment. With simplified pleading, an effective procedure testing the merits was especially important to avoid unnecessary trials and settlement pressure from weak claims. Clark regarded the majority’s factual-doubt formula as judicial rewriting of a nationally uniform rule. Protecting trial when a real dispute existed was essential; postponing judgment when the legal result was already clear was also an error. 478–480 & nn.5–6.

## Judgment and assigned significance

The panel divided **2–1**: Frank and Learned Hand formed the majority; Clark dissented. The judgment was **modified in part, otherwise reversed and remanded**. The identified uncopyrighted-work and title claims were converted to jurisdictional dismissals; the remaining adverse judgment was reversed for further proceedings. The result required a trial opportunity, not a finding that Porter copied or infringed. 474–475.
