# Little Sisters of the Poor Saints Peter and Paul Home v. Pennsylvania

**Course:** Legislation, Regulation & the State  
**Citation:** 591 U.S. 657 (2020)  
**Court and date:** Supreme Court of the United States, July 8, 2020  
**Opinion:** Justice Thomas, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Alito, Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh. Alito, joined by Gorsuch, concurred. Kagan, joined by Breyer, concurred in the judgment. Ginsburg, joined by Sotomayor, dissented. Judgment 7–2.  
**Assigned source:** LRS November pages 1–165 scan, PDF pages 92–97, printed pages 672–677; principal excerpt ends before Note, with judicial footnote 14 retained below it.  
**Brief ID:** LRS-MAX-LITTLESISTERS

## Case orientation

The Court held that the ACA authorized the agencies' religious and moral exemptions from the contraceptive-coverage mandate and that the 2018 final rules satisfied APA procedure. It reversed the Third Circuit and remanded, requiring dissolution of the nationwide preliminary injunction. The majority did not decide whether RFRA independently required or authorized these exemptions, whether the final rules were arbitrary and capricious, or whether the agencies had good cause to issue the earlier interim final rules without advance comment. Those distinct questions should not be folded into the holding.

## Statutory and regulatory background

The ACA required covered health plans to provide women's preventive care without cost sharing as specified in comprehensive guidelines supported by the Health Resources and Services Administration, HRSA. The statute did not expressly list contraception. HRSA's guidelines supplied that requirement. HHS, Labor, and Treasury jointly administered the relevant provisions and implemented the guidelines through rules. Grandfathered plans were exempt under separate statutory provisions. (Thomas slip opinion at 2–5; notes 1–4.)

An initial church exemption was followed by an accommodation for other eligible religious employers. Under that accommodation, an employer could certify its objection; the insurer or administrator would separately provide contraceptive services without charging the employer or beneficiaries. Some organizations nevertheless believed that certification and participation in the plan mechanism made them complicit in conduct contrary to their faith. The Little Sisters, Catholic religious organizations serving elderly poor people, were among those objectors.

Hobby Lobby held that the unaccommodated mandate violated RFRA as applied to the closely held corporate objectors before it. Zubik later remanded accommodation challenges without deciding the RFRA question, allowing attempts to devise an approach respecting religious exercise while preserving employees' coverage. The agencies subsequently reported that efforts to identify a feasible alternative had failed. The relevant disagreement concerned complicity, not simply whether employers could object to contraception. (At 6–10.)

In 2017, the agencies issued two interim final rules. One expanded the religious exemption, including to publicly traded employers with sincere religious objections. The other established a moral-objection exemption covering certain nonprofits and for-profit entities without publicly traded components. The accommodation remained available. The rules invoked good cause to take effect immediately but also requested comments. The agencies then issued 2018 final rules after considering comments, largely preserving the exemptions. (At 10–12.)

## Proceedings

Pennsylvania challenged the interim rules and obtained preliminary relief. New Jersey joined after promulgation of the final rules. They alleged lack of statutory authority and defective APA procedure. The district court preliminarily enjoined the final rules nationwide. The Third Circuit affirmed, finding no authority under the ACA or RFRA and concluding that the prior interim rules lacked good cause and the later comment process did not demonstrate an open mind.

The Little Sisters intervened to defend the exemption and appealed alongside the federal government. The Third Circuit's inquiry into their independent appellate standing was erroneous: the government had standing and both sought the same dissolution of the injunction. An intervenor's independent standing is required when seeking broader or different relief, not merely because it joins that request. This was not a holding that an intervenor never needs standing. (At 12–14; notes 5–6.)

## ACA authority

The five-Justice majority read the phrase specifying services as provided for in HRSA-supported guidelines to confer broad discretion over the guidelines, including exemptions. Congress had neither listed particular women's services nor supplied the limitations found in other statutory provisions. The same grant that authorized defining preventive services allowed identifying exceptions to HRSA's own requirements. Restricting the agency to selecting services while forbidding exemptions would add a limitation the text did not contain. (At 14–18.)

The majority did not resolve the empirical dispute about the exemptions' impact on women. It held that policy concerns could not replace what it considered the statute's plain meaning. It also noted tension between the states' reading and the earlier church exemption and accommodation. No party pressed a constitutional challenge to the breadth of the delegation; the Court decided statutory meaning, not nondelegation validity. Note 8 discussed possible alteration of the guidelines as contextual support, not a holding that every future removal of coverage without rulemaking would be valid. (At 16–18; notes 7–9.)

## Considering RFRA without deciding its independent effect

Because the ACA supplied authority, the majority did not reach the claim that RFRA independently compelled or permitted the exemption. It rejected the contention that the agencies could not consider RFRA at all. RFRA applied to federal law and its implementation, and the ACA did not exclude it. Hobby Lobby and Zubik gave the agencies substantial reason to account for sincere complicity objections. Ignoring RFRA altogether could expose a rule to arbitrary-and-capricious review for overlooking an important aspect of the problem. That observation did not establish that these particular final rules satisfied substantive reasoned-decisionmaking review. The majority also expressed no view on an unpresented constitutional claim. (At 19–22; notes 10–12.)

## APA procedure: content over title

The states argued that an interim final rule with a request for comments did not count as a notice of proposed rulemaking. The Court found the published material supplied the required legal authority and described the issues and substance in adequate detail. It invited comments on whether the rules should become permanent or change. The title did not defeat meaningful notice. Even assuming a formally titled notice was required, the states had not shown prejudicial error from the labeling. (At 22–24.)

The Court also rejected the Third Circuit's additional open-mindedness test. APA procedural compliance depended on objective statutory requirements, rather than an extra judicial demand that the agency demonstrate a flexible attitude by changing its proposal. Under Vermont Yankee and Perez, courts could not impose procedures beyond the APA's requirements. Substantial similarity between initial and final rules did not itself show procedural invalidity.

The agencies provided adequate notice, allowed sixty days for written comments, stated the final rules' basis and purpose, and published them more than thirty days before their effective date. The Court therefore found procedural compliance. It expressly declined to decide whether good cause justified the 2017 interim rules. The holding concerned the later final rules and did not retroactively establish that any earlier enforcement under an unlawfully issued interim rule would be valid. Nor did it excuse inadequate responses to significant comments or substantive irrationality. (At 24–26; notes 13–14; assigned printed pages 675–677.)

## Separate writings

Alito, joined by Gorsuch, joined the majority and would go further: RFRA required the religious exemption, or something very close to it, for employers sharing the Little Sisters' objections. He found a substantial burden from sincere complicity objections and severe noncompliance penalties. Courts could assess sincerity but could not redraw the religious line of moral causation. He considered the government's claimed interest insufficiently compelling given the many existing coverage exceptions and found alternatives less restrictive even if an interest were compelling. He rejected requiring the narrowest possible remedy for a RFRA violation and disputed that lack of an otherwise unrequired benefit constituted a government-imposed burden. This was a two-Justice concurrence, not the majority's RFRA holding. (Alito at 1–19; all 13 notes.)

Kagan, joined by Breyer, agreed that the final rules were procedurally valid but grounded statutory authority in Chevron deference rather than unambiguous text. She found the who-must-provide question ambiguous and the agencies' longstanding interpretation reasonable. She stressed that broad statutory authority did not establish reasoned decisionmaking. The exemptions extended to employers satisfied with the existing accommodation, beyond the particular unresolved complicity problem the agencies identified. She questioned that mismatch, inclusion of publicly traded companies, and the separately justified moral exemption. These concerns remained for the lower courts; she did not finally adjudicate their validity. (Kagan at 1–7; all 4 notes, particularly note 2.)

Ginsburg, joined by Sotomayor, would affirm on the merits. She read the ACA as assigning HRSA selection of which preventive services must be covered, while Congress itself specified who must provide them. Text, context, the parallel children's provision, and HRSA's health-access mission did not authorize wholesale employer exemptions. She distinguished the initial church exemption's constitutional rationale from the blanket exemption and argued RFRA did not permit disregarding employees' interests. In her view, the accommodation transferred the coverage obligation to insurers and did not substantially burden employers' own religious conduct. Sincerity did not entail a right to control third parties' actions. She emphasized loss of coverage and barriers to government programs or out-of-pocket purchase. She did not reach procedural validity and separately defended nationwide preliminary relief. (Ginsburg at 1–22; all 28 notes, particularly notes 17, 27–28.)

## Judgment and exam use

The judgment reversed and remanded, with the majority specifying dissolution of the nationwide preliminary injunction. Five Justices joined Thomas's reasoning; two additional Justices agreed with the judgment and procedural conclusion on different statutory grounds. Ginsburg and Sotomayor dissented. Do not describe the decision as unanimous or treat seven votes for the result as seven votes for the majority's plain-text rationale.

Separate legal authority, objective procedure, good cause for immediate interim effectiveness, RFRA compulsion, and reasoned decisionmaking. A final rule can satisfy notice and comment after a prior interim rule without proof of the agency's subjective open mind. That does not turn every substantive challenge into a procedural objection or foreclose review of an inadequately explained exemption.
