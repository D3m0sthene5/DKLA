# Henry Schein, Inc. v. Archer & White Sales, Inc.

**586 U.S. 63 (2019) — Supreme Court of the United States, January 8, 2019. Civil Procedure.** Justice Kavanaugh delivered a unanimous opinion. The Court rejected the “wholly groundless” exception to a valid contractual delegation of arbitrability, but did not decide whether this particular contract actually made such a delegation.

## Facts and procedural history

Archer & White, a small dental-equipment distributor, entered a distribution contract with manufacturer Pelton & Crane. After their relationship deteriorated, Archer & White sued Pelton & Crane’s successor and Henry Schein in federal district court in Texas. The complaint alleged federal and state antitrust violations and sought damages and an injunction. The Supreme Court’s opinion used “Schein” collectively for the defendants; it did not adjudicate their antitrust liability. 586 U.S. at 65–66.

The contract selected North Carolina law and required arbitration of disputes arising under or related to the agreement, with exceptions for actions seeking injunctive relief and certain intellectual-property disputes. It specified arbitration under American Arbitration Association rules in Charlotte. When Schein requested arbitration, Archer & White relied on the injunctive-relief exception: because its complaint requested an injunction, at least in part, it maintained that the case was outside the agreement. Id. at 66.

A second dispute concerned who should decide that first dispute about contractual scope. Schein argued that incorporation of AAA rules, which empower arbitrators to address arbitrability, delegated the question to an arbitrator. Archer & White responded that a court could reject arbitration itself when the argument for coverage was wholly groundless. Applying Fifth Circuit precedent, the district court accepted that exception, found Schein’s position wholly groundless, and denied its motion to compel. The circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted review because circuits disagreed about the exception’s validity. Id. at 66–67.

## Issue and holding

If parties have delegated arbitrability to an arbitrator, may a court nevertheless decide it because the argument for arbitration seems wholly groundless? No. The Federal Arbitration Act contains no such exception. A valid delegation allocates the decision to the arbitrator even when a court considers the proposed answer obviously wrong. Id. at 67–69.

The premise matters: the Court did not determine that this contract satisfied the requirement of clear and unmistakable delegation. That issue, along with other properly preserved arguments, remained for the Fifth Circuit on remand. The Court neither ordered ultimate arbitration of the antitrust claims nor decided how the injunctive-relief exception should be construed. Id. at 71–72.

## Contractual allocation and precedent

Section 2 of the FAA requires enforcement of covered written arbitration provisions, subject to generally applicable grounds for revocation of a contract. Arbitration is therefore a matter of the parties’ agreement. They may assign both a dispute’s merits and gateway questions about whether the agreement covers it to an arbitrator. A gateway delegation is itself an additional antecedent agreement entitled to enforcement under the Act. Id. at 67–68.

The Court rejected an exception that substituted a judge’s view of the arbitrability answer for the decisionmaker the parties selected. Precedent already prevented judges from deciding the merits of a delegated grievance merely because it appeared frivolous. The same principle governed a delegated gateway question. The contract allocates who decides, and an unfavorable judicial prediction about the likely answer does not change that allocation. Id. at 68–69.

This reasoning does not eliminate a court’s initial role in determining whether a valid arbitration agreement exists. Nor may a court assume that parties delegated arbitrability. The opinion reaffirmed the need for clear and unmistakable evidence of that agreement. The forbidden step is merits screening of a gateway issue after it has validly been assigned to the arbitrator. Id. at 69, 71–72.

## Archer & White’s four arguments

First, Archer & White relied on §§ 3 and 4, which describe a court’s satisfaction that an issue is referable to arbitration and that an arbitration agreement has been made. It interpreted those provisions as requiring courts always to decide arbitrability. The Court answered that its established decisions already permitted clear and unmistakable delegation. The existence of a valid agreement still required judicial determination, but the asserted statutory reading could not erase a valid delegation. Id. at 69.

Second, Archer & White relied on § 10(a)(4), authorizing later review when an arbitrator exceeds assigned powers. It argued that courts able to review arbitrability at the end should also be able to decide it at the beginning. The Court rejected that proposed redesign of Congress’s scheme. The argument would also invite front-end judicial decision of supposedly frivolous merits claims delegated to arbitration, contrary to existing precedent. Authorized later review did not supply the asserted exception. Id. at 69–70.

Third, Archer & White argued that referring an obviously unarbitrable dispute wasted money and time because the arbitrator would send it back. The Court first found no textual exception it could insert on that basis. It also doubted the asserted systemic savings: litigation over the difference between groundless and wholly groundless could become a costly collateral proceeding, and an arbitrator might disagree with a judge’s supposedly obvious answer. Possible savings in a particular case did not establish a justification for altering contractual authority. Id. at 70–71.

Fourth, Archer & White argued that the exception was needed to deter frivolous motions to compel arbitration. The Court again declined to rewrite the statute for that policy objective. It observed that arbitrators could quickly reject unsupported arbitrability arguments and, in appropriate circumstances, use fee- or cost-shifting sanctions. It did not establish that sanctions were automatically available in every arbitration. The Court also was unaware of a substantial frivolous-motion problem in circuits rejecting the exception. Id. at 71.

Together, these answers distinguish who has authority to resolve arbitrability from who should prevail on that issue. They do not presume that arbitration is the correct ultimate forum for every disputed claim. Id.

## Judgment, assigned account, and source scope

The Supreme Court **vacated the Fifth Circuit’s judgment and remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion**. Whether this agreement actually delegated the gateway issue remained undecided. The circuit could address that question in the first instance and consider other properly preserved arguments. Id. at 72.
