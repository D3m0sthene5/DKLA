# Nollenberger v. United Air Lines, Inc.

**CP-118 · Civil Procedure · 216 F. Supp. 734 (S.D. Cal. 1963) · April 12, 1963 · Chief Judge Hall**

This district-court decision illustrates the power—and danger—of converting answers to damage interrogatories into a judgment different from the jury’s general verdict. Hall treated the answers as a sufficiently complete set of factual findings to calculate an increased award. The assigned material also reports that the Ninth Circuit later vacated that disposition because the answers did not exhaust the relevant damage factors. These are distinct procedural stages; the district court’s mathematical approach should not be presented as the final resolution of the litigation.

## Facts and procedural setting

These wrongful-death actions arose from an aircraft midair collision on April 21, 1958. Several related actions had previously been tried together in Nevada, with questions between the United States and United Air Lines severed from the passenger claims. Following earlier liability rulings, counsel stipulated to transferring specified actions to the Southern District of California. The immediate opinion concerns the Nollenberger, Matlock, Theobald, and Thompson families’ damages proceedings. Their cases were tried successively to one jury, with a verdict in each before the next case began. Entry of judgments was withheld to permit simultaneous judgments and a uniform appeal period. The separate Paris verdict was mentioned for procedural context; no motion for a new trial had yet been made in that case. 216 F. Supp. at 736–738 & nn. 1–3.

In Nollenberger, the jury found that William Edward Nollenberger had a 25-year life expectancy and a 15-year work expectancy. It specified $235,210 in expected earnings, $100,200 in subsequent pension receipts, and $25,000 in personal services to the family. It supplied percentages for the decedent’s personal expenses, income taxes, taxation of income from the eventual award, inflation, and discounting. Despite those subsidiary findings, it returned general damages of $114,655. The other families likewise received general verdicts accompanied by answers to interrogatories. The plaintiffs first sought new trials for insufficiency, inadequacy, and inconsistency; they renewed their requests in the alternative for additional jury questions, judgments calculated from the answers, or new trials. United moved to strike an affidavit of Chapin. Id. at 737–740, 745–753.

## Issue and governing rule

Could the court reconcile the special answers with the general verdict, and, if it could not, could it enter damages calculated from those answers or submit additional interrogatories after the jury had returned both forms of decision?

The historical Rule 49(b), reproduced in full in judicial note 4, differentiated several conditions. Harmonious answers and verdict required an appropriate judgment on both. Consistent answers that conflicted with the general verdict permitted judgment on the answers, further jury consideration of the existing answers and verdict, or a new trial. Answers inconsistent with each other and with the verdict did not permit the court to choose a judgment from them; resubmission or a new trial remained available. Before finding a conflict, the court had to attempt a fair reconciliation. Hall applied that obligation from *Gallick*. Id. at 737–740 & n. 4.

## Holding and reasoning

Hall found the eleven Nollenberger answers internally consistent and supported by the evidence, but irreconcilable with the general verdict after repeated efforts to harmonize them. He reached the same conclusion for Matlock. His conclusion depended on treating the answers as supplying the components necessary to compute compensation; arithmetic, in his view, could implement the jury’s findings without substituting new factual judgments. Id. at 740–742.

The court rejected the request for additional interrogatories. It read the rule’s direction to obtain written answers and a general verdict together, and its authorization to return the jury for further consideration of “its answers and verdict,” as confining that procedure to the questions already submitted. Advisory Committee authorities emphasizing the controlling force of special findings supported that reading. Hall examined contrary procedural examples but found none sufficiently authoritative to justify the proposed additional questions under this rule. He expressly reserved whether inquiry would be permissible after a jury had returned only a general verdict. Id. at 740–742 & n. 5.

Having rejected new questions, Hall concluded that the court should calculate judgment from the consistent answers before granting a new trial. Civil-service salary and step increases were prescribed by statutes already explained to the jury; the court characterized their application as legal calculation rather than replacement of jury factfinding. It divided compensation into past earnings, remaining work earnings, pension receipts, personal services, and provision for taxes on income produced by the award. Past losses accrued interest; future losses were discounted. Personal expenses, income taxes, and required pension contributions were deducted where appropriate. The opinion includes detailed calculations, jury-answer tables, an income-tax instruction in Appendix I, and present-value factors in Appendix II. Id. at 742–755 & nn. 5a–8.

## Exact disposition and assigned later treatment

Hall denied additional interrogatories and new trials, granted United’s motions to strike Chapin’s affidavit, and directed preparation of judgments: $171,702 for Nollenberger, $207,420 for Matlock, $162,412 for Theobald, and $138,678.40 for Thompson. Theobald and Thompson’s answers yielded substantially the same figures as their verdicts, so their requests for different awards were denied. The court additionally directed counsel to account for stipulated special damages as specified in the closing paragraph; the Thompson verdict table already separately displays $1,055.40 in stipulated special damages. Id. at 745, 749, 751, 753. This is a single district judge’s opinion; the report contains no separate judicial writing.

The assigned Notes quote the later appellate decision, 335 F.2d 379, 407–409, and report vacatur. The quoted passage explains that the jury instructions allowed consideration of additional factors, including the decedent’s disposition and inclination to provide support and the pecuniary value of society, care, comfort, and protection. Thus the eleven answers did not dictate a single fixed formula or squarely conflict with the verdict. The assigned account gives plaintiffs a choice between the general verdict and a return for a ruling on new-trial motions. That later-stage discussion is drawn from the assigned quotation and its retained judicial note, not a claim that this brief covers the complete appellate opinion. The study lesson is to test whether the interrogatories truly exhaust the relevant factual issues before treating an apparently different total as an inconsistency.
