# be2 LLC v. Ivanov

**642 F.3d 555 (7th Cir. 2011), decided April 27, 2011; amended May 19, 2011.** Civil Procedure — specific personal jurisdiction, Internet contacts, and relief from a default judgment. Judge Hamilton wrote for Judges Flaum, Wood, and Hamilton; there was no separate opinion. The assigned account is CivPro PDF page 126, printed page 203, Note 3. Pinpoints below use the court's ten-page April 27 slip opinion, read together with its one-page amendment.

## The dispute and the jurisdictional evidence

Two companies operated the dating website be2.com: a Delaware LLC headquartered in Delaware and its German parent, be2 Holding, A.G. The service initially served European singles and subsequently expanded to about fourteen million users in thirty-six countries, including the United States. They sued Nikolay Ivanov, a New Jersey resident, in federal court in Illinois. Their complaint asserted Lanham Act trademark claims, federal common-law claims, and claims under the Illinois Uniform Deceptive Trade Practices Act. The alleged infringement was choosing and using a competing website's domain name and design in a way intended to confuse consumers. These were allegations, not merits findings made by the appellate court. Slip pp. 1–2.

According to the complaint, Ivanov was the cofounder and CEO of be2.net and handled most of its business transactions. He previously offered matchmaking through sladurana.com. In December 2006, he allegedly moved that service to be2.net, which had previously contained links to other sites, deliberately adopting a domain and presentation confusingly similar to be2.com. The alleged commercial connection to a rival dating service supplied the substance of the trademark dispute. It did not itself explain why Illinois could adjudicate it. Slip p. 2.

Ivanov failed to answer and did not attend a scheduled status hearing. The district court entered default under Rule 55(a), followed by a default judgment under Rule 55(b)(2). In support of damages, the plaintiffs submitted their co-CEO Andreas Etten's declaration with Internet printouts. Those documents also became central to personal jurisdiction. One printout, made a year before the hearing, showed ten men and ten women with Chicago addresses registered on be2.net. Other pages described Ivanov as a cofounder and CEO, associated him with profile approval, design, advertising, and censorship, and advertised free dating services. A be2.net-branded page also disclaimed affiliation with be2.com. Slip p. 3.

The Chicago printout required careful interpretation. It listed users' nicknames, ages, reported cities, and desired relationships. It did not establish twenty paying Illinois customers, ongoing individual interactions with Ivanov, or an Illinois advertising campaign. Even whether those individuals remained active users and Illinois residents was uncertain. The distinction between evidence of users' locations and evidence of the defendant's conduct drove the result. Slip pp. 9–10.

## Default, the motion to vacate, and appellate review

After judgment, Ivanov appeared through counsel and moved to vacate it as void for lack of personal jurisdiction. His affidavit claimed that he merely volunteered for Sladur, a Bulgarian company that owned be2.net. He said that he translated content, answered some inquiries, and approved profiles as an uncompensated hobby while holding another full-time job. He denied being a conventional CEO, offering the explanation that the initials meant “Centralized Expert Operator,” and said he had never entered Illinois. The district court had ample grounds to disbelieve that explanation. It denied relief, treating the list of Chicago registrations as sufficient Illinois contacts attributable to him. Slip pp. 4–5.

On appeal, Ivanov again represented himself. Because he knew about the litigation and elected to default rather than defend, he bore the burden on his postjudgment challenge to personal jurisdiction. The court did not treat his default as an affirmative submission to Illinois jurisdiction. Nor did it allow the plaintiffs to insulate the judgment by describing the denial of relief as layered exercises of discretion: a default judgment entered without personal jurisdiction is void, and denying a motion to vacate it is necessarily an abuse of discretion. Slip pp. 5–6.

There is an important amendment to the procedural description. The original slip opinion's footnote 1 classified the motion technically under Rule 59(e), based on its timing, while relying on Rule 60(b)(4) precedents. The May 19 order expressly deleted that footnote in its entirety. The operative decision should therefore be described as reviewing the postjudgment motion to vacate for want of personal jurisdiction, rather than presenting the deleted timing-based classification as a holding. The opinion's surviving text still discusses void-judgment and Rule 60(b)(4) precedents. Slip pp. 5–6; May 19 amendment.

## Why the Illinois registrations did not establish jurisdiction

The Lanham Act supplied no nationwide service of process for this suit. Rule 4(k)(1)(A) therefore required a basis under Illinois law consistent with the Constitution. Illinois's long-arm statute extended jurisdiction to the limits permitted by the state and federal constitutions, making the federal due-process question dispositive here. The question was whether Ivanov had minimum contacts supporting specific jurisdiction over these claims—not whether a federal trademark claim existed or whether his conduct might be unlawful. Slip pp. 6–7.

The court asked whether he had purposefully exploited the Illinois market. An Internet defendant must have a forum connection attributable to its own deliberate conduct; merely operating an interactive website accessible there is insufficient. The point is to prevent random, attenuated contacts or third parties' unilateral choices from exposing a defendant to suit everywhere the Internet reaches. A website's functionality can describe what users are able to do without establishing where its operator deliberately seeks business. Slip pp. 7–9.

The court compared the record with a case involving a substantial Illinois advertising campaign, hundreds of thousands of customers, and millions of dollars in annual revenues. Here there was no corresponding evidence of targeting Illinois or interaction between Ivanov and the people whose profiles listed Illinois addresses. They might simply have encountered the site on their own and clicked a button that automatically published their preferences. Their actions did not establish his purposeful exploitation of the state. This was a failure of the demonstrated defendant–forum connection, not a mathematical rule that twenty customers can never suffice. Slip pp. 9–10.

Ivanov's credibility did not change that conclusion. The court agreed that parts of his affidavit were implausible. Even after discounting those assertions, however, the plaintiffs' own materials did not supply the missing Illinois connection. Proving that he overstated or mischaracterized his business role would not prove that he targeted Illinois. A jurisdictional decision requires the necessary affirmative relationship to the forum; disbelief of a defendant's explanation cannot manufacture that relationship. Slip pp. 6–7, 9–10.

## Holding, judgment, and assigned-source comparison

On this record, requiring Ivanov to defend these claims in Illinois would violate due process. The Seventh Circuit **reversed the order denying his postjudgment motion and remanded with instructions to vacate the default judgment and dismiss the complaint for lack of personal jurisdiction**. It did not adjudicate infringement, clear Ivanov of wrongdoing, or decide where a later suit would succeed. Slip p. 10.

The textbook accurately emphasizes the possibility that twenty Chicago registrations resulted from users' unilateral activity. Its compressed account describes evidence that Illinois residents accessed the website; the opinion provides the more precise facts about old printouts and free profiles. The practical lesson is to seek evidence of the defendant's deliberate forum-directed conduct and claim-related interactions, rather than treating the existence of local registrants as sufficient. The textbook's question about where to sue remains a question, not part of the judgment.
