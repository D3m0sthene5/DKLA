# Britton v. Turner
6 N.H. 481 (1834) | Contracts

## A year’s service left unfinished
Britton worked for Turner from March 9 to December 27, 1831. Turner introduced evidence that the work was under an agreement for a year’s labor, extending into March 1832, for $120. He also maintained that Britton left without consent or good cause. Britton sued in assumpsit under common counts, including quantum meruit, alleging that the work actually done was worth $100. No evidence of damage from departure was introduced beyond an inference from failure to complete the term. 6 N.H. at 482–83.

The trial court instructed that, even if the jury found an annual contract and an unjustified early departure, Britton could recover the reasonable value of the service performed. The jury awarded $95, and Turner excepted to the instruction. His argument emphasized an entire contract’s condition of complete performance and distinguished building or goods cases in which a recipient might consent to accept partial performance. Labor already received could not be returned, he argued, so no replacement contract could fairly be inferred against his will. Id. at 483–85.

## No contract recovery, but possible payment for retained benefit
Justice Parker assumed the annual bargain and voluntary noncompletion. Britton could not recover the agreed $120 on that contract because the service earning that sum had not been fully performed. The separate question was whether the beneficial service already received supported quantum meruit. Id. at 485–86.

The court rejected total forfeiture as the necessary consequence. Someone who performed nothing would owe only damages for the breach, while a worker who provided nearly a year of beneficial service could lose everything under the stricter rule. That would sometimes give the employer a windfall far exceeding actual injury. The court discussed conflicting authorities on incomplete labor, buildings, and partial deliveries; it did not suggest that the precedents were uniform. Id. at 486–90.

Turner’s inability to reject yesterday’s labor did not distinguish employment from every other retained-benefit case. By hiring for work received daily, the employer agreed from the outset to receive successive benefits and knew completion might fail. That prior assent could support payment for the net benefit just as later acceptance of incomplete construction or goods could. The court described the received benefit as a new consideration supporting an implied promise; it did not deem the original annual contract performed or divisible into independently payable monthly contracts. Id. at 490–93.

## Contract ceiling, damages, and the employer’s choices
Recovery was limited to reasonable value actually retained over the injury caused by the breach, with the contract price a ceiling. The employer remained entitled to full performance and could recover damages for failure to provide it. Completion expense and other breach damage reduced the benefit payable to the worker; if injury equaled or exceeded the value received, nothing was recoverable. An employer who could reject an incomplete performance and had not already accepted it could refuse benefit and payment. Id. at 492–94.

The court also said the parties could expressly agree that no compensation would be earned upon an unjustified departure before completing the term. Its treatment of ordinary hiring rested partly on a presumed community understanding that beneficial labor earns compensation, absent that express contrary stipulation. Those historical qualifications are part of the reasoning, rather than a present-day wage-law proposition. Id. at 493–94.

The complete opinion adds remedial details omitted from the excerpt. Turner could elect to assert breach damages defensively or allow the labor-value judgment and bring a separate damages action. If he elected an adjustment in the worker’s action, the court treated the damages as confined there to the value received, precluding a later claim for further damages. Here he had neither proved damage nor requested a deduction, so his mere assertion of breach could not defeat the $95 verdict. A later suit for actual injury remained available. Id. at 494–95.

## Judgment and questions expressly left open
The court ordered judgment on the verdict for Britton. It expressly declined to decide whether a departing worker must notify the employer, offer an adjustment and demand payment, or await the contractual payment date before suing: no such objections had been taken. Id. at 495–96. The assigned excerpt omits that reservation.
