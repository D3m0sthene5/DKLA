# Rios v. State of Maryland
186 Md. App. 354, 974 A.2d 366 (2009) | Contracts

## Negotiations and procedural posture
Jeffrey Rios faced eleven charges in Prince George’s County: three counts each of first-degree assault, second-degree assault, and using a handgun in a crime of violence, plus reckless endangerment and unlawful handgun carrying. His attorney, Ronald Schwartz, negotiated with the assigned prosecutor for approximately two weeks. Before the week of April 7, 2008, the prosecutor offered a plea to handgun possession and reckless endangerment with time served. Around April 8 Schwartz rejected that proposal and said he could recommend an Alford plea to reckless endangerment with time served. The prosecutor agreed, but Schwartz expressly said he needed to discuss it with Rios and respond. Rios subsequently authorized acceptance. The parties stipulated to these negotiation facts; they are not findings that Rios committed the charged offenses. (186 Md. App. at 357–359.)

Schwartz left voicemail messages concerning acceptance on April 18 and 21. The motions court found those messages did not actually communicate unconditional acceptance, a finding Rios did not challenge. When the prosecutor returned the call April 22, Schwartz asked whether he would consider a nolo plea to reckless endangerment. The prosecutor refused and said police and his superiors were pressuring him not to offer a bargain. Schwartz then communicated acceptance of the Alford arrangement. The prosecutor responded that he was meeting with those persons and did not think he could offer a plea; later that week he said no offer would be made. (Id. at 359, 368–369.)

Rios moved May 23 to enforce the agreement and dismiss all but reckless endangerment. The circuit court denied the motion after a June 13 hearing. It treated the nolo question as a counteroffer rejecting the outstanding Alford offer, rather than a mere inquiry. Rios appealed July 7 without having entered the plea; the trial was continued pending appeal. The State moved to dismiss the appeal as interlocutory. Judge Meredith wrote the June 12, 2009 appellate opinion for a panel also including Judges Deborah Eyler and Zarnoch; the report contains no separate writing or numerical vote. (Id. at 354, 357, 359–363.)

## Why immediate appellate review was available
The court rejected the State’s jurisdictional objection under Maryland’s collateral-order doctrine. Four strictly construed, conjunctive conditions required conclusive resolution of an important question, separateness from the action’s merits, and effective unreviewability after final judgment. The order conclusively rejected the alleged agreement and concerned an important issue: enforcement would remove all but one charge and avoid trial. The agreement’s existence was distinct from Rios’s guilt or innocence. Waiting for a verdict would destroy a substantial benefit of the alleged bargain—the avoidance of trial’s expense, inconvenience, and uncertainty. (Id. at 363–365.)

The State argued that authorities involving enforcement of an existing agreement were different because formation was disputed here and Rios had not detrimentally relied. The civil settlement decision Clark v. Elza likewise concerned formation without detrimental reliance, defeating that attempted distinction. Although Maryland limits appeals resting on an asserted right to avoid trial to extraordinary circumstances, plea-bargain enforcement qualified under the criminal precedents and the policy favoring negotiated dispositions. This is a specific collateral-order holding, not permission to appeal every contract dispute before final judgment. (Id. at 365–366.)

## Identifying the offer and distinguishing inquiry from rejection
Plea negotiations involve fair play, equity, and due-process safeguards, not rigid application of contract law alone. Courts are not ordinarily bound by a prosecutor-defendant compact, and the State cannot obtain specific performance of a defendant’s promise to plead guilty. Here, however, no detrimental reliance or unfair negotiating conduct was suggested; ordinary formation principles could determine whether an agreement existed. (Id. at 366–368.)

Rios first argued that Schwartz made an offer April 8 and the prosecutor accepted it then. The court rejected that sequence. Schwartz conditioned his proposal on later client approval, so it was not an offer immediately capable of acceptance. The prosecutor’s approval instead constituted the outstanding offer, and actual client acceptance had to be communicated. The unchallenged treatment of the voicemail messages meant the operative communication occurred during the April 22 conversation. (Id. at 368.)

A counteroffer would reject the original offer, making subsequent attempted acceptance ineffective. But Restatement (Second) §39 comment b distinguishes a counteroffer from a tentative inquiry about better or different terms. A counteroffer must itself meet the requirements of an offer and manifest more than curiosity: it proposes substitution rather than keeping the original proposal under consideration. The Restatement’s example of asking a land seller to take less, receiving a refusal, and then accepting the original offer illustrated the distinction. (Id. at 369–370.)

Schwartz asked whether the prosecutor would consider allowing a nolo plea. On the stipulated facts this was a mere inquiry, not a conditional acceptance or a substituted offer. It left the Alford offer available for the immediately following acceptance. The substantive difference between the pleas did not make every question about switching them a rejection. Footnote 1 specifically describes the court’s historical Maryland distinction: nolo was not an admission of conduct or a conviction and could not be inculpatory evidence in another trial; an Alford plea was a form of guilty plea accompanied by continued denial of guilt. Those descriptions explain the proposed change, not a universal current-law rule for every jurisdiction. (Id. at 369 n.1, 370–371.)

## Holding, reservations, and exact disposition
Schwartz communicated acceptance before the prosecutor attempted withdrawal. The parties therefore formed an agreement binding on the State, subject to court approval. Implementation still required presentation to a court with discretion to accept or reject the plea and approval of any specified sentence or judicial action. The appellate court did not itself enter a guilty plea, convict Rios, impose time served, or finally dismiss the ten other charges. It also expressly declined to assess the continuing vitality of Kisamore after Mabry v. Johnson: the State had not raised that issue. (Id. at 362–363, 371.)

The State’s motion to dismiss the appeal was denied. The order denying enforcement was vacated, and the case was remanded for proceedings consistent with the opinion, with costs payable by Prince George’s County. The assigned account labels the case State v. Rios and describes the result as reversed; the original caption is Rios v. State, and its exact operative disposition is vacatur and remand. (Id. at 371–372.)
