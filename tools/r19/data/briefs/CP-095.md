# Fassett v. Sears Holdings Corp.

**Course:** Civil Procedure
**ID:** CP-095
**Review:** Complete source comparison; assigned boundary, party status, and concrete discovery relief clarified. Independent source review passed.

## Citation

319 F.R.D. 143 (M.D. Pa. 2017), No. 4:15-cv-00941, decided January 27, 2017. Judge Matthew W. Brann. Casebook pp. 905–914; source PDF pp. 465–469 (principal excerpt and five following editorial notes); p. 470 begins the next case.

## Parties

Daniel Fassett and related plaintiffs sued Sears Holdings Corporation and entities involved in the design, manufacture, or sale of the Craftsman riding lawnmower and its fuel system. Bemis Manufacturing Company, a third-party defendant, made the gas cap; Briggs & Stratton entities manufactured the mower or relevant systems and were among the defendants from whom discovery was sought.

## Material Facts

Fassett had operated a Craftsman Zero Turn riding mower for about an hour when he heard sputtering from the fuel tank. After shutting the mower down in his garage, he observed pressure and hissing near the gas cap. When he loosened the cap, gasoline sprayed over him and ignited, causing severe burns. His products-liability allegations included pressurized gasoline and an allegedly defective fuel-system design.

During discovery, plaintiffs sought materials concerning alternative gas-cap designs, other mower configurations, testing, warranty information, prior claims, and related litigation. Defendants objected that much of the requested material concerned dissimilar products or imposed disproportionate burdens. 319 F.R.D. at 147–48; CB 905–907.

## Procedural History

Plaintiffs filed the products-liability action in May 2015. After earlier motion practice, discovery disputes developed during 2016 concerning alternative cap and mower designs. The court held several conferences and encouraged negotiated resolution. Plaintiffs ultimately filed motions to compel against Bemis and the Briggs & Stratton defendants. The memorandum resolved those motions under the amended Rule 26(b)(1), granting them in part and denying them in part and setting renewed case-management deadlines. 319 F.R.D. at 148, 158; CB 906–914.

## Issue

How should a court apply Rule 26(b)(1)’s relevance and proportionality requirements to discovery requests in a products-liability case seeking information about alternative components, designs, prior accidents, and other products that differ from the component involved in the plaintiff’s accident?

## Rule and Holding

Discovery under Rule 26(b)(1) must be relevant to a claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case. In a technically complex products-liability dispute, similarity operates on a sliding scale: information concerning designs or components that are significantly similar in function and relevant characteristics is more broadly discoverable, while information concerning increasingly dissimilar designs is correspondingly narrower in scope and permissible use. Prior-accident and claim material requires substantial similarity appropriate to the purpose for which it is sought. Applying those principles, the court compelled some discovery concerning functionally similar caps and mower designs but rejected or narrowed broader requests. 319 F.R.D. at 149–58; CB 907–914.

## Judgment

The plaintiffs’ motions to compel were granted in part and denied in part. A separate order specified the rulings request by request and established renewed case-management deadlines. 319 F.R.D. at 158; accompanying January 27, 2017 Order at 2–4.

## Reasoning

The 2015 amendment to Rule 26(b)(1) placed proportionality in the operative definition of discovery scope. The court treated that requirement as demanding a practical relationship between the likely informational value of requested material and the burden and expense of producing it. In product-design discovery, similarity is therefore not an all-or-nothing category. Functional similarity, common testing standards, interchangeability, and knowledgeable technical testimony can support discovery beyond the precise model involved in the accident; declining similarity reduces the justification for broad production. 319 F.R.D. at 149–53; CB 907–911.

The court distinguished ordinary design and testing information from prior-accident or litigation files. Evidence of prior incidents used to show notice or defect depends more heavily on substantial similarity of circumstances, so broad production concerning materially different caps or systems was unwarranted. The court also limited temporal scope by reference to periods when the relevant products were manufactured and sold rather than mechanically measuring from the date of injury. Throughout, it emphasized that discovery breadth does not decide admissibility or ultimate product liability. 319 F.R.D. at 153–58; CB 911–914.

The concrete rulings reflect those distinctions. Warranty and testing information concerning open, screw, covered, and duckbill caps was discoverable, subject to work-product protection; prior-claim files concerning the three alternative designs were excluded because plaintiffs had not established substantial similarity of the accidents. Mower discovery was confined to the Simplicity Axion, Snapper 150Z, Craftsman ZT7000, Craftsman ZT75000, and Coronet. The court rejected the requested settlement releases and related communications under Evidence Rule 408, allowed specified claims-review records, and required sufficiently specific privilege logs. It set January 1, 2000 as the earliest discovery date, approximately five years before manufacture, and kept plaintiffs’ deposition-containing submissions sealed. These were discovery decisions, with no product-liability merits determination. 319 F.R.D. at 153–158; January 27 Order at 2–4.

None. This was a district-court discovery memorandum.

## Notes

The case illustrates proportionality as a method of calibrating discovery rather than a separate threshold that mechanically defeats relevant requests. The closer the requested material is to the disputed component and the theories actually litigated, the stronger the justification for broader discovery.

The court expressly separated discovery rulings from merits determinations. A component can be sufficiently similar to justify discovery without thereby qualifying as a feasible alternative design or proving defect at trial.
