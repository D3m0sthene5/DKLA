# Kanavos v. Hancock Bank & Trust Co.
395 Mass. 199, 479 N.E.2d 168 (1985) | Contracts

## Sale without the promised opportunity to match
Hancock Bank owned all stock of 1025 Hancock, Inc., which held Executive House, a fourteen-story Quincy apartment building. The corporation was a limited-dividend corporation and its $2.5 million first mortgage carried federal housing guarantees. On July 16, 1976, the bank’s executive vice president, Brown, promised Kanavos $40,000 for surrendering an existing option and a sixty-day opportunity to match the price of a future sale, beginning when the offer was received. In November the bank agreed to sell the stock to a third person for $760,000 and completed that sale in early December without giving Kanavos notice or a chance to match. 395 Mass. at 200.

After an earlier appeal and retrial, the jury found the apartment building worth $4 million. Treating stock value as building equity, the parties subtracted the $2.5 million mortgage and $760,000 stock price to obtain $740,000 damages, then added the $40,000 surrender payment for a $780,000 judgment. Special verdicts addressed Brown’s authority, property value, and the surrender payment. The bank challenged only omission of the issue whether Kanavos could have bought within the option period. Id. at 199–201 & nn.1–2.

## Tender excused, ability still material
The trial judge excluded financial ability as immaterial because the bank had already repudiated. The Supreme Judicial Court disagreed. Once the bank was prepared to accept a third-party offer, the right of first refusal became substantially equivalent to an option on those terms. Stock delivery and payment would have been concurrent performances. Kanavos need not make a meaningless tender after the stock was sold, but excusing an actual offer of performance did not eliminate his ability to perform the condition of the bank’s duty. Id. at 201–03.

Ability was required during the sixty-day option period, not necessarily on the day of the bank’s sale. A buyer unable to carry out the purchase should not receive the profit of that transaction merely because the seller repudiated first. The court distinguished situations in which the repudiator substantially prevented the other party from acquiring the ability to perform; that was not this case. It also rejected a broad reading of older Lowe language suggesting that inability to pay never matters after repudiation. The historical authorities were not uniform, but the court found that contrary principle had lost support and supplied no compelling reason for adoption. Id. at 201–04 & nn.4–6.

## The claimant carried the burden of financing proof
Kanavos had to prove that, given proper notice, he would have been ready, willing, and able to finance the purchase within the option period. His ability was essential to liability and financing circumstances were much better known to him than to the bank. The fact that the bank wrongfully created the hypothetical situation did not transfer the burden of disproving an element of his case. The opinion recognized contrary authorities placing this burden on a repudiating optionor and compared a distinct Restatement approach concerning failed express conditions. It nevertheless applied the plaintiff’s burden here. Id. at 204–05.

The evidence could support ability, so the court did not enter judgment for the bank. Kanavos admitted financial difficulty but identified potential assisting contacts and testified that timely notice would have permitted a purchase. The property’s substantial equity suggested financing was possible; allowing the $40,000 credit would reduce the amount payable to $720,000. Whether income could support financing within the federal mortgage restrictions, or whether his rights could be sold, remained factual questions. The jury needed to decide them. Id. at 205–06.

## A limited retrial, not a reopening of every finding
The court remanded solely to decide whether, with proper notice, Kanavos would have been ready, willing, and able to buy during the option period. It saw no reason to retry issues the jury had already resolved, and the bank did not contest the $40,000 payment. Footnote 3 rejected the preservation objection: the ability issue had been presented before the jury retired, and the appellate challenge encompassed the related missing verdict question. Id. at 201 n.3, 206.
