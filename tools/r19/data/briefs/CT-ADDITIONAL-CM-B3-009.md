# Caspi v. Microsoft Network, L.L.C.
323 N.J. Super. 118, 732 A.2d 528 (App. Div. 1999) | Contracts

## Court and disposition
The Appellate Division of the New Jersey Superior Court decided Caspi on July 2, 1999. Judge Kestin delivered the opinion of the court, with Judges Long and Wefing on the panel; no separate writing or dissent appears. It affirmed dismissal of the New Jersey action pursuant to the membership agreement's exclusive King County, Washington forum clause. This was a determination about where the claims must proceed, not a merits judgment rejecting the subscribers' accusations about increased charges. (323 N.J. Super. at 118, 120, 126.)

## Subscribers, allegations, and procedural chronology
Four MSN subscribers sued Microsoft Network and Microsoft Corporation. Two lived in New Jersey, one in Ohio, and one in New York. Their eighteen-count amended complaint asserted contract, common-law fraud, and consumer-fraud theories arising from alleged conversion of memberships to more expensive plans without notice or permission. They sought to represent approximately 1.5 million subscribers nationwide. The opinion describes the asserted negative-option billing as alleged misconduct, distinguishing it from separate action by state attorneys general concerning competitor America Online. It does not find Microsoft liable for those practices. (Id. at 120–121.)

The original complaint was filed February 24, 1997, and the first amendment March 5. Microsoft's March 26 federal removal petition was withdrawn April 24. After the subscribers sought multistate class certification in May, Microsoft moved to dismiss on the forum clause. The subscribers sought to strike a supporting certification and depose its author. On November 13, Judge Fitzpatrick dismissed the complaint, denied those cross-motions in consequence, and denied class certification as moot. He later modified a passage of his opinion by a December 19 order. The appellate court reviewed the resulting dismissal and was in substantial agreement with the trial judge's reasons. (Id. at 120–121 & nn. 1–2, 124.)

## Agreement and enrollment process
Paragraph 15.1 provided that Washington law governed the agreement and that subscribers consented to exclusive jurisdiction and venue in King County courts for disputes arising from MSN use or membership. The court rejected attempts to construe that language as permitting suit elsewhere or as insufficiently clear. Its limiting effect was plain. The forum ruling should nevertheless be distinguished from a complete adjudication of the separate choice-of-law questions. (Id. at 121, 124.)

Before joining, a prospective subscriber encountered multiple information screens, including a membership agreement in a scrollable window beside “I Agree” and “I Don't Agree” choices. Either could be clicked at any point while scrolling. Registration could proceed only after an opportunity to view the agreement and affirmative assent; charges began only after that process and the agreement click. The court did not describe a system mechanically forcing the subscriber to read every line or scroll to the bottom. It relied on opportunity to review, affirmative acceptance, and the ability to reject before incurring charges. (Id. at 122–123.)

## General enforceability and asserted exceptions
The court accepted New Jersey's general rule treating forum-selection clauses as prima facie valid, with the objector bearing the burden of showing fraud or overweening bargaining power, conflict with strong state public policy, or serious inconvenience of trial. Corporate size alone did not demonstrate unfairly exploited bargaining inequality. MSN operated in a competitive service market; customers could select another service, and the agreement could be rejected without a charge. The clause itself was clear and contained no material misrepresentation. Those circumstances failed to establish the asserted fraud or bargaining-power exception. (Id. at 122–123.)

The court also upheld the public-policy analysis. Earlier New Jersey decisions invalidating particular forum provisions rested on policies not applicable here. Requiring litigation in Washington did not itself defeat consumer-fraud protection. The subscribers had given no reason to conclude Washington's consumer protections were materially different or narrower, considered as a policy matter apart from the agreement's choice-of-law provision. That is a statement about the showing in this case, not an exhaustive comparison of the two states' laws. (Id. at 123–124.)

Because the named plaintiffs lived in several states and the proposed class encompassed many locations, the trial court found Washington no more inconvenient to all parties than another location. The appellate court accepted that reasoning. The nationwide class was still only proposed; affirmance did not certify it or adjudicate its claims. (Id. at 123–124.)

## Notice in an electronic form
The remaining substantial challenge was that the clause had never become part of the contract because adequate notice was absent. The subscribers alternatively sought a jury determination of notice. Microsoft invoked the duty to read a form agreement, conspicuousness, and the signup opportunity to review and reject. The appellate court said Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute did not itself resolve notice: the passengers there had essentially conceded reasonable communication of that clause. Caspi therefore independently addressed this record's presentation. (Id. at 124–125.)

The electronic medium did not materially change the comparison with a printed form. Subscribers could scroll through the terms before accepting. The clause was the first item in the last paragraph, in the same lower-case presentation as most terms. A few other paragraphs used capital letters, but the court found nothing about the clause's placement or mode of presentation showing unfair concealment or de-emphasis. Rejecting the provision on that basis would undermine identically presented terms generally, even though the subscribers necessarily knew they were entering a contract. The court applied the principle that unread form terms may bind a party, supported here by reasonable presentation and an opportunity to reject. (Id. at 125–126.)

## Limits and assigned account
Affirmance left the parties free to make appropriate showings in another jurisdiction's plenary contract proceeding on issues apart from the forum clause's validity and enforceability. It did not establish that every electronic assent system, hyperlink, or forum provision gives adequate notice. The assigned account at printed pages 671–672 accurately emphasizes scrolling opportunity, ordinary presentation, and affirmed dismissal, while the actual opinion supplies the detailed enrollment process and the remaining-issues reservation. (Id. at 126.)
