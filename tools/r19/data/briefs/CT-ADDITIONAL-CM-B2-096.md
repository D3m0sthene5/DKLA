# In re Estate of Horrigan
757 So. 2d 165 (Miss. 1999) | Contracts

## Court, voting positions, and judgment
The Mississippi Supreme Court decided this case on December 16, 1999; the report notes denial of rehearing on March 16, 2000. Justice McRae wrote for the court. Chief Justice Prather, Presiding Justice Sullivan, and Justices Banks, Smith, and Mills concurred. Presiding Justice Pittman and Justice Waller concurred only in the result. Justice Cobb concurred in part and dissented in part without a separate opinion. The court affirmed the grandchildren's right to repayment of their renovation investment with interest through a constructive trust, reversed an award of ownership interests in the property, and remanded. It also sustained a prospective rent obligation for the house, with November 1998 as its starting point. (165–167, 172–173, ¶¶ 1–2, 34–36.)

## Family promise and reliance
Richard Horrigan promised grandchildren Christine Ladner, called Christy, and Theodore Bilbo, called Ted, that if they invested their funds in renovating his house and garage apartment, the property would pass to them after he and his wife Eunice, called Sue, died. The grandchildren alleged that this agreement was made in 1989. They invested $22,178, representing a majority of their savings. The grandfather had served as their Louisiana tutor, a role the opinion's second note equates with guardianship in Mississippi, and was responsible for funds awarded after their mother's death. The grandparents urged them to use money of which they previously had been unaware for the improvements. (166–168, 171, ¶¶ 2, 9, 27 & nn. 1–2.)

Richard died testate on March 5, 1996. The will did not implement the oral arrangement the grandchildren asserted. The opinion describes a disposition to Sue and further allocations involving the grandchildren and their children; its details should not be reduced to an assertion that every estate asset went to the widow outright. The essential conflict was between the claimed promise to preserve the renovated property for Christy and Ted after Sue's death and the actual testamentary disposition. Sue denied the asserted arrangement, while the fact finder credited contrary testimony. (167, 169, ¶¶ 3–4, 20.)

## Probate litigation and the trial remedy
Daughter Eunice LeBlanc sought probate and letters testamentary in September 1996. Christy filed a caveat alleging incapacity or undue influence. After initial admission of the will and appointment of LeBlanc, the grandchildren sought to vacate that order because the caveat had not been considered. The court then set aside the probate order and appointed the chancery clerk as administrator. The grandchildren later withdrew their caveat, and the original will was reinstated before trial. Consequently, the appellate holding does not establish either incapacity or undue influence. (167–168, ¶¶ 4–10.)

In May 1997 the grandchildren petitioned for specific performance and related relief. Sue, LeBlanc, and Roy Horrigan denied any agreement and sought rent for occupancy since Richard's death. Discovery also disclosed a conveyance of one of the disputed property's two lots to LeBlanc, prompting a petition to set it aside. An amended petition sought a constructive trust, quantum meruit, or restitution for money and goods supplied. (167–168, ¶¶ 9–10.)

After the October 1998 trial, Chancellor Thomas Teel granted specific performance, set aside the conveyance, and ordered the grandchildren to pay $600 monthly rent for the house beginning with judgment, but not for the apartment. The report further describes an order directing conveyance of parcels A and B to the grandchildren subject to Sue's life estate. The family appealed, and the grandchildren cross-appealed. The supreme court deferred to supported factual findings but corrected erroneous legal standards. (168, ¶¶ 11–13.)

## Oral agreement proved, holographic will unproved
The chancellor believed the oral agreement existed and also relied on the supposed execution of a holographic will to conclude that the promise had become irrevocable. The supreme court separated those propositions. It found no substantial proof of an actual holographic will: the purported witness could not recall its words, and there was no sufficient evidence about its contents or execution. Yet the absence of that writing did not eliminate the supported factual finding that the grandparents had promised the property and induced the grandchildren's investment. (168–170, ¶¶ 14–21.)

The court's discussion says detrimental reliance and complete performance justified equitable estoppel, but it expressly preserves a remedial limitation. Under Mississippi precedent, proof of an unwritten agreement to devise real property did not permit specific performance. Agreements to exchange property for rendered services without an executed will could support quantum meruit rather than compelled conveyance. Thus the statement that reliance took the agreement outside the statute's scope cannot be isolated into a holding that the land had to be transferred. The court enforced a monetary protection against unjust enrichment while refusing the promised ownership remedy. (169–170, ¶¶ 19–21.)

## Constructive trust and its limited measure
Constructive trusts require clear and convincing proof and more than the bare existence of a family or confidential relationship. The court discussed the need for abuse of confidence or substantial overreaching. Its discussion records countervailing circumstances: the tutor's legal duties ended when the grandchildren attained Louisiana majority, and their father co-signed expenditure checks. It nevertheless ultimately relied on the grandfather's tutor role, the grandparents' solicitation of the savings, the promise-induced investment, and the unfair enrichment that would result from refusing any benefit of the arrangement. The opinion's paragraphs 24 and 27–28 should be read together rather than turning either the family relationship alone or approval by the father into the sole dispositive rule. (170–171, ¶¶ 22–28.)

The trust protected $22,178 plus interest from the date of the grandchildren's last payment. Their contribution was admitted by the family's attorney at oral argument, as note 1 records. Repayment would dissolve the trust. This relief restored their investment; it did not confer the house or apartment's promised ownership interests. The court reversed that aspect of the specific-performance judgment. (166–167, 171–173, ¶¶ 1–2, 28, 34–36 & n. 1.)

## Rent and the cross-appeal
The grandchildren argued that Sue could not begin charging rent because the grandparents had invited their residence and historically required none. Conversely, the family sought rent for both house and apartment beginning at Richard's death. The evidence showed that demands arose after the will contest and associated litigation expenses. The court upheld the finding that the grandchildren were to have the apartment without rent, but rejected their claimed right to occupy the house indefinitely rent-free. They had to vacate the house or pay Sue, as life tenant, $600 monthly; there was no showing that this amount was unfair. The starting point remained November 1998, not the date of death. (171–172, ¶¶ 29–34.)

## Assigned comparison and reading scope
The assigned notes occupy C&M physical pages 130–131, printed 436–437. They correctly emphasize the contrast between repayment and specific performance and connect occupancy with rent. However, their description of a probate repayment lien simply affirmed on review compresses the actual sequence: the chancellor granted specific performance, and the supreme court affirmed in part, reversed ownership relief, and remanded. The actual trust is measured by the precisely established contribution and interest, not the entire value of the promised land.
