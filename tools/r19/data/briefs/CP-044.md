Parties
Atlantic Marine was a Virginia corporation with its principal place of business in Virginia. It was the general
contractor on a federal construction project and the defendant in J-Crew’s contract action. J-Crew Management
was a Texas corporation that performed subcontract work and brought the underlying payment suit. Atlantic
Marine petitioned for mandamus against the district court after that court refused dismissal or transfer; this
procedural route explains why the district court appears in the Supreme Court’s caption. J-Crew was the party
seeking to keep its lawsuit in Texas. 571 U.S. at 52–54.
Material Facts
The United States Army Corps of Engineers hired Atlantic Marine to construct a child-development center at
Fort Hood, within the Western District of Texas. Atlantic Marine subcontracted with J-Crew. Their agreement
required disputes to be litigated in the Circuit Court for the City of Norfolk, Virginia, or the United States
District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia, Norfolk Division. The clause therefore supplied both a state
and a federal option in Virginia. Its validity was undisputed in the litigation before the Supreme Court. 571
U.S. at 52–53, 62 n.5.
A payment dispute nevertheless led J-Crew to sue in the Western District of Texas under diversity jurisdiction.
Texas was a permissible venue under the federal venue statute, but it was outside the contractually selected
locations. The controversy accordingly required distinguishing a court’s statutory eligibility to hear a case from
the effect of the parties’ agreement to litigate elsewhere. The Supreme Court did not decide whether Atlantic
Marine owed the disputed payment. 571 U.S. at 53, 55–56.
J-Crew opposed moving the case because important witnesses could not be compelled to attend in Virginia and
willing witnesses would incur substantial expense. Those were concrete litigation burdens, but they were
burdens associated with the distant forum to which J-Crew had already agreed. Their relevance to transfer,
rather than their factual existence, became a central issue. 571 U.S. at 53–54, 67–68.
Procedural History
Atlantic Marine first sought dismissal under 28 U.S.C. § 1406(a) and Federal Rule 12(b)(3), arguing that the
contractual clause made venue in Texas wrong or improper. Alternatively, it requested transfer to the Eastern
District of Virginia under § 1404(a). The district court denied both requests. It correctly treated § 1404(a) as the
mechanism for a clause selecting another federal court, but assigned Atlantic Marine the burden of showing
that transfer was appropriate and weighed the clause alongside ordinary public and private convenience factors.
Witness availability and expense helped persuade it to keep the case. 571 U.S. at 53–54.

Atlantic Marine sought a writ of mandamus from the Fifth Circuit directing dismissal or transfer. The court of
appeals denied relief because Atlantic Marine had not shown a clear and indisputable entitlement to the writ. It
agreed that § 1404(a) governed this federal-forum clause and found no clear abuse of discretion in the district
court’s balancing. It also stated that a clause selecting a nonfederal court could be enforced through Rule
12(b)(3), a proposition the Supreme Court rejected. 571 U.S. at 54, 59–61.
The Supreme Court granted certiorari, resolved the appropriate enforcement mechanisms and transfer analysis,
reversed the Fifth Circuit’s judgment, and remanded. It left the lower courts to decide whether public-interest
considerations could justify refusing transfer under the corrected standard. It did not itself enter judgment on
the subcontract or order the Texas action dismissed. 571 U.S. at 54, 68.
Issue
When a plaintiff files in a statutorily proper federal district despite a valid clause selecting another forum, may
the defendant obtain dismissal for improper venue? When the selected forum is another federal district, how
does the clause change the burden, the convenience analysis, and the choice-of-law consequences of a §
1404(a) transfer? 571 U.S. at 55–66.
Rule and Holding
A forum-selection clause does not make otherwise proper federal venue wrong or improper for purposes of §
1406(a) or Rule 12(b)(3). Federal venue legislation determines that question. A valid clause selecting another
federal district is enforced through § 1404(a); a clause selecting a state or foreign tribunal is ordinarily enforced
through forum non conveniens. The latter entails dismissal rather than a transfer outside the federal court
system. 571 U.S. at 55–61.
A valid clause changes ordinary § 1404(a) analysis in three respects. The plaintiff’s later choice of an
unselected forum receives no weight, and that plaintiff bears the burden of showing why the agreed forum
should not be used. Private-interest factors are treated as favoring the agreed forum because the parties waived
objections to its inconvenience. Finally, a transfer enforcing the clause does not carry the transferor court’s
choice-of-law rules with it. Public-interest considerations remain available, but ordinarily cannot overcome the
agreement. 571 U.S. at 62–66.
These rules required reversal because the district court assigned the wrong burden, relied on J-Crew’s private
convenience, and assumed that Texas choice-of-law rules necessarily would follow the case to Virginia. The
analysis presupposed a contractually valid clause; the Court did not announce that every purported forum
clause is enforceable regardless of formation, scope, or validity. 571 U.S. at 62 n.5, 67–68.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Fifth Circuit and remanded for the lower courts to consider public-interest
factors under the correct framework. It saw no apparent public-interest reason in the existing record to deny
Atlantic Marine’s motion, but left that question for the proceedings below. The judgment therefore corrected
the standards governing transfer without adjudicating the payment claim. 571 U.S. at 68.

Reasoning
The Court began with the statutory meaning of venue. Section 1391 generally identifies districts where a
federal civil action may be brought. If the selected district satisfies the applicable statutory provisions, a private
agreement cannot transform it into an improper venue under § 1406(a) or Rule 12(b)(3). Contractual selection
and statutory venue have different sources and operations. Treating them as identical would allow an
agreement selecting a foreign court to erase venue in every federal district even though Congress’s venue
scheme supplies a federal location when its requirements are satisfied. 571 U.S. at 55–58.
Section 1404(a) solves the enforcement problem without distorting statutory venue. It permits transfer from a
proper district to another eligible federal district, including a district to which all parties consent. A clause can
therefore warrant moving a case even though the original filing satisfied venue legislation. When the chosen
tribunal is nonfederal, forum non conveniens supplies the corresponding remedy because § 1404(a) authorizes
transfers within the federal system. The same contractual considerations inform the analysis under both
mechanisms. 571 U.S. at 59–61.
Ordinary deference to a plaintiff’s forum choice rests on the plaintiff’s ability to select a permissible location.
J-Crew had exercised that choice in advance by promising to litigate in Virginia. Giving independent weight to
its later Texas filing would reward disregard of the agreement and deprive the agreed selection of its intended
effect. Consequently, J-Crew had to justify departure from the selected forum, rather than requiring Atlantic
Marine to prove that Virginia was independently more convenient. 571 U.S. at 63–64.
The same contractual reasoning excluded the witness-expense objections on which the district court relied. A
party agreeing to a distant forum accepts the foreseeable private burdens of litigating there, including
difficulties involving witnesses. Allowing those same burdens routinely to defeat enforcement would reopen
the bargain after a dispute arose. The court must therefore treat private interests as favoring the selected forum
while still considering genuinely public concerns, such as court congestion and local interests. This distinction
explains why the rule strongly favors enforcement without making it literally automatic. 571 U.S. at 64 & n.6,
67–68.
The choice-of-law adjustment prevents a second form of advantage from violating the clause. Under the usual
Van Dusen and Ferens transfer rules, a § 1404(a) transfer generally preserves the state-law framework
associated with the original forum. That protects a legitimate initial forum choice from being stripped of its
legal benefits by a convenience transfer. The rationale does not protect a plaintiff who filed contrary to its
contractual commitment. Otherwise, J-Crew could obtain Texas choice-of-law rules merely by filing there first,
even when the case eventually reached the agreed Virginia court. 571 U.S. at 64–66.
The transferee court accordingly would use Virginia choice-of-law rules. That does not necessarily mean
Virginia substantive contract law would govern: choice-of-law rules determine which substantive law applies.
The district court’s assumption that Texas conflicts rules necessarily followed the case was erroneous, and
ordinary unfamiliarity with another state’s contract law did not provide a persuasive reason to disregard the
agreement. Federal judges commonly apply the law of other states. 571 U.S. at 67–68.
Finally, enforcement protects the economic and practical expectations underlying the contract. Forum selection
may influence price, allocation of risk, and a party’s willingness to enter the transaction. The interest of justice
ordinarily favors preserving that negotiated expectation. Extraordinary public concerns can still matter, but the
lower courts must identify such concerns under the properly adjusted analysis rather than repeat the ordinary
private-convenience balancing. 571 U.S. at 66–68.

Separate Opinions
Justice Alito wrote for a unanimous Court. No separate opinion was filed. The absence of disagreement on this
disposition should not obscure the questions the opinion expressly reserved, including the contractual-validity
assumption and an alternative Rule 12(b)(6) theory. 571 U.S. at 52, 61 n.4, 62 n.5.
Notes
The case supplies a sequence for analysis: determine statutory venue; determine the meaning and validity of
the clause; select § 1404(a) or forum non conveniens according to the chosen tribunal; then apply the adjusted
analysis for a valid clause. An agreement does not by itself eliminate subject-matter-jurisdiction requirements.
The decision addressed enforcement of the undisputed Virginia clause, not a general power to transfer a case to
any court named in any contract. 571 U.S. at 55–62.
The Court did not decide whether a defendant could use Rule 12(b)(6) to enforce a clause. That argument came
from an amicus and was not pressed by Atlantic Marine. It is therefore inaccurate to convert the opinion into a
holding that § 1404(a) is the only conceivable procedural device in every circumstance. Its actual holding
rejects improper-venue treatment and establishes the transfer and forum-non-conveniens routes described
above. 571 U.S. at 61 n.4.
