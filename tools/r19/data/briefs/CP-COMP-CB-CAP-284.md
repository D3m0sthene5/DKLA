# Whittlestone, Inc. v. Handi-Craft Company

**Civil Procedure — 618 F.3d 970 (9th Cir. 2010). Decided August 17, 2010.** N. R. Smith wrote for a panel comprising Reinhardt, Fletcher, and Smith; no separate writing is reported. Assigned: CivPro physical page 352, printed page 669, Note 3. Judicial pinpoints below use the preserved court-issued slip pagination.

Whittlestone concerns a defendant's attempt to eliminate damages at the pleading stage through a motion to strike. The Ninth Circuit held that Rule 12(f) does not authorize striking a damages claim merely because the defendant says those damages are legally unavailable. The panel also concluded that this plaintiff's interpretation of its contract was sufficiently plausible to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) analysis. It reversed and remanded without resolving the parties' ultimate contractual rights. (Slip 12066–12070.)

In March 2006, California-based Whittlestone and Missouri-based Handi-Craft entered a written twenty-year agreement. Handi-Craft undertook minimum annual unit or dollar purchases of Whittlestone products for resale. The agreement's term would last twenty years unless ended pursuant to its provisions. Paragraph 20 authorized termination by mutual written consent, by one party upon the other's insolvency or material breach after notice and an opportunity to cure, and following a change in the other party's ownership under specified timing and notice conditions. (12062–12064.)

Paragraph 20(d) limited compensation arising from termination or nonrenewal, including anticipated sales, prospective profits, and expenditures connected with either party's business. That provision mattered because Handi-Craft unilaterally withdrew in June 2008, just two years into the relationship. Whittlestone sued for breach, seeking more than $250,000 in damages encompassing lost contract value, profits, consequential damages, and credits or discounts, as well as restitution. The amount was demanded relief, not an award. (12063–12064.)

Handi-Craft moved under Rule 12(f) to strike the damages allegations on October 9, 2008. It argued that paragraph 20(d) barred them. The district court granted the motion on November 18. Without choosing between Missouri and California law, it concluded that the limitation was enforceable under either and that the contract clearly excluded the requested damages. Reconsideration was denied, and Whittlestone appealed. (12064–12065.)

The ordinary decision to strike matter under Rule 12(f) is reviewed for abuse of discretion. This appeal, however, first asked whether the rule authorized the action at all. That interpretation of the Federal Rules was a legal question reviewed de novo. The assigned account calls the ruling an abuse of discretion; the complete opinion expressly distinguishes ordinary discretionary review from the de novo question of the rule's authority. (12065; assigned Note 3.)

The panel began with Rule 12(f)'s five categories: an insufficient defense, or redundant, immaterial, impertinent, or scandalous matter. Whittlestone's damages claim fit none. It was not a defense, did not duplicate another claim in the complaint, and directly concerned the relief sought and the harm alleged. That connection defeated the labels immaterial and impertinent. No one claimed it was scandalous. A contention that damages fail as a matter of substantive law does not itself bring a pleading within these categories. (12066–12067.)

Handi-Craft was effectively seeking dismissal of a portion of the complaint or summary judgment against it. Rules 12(b)(6) and 56 perform those functions. Permitting Rule 12(f) to do the same work would duplicate the other mechanisms and produce inconsistent appellate review: an identical substantive dismissal could receive abuse-of-discretion review when labeled a motion to strike but de novo review when brought under Rule 12(b)(6). The panel rejected that consequence. Footnote 1 adds the related protection in Rule 12(d): consideration of nonexcluded material outside the pleadings on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion requires conversion to summary judgment and a reasonable opportunity for the parties to submit pertinent material. (12067–12068 & n.1.)

Footnote 2 supplies an additional, case-specific reason the damages should remain. Under either state's contract law, the agreement must be read as a whole. The twenty-year term and enumerated termination methods supported Whittlestone's position that paragraph 20(d) applied to a termination authorized by the agreement, rather than every unilateral abandonment. Withdrawal alone was not one of the four listed methods. On the pleaded account, Handi-Craft was the breaching party; the breach provision allowed the nonbreaching party to terminate following notice and failure to cure. (12068–12069 n.2.)

The district court had relied on paragraph 20(f), which described the parties' remaining shipment and order obligations following termination resulting from a material breach. But that language was consistent with the nonbreaching party's right to end the contract after the other's breach. It did not necessarily permit a breaching party to end the agreement itself and thereby activate the damages exclusion. Whittlestone's reading was at least as plausible as Handi-Craft's when the pleadings were construed in Whittlestone's favor. Intent and properly admitted extrinsic evidence could support either side at a later stage; neither had been conclusively decided on the pleadings. (12069–12070 n.2.)

The precise disposition was **reversed and remanded**, with instructions not to dismiss the lost-profits and consequential-damages claim under Rule 12(f). The case does not establish that those damages must ultimately be recovered, that the exclusion is invalid, or that Rule 12(b)(6) can never decide a contractual damages restriction. It establishes the proper procedural mechanism and preserves this plausible contractual claim for further proceedings. (12070.)
