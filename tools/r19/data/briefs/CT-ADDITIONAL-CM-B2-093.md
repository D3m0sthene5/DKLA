# Gladding v. Langrall, Muir & Noppinger
285 Md. 210, 401 A.2d 662 (1979) | Contracts

## Court and procedural posture
The Maryland Court of Appeals decided this case on May 28, 1979. Judge Digges delivered the court's opinion; the report lists Chief Judge Murphy and Judges Smith, Digges, Eldridge, Orth, Cole, and Davidson on the panel and contains no separate writing. It affirmed the Court of Special Appeals' judgment enforcing the accountants' contingent fee and placed costs on the petitioners. The case concerns that final merits decision, rather than the separately reported 1978 procedural decision identified in note 1. (210–211 & n. 1, 216.)

The accounting partnership sued Harry and Virginia Gladding in the Superior Court of Baltimore City to collect compensation under a contingent-fee agreement. They defended on both nonperformance and unconscionability, and counterclaimed for return of the retainer. After a nonjury trial, Judge Basil A. Thomas resolved all issues for the partnership and entered judgment for $30,639. The Court of Special Appeals affirmed in an unreported decision, and the Court of Appeals granted certiorari. In the latter court the taxpayers abandoned the contention that the accountants had breached their performance obligation. The remaining question was whether the disparity between the accountants' modest work and substantial compensation made the bargain unconscionable. (211, 213.)

## Tax exposure and the coordinated professional engagement
An IRS audit report dated June 16, 1969 assessed the Gladdings and corporations they owned or controlled approximately $1.3 million in additional taxes and penalties for 1961 through 1966. On the advice of his personal attorney, John Demyan, Harry contacted Herbert Langrall, senior partner of the accounting firm. Langrall recognized that resisting the assessment would require substantial legal expertise and proposed bringing in Jacques Schlenger of Venable, Baetjer and Howard. At an early January 1970 meeting attended by Gladding, Demyan, Schlenger, and Langrall, the participants orally agreed to employ the two professional firms and defined their respective obligations. They executed separate written agreements memorializing that understanding several weeks later. (211–212.)

The accountants' written agreement provided for a $10,000 retainer and described an additional contingent payment of four percent of the first $700,000 in tax savings and three percent of savings above that amount, payable immediately upon settlement however achieved. It promised best efforts and expressly denied any guarantee of results. The law firm's companion agreement provided a $20,000 retainer and sixteen percent of the first $700,000 saved plus twelve percent of additional savings. It placed control of accounting work with the law firm and acknowledged the accountants' fee agreement. The arrangement therefore assigned coordinated roles while using the ultimate tax savings as the compensation measure. (212 & n. 2.)

The IRS's internal review procedures produced a settlement saving the taxpayers $1,121,000, largely through the law firm's work. The accountants' time records showed only seventeen professional hours and a minimal contribution to that favorable settlement. The taxpayers refused the claimed balance. The opinion describes $30,639 as allegedly due after crediting the $10,000 retainer, and that is the amount of the affirmed trial judgment. Although the quoted fee clause describes a contingent payment in addition to the retainer, the report does not reconcile that language with its subsequent credit description. The reported award should be preserved rather than replaced with a newly calculated fee. (212–213.)

## Formation-time fairness and the allocation of risk
The court held that unconscionability must be evaluated when the bargain is made, rather than retrospectively from the work eventually required or the outcome eventually achieved. Subsequent circumstances can make a bargain considerably more favorable to one party without making its original terms unconscionable. The court invoked Maryland's established rescission and cancellation decisions and referred by analogy to the commercial statute's formation-time treatment of unconscionable goods contracts. It did not reclassify this professional engagement as a sale of goods. (213–214.)

The taxpayers made no assertion that the agreement was unfair when negotiated. Their objective at that point was relief from an extraordinary tax assessment, rather than purchasing a specified number of professional hours. Selecting contingent compensation reflected a willingness to pay for a favorable outcome. Both parties took a chance on the degree of success; the later realization that little accounting work had been needed did not convert the bargain into an hourly engagement. The fact that the law firm did most of the work was undisputed, but it did not by itself defeat the accountants' contractual right once the breach contention had been abandoned. (213–214.)

The court also considered the structure and circumstances of negotiation. The two firms' combined contingent percentages would not exceed twenty percent of the savings, and the taxpayers had their own attorney's advice throughout negotiation. These circumstances reinforced the conclusion that the agreement was unobjectionable at formation. They were reasons supporting this result, not a categorical safe harbor for every fee of twenty percent or less. Enforcing the parties' original valuation of the prospective benefit avoided substituting a new bargain after the outcome was known. (214–216.)

## Professional-fee argument and questions reserved
The taxpayers argued that the special scrutiny applied to attorney-client fee agreements should extend to accountants. Note 5 rejected the proposed equivalence because the broader judicial interest in lawyers' fees arose from their status as officers of the court. The court separately addressed the asserted confidential relationship: even assuming comparable confidentiality could attach to accountant-client relations, the relationship here did not exist until after the employment contract was formed. That reasoning concerns this initial agreement and does not announce that accountants can never owe duties of confidence to established clients. (215 n. 5.)

The court also reserved two procedural questions. It assumed without deciding that the requested rescission or cancellation could be asserted in an action at law, and that the taxpayers could raise it through their general-issue pleas rather than a special equitable plea under Maryland Rule 342 d 1(a). Thus the merits rejection does not establish either procedural proposition. Note 3 supplies a traditional definition of an unconscionable bargain, while the actual reasoning applies the formation-time rule to this negotiated contingent engagement. (213 nn. 3–4.)

## Judgment and assigned-source comparison
The Court of Appeals affirmed the Court of Special Appeals and ordered the petitioners to pay costs. The $30,639 accountants' judgment therefore stood. The decision neither found renewed nonperformance nor reduced the fee to the reasonable value of seventeen hours of labor. (211, 213, 216.)
