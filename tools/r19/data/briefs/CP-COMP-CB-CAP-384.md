# National Hockey League v. Metropolitan Hockey Club, Inc.

**427 U.S. 639 (1976).** The Supreme Court issued a per curiam decision on June 30, 1976. Justices Brennan and White dissented without written explanations; Justice Stevens did not participate. The judgment was therefore 6–2 among the eight participants. The assigned account is CivPro physical pages 498–499, printed page 974.

## Facts and procedural path

The respondents brought an antitrust action against the National Hockey League and other petitioners. The Supreme Court’s short opinion focuses on their failure to answer court-ordered written interrogatories rather than on the substantive antitrust allegations. Crucial interrogatories remained substantially unanswered for seventeen months. During that period, the district court granted numerous extensions, including late extensions, issued admonitions, and received promises of compliance. When a definite June 14, 1974 deadline passed, the plaintiffs failed to perform and waited five additional days before moving for relief. They had been warned that noncompliance could lead to Rule 37 sanctions. (639–641.)

The district court dismissed under Rule 37(b)(2), finding flagrant bad faith by the plaintiffs and callous disregard of professional responsibilities by their counsel. It supported dismissal with a comprehensive memorandum reviewing the discovery record. The sanction terminated the action because of discovery misconduct; it was not a determination that the antitrust allegations lacked substantive merit. (640–642.)

A divided Third Circuit reversed for abuse of discretion. It did not dispute the district court’s historical factual findings, but gave weight to extenuating circumstances. Discovery had not been pressed until a consent decree resolved the claims of other original plaintiffs roughly a year into the litigation. Respondents’ current attorneys took over after that decree and had difficulty obtaining some information. Lead counsel had also assured the judge that he would not knowingly or willfully disregard the final deadline. The circuit considered those circumstances insufficient to support the district court’s characterization of the deadline failure as flagrant bad faith, willful, or intentional. (641.)

## Issue and governing limits

Was the appellate court correct to conclude that dismissal exceeded the district court’s discretion? The Supreme Court held that it was not. The question was not whether the appellate judges—or the Supreme Court itself—would have chosen dismissal in the first instance. It was whether the trial judge had abused the discretion conferred by Rule 37 on the record of persistent noncompliance. (642–643.)

The opinion began with two limits rather than an unqualified approval of dismissal. Rule 37 authorized only such discovery sanctions as were just, with dismissal among the listed options. Societe Internationale had also established that failure attributable to inability, rather than willfulness, bad faith, or fault, did not support dismissal. Nobody in this litigation suggested that amendments to Rule 37 had displaced that principle. National Hockey League therefore did not authorize dismissal merely because a party could not comply, or make dismissal the required response to every missed deadline. (639–640.)

## Why dismissal fell within discretion here

The full record supported the district court’s assessment. It had been exceptionally patient and given ample opportunity to answer. The respondents not only missed deadlines; the answers ultimately submitted were grossly inadequate. The Supreme Court rejected the suggestion that the district court had failed to consider the full record: its detailed memorandum demonstrated the opposite. Mitigating circumstances and lenity mattered, but they could not entirely displace the other purposes of Rule 37. (641–642.)

The Court then explained why the prospect of future compliance did not necessarily justify reversing the sanction. A reviewing court may naturally recoil from dismissal and reason that the experience of being dismissed has sufficiently chastened this particular litigant. Even if successful on appeal, that litigant might thereafter comply promptly. But sanctions also serve general deterrence. Other litigants need reason to obey discovery orders before a court reaches its limit of patience. If severe sanctions were regularly reversed once they had produced contrition, others could feel freer to disregard orders until threatened with termination. (642–643.)

The availability of dismissal in an appropriate case therefore served both punishment of the present misconduct and deterrence of similar conduct elsewhere. The Court connected that purpose to the supported findings of bad faith and counsel’s disregard here; it did not replace the requirement of an appropriate factual basis with deterrence alone. Nor did it require every discovery sanction to be dismissal. The holding sustained this district judge’s choice under these circumstances. (643.)

## Exact judgment and separate positions

The Supreme Court **granted certiorari and reversed the Third Circuit’s judgment**, leaving the district court’s dismissal sustained against the appellate abuse-of-discretion objection. It found no abuse in either the bad-faith assessment or the choice of dismissal as the sanction. Brennan and White dissented, but the report supplies no reasons that could responsibly be reconstructed for them. Stevens took no part in consideration or decision. There were no judicial footnotes, written separate opinions, or appendices in the complete decision. (643.)
