# Lightfoot v. Cendant Mortgage Corp.

**580 U.S. 82, 137 S. Ct. 553 (2017) — Supreme Court of the United States, January 18, 2017.** Justice Sotomayor wrote for a unanimous Court; no separate opinion appears. Assigned account: *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 188, printed page 330, Note 1 following *Osborn*. The assigned caption spells the respondent “CEDANT”; the actual report identifies **Cendant**.

## Mortgage dispute and removal

Beverly Ann Hollis-Arrington refinanced her mortgage with Cendant Mortgage in summer 1999. Fannie Mae bought the mortgage while Cendant continued servicing it. When Hollis-Arrington could not make payments, she sought a forbearance arrangement, but no agreement resulted and foreclosure followed. Cendant meanwhile repurchased the mortgage because it did not meet Fannie Mae’s credit standards. Hollis-Arrington and her daughter, Crystal Lightfoot, pursued bankruptcy and transferred the property between themselves to prevent foreclosure. Those efforts failed, and the home sold at a trustee’s sale in 2001. **86–87.**

After two unsuccessful federal suits, they sued in state court alleging defects in refinancing, foreclosure, and sale. Fannie Mae removed the case under § 1441(a), which permits removal of civil actions within federal district courts’ original jurisdiction. Its asserted jurisdictional basis was its federal charter’s authorization “to sue and to be sued, and to complain and to defend, in any court of competent jurisdiction, State or Federal.” The district court denied remand, dismissed the Fannie Mae claims on claim-preclusion grounds, and eventually entered final judgment. It also denied the plaintiffs’ Rule 60(b) motion alleging fraud on the court. **87.**

The Ninth Circuit initially affirmed, then withdrew that opinion on rehearing and requested briefing about jurisdiction. A divided panel again affirmed. Its majority read *American National Red Cross v. S. G.* as establishing that an express reference to federal courts in a federal charter’s sue-and-be-sued clause necessarily conferred federal jurisdiction. The dissent treated that reference only as a starting point, overcome here by the additional words “of competent jurisdiction.” With other circuits divided over similar wording, the Supreme Court granted review. **87–88.**

## Issue, holding, and judgment

Does Fannie Mae’s sue-and-be-sued clause itself grant federal courts subject-matter jurisdiction over every case involving Fannie Mae? **No.** The clause gives Fannie Mae the capacity to litigate in state or federal courts that already possess jurisdiction from another source. The Court **reversed the Ninth Circuit’s judgment**. Its ruling removed the asserted charter basis for jurisdiction; it did not decide whether the foreclosure was unlawful or whether the plaintiffs deserved substantive relief. **84, 93, 99.**

## The charter’s history and the meaning of its qualifier

The opinion distinguishes corporate capacity—the ability to sue and be sued—from a court’s authority to decide the particular controversy. Fannie Mae began as a federally owned institution supporting the mortgage market. Congress rechartered it in 1954 with mixed public and private ownership and replaced its original litigation clause with the present reference to courts “of competent jurisdiction.” In 1968, it became privately owned while Ginnie Mae retained governmental functions. These developments explain the statutory setting, but the operative question remained the meaning of the enacted words. **84–86, 88–89.**

Prior charter cases gave the parties competing analogies. *Deveaux* and *Bankers Trust* construed general capacities to sue without sufficiently specifying federal courts as nongranting provisions. *Osborn*, *D’Oench, Duhme*, and *Red Cross* found jurisdictional grants in clauses expressly authorizing litigation in federal courts. Fannie Mae’s clause cleared the federal-reference hurdle, but it differed from those granting clauses by qualifying the available courts as competent. **89–91.**

A competent court is one empowered to adjudicate the case. In this context, competence therefore presupposes subject-matter jurisdiction covering the controversy. Other decisions had similarly understood “court of competent jurisdiction” to look outside the provision for the necessary authority. Applied here, the qualifier means Fannie Mae may use either court system when an independent jurisdictional grant supports the suit. Reading the reference to federal courts as a self-sufficient grant would bypass the very limitation Congress included. **91–93.**

## Why Red Cross and the other arguments did not change the result

The Ninth Circuit mistook *Red Cross*’s requirement for an invariably sufficient condition. *Red Cross* said a clause may confer federal jurisdiction only if it specifically mentions federal courts; it did not require courts to ignore additional qualifying language whenever that condition appeared. Its holding depended on language materially identical to a clause already found jurisdiction-conferring and on analysis of text, purpose, and history. Although the *Red Cross* dissent described the majority’s rule broadly, that characterization did not enlarge the actual holding. **93–94.**

Fannie Mae proposed that “competent jurisdiction” might instead concern personal jurisdiction, venue, or general rather than specialized courts. These possibilities assumed the rejected premise that mentioning federal courts had already supplied subject-matter jurisdiction. Even if competence also included personal jurisdiction, that would not eliminate its subject-matter component. Nothing limited the phrase to personal jurisdiction alone. **94–95.**

Fannie Mae also invoked the prior-construction canon: Congress ordinarily carries a settled interpretation into a new enactment using the same language. But the cases it cited did not establish the necessary settled meaning by 1954. Decisions about FHA monetary claims addressed the Court of Claims restriction without identifying the sue-and-be-sued clause as the source of district-court jurisdiction. A brief statement about a Fair Labor Standards Act suit was ambiguous. Housing-and-rent decisions were divided, and Congress’s later elimination of an amount-in-controversy barrier suggested that an outside jurisdictional source mattered. Decisions requiring an exclusively federal forum did not resolve the source of jurisdiction either. **95–97.**

Finally, Fannie Mae argued that Congress had not intended to reduce its original federal access or disadvantage it beside Freddie Mac. The clear qualifier did not require an additional indication that Congress understood it as a change. Indeed, the legislative discussion quoted in the Court’s starred footnote suggested that the original clause itself merely granted capacity. Freddie Mac’s charter contained distinct jurisdictional provisions, and its governmental ownership when those provisions were enacted supplied a plausible reason for different treatment. Fannie Mae could still invoke ordinary diversity or federal-question jurisdiction where available. No demonstrated competitive concern justified disregarding the text. **97–99 & 98 n.*.**
