Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley
Citation
Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 29 S. Ct. 42, 53 L. Ed. 126 (1908). Supreme
Court of the United States, argued October 13, 1908, decided November 16, 1908. Justice Moody delivered
the Court’s opinion. No separate opinion is printed in the report. Casebook pages 331-333. Full judicial
opinion.
Parties
The Mottleys, husband and wife, were Kentucky citizens who sued to enforce a railroad’s promise of
transportation. They were appellees defending the trial court’s decree. Louisville & Nashville Railroad
Company, also a Kentucky citizen, was the defendant and appellant. Because both sides had the same state
citizenship, diversity of citizenship did not supply federal jurisdiction. 211 U.S. at 150, 152.
Material Facts
The Mottleys alleged that they were injured through the railroad’s negligence while traveling as passengers
in September 1871. They released their damage claims in return for transportation during their lives.
According to the bill, the railroad honored the agreement until January 1, 1907, when it refused to renew the
passes. 211 U.S. at 150-51.
The bill attributed that refusal solely to a federal statute enacted June 29, 1906, prohibiting free passes or
free transportation. The Mottleys alleged that the statute did not prohibit passes issued under their settlement
and, alternatively, that such a prohibition would deprive them of property without due process in violation of
the Fifth Amendment. These were the plaintiffs’ arguments anticipating the railroad’s defense, not holdings
that the statute permitted the passes or was unconstitutional. Id. at 151.
The full report, in text omitted from the excerpt, reproduces the contract and identifies the requested remedy
as specific performance: a decree ordering the railroad to perform its promise rather than merely pay
damages. The agreement called for annual renewal of the passes during the lives of the Mottleys or either of
them. Those details explain the recurring renewal dispute but do not change its contractual basis. Id. at 150.
Procedural History
The Mottleys filed their bill in equity in the United States Circuit Court for the Western District of
Kentucky, the federal trial court then hearing the suit. The railroad demurred, asserting that the pleaded facts
did not justify relief without proceeding to a factual trial. The circuit court overruled that objection and
entered a decree granting the requested relief. The railroad appealed directly to the Supreme Court. 211 U.S.
at 150-51.
The parties argued whether the federal statute prohibited this preexisting settlement and whether such an
application violated the Fifth Amendment. Neither challenged the circuit court’s jurisdiction. The Supreme
Court raised that threshold issue itself and found it dispositive without resolving either merits question. Id. at
151-52.

Issue
Did a federal trial court have statutory federal-question jurisdiction over a suit to enforce a transportation
contract when the complaint anticipated a federal statutory defense and pleaded a constitutional answer to it,
but the plaintiffs’ own claim arose from the contract and the parties were not diverse?
Rule and Holding
The circuit court lacked jurisdiction. Under the statutory grant for suits arising under federal law, the
plaintiff’s statement of the plaintiff’s own cause of action must establish the federal basis. Alleging an
anticipated federal defense, and then alleging that the Constitution defeats that defense, does not make the
original cause of action arise under federal law. The likelihood that a federal issue will become important
during litigation is insufficient. 211 U.S. at 152-54.
The Supreme Court had a duty to examine the lower federal court’s limited statutory jurisdiction even
though neither party objected. The Court therefore could not decide the contract, statutory-interpretation, or
constitutional merits merely because the parties had presented them for decision. Id. at 151-52.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the decree and returned the case to the circuit court with instructions to dismiss
for want of jurisdiction. This was not a decision that the Mottleys’ settlement was invalid or that the
railroad’s statutory defense prevailed on its merits. 211 U.S. at 154.
Reasoning
The Court separated the plaintiffs’ claim from the legal issues expected in response to it. The claim sought
enforcement of a transportation promise exchanged for release of injury claims. Federal law entered because
the railroad was expected to invoke the later statute as a defense, and the Mottleys expected to challenge that
defense under the Constitution. That sequence did not make federal law the basis of the original claim. 211
U.S. at 150-52.
The absence of diversity made the federal-question issue decisive. With no other jurisdictional ground
suggested, the Court applied its established interpretation of the statutory arising-under language. The
inquiry concerned the authority Congress had conferred on the lower court, not simply whether a federal
legal dispute was present somewhere in the lawsuit. Id. at 152.
The Court used Tennessee v. Union & Planters’ Bank as an example. There, a state tax claim did not become
a federal cause merely because the plaintiff anticipated a federal constitutional objection to the tax. That
example supported the distinction between a plaintiff’s affirmative claim and the adversary’s expected
response. Id. at 152-53.
The Court also relied on Boston & Montana Consolidated Copper & Silver Mining Co. The quoted
reasoning in that decision confined a complaint to the claim it had to establish and left affirmative defenses
to the defendant. A plaintiff could not manufacture federal jurisdiction by pleading a hypothetical defense
and then its own reply before the defendant pleaded. The Mottleys’ inclusion of federal allegations in their
actual complaint did not avoid that substantive distinction. Id. at 153-54.
Finally, the parties’ agreement to litigate the federal merits could not relieve the Court of its responsibility to
police the trial court’s statutory authority. Dismissal followed even though the merits questions had been
briefed and argued. The Court deliberately left those questions unanswered in this decision. Id. at 151-52,

Notes
Narrow reading — interpretation. This same-state contract action did not qualify for federal-question
jurisdiction merely because the plaintiffs anticipated the railroad’s federal defense and raised a Fifth
Amendment response. Writing the federal issues into the complaint was insufficient when they were not part
of the claim the plaintiffs had to establish. 211 U.S. at 150-54.
Broad reading — interpretation. The decision, identified by the casebook as the well-pleaded-complaint
rule, allocates original federal-question jurisdiction by the plaintiff’s affirmative claim rather than the set of
federal questions likely to arise in litigation. It also illustrates independent judicial enforcement of
subject-matter limits. The casebook distinguishes this statutory inquiry from Article III’s potential
constitutional reach; the opinion does not purport to define every possible constitutional grant of
jurisdiction. Id. at 152-54; casebook pp. 331, 333-334.
Subsequent proceedings reported by the casebook. The note says that the Mottleys later sued in Kentucky
court and the dispute returned to the Supreme Court in 1911. That later decision is not the judgment briefed
here; no later merits holding is attributed to the 1908 opinion. Casebook p. 334.
