# GCIU Employer Retirement Fund v. Chicago Tribune Co.

**8 F.3d 1195 (7th Cir. 1993) · November 3, 1993 · Civil Procedure**

## Facts and procedural history

The Tribune contributed to a pressroom employees' pension fund under a pension subscription agreement and successive collective bargaining agreements. After a July 1985 strike, it hired permanent replacements. Suspecting unpaid contributions, the Fund demanded an audit four times and, after refusals, sued on October 15, 1986, to compel an audit and payment of any shortfall. The action rested on LMRA §301 and ERISA §502; the trustees' fiduciary status supplied ERISA standing. (1196–1197 & n.1.)

The Tribune argued that expiration of the collective bargaining agreement ended its obligations. The Fund relied on the subscription agreement. In 1988, the district court accepted a magistrate judge's recommendation favoring the Fund and purported to enter judgment. An audit followed and allegedly disclosed a shortfall. The Seventh Circuit dismissed the Tribune's appeal in October 1989 because the supposed judgment did not specify the Fund's relief sufficiently to create an appealable judgment. (1197.)

The parties then negotiated for 22 months without contacting the district court. A tentative February 1990 agreement failed over release language: the Fund believed an NLRB make-whole order could require additional contributions for strikers, and would not surrender that possibility. Negotiations resumed but reached an impasse in August 1991. The Tribune and Fund returned with cross-motions labeled summary judgment, which the opinion recognized were more accurately reconsideration motions in light of the earlier ruling. (1197–1198 & n.2.)

The district court accepted a briefing schedule that the parties followed. When its file proved missing in March 1992, they reconstructed it immediately. At a subsequent conference the judge indicated that no further briefing was needed and a merits ruling should follow. Instead, the court dismissed the Fund's action with prejudice for want of prosecution, citing the earlier inactivity, the absence of a requested stay, supposedly deficient briefs, and a local six-month-inactivity rule. The Fund appealed. (1198–1199.)

## Issue, rule, and holding

**Did the 22-month period of unreported settlement negotiations justify dismissal with prejudice under these circumstances?** No. Dismissal was an abuse of discretion. District courts possess inherent authority to dismiss sua sponte for failure to prosecute, but must balance docket management against adjudication on the merits. Dismissal with prejudice is an extreme sanction, appropriate in extreme situations involving a clear record of delay or contumacious conduct, or unavailable less drastic sanctions. Review considers the particular history and circumstances at dismissal. (1199–1201.)

Neither absence of a warning nor absence of prejudice to the defendant necessarily makes an involuntary dismissal invalid. The opinion therefore does not answer the textbook's warning question with an absolute requirement. Its holding rests on this record's failure to justify the drastic sanction. (1199.)

## Reasoning and limits

The Fund had not missed hearings or deadlines, frustrated discovery, sought repeated continuances, or disobeyed orders. It attended conferences, submitted the scheduled materials, and helped reconstruct the missing file. Its only apparent omission was failure to inform the court of negotiations. Keeping the court advised would have been wiser, but that omission did not demonstrate abandonment or justify dismissal with prejudice. (1199–1200.)

Settlement activity was not a universal excuse for inactivity. Courts should examine its circumstances to distinguish genuine progress from a pretext: negotiations may fail to excuse an unreasonably long delay or continued inactivity after their futility becomes apparent. Here they were complicated, undertaken in good faith, neither unreasonably prolonged nor continued after impasse, and followed by prompt resort to the court. (1200.)

The local rule permitting dismissal after six months could not support an application inconsistent with Rule 41(b) and governing precedent. Rule 83 permits local practice rules consistent with the Federal Rules. The court did not invalidate every inactivity rule or foreclose dismissal in a genuinely neglected case; it rejected the rule's application here. (1200–1201 & n.3.)

Even assuming earlier lack of diligence, current diligence defeated reliance on that earlier history. The judge had accepted the submissions and said further briefing was unnecessary. If the Fund had inadequately answered the Tribune's supersession argument, the response was a merits ruling, not a prosecution sanction. The hearing transcript also contradicted the suggestion that the parties had concealed the procedural history. A stay was unnecessary on these facts, and neither side had said settlement was imminent. (1201.)

## Disposition and textbook connection

**Reversed and remanded for further proceedings pursuant to Circuit Rule 36.** Judge Cudahy agreed with the disposition but would not invoke that rule, as judicial note 4 records. Coffey wrote for Cudahy, Coffey, and Manion; there is no separately developed dissenting opinion. The court did not award the Fund a specified pension recovery, resolve the cross-motions' merits, or finally construe the NLRB order. (1201–1202 & n.4.)
