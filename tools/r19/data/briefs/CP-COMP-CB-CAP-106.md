# Bennis v. Michigan

**516 U.S. 442 (1996), decided March 4, 1996. Civil Procedure.** Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote for O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas, and Ginsburg. Thomas and Ginsburg also concurred separately. Stevens dissented with Souter and Breyer; Kennedy dissented separately. The judgment was 5–4. The assigned account is CivPro physical page 172, printed 296, Note 3, ending before Note 4. Its placement among due-process materials should not obscure the nature of Tina Bennis’s objection: she received notice and an opportunity to contest the forfeiture, but claimed that her innocent ownership interest itself required protection.

## The car, the offense, and the state proceedings

John and Tina Bennis jointly owned an eleven-year-old Pontiac bought for $600. Detroit police observed John engaging in a sexual act with a prostitute in the parked car. After his conviction for gross indecency, Michigan sued both spouses to declare and abate the car as a public nuisance. Tina maintained that she did not know her husband would use the vehicle unlawfully. The trial court rejected that defense and ordered abatement of the entire car. 516 U.S. at 443–445.

The judge recognized equitable discretion, including the possibility of giving an innocent co-owner half the proceeds after costs. He did not do so here, citing the car’s age and low value and the expectation that little would remain after costs. He also noted that the couple had another automobile. These considerations belonged to the remedial decision; they were not findings that Tina participated in or negligently enabled the offense. Id. at 444–445.

The Michigan Court of Appeals reversed, construing state precedent to require knowledge before abating Tina’s interest. It also thought a single occurrence without evidence of payment did not establish the statutory nuisance. The Michigan Supreme Court reversed and reinstated the entire abatement. It construed the statute to reach the incident, assumed Tina lacked knowledge or consent to the misuse, and held that an innocent-owner defense was not constitutionally required. It distinguished use without an owner’s consent from misuse by someone entitled to use the vehicle and emphasized the equitable character of the action. Id. at 445–446.

The United States Supreme Court reviewed the due-process and takings objections. Its decision did not revisit the state-law nuisance construction or adjudicate a separate criminal charge against Tina.

## The majority’s due-process holding

The Court expressly observed that Tina had received notice and an opportunity to be heard. Her claim instead sought a substantive defense: she should be able to avoid loss of her share by proving that she did not know of the unlawful use. The majority rejected that asserted constitutional entitlement on the basis of longstanding forfeiture decisions. Id. at 446.

The historical cases treated property used unlawfully as subject to in rem forfeiture even when its owner was personally innocent. The majority discussed ships engaged in unlawful activity, premises used by a lessee to evade revenue laws, and automobiles entrusted to others and used to transport liquor. Its explanation was not simply that Tina’s car was physically present where a crime occurred. It regarded the conceded facilitating use and joint owner’s authority to use the car as falling within the established line of cases. Id. at 446–450.

Tina relied on Calero-Toledo’s suggestion that a constitutional claim might exist for an owner who was uninvolved, unaware, and had done everything reasonably expected to prevent the wrongful use. The majority treated that suggestion as dictum and adhered to the actual holding permitting forfeiture despite the owner’s lack of involvement and knowledge. It said Tina had shown no more than that absence of knowledge. Id. at 449–450.

Important limits remained. Note 5 distinguishes property stolen or taken without the owner’s privity or consent from property entrusted or available for another’s authorized use but then misused. Because John co-owned the car, Tina could not place herself in the former category. The Court also declined to decide extreme hypothetical applications, such as confiscating an ocean liner because of one passenger’s conduct. It did not hold all innocent-owner interests forfeitable in every conceivable circumstance. Id. at 448–451 & n.5.

The majority also rejected attempts to derive the proposed defense from Foucha’s limits on confinement after an insanity acquittal or Austin’s recognition that punitive civil forfeiture can implicate the Excessive Fines Clause. Neither decision resolved or displaced the innocent-owner precedents controlling this case. The majority emphasized the state court’s remedial discretion and the deterrent purposes of preventing further unlawful use and avoiding arrangements that could conceal collusion. Its decision on the claims before it should not be restated as an independent holding that all forfeitures escape excessive-fines scrutiny. Id. at 451–452.

## The takings claim

Tina separately argued that taking her ownership share for public use required compensation. The Court reasoned that, because the forfeiture lawfully transferred the interest to the state under authority other than eminent domain, the state need not compensate her as if it had condemned the property. The takings argument did not independently undo a forfeiture that survived the due-process challenge. Id. at 452–453.

## Thomas and Ginsburg

Thomas joined the majority but acknowledged that forfeiture without proof of the owner’s wrongdoing could appear deeply unfair. Historical acceptance supplied his constitutional justification, rather than an affirmative finding that Tina was blameworthy. He urged strict attention to the historical limits on what qualifies as an instrumentality, particularly when those limits are the principal protection for innocent owners. He also considered a possible remedial explanation: selling a low-value nuisance vehicle to cover related costs might resemble a permissible destruction remedy. His starred note qualifies the costs discussion, recognizing that the trial judge may have included law-enforcement expenses, not only the sale’s costs. Those observations do not establish that every revenue-producing forfeiture is remedial. Id. at 453–457 & n.* at 456.

Ginsburg highlighted the narrow setting. John had an equal ownership right to use the car; Tina conceded the state could forfeit the vehicle itself, leaving the constitutional issue about her share of any net proceeds. Ginsburg relied on Michigan’s equitable discretion to restrain exorbitant applications and the practical reasons given for refusing a distribution here. She explicitly declined to endorse an experiment in punishing innocent third parties. Id. at 457–458.

## The dissents

Stevens, joined by Souter and Breyer, distinguished contraband, proceeds, and instrumentalities. Ordinary automobiles are not contraband, and this car was neither criminal proceeds nor principally used in a criminal enterprise. He viewed its one-time use as a stationary enclosure as too weak a connection to justify forfeiture. Historical maritime cases involving a ship’s unlawful mission did not support seizing valuable property merely because an isolated illegal act occurred there. Id. at 458–465.

He also regarded Tina as genuinely blameless. The dissent described her work-funded ownership, her expectation that John would return from work, and her call to Missing Persons when he did not. She could not reasonably prevent an unknown act by an equal co-owner. Stevens disputed both deterrence and the asserted need to avoid investigating collusion on these facts. He relied on the reasonable-prevention language the majority treated as dictum and argued that fundamental fairness barred punishment of an innocent person. Finally, he considered the loss excessive under Austin: it was disproportionate to Tina’s complete lack of blame and varied arbitrarily with the car’s value. These were dissenting grounds for reversal, not the Court’s adopted rule. Id. at 466–472.

Kennedy separately accepted the historical maritime precedents but rejected their extension here. Those cases reflected difficulties reaching remote shipowners and obtaining compensation, with liability bounded by the vessel or cargo. An automobile is often a practical necessity, and its tangential involvement did not establish that presumptions of negligent entrustment or complicity would be insufficient. Nothing in Michigan’s reasoning or the evidence linked Tina to negligence or complicity, and her interest was not too small for constitutional protection. Id. at 472–473.

## Exact judgment and significance

The Court **affirmed the Michigan Supreme Court’s judgment**, leaving the forfeiture without an offset for Tina’s interest intact. Id. at 453. The assigned note accurately conveys the innocent-owner holding and the fact that she received procedural opportunities. The full report adds the authorized-use distinction, the reserved extreme applications, the equitable-remedy features emphasized by the concurrences, and the sharply contested connection between the car and the offense. This brief describes the 1996 ruling and does not certify current forfeiture statutes or subsequent constitutional doctrine.
