# Kizziar v. Dollar
268 F.2d 914 (10th Cir. 1959) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural posture
Judge Pickett wrote for the Tenth Circuit panel of Murrah, Pickett, and Breitenstein on July 23, 1959; rehearing was denied August 20. Following a bench trial over construction of a medical clinic, the court accepted substantial performance and sustained several challenged findings. It nevertheless remanded for additional findings and modification of the judgment because the deductions for inadequate central cooling and uncompleted contractual items did not properly protect the owners. The assigned foundation example therefore does not mean that the entire award was affirmed unchanged. (914, 916–919.)

## Contract, construction, and claims
On November 12, 1956, Dollar agreed in writing with O. Q. Kizziar to construct a building on property owned by Kizziar and his wife, Mildred, for $20,000. Dollar sought that contract price and $2,863.50 under an asserted later oral enlargement agreement, together with foreclosure of his mechanics’ and materialmen’s lien and a reasonable attorney fee. A separate count sought the unpaid balance on an earlier building contract. The trial court awarded the requested construction amounts less deductions, ordered foreclosure by sale, allowed $2,500 for costs and attorney fees, and awarded $902.60 on the earlier contract. (916.)

The building was a medical clinic occupied by the defendants’ lessee from early April 1957. Changes had been made during construction at the request of the owners and lessee. The trial findings also established Mildred’s knowledge and participation and the owners’ possession and acceptance of rental benefits. These were findings supporting the appellate court’s treatment of performance, rather than a conclusion that every contractual deviation had been expressly waived. (916–917 & n. 1.)

## Substantial performance and the contract-price remedy
The defendants argued that the agreement was entire and indivisible and that deficient performance barred recovery on it. Applying Oklahoma law, the court rejected a requirement of literal compliance with every detail where a builder had made a good-faith effort and substantially supplied the contracted building, especially where the owner had occupied and used it. Substantial performance permitted recovery of the contract price less reasonable costs of curing defects and omissions chargeable to the builder. The court found no indication of Dollar’s bad faith and concluded that he had substantially performed. That threshold conclusion did not eliminate the owners’ right to appropriate deductions. (916–917.)

The specified roof was five-ply; Dollar installed four-ply. The trial court allowed $150, the evidenced reasonable cost of adding the missing ply. The central heating and cooling system presented a different problem. The specifications called for a ducted Chrysler Air Temp system or its equal, with a duct in each room, though they did not state the unit’s size. The installed make was equivalent, but its cooling capacity was inadequate. The trial court deducted $750 for window air conditioners. The appellate court required an allowance based on an adequately functioning central system, because that was the contractual performance the owners had purchased. Approval of a make did not authorize an inferior type or insufficient capacity. (917.)

Other required items had not been completed or waived, including painting, tile cleaning, mouldings, bathroom locks, and medicine cabinets. The owners were entitled to the necessary completion allowance unless their own conduct had solely prevented completion. The allowable items were limited to those required by the contract; the trial court could determine them from the existing record, additional evidence, or both. (917 & n. 2.)

## The foundation finding emphasized by the assignment
Although the foundation did not conform to the specifications and the variance had not been authorized, undisputed evidence established that it was standard and adequate for this building. No evidence showed the cost of a conforming foundation or a particular reason for insisting on the specified design. On that record, the court held the foundation sufficient under substantial performance. This was a factual application involving adequacy and the absence of evidence of a special contractual purpose; it did not establish that every structural deviation is immaterial. Its treatment also differed from the cooling deficiency, which impaired the actual central system the contract required. (917.)

## Oral enlargement, credits, and other challenged rulings
Dollar testified that the planned 70-by-35-foot building was extended to 77 feet in length by later agreement for an additional $2,863.50. The defendants denied the agreement. The doctor who would occupy the building confirmed the original dimensions and his pleasure at learning of the extension, and other evidence supported Dollar. The finding of the oral agreement was therefore not clearly erroneous. (917–918.)

Before construction, the lessee advanced Dollar $3,500 and took a note signed by Dollar and O. Q. Kizziar. Dollar bore primary liability, and the trial court directed payment from foreclosure-sale proceeds. Both signers needed protection against double payment. Because all concerned agreed the note should be paid from judgment funds, the court left a protective method to the trial court and parties. The lessee had credited rentals against the note rather than paying rent to the owners; Dollar conceded Kizziar’s entitlement to credit for amounts actually paid through rental credits or otherwise. The opinion did not simply direct an unconditional additional $3,500 deduction from the judgment. (918 & n. 3.)

The court also sustained the $2,500 attorney-fee allowance despite the complaint’s $2,000 allegation. The fee-hearing evidence was absent from the appellate record, so the court presumed adequate support, and Rule 15(b) permitted treating the pleading as amended to conform to sufficient proof. Conflicting testimony about payment on the earlier contract supported the trial court’s $902.60 finding. Finally, the court found no abuse of discretion in denial of the motions for a new trial or additional evidence. (918–919.)

## Exact disposition and source scope
The final direction was remand for additional findings consistent with the opinion and modification of the judgment accordingly. There was no appellate computation of a final net construction award. (919.)
