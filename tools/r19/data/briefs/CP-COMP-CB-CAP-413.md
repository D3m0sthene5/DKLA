# Ross v. Bernhard

**Civil Procedure — Jury rights on a corporation’s legal claims in a shareholder derivative action.** Supreme Court of the United States, February 2, 1970. **396 U.S. 531.** Justice White wrote the Court’s opinion. Justice Stewart dissented, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justice Harlan; the assigned account reports the decision as 5–3. The principal assigned passage spans physical PDF pages 540–541, printed pages 1063–1065; all four attached notes continue through physical page 542, printed page 1067.

## Alleged corporate injury and the jury demand

Shareholders of the Lehman Corporation, a closed-end investment company, brought a derivative suit against its directors and its brokers, Lehman Brothers. They alleged that the brokers controlled the corporation through illegally excessive representation on its board, contrary to the Investment Company Act of 1940, and used that control to extract excessive brokerage charges. They accused the directors of conversion and serious misconduct, and both the directors and brokers of breaches of fiduciary duty. The payments allegedly constituted corporate waste and violated the brokerage contract. These were allegations awaiting adjudication, not findings made in the jury-right decision. 396 U.S. at 531–532.

The shareholders sought an accounting and payment to the corporation of defendants’ gains and the corporation’s losses. They demanded a jury for the corporation’s claims. The recovery sought would belong to the corporation, rather than compensate the shareholders directly for individual injuries. That derivative structure was the source of the procedural dispute: shareholders invoked a remedy historically provided by equity to enforce a claim the corporation’s managers would not pursue. Id. at 532, 534–535, 538–539.

## Interlocutory review

The district court refused to strike the jury demand. It separated the shareholders’ entitlement to speak for the corporation from the merits of the corporation’s claims. The former was equitable and for the judge; the latter should receive the same jury treatment as if the corporation itself sued. The court authorized an interlocutory appeal under § 1292(b), recognizing a substantial dispute whose resolution could advance the litigation. Id. at 532.

The Second Circuit reversed, treating the derivative action as entirely equitable and therefore allowing no jury as of right on any part of it. Its view conflicted with the Ninth Circuit’s *DePinto* decision. The Supreme Court reviewed that categorical denial, not a judgment deciding whether the defendants actually overcharged the corporation or breached their duties. Id. at 532–533.

## Holding: examine the underlying corporate issues

The Court held that the Seventh Amendment jury right extends to those issues in a derivative suit for which the corporation would have been entitled to a jury had it sued directly. The shareholder’s equitable means of obtaining access to court does not convert the corporation’s legal claim into an equitable one. The judge may decide whether the shareholder is entitled to proceed for the corporation, while the relevant legal issues remain jury-triable. Id. at 533, 538–540.

The Court described a derivative action as having two aspects. First, the shareholder must establish the right to sue on the corporation’s behalf, including a valid corporate cause of action and satisfaction of the demand requirement or an excuse from it. Second, the corporation’s actual claim must be adjudicated. The corporation remains a necessary party and the real party in interest even if formally aligned as a defendant; the shareholder acts as its nominal representative, and the corporation receives the proceeds and is bound by the result. Id. at 534–535 & nn.4–5; 538 & n.11.

This analysis does not require a jury for every issue in every derivative action. If the corporation’s own claim would have been equitable, the derivative mechanism creates no new jury right. The decision addresses a corporation’s legal issues presented through the shareholder, rather than transferring the equitable threshold question to the jury. Id. at 535, 538–539.

## History and the effect of merged procedure

At common law, shareholders could not ordinarily sue at law to call managers to account for corporate injuries. Equity developed the derivative remedy to avoid leaving corporate wrongs unredressed when the managers refused to act. Under that older arrangement, equity could resolve legal corporate claims because the shareholder otherwise had no adequate legal remedy. The majority acknowledged the traditional understanding that properly pleaded derivative suits could be tried without a jury. Id. at 534–537 & nn.3–9.

The Federal Rules removed the procedural barrier separating actions at law from suits in equity. A single civil action could now combine legal and equitable remedies; separate issues could be tried separately or handled in the same proceeding. Once the court determined the shareholder’s right to proceed, it could adjudicate the corporation’s damages claim with a jury. For the majority, denying that legal mode of trial merely because shareholders rather than directors brought the claim preserved an obsolete procedural limitation. Id. at 539–540 & nn.12–13.

The Court applied the principle of *Beacon Theatres* and *Dairy Queen*: equitable claims cannot absorb legal claims as merely incidental or permit judicial resolution of common factual issues to destroy a jury entitlement. The nature of the issue, rather than the overall action’s label, determines the Seventh Amendment inquiry. The majority also drew an analogy to class actions and other historically equitable procedural devices through which parties could now present legal claims to juries. Id. at 537–542 & nn.10–15.

Its treatment of *Fleitmann* illustrated the difficulty under older procedure. That case had recognized the jury implications of a corporation’s antitrust damages claim but rejected the shareholder’s equity suit; *United Copper* also denied a shareholder’s attempt to bring that corporate claim at law. The majority read the merged procedure as resolving this old procedural dilemma, rather than treating the historic identity of the spokesperson as decisive forever. Id. at 535–537 & nn.6–7.

## Application and the limits of note 10

The complaint sought money damages and included ordinary breach of contract and gross negligence, alongside fiduciary-duty allegations. At a minimum, the corporation suing directly could demand a jury for damages under its brokerage contract and its negligence claims against directors. That was enough to reject the blanket denial. The Court expressly found it unnecessary to decide whether the corporation’s other claims were also jury-triable. Id. at 542–543.

Note 10 listed three considerations for identifying a legal issue: historical practice before merger, the remedy requested, and juries’ practical abilities and limitations. The majority noted the difficulty of the historical inquiry. It did not deny a jury here because the investment dispute was complicated or announce an unrestricted power to withhold juries whenever a judge preferred to decide the facts. Id. at 538 n.10.

## Stewart’s dissent

Stewart, joined by Burger and Harlan, considered the decision an unjustified enlargement of a preserved constitutional right. In his view, the Seventh Amendment protected historically legal actions, Rule 38 likewise preserved rather than expanded that protection, and the Rules Enabling Act prohibited changes to substantive rights. Because a derivative action was historically a unitary creation of equity, procedural merger could not make its internal issues constitutionally jury-triable. Id. at 543–545 & nn.1–4.

He disputed the majority’s historical account of two independently triable aspects. Early shareholder suits enforced the beneficiary’s own equitable rights against fiduciaries; the corporate-cause-of-action description did not identify an actual historical suit at law. Moreover, earlier equity rules had already permitted trial according to legal principles where a legal matter genuinely arose, yet courts continued to treat derivative suits as equitable. He read *Fleitmann* as confirming that limitation, not supporting the majority’s solution. Id. at 545–548 & nn.5–10.

Stewart also distinguished *Beacon Theatres* and *Dairy Queen*, which combined historically separable legal and equitable claims. The derivative action, in his view, was not such a combination. Breaking every equitable action into factual components that could appear in a damages suit would threaten the remaining domain of nonjury equity adjudication. An injunction action often involves facts that would be jury-triable if damages were requested, but that did not itself generate a jury right. Nor did the ability to use an advisory or discretionary jury establish a constitutional entitlement. Id. at 548–551 & nn.11–13.

## Judgment and assigned context

The Court **reversed the Second Circuit’s decision** categorically denying a jury. Its mandate did not establish liability, award damages, or decide every pleaded claim’s mode of trial. Id. at 543.

The four assigned notes develop questions left by the opinion: the meaning of practical jury limitations, competing lower-court approaches to a complexity exception, possible procedures for helping juries handle difficult evidence, and whether equity litigants possess a corresponding right to judicial trial. Those later cases, research, and questions are editorial context, not additional holdings in *Ross*. The brief preserves the actual note 10 framework while avoiding the inference that *Ross* itself settled a broad complexity exception.
