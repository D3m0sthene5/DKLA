# United States v. Wegematic Corp.
360 F.2d 674 (2d Cir. 1966) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
The Second Circuit decided the case on May 5, 1966, after argument on March 28. Judge Friendly wrote for a panel with Chief Judge Lumbard and Judge Anderson; no separate writing or division was reported. It affirmed a Southern District of New York judgment awarding the United States $235,806, with six-percent interest from October 6, 1958. Wegematic’s sole appellate defense was practical impossibility of delivering the computer it had promised. The court rejected that defense; it did not independently decide every possible dispute about the damages calculation. (360 F.2d at 674–675, 677.)

## A promised computer, not a research program
In June 1956 the Federal Reserve Board invited five manufacturers to propose an intermediate, general-purpose electronic digital computing system, emphasizing early delivery. Wegematic had succeeded with a smaller ALWAC III-E and offered a new ALWAC 800 as a revolutionary system incorporating the latest advances. It stressed magnetic cores in memory, logical elements, and registers as reducing maintenance problems. Delivery was offered within nine months after an order. In September the Board ordered components at an aggregate price of $231,800, for delivery June 30, 1957. The order imposed $100 a day in liquidated damages for delay and authorized substitute procurement with the contractor responsible for excess cost if it failed to comply with the agreement. Wegematic accepted. (Id. at 674–675.)

In March 1957 Wegematic suggested postponement. In April it promised delivery by October 30, citing redesign, and sought waiver of delay damages. In August it projected delay possibly into 1959, proposed temporary use of its smaller machine, and again sought waiver. The Board considered these requests but expressly reserved its rights. In mid-October Wegematic said engineering difficulties made delivery impracticable and sought cancellation without damages. The Board procured an IBM 650, delivered October 6, 1958, serving substantially the same purpose, at annual rent of $102,000 with a $410,450 purchase option. (Id. at 675.)

The district court awarded $46,300 in delay damages, $179,450 for excess IBM cost, and $10,056 in preparatory expenses unusable with the IBM system. The opinion reports those components and their $235,806 total; it does not explain the difference between the stated excess-cost figure and simple subtraction of the two stated equipment prices. They are retained as reported findings, not silently recalculated. (Id.)

## Engineering defense and the relevant question
Wegematic asserted that basic engineering problems would require one to two years and $1 million to $1.5 million to correct, with success probable but uncertain. The record did not clearly establish the precise technical problem; experts suggested that the magnetic cores lacked sufficient uniformity. Both parties accepted federal law as governing. Wegematic maintained that practical impossibility excused its default. (Id. at 675.)

Friendly first separated the government’s precedents by the actual relief and posture involved. Cases involving completed performance and demands for added compensation were not necessarily controlling on a failure to perform and resistance to damages. Another contractor sought pretermination costs under a special clause, while another lacked funds peculiar to itself. Subjective inability did not establish that the promised performance was unavailable to others. A technological-delay case involved construction of a specified unavoidable-causes clause. These distinctions prevented the government’s authorities from mechanically resolving this appeal, but did not establish that Wegematic was excused. (Id. at 675–676.)

## The UCC as federal-law guidance and assumed risk
The court accepted Wegematic’s suggestion that the Uniform Commercial Code could guide federal sales law. It described the Code’s widespread adoption at the time and the value of commercial uniformity. The opinion did not rest on a finding that a particular state’s enactment governed the federal contract directly. Section 2-615 made nonperformance excusable when an unforeseen contingency defeated a basic assumption and rendered performance impracticable, except insofar as the seller had assumed a greater obligation. Friendly treated the basic-assumption inquiry as substantially a question of how much risk the promisor accepted. (Id. at 676.)

Marketing the machine as a technological breakthrough did not place the risk of discovering that breakthrough on the buyer. The reasonable understanding was that it had already occurred, or that the manufacturer assured its realization when the system was assembled. The Board solicited a computer, not proposals to conduct a development program. Otherwise a manufacturer could induce and bind the purchaser with an attractive promise, gamble on technical success, and escape liability if it failed while demanding the purchase price if it succeeded. The express delay and replacement-cost provisions made that reading particularly implausible. A seller wishing to exclude development risk could use appropriate exculpatory terms; this contract did not do so. (Id. at 676–677.)

## Impracticability evidence and the issue expressly reserved
The evidence of genuinely prohibitive cost was also unpersuasive. The proposed redesign expense had to be assessed against the whole contemplated ALWAC 800 program, not solely this bargain-priced sale. The court’s twenty-five-machine illustration suggested possible gross revenues of approximately $10 million at the comparable IBM price; it was not a finding of actual orders or certain profits. The redesign might make the venture unattractive without establishing the asserted excuse. (Id. at 677.)

On-time performance appeared impossible. But Wegematic had not offered prompt rectification and presented an issue about whether the government could refuse a chance to correct the design yet recover both delay and replacement costs. The court expressly left that hypothetical issue outside the appeal. Its affirmance concerned the nonperformance actually presented and the risk this manufacturer had assumed. (Id.)
