# Arguments Proving From Antiquity the Dignity, Power, and Jurisdiction of the Court of Chancery

**Course:** Civil Procedure

## Citation and source identity

Royal judgment of James I, July 18, 1616, reproduced within *Arguments Proving From Antiquity the Dignity, Power, and Jurisdiction of the Court of Chancery*, 1 Chan. Rep. App. 1–88, 21 Eng. Rep. 576–598. The assigned extract cites App. 1, 20, 23–24, 49–50 and English Reports pp. 576, 581–582, 588. Civil Procedure casebook pp. 582–583, repository PDF p. 309.

This entry covers a developed historical legal account and a royal jurisdictional instrument. The reproduced tract has no conventional plaintiff–defendant caption. Its hypothetical parties A and B do not supply an actual case name. The entry therefore retains its assigned heading rather than substituting an Earl of Oxford caption or counting the tract as an ordinary reported judgment. The anonymous compiler's discussion, counsel's certificates, and the King's judgment are distinguishable source components. The compiler's opening refers to Coke's Institutes appearing after Coke's death; the tract as reproduced is retrospective, although the embedded royal judgment dates to 1616. 21 Eng. Rep. at 576, 587–589.

## Historical dispute and material assumptions

The dispute arose between King's Bench and Chancery over whether Chancery could grant equitable relief after a common-law judgment. The asserted statutory obstacles were the praemunire statute of 27 Edward III, chapter 1, and the prohibition associated with 4 Henry IV. The tract identifies the latter as chapter 23 in its introductory and counsel discussions but chapter 22 when reproducing its statutory text. That internal numbering variation is preserved rather than resolved through an unsupported modern correction. Id. at 576, 582, 586.

James I directed his Attorney General, assisted by other learned counsel, to examine original Chancery precedents and report on the practice. Counsel's first certificate said the extracted precedent note was supported by the originals and documented a continuing practice from Henry VII's reign through the current Chancellor. The examples included real and personal actions, several common-law courts, verdicts and demurrers, and cases involving writs of error. Relief could follow judgment and even execution. Equitable grounds often existed before the judgment, rather than arising from a later agreement. Id. at 581.

The second referral presented a hypothetical. A obtained judgment and execution against B for a £1,000 debt and for ejectment concerning the manor of D. B accepted that the judgments were legally sound under strict common-law rules but sought relief on equitable grounds that the common-law judges could not remedy by error, attaint, or other means. The report gives no underlying debtor biography, actual manor litigation, factual trial, or final monetary recovery for B. Id. at 581–582.

## Procedure and decisionmakers

The King first requested verification of the precedent record. Counsel returned thirteen observations. The Chancellor then sent the hypothetical for a further legal opinion; the Prince's Attorney joined the advisers. Their second certificate described examination of original statutes and parliamentary petitions, including the King's answers, as well as authorities supporting and opposing Chancery's jurisdiction. All subscribed to the opinion that Chancery could relieve in the proposed case and that no praemunire or other statute restrained that relief. Id. at 581–582.

The certificate names Francis Bacon, Henry Montagu, Henry Yelverton, Randall Crew, and John Walter. James subsequently ratified the practice and legal opinions and ordered enrollment of the proceedings in Chancery. The source records the royal judgment as July 18, 1616, in the fourteenth regnal year. Bacon and Yelverton appear beneath its concluding formula. This was a royal resolution of a conflict between courts, not a modern appellate panel with an affirmance, remand, or recorded judicial vote count. Id. at 588.

## Issue

Could Chancery grant relief in equity and conscience after a judgment valid at common law, where the common-law tribunal could not remedy the equitable injustice, or did the cited statutes prohibit that intervention? Id. at 582.

## Holding and operative direction

The royal judgment approved both counsel's account of established Chancery practice and their statutory opinion as applied to the referred case. It commanded the Chancellor or Keeper of the Great Seal to continue giving relief justified by the merits and justice of complaints, notwithstanding common-law proceedings, consistently with Chancery's ancient and continuing practice. It also asserted the King's authority to determine jurisdictional differences between his courts and ordered the proceedings enrolled to reduce future disputes. Id. at 588.

The instrument did not authorize Chancery to retry any losing common-law case merely because the result was unfavorable. Counsel distinguished equitable intervention directed at a party's conscience from reversal of the legal judgment on the point adjudicated. The direction remained tied to the merit and justice of the particular equitable cause. Id. at 587–588.

## Counsel's reasoning

Established practice supplied counsel's foundation. The first certificate documented orders, injunctions, and decrees that released judgments, prevented or corrected possession obtained through them, addressed mesne profits, or released costs. Defendants sometimes expressly argued that an existing judgment barred relief; that objection was overruled. Common-law judges themselves referred parties to Chancery and exercised equitable authority when sitting there during a Chancellor's absence. Counsel considered this sustained conduct by legally trained magistrates a powerful guide to statutory meaning. Id. at 581–582.

Counsel offered eight reasons why praemunire did not bar the relief. The statute's mischief concerned prejudice to the King's jurisdiction through another authority, whereas Chancery was the King's own court. Its remedial procedure included appearance in Chancery, making it implausible that Chancery simultaneously supplied the offense and remedy. Its severe exclusion from royal protection fitted adherence to foreign jurisdiction rather than an excess within a royal court. Related statutes and historical references connected the prohibition to foreign or papally subordinate jurisdictions. The original petition and its translated legal forms referred to another court in a sense excluding the King's own court. Existing common-law forms concerned ecclesiastical proceedings, and the advisers found indictments but no demonstrated convictions for proceedings of this kind in Chancery. Id. at 583–584.

The last point was evidentiary, not a holding that indictment could never establish liability. The surrounding tract expressly explains that an indictment does not itself prove guilt and that obtaining a pardon likewise does not necessarily establish the asserted offense. Id. at 583.

The Henry IV provision addressed domestic proceedings, but counsel gave six reasons for excluding the referred equitable case. Chancery was omitted from the listed extraordinary proceedings. An immediately preceding petition had expressly sought restrictions on Chancery and other ordinary processes, and the King's answer preserved necessary use of them. Equitable consideration did not put the same previously decided legal issue in controversy again: the equity had not been adjudicated at common law, and Chancery was not deciding that common-law issue anew. Error and attaint reversed judgments; Chancery's proper intervention instead corrected the party's conscience. No conviction under the asserted statutory theory had been established. Finally, requiring anticipatory equitable proceedings before a party knew whether common law would provide relief could leave the injured party without a practical remedy. Id. at 587–588.

Counsel acknowledged a limit to the materials examined. Chancery clerks had supplied precedents, while King's Bench officers had not supplied indictment precedents; the advisers offered to examine them if ordered. Their assertion that no contrary conviction had been found should be read with that stated scope. Id. at 588.

## Compiler's further vindication

The remaining tract adds historical and interpretive arguments after the royal judgment. It discusses ancient courts, seals, writs, the Chancellor's functions, and the relationship of law and equity. It then argues from parliamentary lawmaking forms, differences between petitions and printed statutes, the statutory words, contemporary grievances, and subsequent practice. These propositions belong to the compiler's advocacy; they are not additional sentences of James's operative decree. Id. at 576–580, 588–598.

An illustrative bond dispute explains the practical concern: a common-law judgment for £800 could be enforceable even when only £20 remained unpaid, while equity could prevent collection beyond what was actually due. The compiler also examines Throgmorton's litigation and argues that its forfeitures and the Queen's interest distinguish it from a general statutory bar on postjudgment equity. The discussion concludes that exclusion of equitable relief for fraud, breach of trust, or forfeiture would enable injustice that error or attaint might not correct. These are examples and arguments in the tract, not awards or findings in the A/B hypothetical. Id. at 593, 595–598.

## Assigned account and source reconciliation

The textbook reproduces the referral for precedent verification, the A/B hypothetical and jurisdictional question, the second referral, and the royal command. It omits the certificates' enumerated findings, unanimous legal answer, and detailed reasons; those are supplied by the full report. Its surrounding Maitland discussion explains equity's relationship to common law. That commentary is contextual explanation rather than a separate judicial opinion. The full report supplies the omitted statutory analysis and the distinction between proper equitable restraint and reversal of the common-law judgment. Assigned pp. 582–583; 21 Eng. Rep. at 581–588.

The reproduced source has embedded nominate-report page markers, numbered arguments, and quoted historical texts, but no separate modern judicial concurrence, dissent, or numbered judicial footnote series. Neither the compiler's criticism of Coke nor the later vindication is a dissent from a panel judgment. Pinpoints in this brief use printed English Reports pages; the original 23-page tract occupies physical pp. 590–612 of the preserved volume.
