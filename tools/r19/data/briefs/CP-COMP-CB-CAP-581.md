# Migra v. Warren City School District Board of Education

**465 U.S. 75 (1984) · Supreme Court of the United States · January 23, 1984**

Justice Blackmun wrote for a unanimous Court. Justice White also concurred, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justice Powell. The Court vacated the Sixth Circuit’s judgment and remanded for further proceedings under Ohio preclusion law. The assigned account is Note 1 on CivPro PDF page 690, printed pages 1370–1371.

## Facts and litigation sequence

Dr. Ethel D. Migra worked as an elementary-school supervisor for the Warren City School District from August 1976 through June 1979 under successive one-year contracts. On April 17, 1979, all five school-board members voted to renew her contract. Her acceptance letter was dated April 18 and delivered April 23. At a special meeting on April 24, attended by four members, the board president read the letter; the board nevertheless voted three to one against renewal. Migra sued in the Ohio Court of Common Pleas for breach of contract and other alleged wrongdoing. After a bench trial, that court found an enforceable contract and ordered reinstatement and compensatory damages. The state appellate court affirmed, and the Ohio Supreme Court declined review. 465 U.S. at 78–80.

The state litigation included more than the contract claim. Migra had alleged a conspiracy and tortious conduct by individual defendants. Those matters were reserved and continued rather than decided with the contract claim. After receiving the contract judgment, she moved to dismiss the remaining claims without prejudice. That sequence matters: the later federal action raised the effect of a completed state judgment on additional claims, rather than the effect of an adjudication actually rejecting those additional claims. Id. at 79 n.2. The respondents described the damages as back pay less unemployment compensation; the Court reported that as their account, not as a separate damages finding of its own. Id. at 80 n.3.

In July 1980, Migra brought a federal action against the board, its then-current members, and the superintendent in the Northern District of Ohio. She invoked 42 U.S.C. §§1983 and 1985, alleging violations of the First, Fifth, and Fourteenth Amendments. Among other things, she alleged retaliation for her work on a voluntary desegregation commission and a social-studies curriculum, as well as deprivations of property without due process and denial of equal protection. These were allegations, not findings that the Supreme Court adopted as true on their merits. She sought injunctive relief and compensatory and punitive damages. The defendants asserted res judicata and limitations defenses. The district court entered summary judgment on preclusion grounds, and the Sixth Circuit affirmed in an unpublished decision. Id. at 80–81.

## Question and governing rule

Does §1983 permit a federal plaintiff to litigate a civil-rights claim that could have been raised in an earlier state action, even when the state’s own preclusion law would bar it? The answer is no. Under 28 U.S.C. §1738, a federal court must give a state-court judgment the same preclusive effect that the judgment would receive in the courts of the state that rendered it. Section 1983 does not create an exception allowing the plaintiff to divide state and federal claims between successive actions. Id. at 81–85.

The distinction between claim and issue preclusion is essential. Issue preclusion prevents relitigation of an issue actually litigated and decided; claim preclusion can also foreclose grounds for recovery that were available but were not presented in the earlier action. The Court explained this terminology because older decisions often used “res judicata” for both concepts. Id. at 77 n.1. Allen v. McCurry had already held that §1983 did not displace ordinary issue preclusion. Allen had left open the claim-preclusion question presented here. Migra resolved that question by applying the same statutory command to claims that could have been litigated. Id. at 81–83.

## Reasoning and competing positions

Migra argued that the special purpose of §1983 entitled her to reserve federal civil-rights claims for federal court while first obtaining state relief on her contract claim. The Court rejected that proposed division of forums. Nothing in §1983’s language or legislative history established an exception to §1738 for this situation. Congress had provided a federal cause of action, but had not given every plaintiff a right to serial litigation insulated from the consequences of a prior state judgment. The same state judgment could not receive ordinary claim-preclusive treatment in state court and reduced treatment merely because the later action invoked §1983. Id. at 83–85.

The Court connected this rule to the practical purposes of preclusion: limiting repeated litigation, conserving judicial resources, protecting defendants from vexatious suits, and respecting state adjudications. Allowing a plaintiff to withhold available federal claims from a state action and then pursue them in federal court would undermine those purposes. This reasoning concerned the consequences of Migra’s chosen litigation sequence, not a conclusion that her constitutional allegations lacked substance. Id. at 84–85.

Blackmun addressed an apparent tension with his own dissent in Allen. Allen involved a criminal defendant who had been forced to litigate in state court; Migra had affirmatively chosen a state forum to obtain relief. That difference explained why he did not view his earlier position as controlling this case. Id. at 85 n.7. The opinion also distinguished the England reservation associated with federal abstention. When a plaintiff first invokes federal jurisdiction and the federal court abstains so that state-law issues can be resolved, the plaintiff may have a recognized route for preserving federal issues. Migra had not followed that sequence. A plaintiff’s unilateral desire to split claims was not equivalent to a reservation arising within that abstention framework. Id.

## Why the Court remanded instead of finally applying preclusion

The Court’s federal statutory holding did not itself establish which claims Ohio law barred. Ohio’s doctrine had evolved. Vasu had permitted separate litigation of personal-injury and property-damage claims arising from the same event; Rush later overruled that approach. Other decisions, including Henderson and Sharp, and Ohio’s compulsory-counterclaim rule demonstrated the relevance of a broader claim-preclusion inquiry. In Johnson’s Island, the Ohio Supreme Court held that failure to raise a constitutional zoning defense in an earlier enforcement proceeding could bar a later action asserting that constitutional objection. Id. at 85–87.

Those developments required examination of the precise Ohio rule and its application to Migra’s litigation. The district court had relied largely on federal decisions, and its opinion did not make clear that it had applied Ohio law. The Supreme Court therefore left that work to the district court on remand. It vacated the Sixth Circuit’s judgment and remanded so that the district court could determine the preclusive effect of the state judgment under Ohio law. Id. at 87. It did not adjudicate the constitutional claims, decide the limitations defense, or itself enter a final ruling that every federal claim was precluded.

The assigned note compresses this result into a statement that the Supreme Court found the prior state judgment precluded the federal suit. The actual disposition is narrower: the Court required application of Ohio’s preclusion law and remanded for that determination. CivPro PDF 690, printed 1370–1371; 465 U.S. at 87.

## White’s concurrence and significance

White agreed that precedent required exactly the state-law preclusive effect and joined the Court’s opinion. He separately expressed the view that federal courts should be able to give a state judgment greater preclusive effect, although not less, when federal principles warranted it. He accepted the settled interpretation of §1738 because Congress had left it undisturbed. His concurrence did not alter the unanimous judgment or authorize the district court to disregard Ohio law in this case. Id. at 88.

Migra’s historical contribution is the extension of §1738’s ordinary state-law preclusion command to omitted §1983 claims. Its analysis separates two questions that a brief must keep distinct: whether federal civil-rights law exempts the plaintiff from state claim preclusion, and whether the particular state’s rules actually bar the later claims. Migra rejected the proposed exemption and remanded the state-law application.
