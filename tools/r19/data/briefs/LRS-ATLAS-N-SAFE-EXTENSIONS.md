# Safe Extensions, Inc. v. Federal Aviation Administration
509 F.3d 593 (D.C. Cir. 2007) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, issue, and result

A unanimous D.C. Circuit panel—Judges Henderson, Tatel, and Kavanaugh—reviewed an FAA advisory circular that imposed a new torque test on adjustable airport-light extensions while exempting fixed extensions. Judge Tatel wrote the opinion. The agency first challenged the availability of judicial review; the court rejected each barrier before finding that the agency’s three explanations for the different treatment lacked substantial evidentiary support. It granted Safe Extensions’ petition for review. The final judicial paragraph says “grant the petition for review” and “So ordered”; it does not expressly direct vacatur or remand. 509 F.3d at 595, 598–606.

## Regulatory setting and factual chronology

Federal aviation law authorized minimum airport-safety standards, including standards for airports serving aircraft with more than thirty passenger seats. FAA advisory circulars supplied testing requirements for airport-light components and determined which products appeared on the agency’s approved list. Airports receiving federal funds had to use approved products. That condition mattered commercially because virtually every public airport received such funds. A manufacturer could lose its airport market even though the document describing the condition was called “advisory.” Id. at 595.

Airport lights rest on bases that must withstand aircraft and snowplows while retaining their alignment. Extensions adjust the light’s height when an airport is resurfaced. Fixed extensions achieve height changes through stacked components; adjustable extensions use a threaded mechanism. Both ordinarily sit in concrete or grout. For decades, the FAA’s torque testing examined extensions installed in that surrounding material. The installed test applied to both kinds from 1970 to 2005. Id. at 595–596.

An April 2005 circular, AC 42D, apparently applied the established test only to adjustable extensions, but the decisive practical difference arose with AC 42E in May 2006. It required adjustable extensions to survive a more demanding torque test outside concrete or grout, while fixed extensions remained exempt. In August the FAA sent a draft replacement, AC 42F, to selected manufacturers. Safe Extensions did not receive that draft. The draft retained the disputed requirement. Olson and Siemens commented that their adjustable extensions could not satisfy the freestanding test, that fixed extensions would fail if tested in the same fashion, and that extensive field use had produced no observed failures. Id. at 596–597, 603.

An FAA employee responded in September. He asserted that fixed extensions possessed inherent antirotation structures that bore against the ground, whereas adjustable extensions depended on chemical shear strength. He also referred to failures, uncertainty about installation quality, and the inability to perform the desired installed tests. Those were the agency’s assertions; the court later found that the record did not substantiate the material factual premises. The agency distributed that response to the selected companies when it published the final circular. Safe Extensions neither received the earlier draft nor had an opportunity to reply; FAA counsel did not dispute that account. The final AC 42F appeared on October 17, 2006. Safe Extensions petitioned for review on December 15. Id. at 597.

## Availability and timing of judicial review

The circular qualified as a reviewable order under 49 U.S.C. § 46110. It completed the FAA’s decisionmaking and carried legal consequences through the restriction on federally funded purchases. Calling it advisory did not remove those consequences. The court distinguished an agency warning whose practical effect consisted of unfavorable publicity: this circular operated through an actual purchase restriction. Id. at 598–599.

The FAA also argued that an insufficient administrative record defeated appellate jurisdiction. The court rejected that reading of earlier precedent. Older references to an adequate record had addressed whether appellate review was available outside quasi-judicial proceedings; they did not establish an independent jurisdictional requirement after the law accepted review of other agency action. A missing record can require a remand for further agency explanation. It does not transfer review to a district court for factfinding or let the agency avoid review by failing to document its decision. The available record here was sufficient to evaluate whether the agency’s justification had evidentiary support. Id. at 599–600.

Safe Extensions also had the required substantial interest in the order. The court used the then-current language of “prudential standing” and the zone of interests. Although the circular addressed airports directly, manufacturers whose ability to sell approved equipment depended on it were sufficiently connected to the safety scheme. Indirect regulation through airport purchasing did not exclude them from review. This is the opinion’s historical analysis, not a statement that later standing doctrine uses the same classification. Id. at 600–601.

Nor was the decision committed to agency discretion by law under APA § 701(a)(2). The statutory objective of air safety supplied a workable standard, and § 46110 expressly provided review. Describing the decision as managerial or invoking resource burdens did not establish an exemption from review. The agency’s comparisons to military judgments and unusually burdensome review of vast numbers of rate decisions did not fit this product-testing controversy. Id. at 601–602.

The court itself raised a timing issue that neither party had briefed. The petition was within sixty days of AC 42F but outside sixty days of AC 42E, which had already introduced the objectionable differential test. After oral argument the court requested affidavits about the FAA’s statements during reconsideration. Those affidavits bore on the filing deadline, rather than supplying missing merits evidence. Safe Extensions’ witnesses described statements that AC 42E should be disregarded or forgotten while a revised circular was forthcoming. FAA affidavits denied reopening the particular torque requirement but did not eliminate the ambiguity created by the broader communications. Section 46110 expressly permitted a late petition for reasonable grounds; the agency’s conduct supplied such grounds here. The holding depended on that statutory safety valve and the communications in this case. It did not announce an unlimited extension whenever reconsideration is pending. Id. at 602–604.

## Evidentiary standard and rejection of the FAA’s explanations

The court examined the circular under APA § 706(2)(A) and the substantial-evidence requirement governing factual findings under § 46110(c). It treated the decision as an informal adjudication for which a formal hearing and notice-and-comment procedures had not been required. That procedural classification did not reduce the quantum of evidence necessary to support the underlying facts. Drawing on *Association of Data Processing Service Organizations v. Board of Governors*, the court distinguished the amount of evidentiary support from the location of that evidence: a formal proceeding ordinarily confines review to its specified record, whereas informal action may be supported by information the agency possessed when it acted. The latter principle does not permit new litigation explanations to replace evidence absent at the time of decision. Id. at 604–605.

The FAA offered three reasons for requiring the freestanding test only for adjustable extensions. First, it described fixed extensions as established products with a long record of successful use, while adjustable extensions were comparatively new. Such a difference could potentially matter to a testing policy. But the agency produced no evidence substantiating the relevant contrast. Merely offering a plausible explanation did not establish its factual premise. Id. at 605.

Second, it asserted that fixed extensions had mechanical antirotation structures and adjustable extensions relied on chemical bonding. The employee’s explanation was not backed by drawings, documentation, or affidavits showing the asserted design difference. Safe Extensions attached materials to its reply that purported to demonstrate antirotation features in its own products and the absence of those features in fixed products. The FAA objected that those materials had not been before it. The court neither examined nor relied on them: the agency’s explanation failed for lack of support without deciding whether the petitioner’s additional materials were admissible. Accordingly, the holding did not establish that the products’ designs were identical or that Safe Extensions had affirmatively proved the agency’s factual assertions false. Id. at 605.

Third, the FAA invoked actual failures of chemical bonds in adjustable extensions. Again it supplied no supporting evidence. The record instead contained the Olson and Siemens comments reporting substantial installed experience without problems. The court did not declare every adjustable product safe or preclude a supported future testing distinction. It held that the factual justification for this differential requirement was unsupported on the material before the agency. Id. at 605–606.

## Assigned passage and doctrinal significance

The assigned discussion places this case in the notes following *ADAPSO*, quoting its treatment of informal action and evidentiary support. The distinction is between the quantum of evidence and the permissible source of that evidence. Dispensing with a formal hearing record does not dispense with substantiation. Conversely, the court did not equate every organic statute, every form of agency action, or every review standard. Its review rested on the particular aviation statute, APA framework, and unsupported factual reasons offered for this circular. Id. at 604–606.
