# Schiavone v. Fortune

**Civil Procedure — 477 U.S. 21 (1986). Decided June 18, 1986, No. 84-1839.** Blackmun wrote for Brennan, Marshall, Powell, Rehnquist, and O’Connor, producing a 6–3 affirmance. Stevens dissented, joined by Chief Justice Burger and White. Assigned: CivPro physical pages 364–365, printed page 696, own account within the Note on the Relation Back Doctrine.

Three libel plaintiffs filed within the limitations period but named Fortune rather than its publisher, Time, Incorporated. Time received notice only after the limitations period expired, although within the period allowed for service. Applying Rule 15(c) as it then existed, the Supreme Court held that the amendment naming Time did not relate back and affirmed the judgments sustaining dismissal. The decision's notice deadline was later changed by amendment to Rule 15(c); its historical holding must not be substituted for the revised rule. (29–32; Rule 15, 1991 Committee Note.)

The alleged libel appeared in Fortune's May 31, 1982, cover story about charges against Reagan's Labor Secretary. Each plaintiff filed a diversity action in federal district court in New Jersey on May 9, 1983. The complaint named Fortune and described it as a foreign corporation with offices at the Time and Life Building. In fact, Fortune was a trademark and an internal division of Time, a New York corporation. No party claimed that Fortune was a separate legal entity capable of being sued. (22–23 & n.2.)

New Jersey's libel statute allowed one year after publication. The magazine's cover date was not its distribution date: subscription copies were mailed on May 12 and received May 13–19, newsstand copies went on sale May 17, and press materials had gone out on May 11. The Third Circuit concluded that limitations expired by May 19, 1983, at the latest. The complaints were timely filed, but counsel did not mail them to Time's registered agent until May 20. The agent received them May 23 and refused service because Time was not named. In July, plaintiffs amended to designate Fortune also known as Time, Incorporated; the amended complaints were served July 21. (23–25 & nn.3–4.)

The district court found that the amendments adequately identified Time, eliminating the problem of suing an entity without capacity. But it held that Time had not received notice of the suits within the limitations period, as then-Rule 15(c) required for relation back. The magazine's masthead made the publisher readily discoverable, and the court rejected an assertion that Time had deliberately misled plaintiffs. It denied reconsideration. The Third Circuit consolidated the appeals and affirmed, refusing to include the service period in the rule's notice deadline. (24–25.)

Plaintiffs argued that the Federal Rules favored merits adjudication and that Time received the same notice it would have received had counsel correctly named it initially. They relied on the relationship between Fortune and Time and on the 1966 amendment's purpose of relieving mistakes in naming defendants. Time answered that the rule's language required notice within the limitations period and that adopting the plaintiffs' interpretation would rewrite it. The Court accepted that the original filings were timely and all later service and amendments occurred after limitations; the dispute concerned the legal effect of the misdesignation. (25–27.)

The majority first addressed identity of interest. Some appellate decisions allowed notice to an originally named party to be imputed to a related party later added by amendment. The Court did not adopt or categorically reject that exception. Even assuming it applied and Fortune could be a defendant, it could not help these plaintiffs: neither Fortune nor Time had received notice before limitations expired. There was no timely notice to one entity that could be attributed to the other. The masthead's identification of Time also undermined the claim that naming the proper publisher had been difficult. (27–29 & nn.6–7.)

The majority then stated four requirements under the old Rule 15(c). The amended claim had to arise from the original conduct; the new party had to receive notice sufficient to avoid prejudice in defending; it had to know or have reason to know that a mistake concerning identity explained the failure to name it; and the notice and knowledge requirements had to be satisfied within the limitations period. The first requirement presented no problem. The timing requirement did: Time first learned of the suits after May 19. (29–30.)

The Court declined to add the time for service under Rule 4 to the limitations period. Rule 4 concerned process, whereas Rules 3 and 15(c) concerned commencement of the action. The rule's reference to the period provided by law for commencing an action meant the limitations period. The 1966 Committee Note expressly used that formulation, and commentators agreed. General commitments to substantial justice and merits adjudication could not overcome the rule's specific language. The majority acknowledged the resulting arbitrariness but treated it as a consequence of the legislatively imposed limitations deadline. Footnote 8 distinguished government-misnomer examples in which the government had notice within limitations. (30–32 & n.8.)

Stevens's dissent had two independent objections. First, the amendment did not really change the defendant. The original complaint described the publisher and its offices, and Time's agent's contemporaneous letter acknowledged that the papers were directed to Time as publisher of Fortune. Adding the corporate name merely corrected the description, rather than bringing in a new party. The majority's special requirements for changing defendants therefore should not apply. There was no suggestion that the original wording confused Time or impaired its merits defense. (32–36.)

Second, even if a party change occurred, Stevens read the period for commencing an action against the defendant to include both timely filing and the allowed time to serve process. The plaintiffs had filed ten days before limitations expired, and Time received the original complaints well before the historical 120-day service deadline. It would have learned nothing sooner or differently if the initial caption had contained the corporate name. The majority demanded earlier notice from a misnamed defendant than an accurately named defendant would necessarily receive. Footnote 4 develops this interpretation through Ingram and the committee reporter's commentary. (33–34, 36–38 & nn.2–4.)

The dissent also relied on the 1966 amendment's liberalizing history: government cases with naming errors had prompted language intended to permit correction, rather than make correction harder. Stevens analogized the mistake to a misspelling and criticized sacrificing a merits decision without prejudice. His final note observes that famous Supreme Court cases themselves contain misspelled party names. These were dissenting arguments, not the Court's adopted interpretation. (38–40 & nn.5–7.)

The exact disposition was **affirmance of the Third Circuit judgments**, leaving the dismissals intact. The Court did not adjudicate whether the article was libelous. The assigned account describes application of the federal rule rather than New Jersey's more permissive approach. The actual opinion's developed analysis addresses old Rule 15(c)'s timing and the proposed identity-of-interest exception; it does not develop a separate Erie/Hanna analysis comparing the state relation-back rule. (32; assigned printed 696.)

The 1991 Committee Note expressly identifies two changes relevant here: access to a more forgiving applicable limitations law and a notice period linked to the service rule rather than the limitations deadline. The verified text now appears in Rule 15(c)(1)(A) and (C). Those provisions are later rule developments, not propositions the 1986 majority adopted.
