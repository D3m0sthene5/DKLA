# SEC v. Chenery Corp. (Chenery II)

**Course:** Legislation and the Regulatory State
**ID:** LRS-CB-0544
**Review:** Full primary and textbook comparison; date-source attribution corrected.

## Citation

Securities and Exchange Commission v. Chenery Corp. (Chenery II), 332 U.S. 194 (1947), decided June 23, 1947. Justice Murphy delivered the Court's opinion. Justice Jackson's dissent, joined by Justice Frankfurter, was filed October 6, 1947. Assigned excerpt: casebook pp. 544–549, October second scan, PDF pp. 68–73. Full judicial opinion: [Justia reproduction](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/332/194/); [original U.S. Reports scan](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep332/usrep332194/usrep332194.pdf). Reporter page references below distinguish the Court's opinion, pp. 196–209, from the dissent, pp. 209–218.

## Parties

The Securities and Exchange Commission, petitioner, sought to sustain its renewed refusal to give management-held preferred shares the same treatment as publicly held preferred shares in a utility holding company's reorganization. The respondents were the management interests associated with Federal Water Service Corporation whose stock purchases had produced the dispute in Chenery I. After the first remand, Federal Water and Gas Corporation, the surviving reorganized company, applied for an amendment authorizing distribution of new common shares to management, and the individual management interests intervened in support. Their immediate objective was participation in the reorganized company's equity and voting power on the same basis as the public preferred shareholders. 332 U.S. at 197–199.

## Material Facts

During the consideration of successive reorganization plans under the Public Utility Holding Company Act of 1935, Federal's officers, directors, and controlling shareholders bought preferred stock in the open market. The fourth plan would have converted preferred shares into common shares of the reorganized enterprise. Management's purchases would therefore have yielded more than ten percent of the new common stock. Management acknowledged that it sought to protect its position in the new company. The Court expressly described the purchases as involving neither fraud nor inadequate disclosure. Those facts matter because the SEC's eventual rationale depended on structural conflicts of interest, rather than a finding that these purchasers had actually defrauded anyone. Id. at 197–198.

The SEC had originally required different treatment of the management-acquired shares. Public holders could receive new equity, while management would recover the purchase price with an additional payment instead of obtaining the corresponding appreciation and control. The opinion's introductory account describes the earlier plan in terms of accumulated dividends; Jackson's account of the operative recovery describes cost plus four-percent interest. These descriptions concern the transaction's payment history and should not be collapsed into a claim that management received the full market or reorganization value of its stock. Id. at 198, 210–211 (Jackson, J., dissenting).

The renewed proceeding involved the same basic purchases, but the SEC supplied a different legal and administrative justification. It emphasized management's influence over operating policies, accounting, financial disclosure, dividends, the timing and terms of proposed plans, and communications with the Commission. Purchasing stock for personal gain while exercising those powers could place management's interest in a low acquisition price and favorable allocation of new securities in conflict with its responsibility to investors generally. The agency did not reverse its earlier acceptance of management's personal good faith. Id. at 204–207.

## Procedural History

In Chenery I, the Supreme Court held that the first SEC order could not stand on the judicial principles of equity that the agency had invoked. It directed further proceedings before the Commission and did not decide whether the same result could be justified by a properly exercised administrative judgment under the Act. 332 U.S. at 196–198, 200–201, discussing 318 U.S. 80.

Following remand, the surviving corporation applied to amend the plan so that management would receive new common shares on the basis of its old preferred holdings. The Commission denied the application, this time expressly invoking the statutory standards governing fairness, investor protection, and the distribution of voting power. The original U.S. Reports scan identifies the renewed order as issued February 8, 1945, Holding Company Act Release No. 5584, and the assigned excerpt prints the same date. Justia’s judicial reproduction instead prints February 7. The discrepancy is recorded as a difference between reproductions; this brief does not independently settle the underlying SEC order’s date. 332 U.S. at 199; casebook p. 546.

The Court of Appeals reversed, 154 F.2d 6, interpreting the first Supreme Court decision as foreclosing this renewed refusal. The Supreme Court granted review to decide whether the new SEC order was permissible under its earlier mandate and the Commission's statutory authority. This is review of a new agency determination made after remand, not an attempt by litigation counsel to supply a substitute rationale while leaving the original administrative decision untouched. 332 U.S. at 196, 199–200.

## Issue

After a reviewing court rejects an agency's stated legal rationale and remands without resolving the underlying policy question, may the agency reach the same result on a newly articulated, statutorily authorized administrative rationale? More specifically, could the SEC announce and apply a standard against management profiting from these reorganization-period purchases through this adjudication, despite the absence of an earlier general rule and the resulting retroactive effect? If that method was permissible, did the SEC's conflict-of-interest explanation provide a rational and statutory foundation for this particular order? Id. at 196, 199–204, 207–209.

## Rule and Holding

The Court reaffirmed the rule that an administrative decision must be reviewed on the grounds the agency actually invoked, stated clearly enough for judicial examination. It then held that the first remand left the SEC free, and required it, to exercise its delegated function afresh. The failure of the original rationale did not create a vested right to the proposed parity treatment. Id. at 196–197, 200–201.

An agency's choice between developing policy through a general rule and through an individual adjudication lies primarily within its informed discretion. Prospective rulemaking is often desirable, but novelty, limited experience, and highly variable circumstances can justify case-by-case development. An adjudication's retroactive effect is not automatically fatal; its adverse effect must be weighed against the statutory harm that would follow from allowing the challenged arrangement. Id. at 202–204.

Applying those principles, the Court sustained the renewed SEC order. The agency had grounded its decision in the Act, explained why management's purchase program created conflicts relevant to investor protection, and applied that explanation to uncontested facts. The Court found no plain abuse of the Commission's discretion. The holding does not dispense with statutory authorization or reasoned explanation whenever an agency invokes expertise. Id. at 204–209.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals, thereby sustaining the Commission's renewed order denying approval of the proposed amendment. The operative result was that management did not obtain parity conversion into the new common stock under that application. The Court did not order another remand merely to improve the explanation, and it did not direct approval of management's proposed distribution. The disposition is stated at 332 U.S. 209. Justice Burton concurred in the result; Chief Justice Vinson and Justice Douglas did not participate.

## Reasoning

1. The first decision controlled the adequacy of the original grounds, not the eventual answer to every permissible administrative question. The initial SEC order had rested on an incorrect understanding of judicial equity precedents. That defect required correction, but the first Court had reserved what an informed exercise of the agency's statutory judgment might establish. On remand, the SEC still had to decide whether the proposed treatment met the Act's requirements. Treating the prior legal error as an entitlement to approval would have displaced the Commission's continuing responsibility. Id. at 196–201.

2. Reconsideration by the agency respects the allocation of decision-making authority. Chenery I prohibited the reviewing court from inventing an administrative rationale. Here the Commission itself reexamined the proposal, identified the pertinent statutory standards, and explained its judgment. The reviewing court therefore had an actual agency rationale to test. The requirement of intelligible grounds remained in force throughout; the change was that the renewed order satisfied it. Id. at 196–197, 199–200.

3. The absence of a preexisting general rule did not relieve the Commission of its duty to decide the application. Approval or denial of the requested amendment necessarily took the form of an order addressing a particular proposal. An agency could not knowingly approve a transaction contrary to the statutory standards simply because it had not anticipated the precise problem in advance. The Court therefore rejected a rigid rule under which every new administrative principle must first be promulgated prospectively. Id. at 201–202.

4. The choice of procedure depends on the agency's task and experience. The Court acknowledged the advantages of filling statutory gaps through rules announced for future application. It nevertheless identified situations in which adjudication may be appropriate: an unforeseen problem requiring an immediate answer, insufficient experience to formulate a durable rule, or circumstances too specialized and variable for a uniform prescription. Those reasons support informed choice, rather than an unrestricted power to avoid whatever rulemaking procedures Congress has independently required. Id. at 202–203.

5. Retroactivity required evaluation, not automatic invalidation. The new standard affected the gains management expected from purchases already made. For the Court, that consequence had to be compared with the injury to the statutory design that would result from permitting those gains and the associated control. The opinion likened the problem to a first-impression judicial decision, whose application also reaches conduct preceding the announcement of its governing principle. Its holding concerned this adjudicative application; it did not establish a general power to promulgate retroactive legislative rules or impose any conceivable retrospective sanction. Id. at 203–204.

6. The new SEC rationale addressed institutional incentives and opportunities. Management could influence both the market for the old securities and the distribution of the new ones. In a voluntary reorganization, it also represented shareholders, proposed and amended plans, and obtained information concerning the Commission's likely response. A personal purchasing program created incentives to buy cheaply, preserve or enlarge control, and shape the process to achieve those objectives. The Commission connected these conflicts to the Act's requirements of fair treatment and an equitable allocation of voting power. Id. at 204–206.

7. A prophylactic response could be justified without proving dishonesty in each transaction. The SEC reasoned that the effects of conflicting interests might occur without conscious misconduct, while investigation of motives could be difficult, costly, and inconclusive. Management's good faith therefore did not eliminate the danger the agency was addressing. The Court accepted that reasoning as a permissible basis for protection of investors under the broad statutory standards. This was an administrative policy judgment about conflicts; the Court did not find a previously undiscovered fraud or reinterpret the purchases as an established common-law breach. Id. at 206–208.

8. The order had a sufficient connection to both the record and the statute. Management admitted that the purchases sought the voting power and investment advantages associated with reorganization. The facts were uncontested, and the SEC explained why the dangers it identified applied to them. The Court consequently found a rational statutory foundation, substantial evidentiary support, and no plain abuse of discretion. It declined to substitute its own view of the policy's wisdom for the Commission's authorized judgment. Id. at 207–209.

## Separate Opinions

Justice Burton concurred in the result without an accompanying explanation. His vote supports the judgment, but the reported notation supplies no basis for assigning him agreement with each proposition in the Court's reasoning. Chief Justice Vinson and Justice Douglas took no part. Id. at 209.

Justice Jackson, joined by Justice Frankfurter, dissented. Their disagreement was announced with the June decision; the explanatory dissent was filed October 6, 1947. Jackson regarded the renewed approval of the SEC's position as an abandonment of the protection Chenery I had supplied. The underlying conduct, facts, statutory language, and economic deprivation had not changed. In his view, the Commission had repackaged the same result as administrative expertise after its asserted legal basis had failed. Id. at 209–213 (Jackson, J., dissenting).

The dissent emphasized that the owners had acquired their stock lawfully, without an announced prohibition, and were now denied its ordinary appreciation while receiving only a limited recovery. General authority to administer reorganizations did not, for Jackson, provide an ascertainable standard authorizing that deprivation. He accepted that administrative experience deserved attention within lawful bounds, but rejected its use as a substitute for the law defining those bounds. Id. at 210–215.

Jackson also challenged the majority's reliance on accumulated expertise concerning a problem it described as newly encountered. More fundamentally, he objected to announcing a prohibition after the transaction and applying it to the very parties whose conduct had been lawful under the standards previously in place. The dissent treated fair notice, constraints on retroactive lawmaking, and meaningful judicial review as the controlling concerns. Its characterization of the Commission's action as decision without law is the dissent's critique; the majority found statutory authority and an adequately explained administrative judgment. Id. at 214–218.

## Notes

Read Chenery I and Chenery II as successive proceedings with different agency explanations and different judicial dispositions. The first requires review of the agency's actual grounds; the second permits the agency on remand to exercise discretion that the reviewing court could not exercise for it. A lawful new decision after remand should therefore be distinguished from a lawyer's post hoc defense of an unchanged decision. Id. at 196–201.

For a problem involving policy announced through adjudication, identify the governing statute, the agency's procedural choice, the actual explanation, the reliance or retroactivity concern, and the connection between the adopted principle and the specific facts. The opinion's preference for prospective rulemaking and its allowance for case-by-case development both matter. Neither should be omitted from the statement of the rule. Id. at 202–204.

The casebook abbreviates the Commission's substantive rationale and the Court's review of it with an editorial bracket on p. 548. The full opinion supplies the conflict-of-interest analysis summarized above. The casebook's ensuing discussion of Wyman-Gordon is editorial comparative material, not an additional holding of Chenery II. The February 7/February 8 order-date discrepancy and the differing descriptions of the payment formula are preserved explicitly above. This brief describes the 1947 decision and does not certify the present status of every proposition associated with it.
