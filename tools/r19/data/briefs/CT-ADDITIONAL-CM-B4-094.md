# Kornblut v. Chevron Oil Co. — Court of Appeals
48 N.Y.2d 853, 400 N.E.2d 368 (1979) | Contracts

## Holding and judgment
On November 27, 1979, the New York Court of Appeals affirmed, with costs, the order dismissing the widow's action against Chevron and its roadside-service contractor. The court adopted the reasons stated in Hopkins's Appellate Division opinion rather than issue a new extended majority analysis. Cooke, Jasen, Gabrielli, Jones, Wachtler, and Meyer concurred; Fuchsberg dissented and would reverse and remit for appellate consideration of the facts. The result was six to one. This highest-court judgment is distinct from the 1978 reversal of the trial judgment. (853–857.)

## Case reaching the highest court
The underlying action concerned a disabled motorist who received no contracted assistance after repeated reports and assurances, changed his tire in extreme summer heat, suffered myocardial infarction, and died twenty-eight days later. The jury awarded his widow damages under a third-party-beneficiary contract theory. The Appellate Division found the service evidence sufficient to support breach but held the personal-injury and death loss outside the contemplated contractual responsibility, reversed, and dismissed. These underlying details and the earlier court's reasoning were read in the complete 1978 report, not supplied by an invented fresh factual finding in the 1979 memorandum. (62 A.D.2d 832–838.)

The Court of Appeals' majority expressly affirmed for Hopkins's reasons. Those reasons recognized particular contractual benefits to motorists, including regulated charges and overcharge refunds, while rejecting consequential injury recovery under the contractual foreseeability analysis. Martuscello's separate appellate concurrence was not the opinion named as the basis for adoption. The majority therefore was not affirming on a statement that motorists lacked beneficiary rights under every clause. (48 N.Y.2d 854; 62 A.D.2d 835–839.)

## Fuchsberg's agency-and-undertaking approach
Fuchsberg viewed the issue through the circumstances of an exclusive protective undertaking and a prolonged, knowing failure to respond. He invoked Restatement (Second) of Agency section 354: an agent undertaking action necessary to protect another could incur liability for physical harm caused by reliance and an unexcused failure to act creating a foreseeable unreasonable risk. He emphasized that contractual terms alone did not exhaust responsibility arising from undertaking protection and failing to provide it. This was his dissenting approach, not an additional holding of the court. (854–855.)

He examined five elements: necessary protective action, reliance, unexcused nonperformance, unreasonable risk, and awareness of that risk. A stranded vehicle on a limited-access high-speed highway created an evident need for protection. The exclusive service arrangement and thirty-minute response promise supported reliance; more than three hours without help was not the envisioned performance. Whether the failure was excusable belonged to the jury. Limited access, common vehicle breakdowns, and motorists lacking physical vigor could make self-help dangerous, while the contract's concern with avoiding danger to the traveling public supported awareness. (855–856.)

Fuchsberg also challenged rigid reliance on Moch, noting critical commentary and distinguishing the direct service here. Highway users received and paid for the roadside service themselves, whereas Moch concerned hydrant water furnished directly to the city. He considered contract, agency, and tort principles interrelated in a setting where departure from a protective contractual undertaking predictably could have tort consequences. In his view the plaintiff had proved a prima facie case. (856–857.)

## Proposed remedy versus actual result
Fuchsberg would have reversed the appealed order and remitted to the Appellate Division to consider the facts. He did not propose immediate entry of the jury judgment without that appellate consideration. His position did not prevail: the order remained affirmed with costs for the reasons adopted from Hopkins. The court neither awarded damages anew nor ordered the dissent's remittal. (854, 857.)
