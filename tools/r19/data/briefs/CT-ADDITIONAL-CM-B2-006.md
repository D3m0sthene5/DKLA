# Kajima/Ray Wilson v. Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority

23 Cal. 4th 305, 1 P.3d 63 (2000) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B2-006

## Bidding dispute and findings

MTA sought bids in April 1994 for construction of the Hollywood/Highland station and tunnels on the Los Angeles Metro Red Line. Tutor-Saliba submitted the lowest bid. Kajima protested that Tutor-Saliba had not met the disadvantaged-business-enterprise participation goal. MTA determined that none of the bids was responsive and exercised its statutory discretion to reject all bids. Tutor-Saliba sued over that rejection. The later damages decision did not hold that this first-round rejection itself entitled Kajima to reimbursement. 23 Cal. 4th at 308–309, 312.

MTA solicited new bids in November, with a DBE participation goal of thirty percent of the total bid. Kajima bid $68,912,089, Tutor-Saliba $69,887,867, and Kiewit-Shea $72,970,345. MTA nevertheless selected Tutor-Saliba, explaining that Kajima had failed the participation requirement. The difference came from their designation of the same firm, Manual Tejeda Trucking: Kajima described it as a broker, Tutor-Saliba as a subcontractor. MTA had an unwritten policy crediting only five percent of amounts assigned to brokers but one hundred percent of amounts assigned to subcontractors. Had Kajima received the same credit, it would have exceeded the thirty-percent goal. The judicial account establishes the participation dispute; it does not identify Kajima itself as a DBE. Id. at 309.

Kajima sought injunctive and writ relief and damages. Its requested amounts were $93,411 for first-round bid and protest expenses, $134,092 for second-round bid and protest expenses, $1,298,589 in unabsorbed overhead, and $1,544,034 in lost profit: $3,070,126 plus interest altogether. Following a bench trial, the court issued a peremptory writ requiring MTA to stop using the five-percent broker policy instead of evaluating the proposed subcontracted work. It awarded $44,869 in second-round bid expenses, $89,223 in second-round protest expenses, $300,000 in overhead, $350,000 in lost profits, and $139,829.74 in prejudgment interest, for a judgment of $923,921.74. These are adjudicated award amounts, distinct from the amounts requested. Id.

## Appellate posture and question

The Court of Appeal affirmed. It held the differential credit policy arbitrary, contrary to federal regulations, and an abuse of discretion; MTA did not challenge that determination in the Supreme Court. The Court of Appeal also approved lost profits as an available case-specific equitable remedy and held the overhead issue waived. On MTA's petition for review, the Supreme Court considered only the recoverability of bid preparation costs and lost profits. Review of bid protest costs and overhead was waived. The resulting decision therefore supplies no adjudicated rule approving or rejecting those other categories. Justice Brown wrote for a unanimous seven-member court, with George, Mosk, Kennard, Baxter, Werdegar, and Chin concurring. No separate writing appears. Id. at 309–310, 321.

The question was whether a lowest responsible bidder wrongfully denied a public contract could obtain monetary relief from the public entity and, if so, whether that relief included the profit expected from performing the contract. The court addressed the situation in which effective injunctive relief was no longer available. Setting aside an improper award was ordinarily the most effective enforcement method, but by the time the wrong was established the project could already be substantially or completely performed, apparently the situation here. Id. at 313 & n.1.

## Basis for monetary relief

The public contracting statutes did not expressly address a disappointed bidder's damages remedy. The court declined to treat this silence as necessarily prohibitory: it could equally reflect an assumption that existing promissory-estoppel principles supplied relief. California courts had long authorized writ relief against improper awards without express codification. The statutes governing claims against MTA incorporated law that included judicial decisions and preserved liability based on contract. The court consequently found no statutory barrier to a valid equitable theory of recovery. Id. at 313–314.

Promissory estoppel under Restatement § 90 protects a promise expected to induce, and actually inducing, action or forbearance where enforcement is necessary to avoid injustice; the remedy may be limited as justice requires. When a public entity solicits bids, it represents consistently with its statutory obligation that, if it awards the contract, the award will go to the lowest responsible bidder. Bid preparation expenses are incurred in reliance on that representation. MTA did not dispute that these circumstances appeared to establish the estoppel elements; Kajima conceded that the improper award supplied neither a tort claim nor a breach-of-contract claim. Id. at 310, 315 & n.2.

The fit with ordinary promissory estoppel was imperfect. The court was principally furthering the public policies behind competitive bidding, rather than enforcing a private contractual exchange. The entity retained discretion to reject all bids, so the lowest bidder had no absolute right to the contract. Earlier decisions denying reimbursement after a valid rejection of all bids remained distinguishable: that was an ordinary risk of pursuing public work, whereas this case involved an award to a higher bidder through an unlawful evaluation. Monetary relief for reasonable reliance encouraged proper challenges and deterred misconduct while serving competitive bidding's protection of the public against favoritism, improvidence, fraud, and misuse of funds. Id. at 312–315.

## Why preparation costs were recoverable but profits were not

Bid preparation costs were reasonably incurred before submission in reliance on fair statutory evaluation. Because bidding was secret and MTA could reject all bids, Kajima then knew neither whether it was the lowest responsible bidder nor whether any award would occur. Expected profits were not expenditures incurred in that reliance. They were also uncertain: a low bid might prove unprofitable through error or increased costs. Awarding an unperformed contract's expected profit could place the bidder in a better position than performance would have produced. Id. at 315–316.

Public policy independently limited the remedy. Competitive bidding primarily protected taxpayers and the public, although fair evaluation also benefited bidders. Paying the higher contractor and additionally paying an unperformed contract's profits would compound public loss and give the disappointed bidder a gain without performing work or accepting performance risks. Equity could not defeat the public policy it was meant to advance. The possibility of large profit awards could encourage opportunistic litigation and drain public resources; judicial caution was especially appropriate within an extensive statutory scheme. The court found Kajima's prediction that bidders would otherwise stop participating or challenging improper awards unpersuasive in light of existing writ litigation. Id. at 316–318.

Kajima proposed that payments to Tutor-Saliba under an allegedly void contract could probably be recouped, avoiding double payment. Neither that contract's validity nor MTA's power to recover its payments was before the court, so this speculation could not support the remedy. Nor did rules governing ordinary private breach-of-contract damages control: Kajima had no contract with MTA and sought an equitable remedy measured by the justice of the public consequences. Id. at 317–318.

The court surveyed other jurisdictions, many allowing preparation costs but not profits and some allowing no damages. It acknowledged limited contrary awards and dicta concerning bad faith, without adopting a bad-faith exception to its own public-works rule. The California cases relied on below involved private parties: C & K Engineering decided the equitable nature of the action and jury-trial entitlement, not damages, while Signal Hill involved reliance on a lease assignment and the defendant's resulting profits, including a constructive-trust theory. Neither resolved the competing public fiscal concerns here. This reasoning does not impose a general prohibition on lost profits in all private promissory-estoppel actions. Id. at 318–321 & nn.4–6.

## Exact result and assigned-source precision

The court held that reasonable bid preparation costs were recoverable under promissory estoppel for this wrongful public award, but expected profits were not. It **reversed the Court of Appeal's judgment and remanded to that court for proceedings consistent with its opinion**. It did not enter a newly calculated damages judgment, decide the waived protest-cost and overhead issues, or authorize recovery for every disappointed bid. Rehearing was denied August 23, 2000. Id. at 310, 315–321.
