# Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach

**Civil Procedure — 523 U.S. 26 (1998). Supreme Court of the United States, March 3, 1998. Justice Souter wrote for the Court. The judgment was unanimous; Justice Scalia joined the opinion except Part II-C, which discussed legislative history. No separate opinion was filed.**

## Why Lexecon’s Illinois suit ended up in Arizona

Lexecon, a law-and-economics consulting firm, and one of its principals had been defendants in litigation arising from the failure of Lincoln Savings and Loan. They were accused of giving banking regulators misleading reports about the financial condition of Lincoln Savings and its parent, American Continental Corporation. That litigation was transferred to the District of Arizona for coordinated pretrial proceedings under the federal multidistrict-litigation statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1407. The claims against Lexecon were dismissed in August 1992 under an agreement described as a resolution. 523 U.S. at 28–29.

Lexecon then brought a diversity suit in the Northern District of Illinois against the plaintiff-side law firms Milberg Weiss and Cotchett, Illston & Pitre. It alleged malicious prosecution, abuse of process, tortious interference, commercial disparagement, and defamation. Lexecon characterized the earlier claims as a meritless vendetta that had ended in its favor. Judge Bilby, who had handled the Arizona proceedings, issued an order describing the resolution differently; Lexecon appealed that order. The Supreme Court reported these competing characterizations as part of the procedural background, rather than deciding that Lexecon’s description was correct. Id. at 29.

The law firms sought transfer of Lexecon’s new suit to Arizona under § 1407. Although Judge Bilby recused himself, the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation transferred the case in June 1993 and assigned it to Judge Roll. The Panel identified shared factual questions concerning the earlier settlement, a large Arizona document depository, and the pending Ninth Circuit appeal. The appeal was later dismissed and the depository closed. The remaining Lincoln Savings litigation settled, with judgment entered in March 1994. Id. at 29–30.

Lexecon repeatedly asked Judge Roll to suggest that the Panel remand its suit to Illinois. The firms opposed remand and requested a transfer under § 1404(a) assigning the case to the Arizona court itself for trial. Judge Roll eventually dismissed or granted summary judgment on the claims except a defamation claim against Milberg, dismissed Milberg’s counterclaims, and assigned the surviving case to himself for trial. A separate Rule 54(b) judgment was entered for Cotchett. Id. at 30–31.

## Review posture and the preserved objection

Lexecon unsuccessfully sought mandamus from the Ninth Circuit to set aside the self-assignment and obtain remand. The appellate majority left the statutory question for direct appeal, reasoning that Lexecon could seek relief after a trial loss. Judge Kozinski dissented. Milberg then won judgment on the defamation claim at the Arizona trial. Lexecon appealed, again contesting the refusal to remand and the self-assignment, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed over another Kozinski dissent. Its majority relied on established practice, the Panel’s rules, and efficiency. Id. at 31–32.

Lexecon did not challenge the Arizona court’s subject-matter jurisdiction or allege an independent error in the conduct of trial. Its complaint was that the case had been tried in a district where the statutory MDL procedure did not permit the court to keep it over Lexecon’s objection. The Supreme Court granted review of that statutory transfer question. Id. at 32.

## Holding: pretrial consolidation cannot defeat mandatory remand

A district court receiving an action under § 1407(a) could not use § 1404(a) to assign that transferred action to itself for trial. Section 1407 allowed transfer for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings, but also directed the Panel to remand each transferred action to its original district at or before the conclusion of those proceedings unless the action had already terminated. The mandatory remand command controlled. Id. at 28, 32–40.

The Court acknowledged why respondents’ position initially looked plausible. The Panel’s Rule 14(b) expressly recognized such transfers, and courts had followed the practice for decades. A motion under § 1404 normally occurred before trial, and an MDL proceeding could coordinate related cases without literally consolidating identical claims. Those observations alone did not exclude the motion from the transferee court’s pretrial activities. Id. at 32–34.

The problem appeared when the statute was read as a whole. Self-assignment would prevent the Panel from obeying Congress’s separate command to remand. Respondents argued that the command governed the Panel rather than the district judge, but dividing the provisions that way would allow one statutory actor to disable another actor’s mandatory duty. The Panel’s rulemaking authority required consistency with Acts of Congress and could not authorize that result. Longstanding judicial practice could not overcome the statute. Id. at 34–37.

The Court also rejected the suggestion that Lexecon had lost its protection by asking the Arizona judge for a remand suggestion rather than moving directly before the Panel. The Panel’s own rules strongly discouraged remand without a suggestion from the transferee court. In any event, the judge simultaneously refused that suggestion and made the very self-assignment the Panel’s rules purported to permit. Note 1 rejects an inference of waiver from that sequence; it does not decide that the remand requirement can never be waived under other circumstances. Id. at 36–37 & n.1.

## Respondents’ alternative readings failed

Respondents argued that a § 1404 self-assignment terminated the case’s special status as an MDL action, thereby eliminating the remand duty. The Court answered that the statute referred to termination of the civil action itself. A dismissal or summary judgment could end the action; changing its asserted venue status could not. Id. at 37.

Section 1407(h), which expressly permitted transfer of certain Clayton Act actions for trial as well as pretrial proceedings, did not create an implied trial-transfer power under subsection (a). It was an independent source of authority. Indeed, the express trial provision showed that Congress knew how to authorize trial consolidation when it wanted to do so. Notes 2 and 3 cautioned that remand followed by an ordinary transfer would not always be available: § 1404 transfers remained subject to general venue restrictions that § 1407 transfers did not share. Id. at 37–39 & nn.2–3.

In Part II-C, Souter’s opinion examined legislative history and found no support for respondents. A statement that § 1404 remained available did not establish that the transferee court could invoke it; an originating court could consider an otherwise proper transfer after remand. Other legislative material described § 1407 as affecting pretrial proceedings rather than the place of trial. Scalia did not join this legislative-history discussion, but he joined the textual holding and the judgment. Id. at 39–40.

The Court therefore held the Panel’s self-assignment rule invalid. Any policy argument that keeping cases in the MDL court would be more efficient belonged before Congress. Note 4 expressly reserved whether § 1404, considered independently, permitted self-transfer despite its reference to an “other” district: § 1407 already resolved this case. Id. at 40–41 & n.4.

## Remedy and limits of the judgment

Milberg urged harmless error because Lexecon could have filed in Arizona originally and identified no independent trial defect. The Court held that argument insufficient. Congress had categorically protected the plaintiff’s entitlement to remand after pretrial proceedings; ignoring a continuously preserved objection would turn an impermissible trial into its own justification. The statutory interest in the prescribed venue was substantial even without a showing that Arizona produced an erroneous verdict on the merits. Id. at 41–43.

The Court distinguished *Caterpillar*: there, the jurisdictional defect associated with an improper removal had been cured before judgment. Here, the violation persisted through trial. Finality and efficiency could not excuse an uncorrected refusal to observe § 1407’s mandate. The Court **reversed the Ninth Circuit and remanded for further proceedings**, requiring relief from the improper trial venue rather than declaring Lexecon’s defamation claim successful. Id. at 42–43.

Not all prior work was undone. Note 6 expressly left Cotchett’s Rule 54(b) dismissal in place because it was a proper pretrial disposition reflecting an earlier merits decision. Note 5 declined to address prospective-only application, which had not been timely presented, or Milberg’s contention that the surviving defamation claim should have been dismissed before trial, which lay outside the question granted. Id. at 42–43 & nn.5–6.
