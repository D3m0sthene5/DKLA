# Harris v. Avery

**Civil Procedure · CP-077 · 5 Kan. 146 (Kan. 1869).** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, PDF pp. 377–378, printed pp. 720–722; the principal passage ends before “Note and Questions.” Justice Valentine wrote for a court in which all justices concurred.

## One encounter, two pleaded injuries

James Avery alleged that John Harris encountered him in Fort Scott, called him a thief in front of others, accused him of possessing a stolen horse, took that horse and kept it for four or five days, and arrested and confined him with felons in the county jail for approximately the same period. Avery sought relief through a petition pleading false imprisonment and slander. The question before the Supreme Court was whether he could litigate those two claims together. It was not whether the accusation was true, whether Harris ultimately owed damages, or whether the horse belonged to someone else. The court recounted the petition's allegations to decide a pleading objection. (Full opinion, opening three paragraphs.)

Harris demurred on the ground that the petition improperly joined several causes of action. The district court overruled that demurrer, and Harris sought appellate correction of that ruling. Avery's petition expressly alleged that both claims arose from the same transaction. The described encounter supported that allegation: the accusation, taking of the horse, and detention were parts of one episode rather than unrelated disputes assembled merely because the parties were the same.

The court thought it debatable whether these facts even constituted more than one cause of action under the code. Because nobody had asked it to resolve that question, it assumed two causes for purposes of deciding joinder. The case therefore does not establish that every event producing several legal theories necessarily comprises several causes of action. (Opening third paragraph.)

## The governing joinder provision

Section 89 of the Kansas code permitted legal and equitable causes to be joined within listed classes, including causes arising from the same transaction or transactions connected with the same subject of action. The assigned excerpt supplies a clear rendering of that statutory language, correcting an OCR defect in the archived full report. Harris's objection depended on preserving a distinction between injuries to the person and injuries to character: false imprisonment belongs to the former and slander to the latter. The court acknowledged that those distinct categories might prevent joining the claims if they arose from unrelated transactions. But the same-transaction category supplied an independent basis for joining them here. (Opinion, paragraph beginning “Section 89”; final two reasoning paragraphs.)

## Why the older forms of action did not control

Justice Valentine explained the old common-law rules to show why they were a poor guide to the code. Traditionally, joinder depended heavily on the form of action, the pleas available, and the judgment to be entered. Claims classified together could sometimes be combined despite having arisen at different places and times. Conversely, injuries from a single encounter could be separated if their procedural forms differed.

The opinion illustrates this with a counterfactual. Had Harris maliciously procured a warrant without probable cause and converted the horse, certain claims could have been brought together in an action on the case, including slander and trover. But the pleaded detention here involved no process. At common law an irregular detention without process would be pursued in trespass, while slander would be pursued in case. Their connection to the same event would not overcome that formal difference. The hypothetical warrant and conversion claims are explanations of the former system, not additional facts found or causes adjudicated in Avery's case. (Opinion, paragraphs beginning “In the action at bar” and “But if Harris arrested Avery.”)

The Kansas code abolished those forms of action. Its joinder provisions moved toward equity's objective of avoiding multiple suits and resolving as much of a controversy as practicable in one proceeding. Importing the old trespass-versus-case distinction would frustrate that change. The relevant question was therefore the relationship of the events under the code, rather than the historical writ through which a particular injury would have been presented. (Paragraph beginning “Our code has abolished.”)

Harris relied on early New York code decisions. The court doubted that they supported his reading and added that later New York decisions were contrary. It approved Robinson v. Flint's explanation that the same-transaction provision could unite legal claims, equitable claims, or both; it could also reach different transactions connected with the subject of the action. Thus the mixed character of Avery's injuries did not defeat joinder once the necessary transactional connection existed. This additional discussion appears in the full opinion and is omitted from the assigned excerpt. (Final reasoning paragraph beginning “It is claimed by counsel.”)

## Holding, judgment, and practical significance

**False imprisonment and slander could be joined because the petition showed that they arose from the same transaction. The order overruling Harris's demurrer was affirmed.** All justices concurred; the report contains no separate opinion or judicial footnotes. Avery could proceed with the combined petition, but he had not yet won a judgment establishing the alleged torts or awarding damages.

Harris shows a particular stage of procedural reform: a transactional code category could overcome distinctions between older forms of action, while transactional limits still mattered. The assigned materials subsequently discuss Federal Rule 18's different approach. Those editorial materials are not part of the court's 1869 holding, and this brief does not substitute modern federal rules for the historical Kansas code.
