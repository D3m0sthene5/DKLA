# Aluminum Company of America v. Essex Group, Inc.
499 F. Supp. 53 (W.D. Pa. 1980) | Contracts

## Court, governing law, and decision
On April 7, 1980, Judge Teitelbaum issued findings of fact and conclusions of law after trial in this diversity action. Pennsylvania conflicts rules required enforcement of the parties' Indiana choice-of-law clause. For issues not settled by Indiana authority, the district court predicted how Indiana courts would decide; its discussion therefore is not an Indiana Supreme Court holding. ALCOA obtained equitable modification of the pricing formula on count one. Its alleged oral-modification and contractual-termination claims failed, and both parts of Essex's counterclaim were dismissed. The opinion concludes that an appropriate order will issue, rather than reproducing a separate later judgment. (53, 55–60, 81, 84–89, 93.)

## Negotiated bargain and failure of the price index
Essex wanted a secure long-term aluminum supply to support its expansion into aluminum wire manufacturing and compete with integrated producers. After negotiations beginning in spring 1967, the parties made the Molten Metal Agreement on December 26. Essex would supply alumina, ALCOA would convert it to molten aluminum at its Warrick, Indiana, works, and Essex would collect the output. The agreement ran through 1983, with Essex entitled to extend it through 1988. This was a toll-conversion service arrangement, not an outright sale of ALCOA's aluminum. (56–58, 81–84.)

The initial charge was fifteen cents per pound: five cents for a demand component linked to a construction-cost index, four cents fixed, three cents for non-labor production costs linked to the Wholesale Price Index–Industrial Commodities, and three cents for labor linked to ALCOA's local hourly labor costs. The overall price could not exceed sixty-five percent of a specified published aluminum market price. ALCOA devised the formula with economist Alan Greenspan's assistance, examined how the non-labor index had tracked its costs, and sought a stable return near four cents per pound. The court found that both parties contemplated ordinary variation yielding roughly one to seven cents per pound, rather than a guaranteed four-cent return every year. Essex examined the indices' stability before accepting them. (58, 63–65, 68–70.)

Starting in 1973, oil-price increases associated with OPEC and unexpected pollution-control costs sharply increased electricity costs, the principal non-labor input in smelting. The non-labor index did not track that increase. Table I shows annual profits turning into increasingly serious out-of-pocket losses in 1976–1978, with a temporary surcharge reducing those losses. By 1978 the table records a loss of 10.484 cents per pound, and a larger loss without the surcharge. Meanwhile Essex could obtain converted metal at a fraction of market value and profit substantially from reselling surplus metal. (58–59 and Table I.)

The court projected losses exceeding $75 million during the remaining potential contract term absent relief or an unforeseeable economic change. Elsewhere, accounting for earlier profits, it found losses exceeding $60 million over the agreement's entire life. These are projections of different periods, not two awarded damages amounts. Essex noted that ALCOA had still earned about $9 million cumulatively by trial and challenged both materiality and the lack of expert predictions. The court relied on proven recent losses and a continuation of present economic conditions, rather than treating speculative lost market profits as the basis for relief. (59, 65–66 & nn. 6–8, 73.)

## Mutual mistake and allocation of a bounded risk
ALCOA sought relief on several theories; the court first held that mutual mistake warranted a remedy. Essex argued that the index's failure was an erroneous forecast of future economic events, that any mistake was ALCOA's alone, and that ALCOA had accepted the risk by drafting an indexed price without a protective floor. The court distinguished the parties' actual assumption about the index's capacity from an unprotected prediction about future profits. It characterized the assumption as a present actuarial error: the parties had selected an elaborate risk-limiting device believing it would track costs within a tolerable range, but its capacity to do so was mistaken. (60–64.)

Essex's desire to maximize its own bargain did not make the error unilateral. Its chairman understood the index's function as protecting ALCOA against cost fluctuations, even though he had no altruistic interest in ALCOA's prosperity. Mutual mistake concerned their shared understanding of a material fact, not identical commercial motives. The assumption was basic to this unusually long agreement and its failure seriously distorted the exchange. (64–66.)

Risk allocation remained central. The court recognized express allocation, trade usage or common understandings, reasoned judicial allocation when neither governs, and contracting in conscious ignorance. An indexed price, ALCOA's sophistication, its drafting role, and the omitted floor did make it bear ordinary variation. But the court refused to infer that it had assumed every possible deviation, regardless of scale or probability. The elaborate indices, ceiling, favored-customer term, force-majeure clause, and extensive negotiations showed an effort to limit risks. The court found even exceptionally prudent parties would not have anticipated needing an additional floor to secure their objectives. Their calculated acceptance of a limited range differed from a limitless gamble in conscious ignorance. (64 n. 5, 66–70.)

The opinion discusses then-current Restatement drafts, whose section numbers differ from the final Restatement numbering used in the casebook. Its extended treatment of deleting the word “existing” from the mistake definition also acknowledges a comment excluding erroneous future predictions. The casebook's challenge to whether this genuinely was a present mistake is an editorial question; the district court's characterization is the judicial holding described here. (60–64, 70–71; assigned 1158–1159.)

## Impracticability and frustration as additional grounds
The court separately concluded that ALCOA qualified for relief through impracticability and frustration. These doctrines share mistake's concern with basic assumptions and risk allocation, but focus on severe hardship. Increased cost ordinarily is part of contractual risk; the court required expense well beyond ordinary variation and a loss not fairly within the undertaking. It found ALCOA's severe out-of-pocket losses, extraordinary index divergence, and absence of fault met those requirements. It distinguished earlier cases denying relief based on their smaller or insufficiently proven losses and different contractual allocation of foreseeable risks. (70–76 & nn. 11–18.)

Impracticability addressed the extreme expense of ALCOA's own performance. Frustration addressed its principal purpose of earning money rather than sustaining serious losses. The court recognized that many frustration illustrations involve more specific purposes and that business leases commonly leave the risk of commercial failure with tenants. It nevertheless declined a categorical prohibition on frustration relief where a profit objective is severely defeated. Here the gravity of loss and the negotiated limitation of risk justified relief. This is a fact-specific conclusion of this court, not a rule that any lost profit frustrates a contract. The concluding discussion expressly cautions that merely losing the opportunity to sell at a higher market price ordinarily is insufficient. (73, 76–78, 89–93.)

## Exact modification and its retained ceiling
Essex correctly argued that traditional reformation ordinarily corrects a writing that inaccurately records an antecedent agreement. The court acknowledged that this case did not fit that category. It relied instead on equitable restitution and modification to preserve the parties' exchange and avoid unjust enrichment. Rescission would give ALCOA a market-price windfall and deprive Essex of its secure supply; complete enforcement would put the extraordinary loss on ALCOA. Continuing performance under an adjusted price best preserved both parties' legitimate expectations. (78–79 & n. 21.)

The remedy did not replace the entire bargain with uncapped actual cost plus profit. The price per pound is the lesser of A and B. A is the original contractual ceiling. B is the greater of B1, the original indexed contract price, and B2, a price calculated to yield ALCOA one cent per pound under the accounting methods used for its exhibit 431. Thus the original index remains operative when it yields more than B2, and the ceiling still controls even if it leaves ALCOA with a loss. The parties agreed during trial that the ceiling was an appropriate limitation if relief was granted, and ALCOA did not challenge it on the same assumption-based theory. (79–80.)

B2 is calculated quarterly. When it applies, interim invoices use the most recent quarterly computation; the next computation adjusts the prior quarter retroactively through refunds or additional bills. ALCOA must maintain detailed supporting records until two years after termination and supply Essex quarterly B2 statements whether or not B2 controls. The court allowed protection of underlying trade-secret cost information while requiring record retention to make the remedy enforceable. (80 & n. 22.)

## Oral amendment, termination, and delivery counterclaims
Count two alleged that company leaders orally replaced the non-labor index with actual costs at a July 21, 1975 meeting. ALCOA's witness recalled acceptance, whereas Essex's recalled only agreement to consider the proposal. The court found both accounts credible but ALCOA had not carried its preponderance burden. It found no proven modification and therefore did not decide whether the statute of frauds would defeat one. ALCOA obtained neither discharge nor its requested damages on this theory. (56–57, 80–81 & nn. 23–28.)

Count three invoked a side-letter clause allowing termination if a final court determination classified the agreement as a sale of goods. The court found a genuine service arrangement: Essex supplied and owned the alumina, bore its taxes and transportation risks, and dealt separately with the Australian alumina supplier despite ALCOA's majority ownership. The parties intentionally kept the supply and conversion arrangements distinct. Independently, estoppel by contract prevented ALCOA from contradicting the side letter's settled service characterization to trigger cancellation; the contemplated threat had been a third party's Robinson-Patman challenge. The pricing mistake did not unsettle that separate characterization. (81–85 & n. 29.)

Essex's first counterclaim concerned reduced deliveries during 1977–1978. Extreme cold, generator failures, and coal-supply and related labor difficulties interrupted the designated Warrick source. Section 24 excused performance to the extent affected. ALCOA reduced deliveries proportionately to lost operating capacity, had notified Essex, resumed deliveries as conditions improved, and allocated the reduction equally between Essex and its own ingot operation. Other ALCOA plants' metal inventories did not displace the agreed exclusive source. Prior performance also supported proportionate allocation. The counterclaim was dismissed. (85–87 & nn. 30–33.)

The second counterclaim challenged ALCOA's 1979 fifteen-percent reduction. The court construed the agreement's tolerance as two-way: Essex could nominate a quantity within fifteen percent of the nominal amount, and ALCOA could deliver within fifteen percent of the nomination, subject to the governing nominal limits. Negotiations had specifically rejected Essex's proposed sole discretion. ALCOA's delayed use of the option reflected an effort to negotiate the larger pricing dispute, not an admission that no option existed. Its reduction could restore deliveries to nominal amounts, and this counterclaim also was dismissed. (87–89.)
