# Reasor-Hill Corp. v. Harrison

**220 Ark. 521, 249 S.W.2d 994 (1952) · Civil Procedure · CP-040**

The Arkansas Supreme Court refused to prohibit an Arkansas trial court from hearing a damages claim for injury to a growing cotton crop in Missouri. Although most American jurisdictions then treated injury to foreign land as a local action beyond their courts' jurisdiction, the majority rejected that rule for this interstate controversy. Its central concern was that the Arkansas manufacturer could otherwise avoid any remedy by remaining outside Missouri. The dissent considered the departure inconsistent with precedent, statutory localization of land actions, and state sovereignty. The court decided the availability of an Arkansas forum, without finding that the insecticide was defective or that the manufacturer owed damages.

Planters Flying Service sued D. M. Barton in Mississippi County, Arkansas, to collect an account for spraying insecticide on his Missouri cotton crop. Barton answered that the service had damaged his growing crop with adulterated insecticide. He also filed a cross-complaint against Reasor-Hill, an Arkansas insecticide manufacturer, alleging negligence in marketing a chemical unsuitable for cotton. Reasor-Hill was not authorized to do business in Missouri. It moved to dismiss the cross-complaint because the alleged injury concerned Missouri real property. After the circuit court denied dismissal, Reasor-Hill sought a writ of prohibition from the Arkansas Supreme Court. (220 Ark. at 521–22.)

The majority framed a question of first impression in Arkansas: could its courts entertain a claim for injury to real property situated in another state? The petitioner relied on near-unanimous American authority to the contrary, Minnesota being the stated exception. The opposing position emphasized that Barton could not serve Reasor-Hill in Missouri. Without an Arkansas action, the manufacturer could remain outside Missouri until limitations expired and thus escape liability altogether. The majority accepted that remedial problem and examined the rule's origins instead of treating the number of jurisdictions following it as decisive. (Id. at 522.)

Historically, English jurors were drawn from neighbors expected to know the parties and dispute. Actions therefore initially had to be brought where they arose. When disputes involved events in different communities, courts developed a distinction between transitory actions that could arise anywhere and local actions tied to particular land. The majority recognized advantages to localization among districts governed by one sovereign. Arkansas itself required actions for injury to real estate to be brought where the land lay, but permitted service throughout the state. That combination located an intrastate action without depriving the claimant of a remedy against an out-of-county defendant. (Id. at 522–23.)

The problem arose when a rule devised for locality was applied to land outside the sovereign's territory. English courts refused actions for injury to foreign land even if the defendant lived in England and could not be reached abroad. American courts followed the same approach between states. The majority discussed Livingston v. Jefferson, in which a Louisiana land-injury action could not be maintained in Virginia. Chief Justice Marshall had followed established English authority while recognizing the possibility of a right without a remedy. Reasor-Hill treated that dissatisfaction as a reason to reassess an inherited doctrine under American interstate conditions. (Id. at 523–24.)

The majority addressed three proposed justifications. First, difficulty determining foreign land title might matter when unfamiliar national legal systems were involved, but other states' statutes and decisions were accessible. Courts already resolved out-of-state title questions incidentally in transitory claims, such as conversion of a mature crop or breach of a land-purchase contract. Refusing the same title issue solely because another claim was labeled local was inconsistent. These examples were hypotheticals, rather than additional transactions established in Barton's case. (Id. at 524.)

Second, requiring a claimant to sue before the defendant left the situs was unrealistic between states with free movement. Here, airplane spraying could be completed and the pilot back in Arkansas before Barton discovered the damage and obtained an attachment. The court contrasted that setting with an independent nation's control of entry and departure. Third, protecting local citizens against outsiders' claims had no persuasive force in the Union: Arkansas should not become a refuge for people who damaged property across a state line. The majority invoked remedial justice, including the Arkansas constitutional assurance of a remedy for injuries, and observed the anomaly of allowing suit if the cotton were stolen outright but refusing suit because it was merely damaged while growing. (Id. at 525–26.)

Justice McFaddin, joined by Justice Ward, dissented. He agreed that the service's account action and Barton's defense to it were transitory matters properly before the court, but regarded the cross-complaint against the manufacturer as a separate cause involving Missouri realty. Western Union v. Bush had treated injury to a growing crop as injury to real estate and localized the action. For him, the majority accepted the real-property classification while abandoning its necessary jurisdictional consequence. (Id. at 526–27; dissent nn.1–3.)

McFaddin also answered the majority's policy reasons. A large Supreme Court library did not establish that trial courts could reliably decide another state's land law; his fourth footnote emphasized specialized knowledge of Texas land litigation. He warned that treating the asserted power broadly could lead to adjudicating ejectment or title to foreign land, without requiring the sister state to recognize the judgment. States remained sovereign, so he rejected the distinction between interstate and international disputes. Finally, whether to remove a refuge from liability was a legislative question. He preferred the longstanding local-action rule and considered judicial revision an improper substitution of policy for enacted law and precedent. The remaining dissent notes supplied sovereignty, conflict-of-laws references, and an illustration of adherence to established boundaries. (Id. at 527–29 & nn.4–7.)

The writ was denied, allowing the cross-complaint to remain before the circuit court. Justice George Rose Smith wrote the majority opinion. The decision permits this damages action for foreign land injury; it should not be expanded into a holding that Arkansas courts can directly transfer title or possession of Missouri land. It records Arkansas's historical departure from the prevailing doctrine without asserting that every jurisdiction followed it or that later federal local-action rules were resolved here.
