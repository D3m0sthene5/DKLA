# Kansas v. Nebraska
574 U.S. 445 (2015) | Contracts

## Decision and judicial alignment

Exercising original jurisdiction over an interstate-water dispute, the Supreme Court overruled the parties' exceptions and adopted the Special Master's recommendations. Nebraska owed Kansas $3.7 million for losses from excess water use and an additional $1.8 million in partial disgorgement. The Court denied Kansas's requested injunction and approved changes to technical accounting procedures that improperly charged Nebraska for imported water. These were remedies to enforce a congressionally approved compact, not a general license to rewrite private contracts or award profits whenever breach proves advantageous. (574 U.S. at 452–456, 464–475.)

Justice Kagan wrote for Justices Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer, and Sotomayor. Chief Justice Roberts joined Parts I and III, including the partial-disgorgement remedy, but opposed modification of the accounting procedures. He generally agreed with the equitable-power discussion in Part II without joining it. Justices Thomas, Scalia, and Alito opposed both disgorgement and modification, while agreeing that Nebraska knowingly breached and that an injunction was unnecessary. Roberts joined Part III of Thomas's separate opinion on modification. Scalia also wrote separately about the authority of modern Restatements. Thus the disgorgement result had six votes and the accounting modification five; the Court was not uniformly divided on all issues. (Id. at 447–448, 475–476, 493.)

## Facts and prior proceedings

The Republican River begins in Colorado, passes through Kansas into Nebraska, then returns to Kansas. Its basin covers approximately 24,900 square miles, including substantial farmland. Following Dust Bowl drought and flooding, federal reservoir and irrigation projects prompted the three states to negotiate an allocation agreement. Congress approved the Republican River Compact in 1943, making it federal law. It apportioned the basin's virgin water supply—water originating within the basin and undepleted by human activity—approximately 49 percent to Nebraska, 40 percent to Kansas, and 11 percent to Colorado. Their joint administration measured annual supply and consumption retrospectively. (Id. at 449–450.)

Kansas's 1998 original action challenged Nebraska's increased groundwater pumping. The Court accepted the proposition that pumping counted against a state's allotment to the extent it depleted stream flow. A 2002 settlement established more detailed accounting and compliance mechanisms. It ordinarily used five-year running averages, shortened to two-year averages in water-short periods, and employed technical Accounting Procedures and a Groundwater Model to calculate stream depletion. It expressly excluded imported water originating outside the basin. The settlement could implement the compact but could not change its allocation rights. (Id. at 450–451 & n.1.)

The first settlement-based compliance period exposed new disputes. Kansas alleged excessive Nebraska consumption in 2005–2006. Nebraska contended that the model improperly counted water imported from the Platte River Basin. Joint-administration proceedings and nonbinding arbitration failed, and Kansas returned to the Court for monetary and injunctive relief. Nebraska sought modification of the accounting procedures. After hearings and evidence, the Special Master found excess consumption of 70,869 acre-feet and a $3.7 million Kansas loss, recommended $1.8 million in additional disgorgement, rejected an injunction, and proposed correcting the imported-water calculations. Nebraska challenged the culpability finding and disgorgement; Kansas wanted a larger award and injunction and opposed the accounting change. Colorado supported one Nebraska exception, although Kansas and Nebraska remained the principal antagonists. (Id. at 452–453 & nn.2–3.)

## Knowing breach and equitable authority

The Court respected the Master's factual findings with a presumption of correctness while independently reviewing the record and retaining ultimate decisional responsibility. Its original jurisdiction over states is fundamentally equitable and substitutes for diplomatic settlement or resort to force. Once states negotiate a compact, the Court must enforce its terms rather than impose its preferred allocation. Within those terms, however, its remedial authority may protect the agreed rights and deter their abuse. Congressional approval further means that a breach violates federal law and implicates the public interest. The majority expressly disclaimed authority to alter the compact for its own notions of fairness. (Id. at 453–456 & nn.4–5.)

Both states accepted the amount of overuse and Kansas's $3.7 million loss; Nebraska agreed to pay that compensation. The dispute concerned additional relief. Nebraska's post-settlement response was too late and too limited: legislation lagged, initial management plans required only a five-percent pumping reduction without evidence that this would suffice, and local boards lacked effective accountability for compact compliance. Pumping reductions would not immediately restore stream flow, and prior annual calculations had repeatedly warned Nebraska of overuse. Drought did not excuse measures that could work only under unusually favorable rainfall. The Court agreed that Nebraska knowingly exposed Kansas to a substantial risk and recklessly disregarded its rights, while recognizing the Master's finding that Nebraska did not purposefully set out to breach. (Id. at 456–462 & nn.6–7.)

## Partial disgorgement and denial of injunction

Evidence showed that water was substantially more valuable on Nebraska's farmland than Kansas's, so Nebraska's gain exceeded Kansas's loss by likely several multiples. Paying compensation alone could leave an upstream state better off after depriving a downstream state of its water. The majority therefore approved partial disgorgement to stabilize the compact and discourage recurrence. Its discussion of the Restatement's deliberate-breach formulation did not erase the distinction between purposeful breach and mere negligence; it explained that conscious disregard of known rights could justify this remedy in the interstate-compact setting. Both states had agreed that replacing lost water would create difficult timing and delivery questions, supporting a monetary remedy. (Id. at 461–464 & n.8.)

The Court rejected Kansas's request for more disgorgement. Equity did not require all-or-nothing recovery of Nebraska's gain. Nebraska had improved its compliance after 2006: new forecasting, stronger reduction plans, and a state regulatory backstop enabled restrictions on local water use. The Master found those measures effective even in extraordinarily dry years if implemented in good faith. The Court acknowledged that the report did not fully explain why precisely $1.8 million was selected, but approved the balancing of past conduct, incentives, and recent compliance. For related reasons, Kansas had not shown a cognizable danger of recurrent violation warranting an injunction. Denial thus rested on subsequent compliance and deterrence, not a holding that profitable breach was legally permitted. (Id. at 464–467.)

## Imported-water accounting

During dry conditions, groundwater pumping could exhaust a stream's native flow; further pumping could not deplete water already absent. Imported Platte water might nevertheless flow through the otherwise dry channel, and the model incorrectly treated its depletion as consumption of basin water. In 2006 the procedures charged Nebraska with 7,797 acre-feet of imported water. The Master found that the designers intended to exclude that water and had not bargained away the unknown systematic error. The majority's footnote 9 distinguished intellectual awareness that the problem could arise from believing that the model would actually produce the material error. (Id. at 467–470 & n.9.)

The majority approved the Master's five-run formula to align the subsidiary procedures with the controlling compact and settlement. It invoked earlier corrections of technical arrangements implementing water compacts and emphasized that erroneous imported-water charging both reached water outside the compact and reduced Nebraska's allocated share of basin water. The Court was correcting implementation, in its view, rather than revising the compact itself. Kansas had proposed no workable alternative, shown no resulting inaccuracy, and agreed that further dispute-resolution proceedings would be futile. (Id. at 470–475 & n.10.)

## Separate opinions

Roberts accepted partial disgorgement but concluded that equitable power did not allow alteration of the agreed accounting procedures. Scalia joined Thomas and separately cautioned that modern Restatements may propose legal changes rather than describe existing law; section 39 could not simply be treated as authoritative current doctrine. (Id. at 475–476.)

Thomas would apply ordinary contract principles more strictly because sovereign agreements and congressional approval warranted restraint. He argued that disgorgement lacked support for a nondeliberate breach and that the $1.8 million figure was arbitrary rather than a reasonably certain measurement of profits. He also objected to using disgorgement to absorb litigation costs contrary to the ordinary rule on attorney's fees. (Id. at 476–487.)

On modification, Thomas distinguished a mutual mistake in expressing an actual agreement from a mistaken belief that the chosen methodology would work well. The states had intentionally selected these procedures, not the five-run formula; they had not made a drafting error. He thought rescission might address a basic-assumption mistake, although no state requested it, and disputed both the majority's reading of prior cases and its claim that imperfect modeling violated the compact. Those were dissenting objections, not limitations adopted by the majority. (Id. at 487–493.)

## Exact result and assigned account

The Court adopted all Master's recommendations and overruled the exceptions. The February opinion approves compensation, partial disgorgement, denial of an injunction, and accounting reform. This brief does not import the payment deadline or detailed appendix of a later decree into the opinion's judgment. (Id. at 453, 474–475.)
