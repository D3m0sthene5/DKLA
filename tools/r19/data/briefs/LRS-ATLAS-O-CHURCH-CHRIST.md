# Office of Communication of the United Church of Christ v. FCC
359 F.2d 994 (D.C. Cir. 1966) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and scope of intervention

On March 25, 1966, the D.C. Circuit reversed the FCC’s one-year renewal of television station WLBT’s license and remanded the record for hearings with public intervention, retaining jurisdiction. Judge Burger wrote for the panel of Burger, McGowan, and Tamm; no separate writing or dissent is reported. The court required the Commission to give standing to one or more of the appellants as responsible representatives of the listening public. It did not declare that every appellant, every viewer, or every self-described public-interest challenger had an unconditional right to intervene. It also required an evidentiary hearing before renewal because the record could not support the statutory public-interest finding without resolving the station’s qualifications and performance. (994, 997, 1005–1009.)

This decision addresses participation and hearings in the particular statutory license-renewal process. The court’s functional discussion of public-interest representation must be read with the FCC’s authority to regulate participation and with the hearing triggers in section 309(e). It is not a free-standing constitutional authorization for anyone to litigate any agency policy. (1005–1007.)

## Complaints and the pending renewal

WLBT operated in Jackson, Mississippi, under a license held by Lamar Life Broadcasting Company, which intervened to defend the renewal. Complaints extended back to 1955, when the station allegedly interrupted a network race-relations program featuring the NAACP’s General Counsel and substituted a cable-trouble notice. A 1957 complaint alleged that WLBT broadcast a pro-segregation program and denied requests for an opposing presentation. During the earlier renewal cycle the Commission deferred action, found Fairness Doctrine failures, but ultimately renewed because it considered the failures isolated. After the September 1962 disturbances associated with the University of Mississippi, further complaints alleged one-sided racial-integration coverage. In 1963 the FCC sought detailed station reports. WLBT filed the renewal application now at issue on March 3, 1964, while those matters were under consideration. These were the complaints and agency history recorded by the court, not judicial findings that every asserted incident had been proved at an evidentiary hearing. (998, 1007 n.26.)

The challengers were the national church’s Office of Communication, Aaron Henry, Robert L. T. Smith, and the United Church of Christ congregation at Tougaloo. The national body identified substantial membership in the station’s service area, and the local congregation was within it. Henry and Smith were Mississippi residents and television owners; one resided within WLBT’s prime service area. Both were civic and civil-rights leaders who had disputed the station’s refusal to provide response time, and Henry was president of the Mississippi NAACP. They sought intervention in their own behalf and for the viewing public. (998.)

Their petition challenged excessive commercials and entertainment, unbalanced treatment of controversial issues, and racial and religious discrimination. The report describes the affected racial population with the historical term “Negroes” and states that it comprised almost forty-five percent of the prime service area. The claims included inadequate and disrespectful coverage of that community and discrimination against local Catholic activities. A monitoring presentation covering a typical programming week particularized the allegations. The challengers asserted their own denied response opportunities, the interests of the community being excluded, and the entire audience’s interest in balanced coverage and public-interest operation. (998–999 & nn.3–5.)

## Conditional renewal instead of a hearing

The FCC denied intervention on the ground that the challengers lacked a direct, substantial invasion of a legally protected interest different from that of the public generally. It nevertheless said it ordinarily considered such complaints without recognizing formal party status, and claimed to have done so here. After considering the claims and station’s responses, it acknowledged serious public-interest issues and called the hearing question close. Instead of holding one, it granted a restricted renewal for one year beginning June 1, 1965, rather than the customary three years. (997, 999.)

The Commission thought a properly run station could contribute immediately to easing Jackson’s racial problems. It conditioned renewal on strict Fairness Doctrine compliance, conformity to station representations, immediate discussions with community leaders including the challengers, an end to discriminatory programming, and detailed reporting of its efforts. The renewal period was intended as a probationary opportunity for WLBT to demonstrate improved performance. The challengers argued that this history and acknowledged misconduct required a hearing; the Commission defended the short renewal as a policy choice within its responsibilities, asserting that it had effectively accepted the allegations and imposed the necessary corrective measures. (999–1000, 1007–1008 & n.27.)

The court reviewed the record before the Commission. Arguments from WLBT and the FCC that post-renewal performance had vindicated the experiment, and counterarguments that programming remained unbalanced, were not used to decide either intervention standing or whether renewal without a hearing had been proper. Note 7 expressly confines the decision to the administrative record. The court separately reserved whether post-June 1965 improvement could later count in WLBT’s favor. (1000 n.7, 1009 & n.30.)

## Why listener representatives could participate

The FCC treated economic injury and electrical interference as the only sufficient bases for standing. Burger explained that the prior cases had answered particular disputes rather than permanently closing the categories. The Commission had itself resisted those earlier expansions. Economic competitors were allowed to participate because they could bring public-interest objections to the agency’s attention; educational and nonprofit broadcasters likewise could have sufficient nonfinancial interests. The underlying purpose was vindication of the communications public interest rather than protection of new private broadcasting property rights. Note 8 records the parties’ common treatment of agency intervention and standing to appeal for this litigation; it is not an independent conclusion that every legal system always uses identical tests for the two. (1000–1001 & nn.8–10.)

Legislative concerns about a flood of protests focused on obstruction and selfish delay, not an express confinement to financial or interference injuries. Those recognized categories did not themselves guarantee that objections served the public. The court also drew analogies to consumer participation in rate, transportation, product, and conservation cases. Listeners had a direct and acute concern with station performance and a substantial collective investment in receiving equipment. The broadcaster’s status as neither an ordinary public utility nor a newspaper did not answer the question: it obtained free, exclusive use of a limited public resource subject to enforceable public obligations. (1001–1003 & nn.11–17.)

The Commission’s own duty to protect the public did not make outside participation unnecessary. It could not continuously monitor every licensee, and it had encouraged community attention to programming quality. Permitting responsible participants could help the agency act as an arbiter of developed claims rather than relying entirely on government-initiated accusations. Letters, complaints, and testimony allowed at the agency’s grace were inadequate substitutes for party participation where sustained efforts by these challengers had exposed matters earlier renewal proceedings left largely unaddressed. (1003–1004 & nn.18–22.)

Renewals made this assistance particularly valuable: actual past performance was available to compare with an applicant’s promises, and the viewing public might supply the only objectors. A sole local outlet had no rival station to object; even where rivals existed, competitive calculations or reciprocal restraint might discourage opposition. Those willing to bear the substantial cost of intervention could therefore be the only effective proponents of the listening public’s concerns. (1004–1005.)

## Limits and the FCC’s regulatory discretion

The court required some audience participation while allowing the Commission broad discretion to establish and apply responsible-participation rules. It could limit numbers, assess a proposed participant’s usefulness relative to others and the claims at issue, consolidate briefs and petitions, and screen captious, obstructive, or disguised private-interest protests. Potentially useful community representatives included civic groups, professional organizations, unions, churches, and educational bodies, but the court refused to supply a definitive catalogue. A petition still had to allege specific facts showing party-in-interest status and a prima facie inconsistency between the proposed grant and the public interest. (1005–1006.)

The narrow operative standing holding required recognition of one or more of these appellants as responsible representatives able to assert and prove their claims. The court did not decide that all were entitled to standing, and emphasized that public intervention existed to vindicate the broad public interest in the licensee’s performance of its trust. The FCC’s separate claim that accepting the allegations deprived the challengers of standing to appeal also failed: denial of the relief requested left them aggrieved. (1006–1007 & nn.24–25.)

## Why section 309(e) required a hearing

Section 309(e) required a hearing where there was a substantial and material factual question or the Commission was unable for any reason to make the necessary public-interest finding. The FCC’s assertion that it accepted the allegations could not end the analysis. Even assuming that eliminated a factual dispute, it still had to affirmatively find renewal in the public interest. A history of the alleged misconduct would preclude that finding without an adequate explanation and evidentiary basis. Past performance was the strongest criterion on renewal, and a licensee with an adverse record bore a heavy burden of reconciling renewal with the public interest. (1006–1008.)

The supposed finding was only that WLBT could serve the public if it changed its behavior. The imposed conditions restated obligations already inherent in a license, and the station denied the allegations rather than confessing past error and demonstrating willingness to reform. Hope for improvement was not evidence or a substitute for findings. Note 28 preserves the possibility of Commission experimentation and calculated renewal risks where a licensee admits its errors; this record was not such a case. Chairman Henry and Commissioner Cox had favored a hearing as of right within the Commission itself. (1007–1008 & nn.27–28.)

The record also lacked adequate support for the policy premise that immediate local need justified risking continued misconduct. The FCC had not considered alternatives such as an interim period with the other local station or allowing a different operator through temporary authorization procedures. The court discussed those possibilities to expose the gap in the agency’s reasoning, without deciding which alternative ultimately should prevail. A hearing exploring performance and qualifications was required. (1008–1009 & n.29.)

## Judgment, reservations, and source scope

The one-year renewal was erroneous. The court directed hearings on WLBT’s application with intervention consistent with its holding, remanded the record, and retained jurisdiction. It did not itself award the channel to another operator, finally deny every future WLBT application, or decide the effect of subsequent good performance. There is one panel opinion and no separate writing. (1009 & n.30.)
