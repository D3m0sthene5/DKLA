Ashcroft v. Iqbal
Citation
Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 129 S. Ct. 1937, 173 L. Ed. 2d 868 (2009). Supreme Court of the United
States. Argued December 10, 2008; decided May 18, 2009. Justice Kennedy wrote for the Court, joined by
Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Scalia, Thomas, and Alito. Justice Souter dissented, joined by Justices
Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer; Justice Breyer also filed a separate dissent.
Casebook pp. 628–639; source PDF pp. 332–337. Full judicial opinion.
Parties
Javaid Iqbal, a Pakistani Muslim detained after September 11, sued federal officials for damages. The
petitioners were former Attorney General John Ashcroft and FBI Director Robert Mueller. Many other
officials were defendants below, but only the sufficiency of the claims against these two petitioners was
before the Supreme Court. CB pp. 628–629; opinion pts. I, IV.B.
Material Facts
According to the background recounted in the full opinion, Iqbal was arrested in November 2001 on
identification-document fraud and conspiracy charges. He was initially held in the general population at the
Metropolitan Detention Center in Brooklyn, then placed in its highly restrictive administrative
maximum-security unit after officials classified him as a detainee of high interest. He later pleaded guilty,
served his sentence, and was removed to Pakistan. These details supplement the shorter casebook
introduction. Full opinion pt. I; CB pp. 628–629.
The complaint alleged that FBI officials detained numerous Arab Muslim men, classified some as being of
high interest because of protected characteristics rather than evidence of terrorist involvement, and subjected
them to harsh confinement. It alleged that Ashcroft and Mueller approved restrictive detention pending FBI
clearance and knowingly created or condoned a discriminatory policy. Allegations of abuse by guards
concerned other defendants and were not the claims decided here. The allegations were pleadings, not
findings established through trial. CB pp. 630–631, 636–638; opinion pts. I, IV.B; Souter dissent pt. II.
Procedural History
The Eastern District of New York denied Ashcroft and Mueller’s motion to dismiss, which asserted
qualified immunity. The Second Circuit affirmed on an interlocutory appeal. The Supreme Court accepted
review and held that the court of appeals had jurisdiction: the appeal presented a legal question about
whether the pleaded conduct stated a constitutional violation sufficient to overcome qualified immunity,
rather than a dispute about which facts a summary-judgment record could establish. The jurisdictional
explanation appears in the full opinion but is omitted from the assigned excerpt. CB pp. 628–629; full
opinion pt. II.
Issue
Does Rule 8 require factual allegations plausibly showing that these particular supervisory officials
purposefully discriminated, and did Iqbal’s allegations satisfy that requirement? Do the general-pleading
provision for intent in Rule 9(b), the non-antitrust setting, or proposed discovery restrictions alter the
answer? CB pp. 629–634.

Rule and Holding
A complaint must contain factual matter that, taken as true, plausibly supports entitlement to relief. Courts
need not accept legal conclusions or unsupported recitations of elements as true. They must then assess the
remaining factual allegations in context. Plausibility requires more than a sheer possibility of liability, but is
not a probability requirement. This interpretation of Rule 8 applies across civil actions, including
constitutional discrimination claims. CB pp. 629–630, 632; opinion pts. IV.A, IV.C.1.
The substantive claim determines what must be plausibly alleged. The Court held that these officials could
not be liable merely because of their supervisory positions: each must be responsible through his own
misconduct. For the discrimination theory at issue, purpose was required; knowledge of a subordinate’s
discrimination alone was insufficient. The complaint did not plausibly allege that Ashcroft and Mueller
themselves adopted the challenged classification because of race, religion, or national origin. CB pp.
630–631, 634; full opinion pt. III; opinion pt. IV.B.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Second Circuit and remanded. The court of appeals was to decide initially
whether to remand to the district court so that Iqbal could seek leave to amend. The decision did not
adjudicate every allegation against every defendant, establish that the alleged mistreatment never happened,
or automatically authorize an amended complaint. CB p. 634; opinion pt. V.
Reasoning
First, the majority classified the assertions that petitioners knowingly and maliciously imposed confinement
solely because of protected characteristics as conclusory recitations of discriminatory purpose. It withheld
the presumption of truth because of the allegations’ conclusory character, not because it declared them
fantastic or unbelievable. This characterization was central to the disagreement with the dissent. CB pp.
630–631; opinion pt. IV.B.
Second, the majority accepted allegations that the FBI detained Arab Muslim men and that petitioners
approved highly restrictive detention until clearance. It reasoned that a security investigation offered an
obvious alternative explanation for those measures, and that the pleaded approval did not itself plausibly
establish a deliberately discriminatory classification policy. This was the majority’s assessment of what
could be inferred from the pleading, not a factual determination that the asserted explanation actually
motivated all officials. CB pp. 631–632; opinion pt. IV.B.
Third, the majority rejected a restriction of Twombly to antitrust. Twombly interpreted Rule 8, which
governs civil pleading generally. Likewise, Rule 9(b)’s permission to allege mental states generally relaxes
the special particularity requirement applicable to fraud and mistake; it does not dispense with Rule 8’s
requirement of factual support. A plaintiff cannot avoid that requirement by labeling an unsupported element
a general allegation. CB pp. 632–633; opinion pts. IV.C.1, IV.C.3.
Fourth, anticipated discovery management did not cure the deficient complaint. The majority emphasized
qualified immunity’s protection against litigation burdens and reasoned that high-level officials might need
to participate even in discovery directed initially at lower-level defendants. It therefore rejected the proposal
to begin discovery first and test the allegations later. CB pp. 632–633; opinion pt. IV.C.2.

Separate Opinions
Justice Souter dissented, joined by Justices Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer. He objected that petitioners had
conceded liability based on actual knowledge and deliberate indifference, and that the majority
unnecessarily rejected that concession without full briefing. He also read the complaint as a whole: its
allegations described a particular discriminatory policy and linked petitioners to it. In his view, separating
those allegations from their factual context wrongly converted them into unsupported conclusions. CB pp.
634–638; Souter dissent pts. I–II.
Souter agreed that the two allegations isolated by the majority would be inadequate standing alone, but
stressed that they were not the entire complaint. He also distinguished assessing entitlement to relief
assuming facts are true from judging whether the alleged facts probably happened. Justice Breyer separately
argued that sequencing discovery and other case-management tools had not been shown inadequate to
protect government officials from unwarranted burdens. CB pp. 637–639; Souter dissent pt. II; Breyer
dissent.
Notes
Narrow reading: the complaint lacked sufficient factual support for purposeful discrimination by these two
officials under the substantive standard the majority selected. Broader reading: the two-stage treatment of
conclusions and factual plausibility applies to Rule 8 complaints generally. Application therefore requires
identifying the claim’s substantive elements before evaluating the allegations. CB pp. 629–634; opinion pts.
III–IV.
Course connection: read with Twombly to distinguish fair notice, factual support, and plausibility; with Rule
9(b) to distinguish ordinary pleading from particularity; and with later amendment materials to distinguish
dismissal of the present pleading from a final merits defeat. The majority and dissent also expose a recurring
problem: whether an allegation is factual or conclusory may depend on how much context the court
considers. CB pp. 629–639.
