# Atlantic Richfield Co. v. Christian

**140 S. Ct. 1335 (2020) · Supreme Court of the United States · April 20, 2020**  
**Civil Procedure — state-court jurisdiction, CERCLA, and the limits on restoration remedies**

Montana courts retained jurisdiction over the landowners’ state-law claims, but the landowners could not undertake their proposed restoration work without EPA approval. The distinction separates a court’s power to hear a case from the federal constraints on a particular remedy. Chief Justice Roberts’s opinion had different majorities for different parts. ## Contamination, cleanup, and the proposed remedy

For nearly a century, copper smelting around Butte, Montana spread arsenic and lead over more than 300 square miles. Atlantic Richfield bought the Anaconda Company, subsequently closed the facility, and faced cleanup obligations under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act, or CERCLA. EPA designated the area a Superfund site in 1983. By the time of the decision, the company estimated that it had spent roughly $450 million implementing EPA’s orders. The Court described extensive completed remediation and further work then projected to continue through 2025; that was a contemporary projection, not a finding that cleanup ultimately finished on that date. **Court 1–5.**

In 2008, ninety-eight property owners sued Atlantic Richfield in Montana state court for trespass, nuisance, and strict liability. Among other relief, they sought restoration damages. Montana law allowed such damages in appropriate residential-property cases even when restoration cost exceeded diminution in value, but required personal reasons for restoration, an injury realistically capable of repair, and assurance that the award would actually be used to restore the land. **Court 5–6.**

Their plan went beyond EPA’s selected remedy. For example, they proposed a fifteen-parts-per-million arsenic ceiling instead of EPA’s 250-parts-per-million level, excavation to two feet instead of one, and an extensive underground groundwater-treatment barrier that EPA had rejected as costly and unnecessary for safe drinking water. They estimated $50–58 million in restoration costs, to be placed in a trust and released for the work. Atlantic Richfield conceded that CERCLA preserved their claims for other compensatory damages, so the Court addressed restoration damages specifically. **Court 6 & n.2.**

The trial court decided competing summary-judgment motions against the company’s argument that CERCLA precluded the restoration claim. The Montana Supreme Court affirmed through a writ of supervisory control. It regarded the proposed work as not interfering with EPA’s work and found the landowners were not potentially responsible parties requiring EPA permission, stressing the absence of prior treatment as such parties and the expired limitations period. **Court 6–7.**

## Two different jurisdictional questions

First, the Supreme Court had jurisdiction under §1257(a), despite continuing trial proceedings. Under Montana law, a supervisory-writ proceeding constituted a separate, self-contained lawsuit. Its completed judgment was therefore final for Supreme Court review. The nature of that proceeding, rather than whether its questions were jurisdictional or substantive, controlled. **Court 8.**

Second, CERCLA did not remove Montana courts’ power to hear these state-law claims. Section 113(b) gave federal district courts exclusive original jurisdiction over controversies arising under CERCLA. The nuisance, trespass, and strict-liability claims arose under Montana law. No element necessarily raised a federal question within the narrow embedded-federal-issue category; Atlantic Richfield invoked CERCLA as a defense, which did not create federal-question jurisdiction. **Court 8–10 & n.4.**

Atlantic Richfield argued that §113(h), which withdrew federal jurisdiction over certain challenges to cleanup actions, implicitly expanded §113(b)’s exclusive jurisdiction to cover state-law cleanup challenges too. The Court rejected that multi-step construction. Section 113(h) expressly addressed federal courts. It did not implicitly strip state courts of their jurisdiction over their own state-law claims. The statute’s diversity exception also permitted some state-law claims in federal court, making it difficult to explain why the same claims would be prohibited in state court. The presumption favoring concurrent state jurisdiction reinforced the absence of a clear exclusion. **Court 10–12.**

The introductory exception referencing §113(h) did not alter that result. It explained which provision prevailed where the two overlapped; it did not make their domains identical. Federal claims outside CERCLA and state-law claims arriving through diversity or supplemental jurisdiction could implicate §113(h) without arising under CERCLA for §113(b). Conversely, CERCLA cost-recovery claims need not challenge a cleanup plan. **Court 12–13 & n.6.**

## Why EPA permission nevertheless was required

Section 122(e)(6) prohibited a potentially responsible party from undertaking remedial action after a remedial investigation and feasibility study began without federal authorization. Both sides agreed that this provision required EPA approval if the landowners qualified as potentially responsible parties. The majority concluded that they did. Section 107’s covered-person categories included owners of facilities where hazardous substances had come to be located, encompassing these contaminated properties. **Court 13–14.**

The majority distinguished this status from actual liability to pay response costs. A limitations defense or an innocent-landowner defense could protect against liability without removing potentially responsible status. EPA’s discretionary policy against suing innocent residential owners likewise did not change the statutory category. Even a failure to give the required settlement notice would not redefine their status. **Court 14–18.**

The approval requirement supported a coordinated cleanup instead of competing individual remedies. Its placement in the settlement provisions was consistent with CERCLA’s strong reliance on negotiated cleanups. Broad saving clauses protecting state-law liability could not erase the specific approval requirement. Other compensatory damages remained available, and restoration beyond EPA’s plan could proceed with approval. The requirement concerned defined remedial actions, not every ordinary gardening or backyard activity. **Court 15–20 & n.7.**

The contiguous-property exception did not rescue these landowners. They failed its no-reason-to-know-contamination criterion; the Court cited overwhelming public knowledge and earlier pollution easements. The Government’s assertion that their proposed work would interfere with EPA’s remedy supplied an additional, expressly conditional cooperation concern, not an unconditional finding of every predicted interference. **Court 20–21.**

## Separate opinions and judgment

Justice Alito agreed that the landowners required EPA approval and that the Supreme Court could hear the case, but would leave state-court jurisdiction undecided. EPA’s decision might end the dispute, while the statutory relationship between §§113(b) and (h) presented difficult questions. He criticized the majority’s potential redundancies but also identified difficulties with the company’s construction, rather than adopting it as settled law. **Alito 1–9 & nn.1–4.**

Justice Gorsuch, joined by Justice Thomas, agreed on jurisdiction but dissented from the approval holding. He distinguished covered persons whom the Government could sue from parties still potentially responsible within the settlement framework. The expired limitations period and absence of notice, CERCLA’s saving clauses, traditional state remedies, and constitutional concerns favored allowing these owners to restore their property without prior permission. He also resisted treating earlier judicial shorthand equating the categories as a decision of the specific interpretive question. **Gorsuch 1–11.**

The Court **affirmed in part, vacated in part, and remanded**. Without EPA approval of the proposed plan, it did not decide the company’s further contention that CERCLA otherwise preempted that plan. **Court 22.** Parts I and II-A were unanimous; Part II-B had eight Justices, excluding Alito; Part III had seven, excluding Thomas and Gorsuch.
