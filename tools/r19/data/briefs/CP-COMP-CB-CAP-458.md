# Unitherm Food Systems, Inc. v. Swift-Eckrich, Inc.

**546 U.S. 394 (2006) · Supreme Court of the United States · January 23, 2006**  
**Course:** Civil Procedure  
**Assigned passage:** CivPro, physical PDF pages 595–596, printed pages 1176–1177; both following editorial notes continue through printed 1178 on physical page 596.

Justice Thomas delivered a 7–2 opinion joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Justice Stevens dissented, joined by Justice Kennedy. The Court reversed the Federal Circuit because ConAgra had failed to renew its evidentiary-sufficiency challenge after the verdict. A denied preverdict Rule 50(a) motion alone did not permit appellate sufficiency review or a new trial on that basis. (396, 407–409.)

## Facts and litigation

ConAgra obtained a patent on a method for browning precooked whole-muscle meat products. In early 2000 it warned companies selling browning equipment and processes that it would protect its patent rights aggressively. It also informed direct competitors that licenses were available at a royalty of ten cents per pound. Jennie-O, one recipient, investigated and concluded that the browning process it had purchased from Unitherm matched the patent, but that Unitherm’s president had invented the process six years before ConAgra’s patent application. Unitherm itself had not received the warning. (397.)

Jennie-O and Unitherm jointly sued ConAgra in the Western District of Oklahoma. As relevant to Supreme Court review, they sought a declaration that the patent was invalid and unenforceable and asserted a Sherman Act § 2 claim based on attempted enforcement of a patent allegedly obtained through fraud on the Patent and Trademark Office. Under *Walker Process*, fraudulent procurement and enforcement can support an antitrust claim when the other necessary § 2 elements are present. (397–398.)

The district court found the patent invalid because of Unitherm’s prior public use and sale of the process. It dismissed Jennie-O for lack of antitrust standing but allowed Unitherm’s antitrust claim to reach the jury. ConAgra moved before submission for judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50(a). The judge denied that motion, and the jury returned a verdict for Unitherm. ConAgra did not file a renewed Rule 50(b) motion or seek a new trial challenging **antitrust liability**. It did file a postverdict new-trial motion about **damages**, which the Court expressly distinguished as irrelevant to preservation of the liability-sufficiency issue. (398 & nn.1–2.)

## The appellate ruling and question presented

The Federal Circuit generally applied regional circuit law to Rule 50 issues not unique to patent law. It therefore used the Tenth Circuit’s approach, under which a party that made a preverdict motion but omitted a postverdict sufficiency challenge could obtain appellate review, although the only available relief was a new trial rather than judgment in its favor. (398–399.)

Applying that approach, the Federal Circuit found sufficient evidence of attempted enforcement of a fraudulently obtained patent, but insufficient economic evidence for the relevant antitrust market and inadequate support for other § 2 elements. It vacated the jury judgment and ordered a new trial. The Supreme Court’s question concerned the authority to provide that appellate relief despite the absent postverdict liability motion. It did not decide whether the antitrust verdict was supported by sufficient evidence on the merits. (399, 407.)

## Rule and preservation rationale

Rule 50 supplied two procedural stages. The preverdict motion identified the claim for judgment as a matter of law and the legal and factual grounds for it. If the judge allowed the case to go to the jury, Rule 50(b) supplied the postverdict opportunity to renew the legal questions and obtain a choice among allowing the verdict to stand, ordering a new trial, or entering judgment as a matter of law. The opinion reproduces the historical rule, including its then-applicable ten-day period; that text is not a statement of today’s filing deadline. (399–401.)

The Court relied on *Cone*, *Globe Liquor*, and *Johnson*. Those decisions required a proper postverdict motion before an appellate court could direct judgment against the verdict. Thomas extended their rationale to a sufficiency-based request for a new trial. In either situation, the trial judge should first assess the requested relief with firsthand knowledge of the witnesses, testimony, and course of trial. Renewal also serves fairness by requiring timely presentation of the particular postverdict remedy. (401–404.)

ConAgra pointed out that the earlier litigants had ultimately received new trials. The Court answered that they had actually sought new trials after the verdict in the district court. Their procedural records did not support obtaining that relief solely from a denied preverdict motion. The judicial note concerning *Cone* identifies its evidentiary objections and subsequent motions, further emphasizing that the preservation inquiry depends on what relief and grounds were presented below. (402–404 & n.5.)

## Why denial of Rule 50(a) was not itself reversible error

A preverdict Rule 50(a) motion did not ask for a new trial. Rule 50(b)’s new-trial option arose when the court ruled on the renewed motion. Without the required renewal, ConAgra could not use its preverdict motion to supply a new-trial request it had never presented on liability. The appellate court accordingly lacked a preserved basis for that relief. (404–405.)

The district court also had discretion to take the verdict rather than grant judgment immediately. Doing so can reduce retrials: if the jury agrees with the judge’s assessment, judgment follows; if the jury disagrees, the judge can consider a properly renewed motion; and an appellate reversal of the later ruling may restore the existing verdict. Thus, merely allowing the jury to decide initially was not the error ConAgra claimed. The relevant omission was counsel’s failure to renew the sufficiency challenge. (405–406.)

Footnote 3 distinguishes cases permitting appellate judgment after review of an actual Rule 50(b) ruling, where the district judge had already received the required postverdict opportunity. Footnote 6 rejects ConAgra’s asserted reliance on the Tenth Circuit’s new-trial practice: it had every incentive to renew because obtaining judgment in its favor indisputably required renewal. (398 n.1, 401 n.3, 406 n.6.)

## Stevens’s dissent

Stevens agreed that failure to make proper motions would ordinarily entail waiver or forfeiture, but denied that it eliminated appellate **power** to correct exceptional injustice. He relied on 28 U.S.C. § 2106, which broadly authorizes appellate courts to modify judgments and require proceedings just under the circumstances. Rule 50(b), in his view, restricted the district court’s authority without expressly withdrawing the appellate court’s statutory authority. Relief might be unfair, an abuse of discretion, or inappropriate under forfeiture principles, yet those limitations differed from an absolute absence of power. (407–409.)

His sole starred footnote also rejected the majority’s constitutional concern. ConAgra had made a Rule 50(a) motion, triggering reservation of legal questions under the rule. Stevens regarded that reservation as sufficient to avert the Seventh Amendment difficulty, with no greater constitutional restriction on an appellate court than on the trial court. (408–409 n.*.)

The majority answered that its precedents had already reconciled § 2106 with strict Rule 50 compliance and that the dissent’s broad approach could create Seventh Amendment problems. It did not treat the statute as a license to disregard the preservation requirements. (402–403 n.4.)

## Judgment and assigned comparison

The Court **reversed the Federal Circuit’s judgment**. Its final paragraph states that there was no basis for appellate review of ConAgra’s sufficiency challenge; it did not grant ConAgra a new trial or decide the antitrust merits. Footnote 7 separately rejects a request for reconsideration based on *Phillips*, an issue the Federal Circuit had already addressed on rehearing. (407 & n.7.)

Two textbook qualifications matter. The assigned summary’s statement that the defendant failed to seek “a new trial” omits the actual damages-only motion documented in 398 n.2. The majority passages which the textbook cites to 404 actually appear at **405** (Rule 50(b)’s new-trial option) and **406** (the permissibility of taking a jury verdict).
