# Tull v. United States

**481 U.S. 412 (1987) — Supreme Court of the United States, April 28, 1987.** Justice Brennan wrote the Court’s opinion. All nine Justices agreed that a jury must determine liability; seven joined the holding that the judge determines the civil-penalty amount. Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Stevens, concurred in part and dissented in part. Civil Procedure: Seventh Amendment, statutory civil penalties, and separate responsibility for liability and remedy. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 544, printed 1070, the developed Tull account following *Curtis*.

## Facts and proceedings

The United States sued a real-estate developer for placing fill without a permit in wetlands on Chincoteague Island, Virginia. The complaint concerned Ocean Breeze Mobile Homes Sites, Mire Pond Properties, and Eel Creek; an amendment added the manmade waterway Fowling Gut Extended. The Clean Water Act prohibited unpermitted discharges of dredged or fill material into navigable waters, including adjacent wetlands. Its enforcement provisions separately authorized injunctions and civil penalties of up to $10,000 per day. The government demanded the maximum claimed penalty, $22,890,000, as well as injunctive relief. (414–415.)

The developer did not deny placing the fill or failing to obtain permits. He disputed whether the property qualified as wetlands, and competing expert evidence presented triable factual questions. His timely jury demand was nevertheless denied, and the district court conducted a 15-day bench trial. By the time the government filed suit, he had sold almost all the affected property, making injunctive relief impractical for much of it. Note 2 contrasts the more than one million square feet alleged to be affected with the 6,000 square feet to which the resulting injunction related. (415, n.2.)

The court found violations and imposed $35,000 for Ocean Breeze, $35,000 for Mire Pond, and $5,000 for Eel Creek. It also imposed a $250,000 fine suspended on the condition that Fowling Gut Extended be restored to its former navigable condition. Tull contended that restoration would require buying land from third parties for more than $700,000, leaving payment as his practical option; the court did not change the order. Other provisions ordered restoration of wetlands still owned by him and removal of fill from five Ocean Breeze lots unless an after-the-fact permit was obtained. (415–416.)

The Fourth Circuit affirmed over a dissent. It reasoned that the district court exercised statutory equitable power in selecting the penalty and fashioned a combined package of legal and equitable remedies. It distinguished older civil-penalty cases as involving fixed statutory sums and disagreed with the Second Circuit’s recognition of a jury right in a statutory-penalty action. The Supreme Court granted review to resolve the jury question and the circuit conflict. (416–417.)

## Questions and competing analogies

Tull claimed a Seventh Amendment right to have a jury decide both whether he was liable and the amount of any penalty. He analogized the government’s action to the historical action in debt, which English law courts used to collect statutory penalties. The government compared the suit to equitable proceedings to abate public nuisances, including obstructions of waterways and polluting activities. It also invoked disgorgement and argued that monetary relief could be incidental to its injunction claim. (417–425.)

The Court could not avoid the constitutional question by interpreting the statute as itself granting a jury right. Note 3 explains that neither its language nor its legislative history fairly implied that entitlement at the liability or penalty phase. Note 1 separately identifies the Rivers and Harbors Act allegation concerning Fowling Gut Extended: Tull did not base his Seventh Amendment argument on that statute, which supplied injunctive relief rather than civil penalties. Those qualifications define the actual question before the Court. (414, n.1; 417, n.3.)

## Liability: a unanimous jury-right holding

The Seventh Amendment preserves jury trial in suits at common law, including newly created statutory actions analogous to cases historically tried in courts of law. The Court examines both the nature of the action and the requested remedy. It compares the statutory action with eighteenth-century English actions and asks whether the relief is legal or equitable. Characterizing the remedy is more important than locating the single nearest historical cause of action. (417–421.)

A government action to recover a civil penalty resembles an action in debt, historically triable by jury. The older *Hepner* and *Regan* decisions supported that understanding. The government's public-nuisance analogy also had some force because the alleged conduct involved obstructing waterways. The Court did not decide which historical analogy was closest: both could illuminate the statutory action, and constitutional protection did not require that the modern suit have existed in exactly its present form in the eighteenth century. (418–421.)

The remedy resolved the central difficulty. A civil penalty intended to punish wrongdoing and deter future violations is legal relief. Section 1319(d) was not confined to restitution, profits received, or restoration of the prior position. Legislative material called for consideration of seriousness, prior violations, good faith, and deterrence. The lower court had imposed fines even for properties from which Tull received no profit, illustrating the punitive component. Disgorgement therefore supplied an inadequate description of the relief. (422–424, nn.7–8.)

The government also could not remove the jury right by joining the legal penalty claim to an equitable injunction claim. Equity could award appropriate monetary restitution, but it did not ordinarily enforce punitive civil penalties. Here, moreover, the enormous potential fine could not plausibly be characterized as incidental to the limited injunction available after most property had been sold. The statute separately authorized each remedy. A legal claim joined with an equitable one preserves the jury right on that claim and on issues common to both. Tull was consequently entitled to a jury determination of liability. (424–425.)

Two notes sharpen the analysis. Note 6 rejects treating the cause of action and remedy as independent, disconnected classifications: the inquiry considers them together in searching for the historical analogy. Note 4 addresses practical compatibility in administrative proceedings, while making clear that such considerations were not an independent ground for expanding jury protection here. This was a civil action in federal court, not an administrative penalty adjudication. (418, n.4; 421–422, n.6.)

## Amount: the seven-Justice holding

The Court drew a separate conclusion about determining the penalty amount. Congress intended trial judges to perform the discretionary calculations after liability was found. The Seventh Amendment preserves fundamental elements of jury trial, but the majority did not regard assessment of a civil penalty as such an element. Congress could fix the penalty itself; it could therefore delegate the selection of an appropriate sum to the judge. The multifactor calculations contemplated by this statute resembled work traditionally performed by judges. (425–427.)

Note 9 explains the majority’s historical and textual reasoning: it found no evidence that the Amendment’s protection necessarily extended to the remedy phase in this setting. Note 8 also records the 1987 amendment enacted after argument, which expressly required consideration of listed factors and other matters justice might require. The holding should not be reduced to a claim that the original statutory text expressly said “judge”: the opinion acknowledges that, at trial, it did not explicitly identify whether judges or juries would set the amount, and relies on legislative history and constitutional analysis. (423, n.8; 425–427, n.9.)

The decision accordingly preserves a jury determination of liability while assigning the civil-penalty amount to the trial court. It does not broadly abolish jury assessment of ordinary compensatory or punitive damages in other civil actions.

## Scalia’s partial dissent, joined by Stevens

Scalia joined the facts, liability analysis, and disposition, but rejected the separation of penalty assessment from the jury’s task. Congress’s ability to enact a fixed sum did not establish that it could remove valuation from the jury when it instead created a claim for an unspecified amount. He compared an ordinary private damages action: the legislature might fix damages, but leaving damages open would not permit judicial assessment merely because that alternative legislative choice existed. (427.)

He found no historical precedent for the majority’s form of civil adjudication and noted that juries ordinarily assess punitive damages. A criminal sentencing analogy was tempting because the government sought punishment, but a criminal prosecution would require proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Having chosen a civil debt-like action with the advantages of civil procedure, the government should also accept a jury’s penalty assessment. He would remand for jury determination of both liability and amount. (428.)

## Judgment and assigned scope

The Court **reversed the Fourth Circuit and remanded** for proceedings consistent with its opinion. The remand did not itself determine whether the disputed areas were wetlands or set a new penalty. All nine Justices accepted jury trial on liability and the reversal; the penalty-assessment rule divided them 7–2. (413, 427–428.)

The assigned account focuses on the two-part historical-and-remedial inquiry and asks why the action’s characterization matters when the remedy receives greater weight. That editorial question accurately highlights the Court’s method, but its compressed statement that a civil penalty is entitled to jury trial does not state the whole liability-versus-amount holding. The complete opinion supplies that distinction and the separate disagreement. The neighboring *Curtis* conclusion and *Atlas Roofing* opening were read as context, not as complete judicial opinions for this brief. (Assigned printed 1070.)
