# Zapatha v. Dairy Mart, Inc.
381 Mass. 284, 408 N.E.2d 1370 (1980) | Contracts

## Court, issues, and result
The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court decided this case on August 5, 1980, on Dairy Mart's application for direct appellate review. Justice Wilkins wrote for the court; the reported panel was Chief Justice Hennessey and Justices Quirico, Braucher, Wilkins, and Abrams, with no separate opinion or dissent. It reversed judgments protecting the franchisees from termination. In this record, the disclosed ninety-day termination provision was not unconscionable, and exercising it after the franchisees refused a replacement agreement was not shown to violate either good faith or chapter 93A's prohibition of unfair or deceptive conduct. The decision rejects an automatic good-cause requirement for this kind of franchise; it does not eliminate good-faith limits on all termination clauses. (284–285, 291–300.)

## Facts and procedural history
Bernard and Elaine Zapatha operated a Dairy Mart convenience store. Bernard had a year of college, evening courses in business administration and business law, and substantial managerial experience: he had supervised as many as 150 employees in an electroplating business before losing that job in May 1973. Seeking a stable business of his own, he received sales assurances and a brochure describing independence, security, and ownership. Dairy Mart approved him for an Agawam store and presented its form agreement on November 8, 1973. Its representative read and explained the termination term, recommended legal advice, and said the terms were nonnegotiable. The couple signed without consulting a lawyer. Bernard testified that he understood every word but had nonetheless taken the clause to permit Dairy Mart to terminate only for cause. (285–287 & nn. 1, 3.)

Dairy Mart provided the equipped premises, paid rent and utilities and some other expenses, and licensed its name and merchandising methods. Its fee was a percentage of gross sales. The franchisees purchased and maintained inventory and paid employee wages, related taxes, and sales taxes. After the first twelve months either side could end the agreement on ninety days' written notice; specified defaults allowed immediate termination, but were not the basis of this case. When Dairy Mart terminated without cause, it agreed to repurchase saleable inventory at retail price less twenty percent. In 1974, the couple elected to transfer to the Wilbraham Road store in Springfield and signed an identical form. (286–287 & n. 4; 291 n. 10.)

In November 1977, Dairy Mart proposed a more detailed agreement with some less favorable terms. Bernard objected to possible relocation, longer daily opening hours, and the new allocation of future utility increases. He chose to remain under the existing contract. On January 20, 1978, Dairy Mart gave ninety days' termination notice, offered to buy inventory, and invited discussion of a possible new agreement without guaranteeing its terms or execution. The trial judge found that refusal to sign the replacement was the sole reason for termination and otherwise found no unconscionable or bad-faith conduct. There was no evidence that the couple pursued compromise negotiations. (287–288 & n. 5.)

The franchisees sued to enjoin termination. The trial court held the clause unconscionable, found termination contrary to good faith and chapter 93A, and declared that Dairy Mart could terminate only for good cause. A separate chapter 93A judgment apparently awarded only costs and attorney fees because the couple continued operating while the action was pending. Dairy Mart obtained direct appellate review. (284–285 & n. 2; 288.)

## Applicable law and the distinction between formation and performance
The court did not subject the entire agreement directly to UCC Article 2 just because some goods were purchased from Dairy Mart. Approximately seventy percent of inventory came from others; Dairy Mart's expected return principally came from its franchise fee. The arrangement licensed an entire business format, premises, methods, and trademark, rather than predominantly selling goods. Splitting the agreement into sales and nonsales portions also risked inconsistent governing rules. Instead, the court selectively applied the Code's policy principles of unconscionability and good faith by common-law analogy. (289–291 & nn. 6–9.)

Unconscionability was a legal issue measured at formation, examining commercial context, unfair surprise, and substantive oppression, with attention to the existing duty of good faith. The court recognized procedural and substantive components and rejected a universal definition detached from facts. Section 2-309's reasonable-notice provision itself indicated that termination without an agreed cause was not inherently unconscionable; no one suggested ninety days was unreasonable here. The immediate-default provisions were outside the controversy. (291–294 & nn. 10–13.)

The court separately examined conduct in performance. The merchant's Code standard required honesty in fact and reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing, but the court also examined broader common-law fairness and chapter 93A rather than treating the Code formulation as exhaustive. A term fair when agreed could still be used in an unfair way; conversely, termination without cause did not establish either invalidity at formation or bad faith by itself. (295–299 & nn. 15–17.)

## Why the clause and termination survived
There was no unfair surprise. The provision was plainly stated, specifically explained, and not concealed in fine print. The franchisees had time to consider both agreements and declined recommended legal advice. Bernard's managerial experience and education, together with his evident concern for security after losing employment, made his mistaken subjective reading insufficient to establish unfair inclusion of the term. Nonnegotiability did not override those particular facts. (293–294 & nn. 13–14.)

The court likewise found no oppressive allocation of termination risks. Dairy Mart supplied the going business, equipment, and premises. The franchisees' investment was the inventory, and Dairy Mart conceded a duty to repurchase all saleable stock, including items obtained elsewhere, at eighty percent of retail value. On this record, there was no showing of forfeited investment, special-purpose equipment, a need for more time to recoup initial expenses, or an unreasonably disproportionate allocation of risks unrelated to legitimate commercial needs. They retained the net profits earned through the full agreed term. These are record-specific conclusions, not a claim that every franchisee suffers no loss on termination. (294–295.)

For Code good faith, no evidence established violated commercial standards—or identified such standards. Dairy Mart openly exercised its clear termination right because the couple declined a new agreement. Even if that choice could be called arbitrary, the record did not make it dishonest. The brochure's assertions about owning a business were misleading, but did not establish dishonesty relevant to the termination power: by contracting, the couple knew Dairy Mart owned most operating assets and that termination was possible. The brochure remained relevant to good faith; it simply did not prove the particular violation asserted. (295–297 & nn. 15–16.)

The broader inquiry reached the same result. Fortune protected an at-will employee against termination designed to withhold an earned commission, but Dairy Mart had not appropriated earned income or funds reasonably due the couple. There was no showing of goodwill developed in their own name, special efforts building this location's trade, or unrecoverable investment. Massachusetts regulated termination of some specific franchises but had not generally imposed cause requirements on all franchises. Note 18 identified New Jersey's different statutory and public-policy approach in Shell v. Marinello without importing it universally. No cited FTC interpretation made termination in these circumstances unfair or deceptive, and the later franchise-disclosure rule was not relied on. The court found no brochure deception bearing on this termination, particularly given disclosure of the term and the invitation to negotiate. (297–300 & nn. 17–19.)

## Exact disposition and assignment scope
The court held that Dairy Mart lawfully terminated because this record showed no unfair, deceptive, or bad-faith conduct, and reversed the judgments. It did not merely vacate a preliminary order for additional factfinding, award new damages to Dairy Mart, or announce that an express termination clause always defeats good faith. (300.)
