Tellabs, Inc. v. Makor Issues & Rights, Ltd.
Citation
Tellabs, Inc. v. Makor Issues & Rights, Ltd., 551 U.S. 308, 127 S. Ct. 2499, 168 L. Ed. 2d 179 (2007).
Supreme Court of the United States. Argued March 28, 2007; decided June 21, 2007. Justice Ginsburg wrote
for the Court, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Breyer. Justices
Scalia and Alito separately concurred in the judgment; Justice Stevens dissented.
Casebook pp. 654–657; source PDF pp. 344–346. Full judicial opinion.
Parties
Purchasers of Tellabs stock, including Makor Issues & Rights, brought a proposed investor class action.
Tellabs manufactured equipment for fiber-optic networks; Richard Notebaert was its chief executive officer
and president during the relevant period. Tellabs and Notebaert sought Supreme Court review of the ruling
allowing the scienter allegations to proceed. Claims against other executives were not before the Court. CB
pp. 654–655; opinion pt. I & n. 1.
Material Facts
The investors alleged that Notebaert misrepresented demand for the existing TITAN 5500 product, the
readiness and demand for the new TITAN 6500, the company’s financial results, and its projected revenues.
They also alleged that unwanted inventory was pushed onto customers to inflate results. According to the
complaint, he knew the favorable public statements did not reflect the company’s actual condition. These
assertions remained allegations, not findings of fraud. CB p. 655; opinion pt. I.
The full opinion adds that the proposed class purchased stock between December 11, 2000, and June 19,
2001. After progressively more cautious disclosures, Tellabs sharply reduced its revenue projections and
disclosed reduced demand. Its share price fell substantially. The later amended complaint relied on 27
confidential sources and added particulars bearing on Notebaert’s knowledge. The appeal concerned the
inference of mental state, not whether the stock decline itself proved fraud. Full opinion pt. I; CB p. 655.
Procedural History
The Northern District of Illinois dismissed the initial complaint without prejudice for insufficiently
particular pleading. After amendment, it dismissed with prejudice: although the alleged misleading
statements were adequately specified, scienter was not sufficiently pleaded. The Seventh Circuit reversed in
relevant part, reasoning that the complaint could proceed if a reasonable person could infer the necessary
intent from the alleged facts. It declined to weigh competing innocent inferences. The Supreme Court
granted review of that standard. CB p. 655; opinion pt. I.
Issue
Under the Private Securities Litigation Reform Act’s requirement of particular facts supporting a strong
inference of the required mental state, must a court compare the inference of scienter with competing
nonfraudulent explanations, and how strong must the inference be? CB pp. 655–656; opinion pts. II–III.

Rule and Holding
The inference of scienter must be cogent and at least as compelling as any plausible opposing inference
drawn from the alleged facts. A merely reasonable or permissible inference is insufficient. The court accepts
factual allegations as true, considers them collectively with permissible Rule 12(b)(6) materials, and
compares the culpable and nonculpable explanations. An equally compelling inference can satisfy the
majority’s standard; the inference need not be irrefutable. CB p. 656; opinion pt. III.A.
The Seventh Circuit’s standard was too lenient because it asked only whether a reasonable person could
infer scienter, without the required comparison. The Court prescribed the proper test but did not itself decide
whether these investors’ complaint satisfied it. CB pp. 655–656; opinion pt. III.A & concluding disposition.
Judgment
The Seventh Circuit’s judgment was vacated, and the case was remanded for reexamination under the newly
specified standard. This was a direction to reassess the pleading, not a final determination that defendants
had committed fraud or that the action necessarily had to be dismissed. CB p. 656; opinion concluding
disposition.
Reasoning
The statutory adjective requiring strength would add little if any rational inference of intent were enough.
The majority reasoned that an inference’s strength depends on the alternatives supported by the same facts.
Thus, a plausible inference of misconduct may still fail when a more compelling innocent explanation
emerges from the allegations. CB pp. 655–656; opinion pts. II–III.A.
At the same time, the Court required a holistic assessment. Courts must consider the entire complaint,
incorporated documents, and matters properly subject to judicial notice, rather than demanding that each
allegation independently establish scienter. The majority treated this approach as consistent with both
screening weak suits and allowing meritorious investor claims to proceed. CB p. 656; opinion pts. II–III.A.
The full opinion explains that motive matters but its absence is not automatically fatal. Tellabs emphasized
that investors did not allege Notebaert sold shares during the class period; the majority refused to make that
omission dispositive. Ambiguities and missing particulars can weaken the inference, but their significance
must be assessed against all the allegations together. Full opinion pt. III.B.
The full opinion also rejects the Seventh Circuit’s jury-trial concern. Congress can define the pleading
conditions for a federal statutory claim; once those conditions are met, the jury retains its role in resolving
factual disputes and credibility. A comparison of inferences at the pleading stage assumes the pleaded facts
are true and is distinct from proving fraud at trial by a preponderance of the evidence. Full opinion pt. IV.
Separate Opinions
Justice Scalia concurred in the judgment but would have required the inference of scienter to be more
compelling than the inference of innocence, rather than permitting an evidentiary tie. He also criticized the
majority’s reliance on committee reports to identify congressional intent. The latter objection appears in the
full concurrence, beyond the casebook excerpt. CB p. 656; Scalia concurrence.
Justice Alito concurred in the judgment. He agreed with Scalia’s stronger comparative threshold and argued
that only allegations pleaded with the required particularity should contribute to the scienter inference.
Justice Stevens dissented; his separate writing is omitted from the excerpt. He preferred a probable-cause

Notes
Narrow reading: this decision interprets the PSLRA’s special scienter-pleading requirement. Broader
procedural lesson: a legislature can make comparative inferential strength part of a pleading threshold. Do
not replace ordinary Rule 8 plausibility with Tellabs’s at-least-as-compelling test merely because both
involve factual inferences. CB pp. 655–658; opinion pts. II–IV.
Distinguish the two statutory tasks: specifying each misleading statement and why it is misleading, and
pleading particular facts supporting the required mental state. This appeal addressed the latter. The Court
expressly did not decide whether or when recklessness suffices as scienter, so the casebook’s general
background discussion should not be presented as an additional holding of Tellabs. CB pp. 654–657; opinion
pt. II & n. 3.
