# Matrixx Initiatives, Inc. v. Siracusano

**563 U.S. 27 (2011) · Supreme Court of the United States · March 22, 2011 · Civil Procedure**

A securities-fraud complaint could adequately plead materiality and scienter concerning reports of a drug's adverse effects without alleging statistically significant proof of causation. The Court rejected a categorical statistical threshold and evaluated the particular reports, public statements, and alleged conduct together. Justice Sotomayor wrote for a unanimous Court. The assigned account appears in CivPro physical page 346, printed page 659, Note 4 following *Tellabs*.

Matrixx developed and marketed over-the-counter products through a subsidiary. Its Zicam Cold Remedy, containing zinc gluconate and sold in intranasal forms, allegedly generated approximately seventy percent of sales. Purchasers of Matrixx securities between October 22, 2003 and February 6, 2004 brought a fraud class action against the company and three executives. They alleged that public statements about product safety and revenue prospects were misleading because the defendants withheld information linking Zicam to anosmia, or loss of smell. The Supreme Court reviewed the allegations as true for this motion; it did not find that every reported adverse event was caused by Zicam. **31–32.**

The complaint identified more than isolated consumer anecdotes. In 1999, a physician contacted Matrixx about a cluster of patients and prior studies concerning intranasal zinc. In 2002, researcher Miriam Linschoten discussed a patient's loss of smell with research executive Timothy Clarot, supplied study abstracts, and learned that Matrixx had not conducted its own studies. By September 2003, Dr. Bruce Jafek and Linschoten planned a national medical-conference presentation describing patients who lost smell after Zicam use. Matrixx objected to use of its product name, and Jafek removed that name from the presentation. During the securities class period, nine plaintiffs filed four product-liability suits. The Court noted uncertainty whether those plaintiffs overlapped with the patients described by medical professionals, rather than counting them as necessarily additional victims. **32–33; 45 note 11.**

Despite this information, Matrixx predicted strong growth and increased its revenue guidance from over fifty percent to eighty percent. Its SEC filing warned generally about potential product-liability claims without identifying existing suits. After adverse publicity in January 2004 and a stock decline, the company publicly called the asserted anosmia connection unfounded and invoked clinical studies supporting safety. The price recovered, then fell sharply after a February 6 television report. A later company filing described a scientific panel's conclusion that evidence was insufficient to determine whether the product affected smell. The complaint claimed these statements and omissions maintained an artificially high securities price. **33–36.**

The district court dismissed for inadequate allegations of materiality and scienter. It demanded a statistically significant correlation between Zicam and anosmia and found insufficient particularized allegations that the defendants disbelieved their safety statements or sought personal profit. The Ninth Circuit reversed. The Supreme Court **affirmed that appellate judgment**, holding the complaint sufficient on the disputed elements. It did not award investors damages, establish medical causation as fact, or decide whether the allegations would ultimately be proved. **36–38, 50.**

Materiality asks whether a reasonable investor would substantially likely regard the omitted information as significantly altering the total mix available. This is a contextual inquiry. As in *Basic*, selecting one fact as always decisive could exclude information investors actually consider important. Statistical significance can matter, but medical researchers and regulators also consider biological plausibility, temporal association, strength and consistency of evidence, competing explanations, and the seriousness of the risk compared with a drug's benefit. Rare events or ethical constraints can prevent statistically significant studies without eliminating meaningful evidence. The Court neither defined all reliable evidence of medical causation nor adjudicated the admission of expert testimony in the cases it cited. **38–43, notes 4–9.**

The Court also rejected the opposite extreme. The mere existence of an adverse report does not automatically make the information material or prove that the drug caused the event. Something more is needed, drawn from the report's source, content, and context. Nor do § 10(b) and Rule 10b–5 impose a duty to disclose every material fact. Disclosure under these provisions is necessary when required to keep the statements actually made from being misleading. Companies can therefore affect their disclosure obligations through what they choose to say. **43–45, note 10.**

This complaint crossed the materiality pleading threshold. Medical professionals had described a plausible causal relationship and supplied earlier biological studies; the company allegedly lacked a basis to dismiss those findings outright. The alleged risk of losing smell could outweigh relief of ordinary cold symptoms, particularly with alternative products available. Because Zicam allegedly supplied most company sales, its viability mattered to investors evaluating the company's favorable projections and categorical safety statements. The alleged facts permitted a reasonable expectation that discovery would substantiate materiality. The Court evaluated plausibility without requiring proof that the studies conclusively established human causation; it separately addressed the objections about zinc sulfate versus gluconate and animal research. **45–47, notes 12–13.**

Scienter required a distinct analysis. Under the PSLRA and *Tellabs*, particularized facts must yield a cogent inference at least as compelling as plausible opposing inferences, assessed holistically. Absence of a personal-profit motive was relevant but not decisive. The Court assumed without deciding that deliberate recklessness sufficed because Matrixx did not challenge that legal premise. Note 14 separately reserved the statutory actual-knowledge issue for forward-looking statements, which Matrixx had not argued applied. Neither reservation became a general holding on scienter's outer limits. **48, note 14.**

The collective allegations supported the inference that Matrixx withheld information because it understood the likely market effect, rather than because it regarded the reports as meaningless. It allegedly hired a consultant, sought participation in animal research, assembled a scientific panel, suppressed use of Zicam's name at the presentation, and issued safety assurances suggesting that studies disproved the risk when its own evidence did not establish that. Note 15 recognizes that careful investigation may supply an innocent inference in other cases; here, the allegedly misleading release made the scienter inference at least as compelling. The plaintiffs adequately pleaded that element, while proof remained for later proceedings. **49–50, note 15.**

Several source limits refine the ruling. The Court did not decide whether some growth language was nonactionable puffery, because Matrixx had not advanced that argument. An accounting-reserve allegation was not part of the appellate court's reliance. Note 5 distinguishes the contemporaneous absence of an FDA reporting obligation for these over-the-counter products from Congress's later reporting legislation. The decision's discussion of regulatory evidence does not itself impose a new blanket securities-disclosure rule or validate every adverse report. **33 note 2; 34 note 3; 39 note 5.**
