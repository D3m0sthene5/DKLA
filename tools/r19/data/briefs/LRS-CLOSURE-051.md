# Campaign Legal Center v. Federal Election Commission
31 F.4th 781 (D.C. Cir. 2022) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Disclosure dispute and administrative proceedings

Campaign Legal Center, a nonprofit watchdog, and its director and registered voter Catherine Hinckley Kelley filed an October 2016 FEC complaint about Correct the Record and Hillary for America. They alleged that Correct the Record coordinated millions of dollars in activities with Clinton’s presidential campaign, making excessive and impermissibly funded in-kind contributions that neither organization reported as such. Correct the Record openly acknowledged coordination but claimed an exception for unpaid internet communications covered associated input costs. It reported $9,617,828.28 in aggregate expenditures without designating any spending as coordinated contributions; the campaign likewise did not report receipt or expenditure of that assistance. The plaintiffs did not know the coordinated portion and did not allege that every expenditure necessarily fell outside the exception. (Official opinion 7–9, 19–20.)

FECA regulates contributions, including coordinated expenditures treated as in-kind contributions, through amount limits, source restrictions, and disclosure duties. Political committees must report qualifying expenditures and contributions with transaction information, and an authorized campaign committee reports in-kind assistance it receives as contributions and expenditures. The internet rules distinguish paid communications from unpaid internet activity. The substantive scope of that exception was disputed here; the court’s standing judgment did not finally adjudicate all input costs’ legal status. (4–6.)

The FEC General Counsel recommended finding reason to believe violations had occurred, reasoning that activities such as opposition research, message development, staff and office costs, and surrogate work did not become exempt communication costs simply because something was later transmitted online. The Commission then had four rather than its authorized six members and split 2–2. Without the required four affirmative votes, it dismissed the complaint. The two controlling commissioners accepted the broad input-cost interpretation in their statement of reasons. The General Counsel’s view and the commissioners’ competing rationale are administrative positions, not established findings that all alleged violations occurred. (8–10.)

## The District Court’s change of position and this appeal

The plaintiffs sued in August 2019 under FECA and the APA. The FEC lacked four votes to authorize a defense and did not appear; Correct the Record and Hillary for America intervened as defendants over the plaintiffs’ objection. Initially the District Court accepted informational standing and organizational resource injury and considered the broad internet interpretation contrary to law. At summary judgment it changed its standing conclusion. Relying on Wertheimer, it treated identification of coordinated spending within already reported expenditures as only a requested legal determination, rather than new facts, and dismissed the FECA claim. A separate opinion dismissed the APA claim as precluded by FECA. (10–12.)

The plaintiffs appealed only the FECA standing dismissal. On April 19, 2022, Senior Judge Edwards’s opinion for Rogers, Walker, and Edwards unanimously **reversed and remanded for further proceedings**. There is no separate writing or judicial footnote in the complete 22-page opinion. Standing review was de novo, with the plaintiffs bearing the burden. The court decided neither the ultimate internet-exception merits nor the unappealed APA dismissal. (1, 12–13, 22.)

## An unknown numerical breakdown is factual information

Under Akins and the circuit’s informational-injury decisions, the plaintiffs had to identify information that their reading of FECA required to be disclosed, whose absence harmed an interest Congress sought to protect, and that could actually be obtained if their legal challenge succeeded. An abstract desire to learn whether somebody violated the law or to enforce it was insufficient. So was already accessible information whose repetition would merely duplicate another source, or a trivial addition to what was public. Widely shared harm could nevertheless be concrete: election-financing information helps voters assess candidates and the role of financial assistance in a particular election. (13–16.)

The aggregate disclosures did not tell these plaintiffs how much Correct the Record had contributed in coordination with the campaign. If their exception theory succeeded, the respondents would have to disaggregate reported overhead and mixed activities into coordinated and noncoordinated amounts. Those unknown numerical amounts were factual information about campaign financing, not simply a legal label pasted onto an already known transaction. The statutory reporting system protected the plaintiffs’ interest in using that information to evaluate candidates. (16–18.)

The court illustrated the point with the District Court’s salary example. A reported biweekly salary payment to David Brock was $4,521.56. If half represented coordinated work, reporting would divide it into two $2,260.78 portions. But the one-half assumption was invented for illustration; neither the plaintiffs nor the District Court knew the actual coordinated amount. Revealing the true division would disclose a new fact. The opinion did not find that Brock’s actual coordinated fraction was 50%. (17–18.)

Wertheimer concerned transactions already disclosed as coordinated by political-party committees. Requiring the candidate to report those same transactions would supply the same information from another source. Here neither organization had supplied the coordinated breakdown. Nor had the plaintiffs manufactured injury by changing an earlier all-expenditures theory: from the start they claimed many activities were coordinated and that the total coordinated amount was unknown. Their record-based request for a nontrivial, statutorily required breakdown therefore met the informational-injury rule. (18–20.)

The District Court’s alternative would permit increasingly broad lump-sum reporting to defeat standing even when a disclosure claim had legal merit. Disaggregated contributions cannot be calculated from an aggregate that does not identify them. The FEC’s dismissal caused the continuing informational deprivation, and success in setting aside the Commission’s allegedly unlawful determination would likely redress it. This established injury, causation, and redressability without treating success on the ultimate FECA interpretation as already adjudicated. (20–22.)
