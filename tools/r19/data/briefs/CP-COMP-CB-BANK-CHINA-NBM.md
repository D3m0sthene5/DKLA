# Bank of China, New York Branch v. NBM L.L.C.

**192 F. Supp. 2d 183 (S.D.N.Y. 2002) — United States District Court, Southern District of New York, March 18, 2002.** District Judge Chin. Civil Procedure: prejudgment attachment, security for a potential judgment, preliminary injunction, and bankruptcy-related restraint. Assigned: CivPro PDF 602, printed 1190, Note 2 before the preliminary-injunction heading.

## Facts and procedural course

Bank of China’s New York branch sued a group of companies and individuals over a credit relationship dating to 1991. The Bank alleged that purportedly independent borrowers were controlled by John Chou and his family, and that loans supposedly financing commercial transactions actually moved money among related entities. It sought $34 million, asserting loan defaults, common-law wrongdoing, and a RICO conspiracy. Alleged devices included false shipping documents, unauthorized changes to the Bank’s mortgages, and attempts to move collateral offshore. The decision concerns evidence supporting provisional remedies; it does not finally establish every fraud allegation. **185.**

On March 14, 2001, Judge Jones issued an ex parte attachment covering defendants’ New York assets. The Bank posted the ordered $5,000 undertaking, delivered the order to the Marshal, and timely sought confirmation following levy. Initially identified attached property included a $720,000 GEG account at Ka Wah Bank; further accounts and real property were later attached. Defendants moved to vacate. The Bank also sought a Rule 65 injunction over New Jersey assets. **185–186.**

After reassignment, Judge Chin denied separate dismissal motions in November 2001 and heard the attachment and injunction motions. Meanwhile NBM, Chou, and Sherry Liu filed for Chapter 11 protection in New Jersey. They and the Bank jointly sought a stay concerning property in that bankruptcy proceeding. The March 2002 decision addresses confirmation, vacatur, the preliminary injunction, and that joint stay request. **184–186.**

## Issue and parties’ positions

The Bank claimed both statutory grounds relating to nonresident defendants and fraudulent disposition of property. Defendants responded that their appearance supplied personal jurisdiction, eliminating the jurisdictional need for attachment, and disputed the Bank’s evidence of fraudulent intent. The question was whether the attachment could nevertheless continue as security for a future judgment on the record presented, with corresponding injunctive protection for assets outside the attachment’s reach. **186–188.**

## Governing attachment standards

Rule 64 made state law govern the attachment remedy. Under New York law, the Bank had to establish a statutory ground, the need to continue the levy, and probable success on the merits. Attachment remained a harsh, extraordinary, discretionary remedy even where statutory criteria were satisfied. **186.**

The relevant grounds included nonresident or unqualified foreign defendants and disposition, concealment, encumbrance, or removal of property with intent to defraud creditors or frustrate judgment enforcement. Section 6212(a) also required written evidence of a cause of action, probable merits success, a qualifying ground, and a demand exceeding known counterclaims. The Bank’s sworn proof and written affirmation met that preliminary burden. **186–187.**

The January 2002 counterclaims did not defeat the demand requirement. The Bank’s demand exceeded known counterclaims when it moved, and the court’s cited interpretation did not require treating every disputed claim for a large amount as a conceded just counterclaim. The Bank did not concede the massive amounts defendants subsequently demanded. **187 n.1.**

## Jurisdiction was not the reason to continue attachment

Defendants filed pre-answer motions or answers without asserting lack of personal jurisdiction and had waived that objection. The court therefore agreed that attachment was unnecessary to establish jurisdiction over them. The Bank could not obtain the requested relief on that justification. **187–188.**

Security required a different inquiry. The Bank had to demonstrate a genuine threat to enforcement, rather than simply invoke defendants’ residence or the possibility that their money might leave New York. Evidence of fraudulent intent mattered; suspicion or a speculative future transfer was insufficient. The court demanded concrete facts showing that drastic security measures were needed. **188.**

## Concrete evidence justified continued restraint

The court found such evidence in several connected transactions. CBL attempted to transfer $1,061,000 in pledged funds to a Cayman Islands account. The Bank’s official identified materially different signatures supposedly made by the same signer on related agreements, supporting an inference that at least one was forged. The Bank intervened and intercepted the funds; otherwise the collateral would have gone offshore. **188–189.**

Fifteen 1999 loans totaling $8,772,036 ostensibly financed NBM purchases from GEG. Cargo receipts purportedly documented the transactions, yet almost every time the Bank sent funds to GEG, GEG sent them back to NBM that same day. Investigation supplied concrete grounds to regard the transactions as shams, including doubt about the existence of the named cargo-receipt company. **189.**

Other evidence concerned properties serving as loan collateral. The Bank alleged that its account officer, acting with defendants without authority, subordinated its first mortgage on a New Jersey property, enabling defendants to borrow another $2 million. A different mortgaged property had been sold using a purported Bank release the Bank said it never authorized. Maritime information and the bills themselves also supported claims that shipping documents identified false ports or nonexistent ships, inducing financing for transactions that never occurred. **189–190.**

Defendants’ explanations did not answer the specific proof. A statement offered to explain the Cayman movement concerned an account eight years earlier. Saying properties had long been for sale did not explain allegedly unauthorized mortgage subordination. Denying responsibility for shipping-document accuracy likewise did not credibly refute the evidence of fabricated transactions. Judge Jones had also previously observed the companies’ use as nominees and the concealment of transactions through their dealings. **190–191.**

Judge Chin concluded that the evidence established a real risk that fraudulent asset disposition would make a future judgment unenforceable. That supported confirmation. He expressly preserved the Bank’s obligation to prove its allegations at trial. Probable success and security need were not a final damages adjudication. **191.**

## Injunction, bankruptcy exception, and exact disposition

For the requested injunction, the court applied the then-stated circuit standard of likely irreparable injury plus either likely merits success or sufficiently serious merits questions with the balance of hardships strongly favoring the movant. Although ordinary monetary loss usually did not justify injunction, the cited decisions permitted preservation of assets where evidence showed an intent to frustrate collection. The same concrete conduct supported irreparable injury and probable success here. **191–192.**

The injunction addressed New Jersey assets potentially beyond the attachment. Both sides agreed the attachment did not implicate the Summit Bank account. In a related interpleader action, Fidelity had been directed to deposit restrained assets with the clerk; the injunction would prevent disposition even if those accounts proved outside the attachment’s scope. That related order is explained in the footnote, not silently treated as this opinion’s separate merits judgment. **191 n.2.**

The court **confirmed attachment except as to the debtor-defendants’ property subject to the New Jersey bankruptcy proceeding; denied vacatur; granted the preliminary injunction; and granted the joint stay regarding that bankruptcy property**. The Bank was to submit an order on notice within five business days. Recognizing the burden of continued asset restraints, the court set trial for June 17, 2002 and a pretrial conference for April 19. There was no jury verdict or final $34 million award. **192.**
