Parties
Thomas & Betts and the other respondents were the plaintiffs in an equity suit alleging patent infringement.
Electrical Fittings and the other petitioners were the accused infringers. Although the infringement suit ended
in the defendants’ favor, they appealed the portion of the decree that formally adjudged one patent claim valid.
Their status as successful defendants created the question whether they could seek appellate correction of an
adverse statement in the operative decree. 307 U.S. at 241–242.
Material Facts
The infringement action concerned two patent claims. The district court determined that claim 1 was valid but
not infringed and that claim 2 was invalid. The report does not describe the invention, accused device, or
evidentiary basis for these determinations. Those technical matters were unnecessary to the Supreme Court’s
procedural question and cannot be supplied from this opinion. Id. at 241–242.
The district court did more than dismiss the infringement complaint. Its decree expressly adjudged claim 1
valid while dismissing the bill because infringement had not been proved. The finding of noninfringement
already sufficed to defeat the suit, so an operative adjudication of validity was unnecessary to the disposition.
The patent owners did not appeal and filed a disclaimer of claim 2 in the Patent Office. The remaining
controversy on review concerned the formal declaration about claim 1. Id. at 242.
Procedural History
The defendants appealed to the Second Circuit solely from the portion of the decree adjudicating claim 1’s
validity. The court of appeals dismissed the appeal because the defendants had obtained all the relief to which
they were entitled and the litigation had ended in their favor. It also reasoned that the validity determination
would not bind them in later litigation. The Supreme Court granted certiorari because of an asserted conflict
among appellate decisions. Id. at 242 & nn.1–3.
The Supreme Court did not reopen the entire infringement dispute. It considered whether the court of appeals
had authority to entertain a narrowly directed appeal seeking correction of the decree. It concluded that such an
appeal was proper and remanded with instructions governing that correction. Id. at 242–243.
Issue
May a defendant who has defeated an infringement suit appeal an unnecessary adverse adjudication of patent
validity contained in the decree itself, even though a prevailing party ordinarily cannot appeal merely to
challenge unfavorable findings? If so, should the appeal produce a decision on the patent’s validity or a
narrower revision of the decree? Id. at 242.

Rule and Holding
A party ordinarily may not appeal a favorable judgment merely to obtain review of findings that the party
considers erroneous and that are unnecessary to the judgment. But a party may seek elimination of an
unnecessary adverse adjudication contained in the operative decree itself. The defendants were entitled to
removal of the decree’s declaration that claim 1 was valid. The appellate court could entertain the appeal for
that purpose without adjudicating validity on the merits. Id. at 242.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Second Circuit’s dismissal of the appeal and remanded with instructions to
entertain it and direct the district court to reform its decree. The correction was to eliminate the unnecessary
validity adjudication. The Court did not hold claim 1 invalid, reinstate the infringement action, or disturb the
defendants’ success in defeating that action. Id. at 242–243.
Reasoning
The starting point was the rule limiting appeals by prevailing parties. Appellate review ordinarily concerns an
adverse judgment, rather than every statement or finding in the reasoning that a successful party would prefer
to erase. A defendant cannot use a victory as the occasion for an advisory appellate ruling about an issue that
had no effect on the disposition. The Court expressly retained that principle. Id. at 242.
The operative decree made this case different. The district court had included a formal adjudication of claim
1’s validity, even though the failure to prove infringement already required dismissal. The adverse matter
therefore was not merely an unnecessary explanation in an opinion. It stood in the decree as a judicial
adjudication of an issue the parties had litigated. The defendants were entitled to have that portion removed. Id.
The court of appeals’ belief that the declaration would not bind the defendants later did not justify leaving the
unnecessary adjudication in place. The Supreme Court did not resolve the full range of possible future
preclusion consequences. Instead, it identified a present, narrower corrective remedy: the decree should reflect
the disposition warranted by the infringement adjudication without the superfluous declaration of validity. Id.
That remedy also confined the appellate court’s role. Entertaining the appeal did not authorize an appellate
determination that claim 1 was invalid. The appeal could be resolved by directing reformation of the judgment,
leaving the technical validity question undecided on review. This avoided turning the exception for correcting
an adverse decree into a general license for prevailing litigants to obtain review of unnecessary findings. Id. at
242–243.
The distinction is therefore between the reasoning supporting a judgment and what the judgment formally
adjudicates. An unnecessary finding in the court’s explanation ordinarily does not give the winner a basis to
appeal. An unnecessary adverse adjudication incorporated into the decree can warrant an appeal limited to
deleting it. The particular language and legal operation of the decree determine which situation is presented. Id.
at 242.
Separate Opinions
No separate opinion or nonparticipation is reported in the judicial text. Justice Roberts’s short opinion resolves
only the defendants’ entitlement to correction of the decree and the proper scope of the appellate remedy. It
supplies no separate reasoning about the technical merits of either patent claim. Id. at 241–243.

issue in the appellant’s favor. Id. at 242–243.
