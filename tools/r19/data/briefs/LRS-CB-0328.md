Parties
The plaintiffs owned land abutting a Denver street and sought to remove the lien resulting from a special
assessment for paving that street. Their objections concerned both Denver's authority to undertake the
improvement and the allocation of its cost to their particular properties. Denver and the municipal officials
defended the assessment. The Supreme Court calls the owners “plaintiffs in error” because they sought review
of the Colorado Supreme Court's adverse judgment by writ of error. 210 U.S. at 374–375.
The municipal bodies performed different functions. The board of public works initiated the project and
calculated the proposed distribution of costs. The city council authorized the improvement and later sat as a
board of equalization to consider assessment objections. The relevant constitutional failure occurred when the
council performed the latter function. Id. at 375–378, 384–386.
Material Facts
Denver's charter permitted local improvements funded by assessments against specially benefited property.
Before ordering paving, the board of public works was to obtain a petition from owners representing a majority
of the affected frontage, prepare specifications and a district map, estimate the cost and approximate
assessment of each lot, and provide the prescribed initial notice. The board then transmitted a resolution and
proposed ordinance to the council. The council could approve or reject the ordinance, and its determination that
the preliminary conditions had been satisfied was conclusive under state law. Id. at 375–378.
The later assessment stage had separate protections. Once costs were ascertainable, the board prepared the total
cost, including collection expenses and interest, and apportioned it among individual lots. The clerk was to
publish notice identifying the cost and each property's proposed share. Owners had thirty days to file written
objections, which the council, sitting as a board of equalization, was to hear before the assessment ordinance
passed. Under the state court's construction, that hearing covered relevant evidence and objections to the
assessment's validity and amount; matters cognizable there could not subsequently be relitigated in court. Id. at
376–377, 380.
After the work, the clerk published a notice inviting written objections and promising that they would be heard
before assessment. It specified the filing deadline, but did not identify a hearing time. The owners timely
submitted a detailed protest on January 13, 1900. They challenged the petition, contracting, notices, charges,
and district boundaries. They also asserted that some benefited property was omitted, some assessed property
received no benefit, individual parcels were charged more than their benefits, and equal charges did not
correspond to equal benefits. These were objections in their filing, not Supreme Court findings that the
proposed amounts were actually excessive. Id. at 380–384.

The council met in a specially called session without notifying the objectors of that session or allowing them to
argue or present proof. It characterized their papers as challenging the city's general assessment authority rather
than the allocation of costs, and confirmed the proposed apportionments. It then enacted the assessment
ordinance without a further hearing. The record thus showed an opportunity to file objections, followed by a
decision without an opportunity to support them. Id. at 384–385.
Procedural History
The owners brought a Colorado state-court proceeding seeking relief from the assessment. The trial court
granted relief and found, among other things, that the required majority petition had not been obtained and that
no opportunity for a hearing had been afforded. The Colorado Supreme Court reversed and directed judgment
for the defendants, treating the assessment as valid under state law. It did not disturb the finding that the
owners had received no hearing. The United States Supreme Court reviewed the federal objections to that
judgment; the state supreme court's construction of Colorado law was binding for that purpose. Id. at 374,
378–380.
The Court addressed several asserted errors separately. It rejected the challenge to the conclusive municipal
finding concerning the preliminary petition, treated another objection to the state court's interpretation as
presenting no federal question, and rejected the suggestion that the state court's interpretive method itself
violated the Fourteenth Amendment. It accepted the charter as constitutionally valid when read to require a
meaningful assessment hearing, then examined whether the city had administered it accordingly. The
assessment-stage due process claim supplied the basis for reversal. Id. at 378–381, 385–386.
Issue
When a state delegates to a municipal body the determination of who will pay a special assessment and how
much each property will bear, does due process permit the body to make the assessment final after accepting
written objections but denying the owners a notified opportunity to support those objections through argument
and relevant proof? Separately, did due process require a hearing before the city authorized the improvement or
conclusively determined that the preliminary petition requirement was met? Id. at 378–381, 385–386.
Rule and Holding
When a subordinate body determines whether a tax will be levied, its amount, and its allocation among
particular taxpayers, the affected owner must receive notice and an opportunity to be heard at some point
before the assessment becomes irrevocably fixed. In these circumstances, permission to submit written
objections alone was insufficient. The owner had to be allowed to support the allegations with argument,
however brief, and proof, if needed, however informal. Denver denied that opportunity and therefore denied
due process. Id. at 385–386.
The earlier authorization of the improvement could occur without a constitutionally required hearing, provided
a hearing was available on the eventual assessment. Those preliminary actions neither imposed an assessment
nor necessarily required one. The state could also make the council's finding about the petition conclusive; the
Federal Constitution did not independently require that preliminary petition safeguard. Id. at 378–379.

Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Colorado Supreme Court's judgment. It held the assessment void and stated
that the owners were entitled to a decree discharging their lands from the lien attributable to it. The Court did
not calculate a corrected assessment, decide the amount of special benefit to each lot, or award damages.
Having resolved the case on procedural grounds, it expressly declined to decide the separate assignment
asserting that assessments on corner lots exceeded their worth and benefits. Id. at 375, 386.
Reasoning
The analysis depended on separating the project-authorization stage from the later imposition of a financial
burden on particular property. Authorizing paving established the possibility of an assessment, while the
subsequent process determined its validity and each owner's allocated amount. Because the legislature could
have authorized the improvement without any petition, its choice to require one did not make every dispute
about compliance with that preliminary safeguard a federal due process issue. An opportunity to contest the
assessment itself could supply the constitutionally necessary protection. Id. at 377–379.
The state court's interpretation of the charter was also consequential. It understood the statute to provide a
hearing before assessment and to permit the city to supply whatever additional notice that hearing required. On
that interpretation, the statute was not facially unconstitutional. The Supreme Court therefore focused on the
actual proceeding rather than treating a defective implementation as proof that the legislature lacked
assessment authority altogether. Id. at 379–381.
Denver's written-objection notice served one function but omitted another. It told owners when their papers
were due and promised that the objections would be considered before action. It did not tell them when they
could appear to support those papers. The council had no stated equalization sessions supplying an
independently knowable hearing time and instead met specially without notice. Filing within the thirty-day
period could not itself provide notice of, or participation in, that later session. Id. at 380–381, 384–385.
The objections also supplied concrete subjects for a hearing. Alongside broad legal attacks, the filing disputed
the relationship between special benefits and charges to individual parcels. The council's recital that no one
objected to apportionment did not eliminate those allegations. The Supreme Court reproduced the filing at
length and treated it as a complaint against the proposed assessment. It did not accept the municipality's
characterization as a substitute for considering the owners' actual position. Id. at 381–385.
The Court recognized that taxation ordinarily permits considerable procedural flexibility. It did not demand
every feature of a judicial trial. A brief opportunity for argument and an informal opportunity for relevant proof
could suffice. What mattered was that an owner facing an individualized assessment could do more than lodge
an unsupported objection that officials then rejected in the owner's absence. The decision therefore identifies a
minimum participatory requirement while leaving much of the hearing's organization unspecified. Id. at
385–386.
The absence of later judicial consideration reinforced the significance of the administrative opportunity.
Colorado confined assessment objections of this kind to the board of equalization. A landowner could not
compensate for the denied hearing by obtaining an ordinary judicial reconsideration of those same matters. The
Court expressly called attention to that feature before holding the paper-submission procedure insufficient. The
resulting rule should be read in that procedural setting rather than detached from the availability and timing of
review. Id. at 380, 386.

Finally, the municipal character of the council did not remove its conduct from the Fourteenth Amendment.
When it sat as the board of equalization, it represented the state for the assessment function. The
constitutionally defective decision was therefore attributable to the state, and the lien founded on that
assessment could not stand. Id. at 386.
Separate Opinions
The report records that the Chief Justice and Justice Holmes dissented. It supplies no dissenting opinion or
explanation of their grounds. Their disagreement should not be supplied from Holmes's later opinion in
Bi-Metallic or attributed to a particular argument made by Denver. 210 U.S. at 386.
Notes
The assigned excerpt foregrounds the owners' extensive written protest and the council's response. That detail
explains why the case cannot accurately be reduced to “no notice was given”: notice of the proposed
assessment and a filing opportunity existed, while notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard on the
objections did not. The full opinion additionally develops the binding state-law construction and the
unsuccessful preliminary challenges. Casebook pp. 328–334; 210 U.S. at 378–386.
Read with Bi-Metallic, the operative inquiry concerns what the government is deciding and the grounds on
which particular people are affected. A small set of parcel-specific benefit assessments supplies information
that individual owners can contest. A general adjustment applicable across the taxing community presents a
different problem. The later opinion expressly describes Londoner as involving a relatively small group
exceptionally affected on individual grounds. Bi-Metallic, 239 U.S. 441, 445–446, read in this edition.
The holding does not establish an automatic entitlement to a courtroom-style trial, cross-examination, or
judicial determination of every tax. It requires a meaningful opportunity at the relevant assessment stage in the
circumstances actually presented. The decree's elimination of this lien follows from the defective assessment; it
does not establish immunity from a properly imposed future assessment. 210 U.S. at 385–386.
