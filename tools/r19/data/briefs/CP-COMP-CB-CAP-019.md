# Grace v. MacArthur

**Civil Procedure · CP-COMP-CB-CAP-019**  
**170 F. Supp. 442 (E.D. Ark. 1959)** · February 16, 1959 · Civil No. B-306  
**Henley, District Judge, ruling on individual defendants' motions to quash service.**  
**Assigned:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 73, printed page 88, the account in Note 4 of service on a passenger flying over Arkansas.

## The lawsuit and the two different service questions

Preston and Charlotte Grace, Arkansas citizens, sued John MacArthur, Ronnie Smith, and Bankers Life & Casualty Company for breach of a written property-exchange agreement and, alternatively, specific performance of that agreement and alleged amendments. Bankers Life was an Illinois corporation authorized to conduct insurance business in Arkansas. MacArthur, an Illinois citizen, was its controlling shareholder, chairman, and president. Smith was a Tennessee citizen. The complaint alleged that Smith acted for himself and as agent for both other defendants. Those were allegations of agency, not findings resolving it. CAP opinion paragraphs 2–3.

The complaint and summons issued July 21, 1958. The corporation was served through Arkansas's Insurance Commissioner and did not challenge that service in these motions. MacArthur was served through Arkansas's Secretary of State under a statute reaching certain nonresidents who did business or performed work or services in Arkansas. Smith was personally served aboard Braniff Flight 337, a nonstop flight from Memphis to Dallas. The marshal's return stated that delivery occurred at 5:16 p.m. while the aircraft was directly above Pine Bluff in the Eastern District of Arkansas. The two individuals raised distinct objections, which the court treated separately. Id. ¶¶ 4–6.

Smith did not dispute the marshal's account of where and how he was served. He argued that a person in an aircraft above the state was outside its “territorial limits” for purposes of then-Rule 4(f). MacArthur instead denied doing business or work in Arkansas and denied any valid statutory basis for personal service on him through the Secretary of State. Id. ¶¶ 5–6.

## Holding concerning the aircraft passenger

The court **denied Smith's motion to quash**. A passenger on an ordinary commercial aircraft traveling through the regular navigable airspace above Arkansas was within Arkansas's territorial limits and could be personally served under then-Rule 4(f). That Rule, reproduced in the opinion's footnote, generally confined service in a simple diversity case to the state in which the district court sat; no federal statute extended the permissible service territory here. The question was therefore whether the aircraft's position fell inside that territory. Id. ¶¶ 7, 22, 31 & concluding note.

The court approached that question through the state's continuing jurisdiction over airspace rather than treating flight as an exemption from ordinary service. Earlier decisions recognized state authority over persons and aircraft above state territory, subject to controlling federal powers. Arkansas's aeronautics legislation also addressed crimes, torts, contracts, and passenger injuries occurring in aircraft over the state. The court reasoned that such legislation presupposed that the aircraft and occupants were within its territorial boundaries. It used a federal aircraft-smuggling decision as another example of a flight's entry into a judicial district even without landing there. Id. ¶¶ 9–14.

Smith's federal-airspace argument did not change that conclusion. Congress had asserted national sovereignty over navigable airspace and extensively regulated air traffic, but those enactments did not withdraw every element of state sovereignty or remove airspace from state boundaries. Relying on the Supreme Court's Braniff tax decision, the court distinguished national regulation under the commerce power from exclusive national ownership or elimination of all compatible state authority. A state's territorial capacity to serve an individual could coexist with federal regulation of the aircraft carrying him. Id. ¶¶ 15–21.

The result also followed, in the court's view, from an ordinary transportation comparison: an interstate traveler is not exempt from personal service merely because he is passing through, whether by car, bus, train, or aircraft. Flight might shorten the duration of presence, but that was a difference in degree rather than principle. The court expressly limited its discussion to the ordinary navigable airspace of 1958. It did not resolve service at hypothetical future altitudes so high that ordinary territorial concepts might become unrealistic. Id. ¶¶ 22–23.

## The unresolved motion concerning MacArthur

The opinion did **not** deny both individuals' jurisdictional objections on the aircraft rationale. MacArthur's arguments concerned the Arkansas statute: he claimed the contract was completed in Illinois, the cause of action did not arise from the asserted Arkansas activity, an isolated transaction was insufficient under the statute, and its application would discriminate unconstitutionally against nonresidents in contract actions. The court considered those questions potentially serious but deferred ruling until trial. Id. ¶¶ 24–29.

The agency issue overlapped the jurisdictional and merits questions. Whether Smith represented MacArthur involved disputed facts; the plaintiffs' theory of Arkansas business activity depended on that agency. Immediate dismissal of MacArthur would not dispose of the action against the other defendants, and his relationship to the corporation meant he probably would need to testify anyway. If dismissed erroneously, his part of the case might require a second trial. Developing the evidence first could clarify or eliminate some questions, including the constitutional one. Id. ¶¶ 29–30.

The court preserved MacArthur's ability to renew his personal-jurisdiction contentions in his answer. Its final order reserved his motion until trial and required both individual defendants to answer within ten days after receiving the memorandum and order. It neither found that Smith was MacArthur's agent nor finally determined that MacArthur was subject to personal jurisdiction. Id. ¶¶ 29–33.

## What the assignment illustrates

The textbook's brief account correctly identifies Smith's in-flight service and the refusal to quash it. The case caption can obscure that the passenger actually served was Smith, while the motion of the named defendant MacArthur remained undecided. The full opinion supplies that distinction and also shows that the holding interpreted the territorial scope of then-Rule 4(f) in an ordinary commercial-flight setting. It is not a holding about the validity of the property contract, breach damages, exclusive state control of aviation, or every conceivable high-altitude vehicle.
