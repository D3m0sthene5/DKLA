# Pacelli v. Pacelli
319 N.J. Super. 185, 725 A.2d 56 (App. Div. 1999) | Contracts

## Decision and exact scope

On March 9, 1999, the New Jersey Superior Court, Appellate Division, reversed enforcement of Antonio and Francesca Pacelli's agreement governing economic rights in a future divorce. Judge D'Annunzio wrote for Judges Petrella and Cuff; no separate opinion appears. The agreement's inherently coercive setting required close scrutiny, and its terms were unfair both when executed and when enforcement was sought. The court expressly reserved whether agreements of this type are categorically unenforceable because of undue coercion. It remanded for equitable distribution, alimony, and other economic determinations rather than awarding the wife a specified share. (185–187, 190–199.)

## Marriage, ultimatum, and agreement

The couple married in June 1975, when Antonio was forty-four and Francesca twenty. She had immigrated from Italy at fourteen. He was a builder, developer, and restaurant owner; his claimed $3 million premarital net worth was unsupported by documents. Their sons were born in 1976 and 1977. The family enjoyed an affluent lifestyle, with gross income of $540,000 in 1984 and $476,000 in 1985. Francesca contributed no income. In mid-1985 Antonio threatened divorce unless she accepted economic terms, moving from the marital bedroom to an apartment over the garage. (187.)

His matrimonial lawyer, Barry Croland, advised him about exposure to equitable distribution and alimony and the need for fair terms, full financial disclosure, and independent representation for his wife. Francesca consulted Gary Skoloff in July and again in fall 1985. He warned that accepting $500,000 in full satisfaction of future distribution and alimony would leave her with much less than an immediate divorce likely would yield. She nevertheless wanted to preserve the family and prevent the children growing up in a broken home. Skoloff testified that she would sign anything to keep the marriage, and that the proposed document was presented without negotiation as the alternative to divorce. He received the husband's draft, financial statements, and four years of tax returns through 1984. She signed in February 1986 and Antonio in March. (188.)

The agreement promised $40,000 immediately and $500,000 upon entry of a final divorce judgment, coupled with waivers of distribution and alimony rights. The marriage continued until Antonio filed for divorce in 1994, when his assets were $14,291,500 and net worth $11,241,500. After a plenary trial the court entered an October 25, 1996 order upholding the agreement, finding no coercion, fairness measured in 1985, and no credible proof of the wife's claimed 1989 mutual cancellation. A July 9, 1997 divorce judgment followed. Francesca appealed; Antonio cross-appealed on credit for interim support and admission of recordings concerning the alleged cancellation. (187–189, 196, 199–200.)

## Why ordinary premarital or divorce-settlement analysis was inadequate

The court affirmed the finding that the parties had not cancelled the agreement because substantial credible evidence supported it. Her claim that Antonio destroyed a signed cancellation paper was not established. Enforceability nevertheless raised distinct legal issues. Existing New Jersey decisions enforced premarital agreements if fair and just, and divorce settlements if fair and equitable. Those contexts did not simply control an ultimatum during an existing, still viable marriage. (189–190.)

A prospective bride can decide whether to enter marriage, whereas Francesca already had a legal marital relationship and two children. The threatened costs included losing an intact family and the perceived stigma of marital failure. At the opposite end, spouses settling a marriage they recognize as over can pursue economic interests in an adversarial setting. Here she was motivated by preserving the relationship, while he wanted to continue it on his terms. Her access to prominent counsel had little relevance to that particular pressure because her choice was driven by family preservation rather than assessment of legal entitlements. (190–191.)

Reconciliation agreements supplied a closer analogy. Nicholson required a genuinely substantial marital rift, fairness in the circumstances and terms, good faith, and attention to changed conditions at enforcement. Evidence from Antonio and his lawyer permitted an inference that the crisis here was artificial: Antonio primarily wanted to limit Francesca's participation in wealth from new business deals and avoid uncertainty about financial exposure. The court also surveyed decisions accepting true reconciliation bargains and contrary concerns that rewarding marital threats can encourage discord and strategic economic leverage. It declined to equate these agreements with ordinary premarital bargains or final divorce settlements. Its holding required careful scrutiny and found these terms unfair, leaving categorical nonenforcement unresolved. (191–195.)

## Unfairness at execution and enforcement

The Uniform Premarital Agreement Act did not govern because this was not a premarital agreement. Although Francesca emphasized fairness in 1994, the court also found the terms unfair when signed. The trial court had treated $540,000 as roughly thirty-two percent of a $1.7 million marital estate. That calculation depended on the husband's financial statement deducting potential income taxes on hypothetical asset sales. Actual listed assets of $7,696,200 less liabilities of $1,643,300 produced $6,053,100 net worth; subtracting hypothetical taxes improperly reduced the figure. No evidence showed that a 1985 distribution would require sale, particularly given substantial liquid assets, receivables, possible in-kind distribution or refinancing, and the statutory treatment of transfers incident to divorce. The court distinguished deducting hypothetical taxes from considering tax consequences in equitable distribution. (195–197.)

Using an approximately $3 million marital estate, the promised $540,000 represented eighteen percent. Both lawyers' testimony placed a potential distribution appreciably higher. The $500,000 also bought out alimony despite annual income averaging about $500,000, a costly family lifestyle, and Antonio's roughly twenty-year life expectancy. The court described a possible substantial annual alimony award, perhaps approaching six figures; it did not award that amount or establish a fixed percentage entitlement. As a further example, the agreement waived Francesca's rights against his estate, including an elective share, although the $500,000 payment required divorce and would not be triggered merely by his death during marriage. (197–198.)

Close scrutiny also required examining enforcement conditions. A spouse's continuing work as mother, homemaker, and helpmate could contribute to later prosperity; conversely a decline in assets might make enforcement unfair to the obligor. In 1994 the husband's net worth exceeded $11 million, with about $8 million in postmarital assets. The fixed $540,000 was about seven percent. Even adding her $600,000 half-interest in the jointly titled Florida home left her below fifteen percent of the marital estate. Given the coercive setting, the result was unfair, inequitable, and unenforceable. Distribution and alimony required fresh trial-court determination. (198–199.)

## Remaining issues and mandate

The court affirmed the finding that Francesca failed to prove an ownership interest in Antonio's Della Presta development based on her claimed investment of the initial $40,000. Her counsel had requested $137,188.99 in fees; the trial court awarded $35,000 with inadequate explanation. The appellate court vacated that award for renewed consideration after the unresolved economic issues were determined. Antonio's requests for a $166,000 interim-support credit against the unenforceable $500,000 obligation and for exclusion of cancellation recordings were moot; his cross-appeal was dismissed. (199–200.)

The exact mandate reversed the October 1996 enforceability order, vacated paragraph two of the July 1997 judgment awarding $35,000 counsel fees, affirmed the divorce judgment in all other respects, and remanded for consistent proceedings. This preserved the divorce itself and the separately affirmed factual determinations, while reopening the economic matters specified by the opinion. (200.)
