# Servomation Mathias, Inc. v. Englert
333 F. Supp. 9 (M.D. Pa. 1971) | Contracts

## Court, interlocutory posture, and disposition

District Judge Muir denied Servomation's application for a preliminary injunction on November 3, 1971. The employer sought to restrain former employee Leonard Englert from managing a college dining hall for a competitor under his employment covenant. The court had denied a temporary restraining order on August 20 because likely success was not established and immediate or irreparable injury did not appear; it held a preliminary-injunction hearing September 2 and considered argument and both briefs. Diversity jurisdiction rested on the Maryland plaintiff's principal place of business in Maryland, Englert's Pennsylvania citizenship, and testimony establishing more than $10,000 in controversy, the then-applicable amount. The opinion supplies findings and conclusions under Rule 52(a). It denies interim relief; it is not a final damages judgment or a permanent-injunction adjudication after a completed merits trial. (9–10, 15.)

## Employment, the lost client, and the covenant

Englert began as a chef for a corporate predecessor in 1964, became assistant manager in June 1965 and manager in March 1966. He received company manuals when promoted to manager. On March 5, 1969, he signed the written agreement providing managerial employment at $175 a week. He worked at Marymount College in Florida until resigning May 15, 1971. (10–11, 14 & n. 9.)

For roughly eleven years before May 1971, Mansfield State College had been served by Servomation Mathias Pa., Inc., the plaintiff's wholly owned Pennsylvania subsidiary. Mansfield announced in April that it would terminate the contract and solicit new bids. The subsidiary bid, but on June 1 Mansfield selected Mark Four Management Services. Several Mark Four officers and employees had previously worked for Servomation or its subsidiaries. After leaving Servomation, Englert joined Mark Four and managed its Mansfield dining hall. Crucially, he had neither participated in negotiations with Mansfield nor had contact with it during his Servomation employment. (11–12 & n. 2.)

The covenant restricted operating or soliciting certain food facilities for one year after termination. Its customer description covered a firm that had been the employer's client during the preceding year, or one with which it had negotiated during employment, followed by a qualification concerning the employee's participation in negotiations or contact with the firm during employment. Footnote 1 reproduces the complete signed agreement: it also prohibited disclosure or use of nonpublic business information during or after employment, barred association with a food-service business during employment, required at least four weeks' initial employment and ordinarily two weeks' dismissal notice or severance, and selected Maryland law. The complaint sought a one-year prohibition on services at Mansfield, measured from termination. These provisions and corporate identities matter independently of the sentence's grammatical ambiguity. (10–12; n. 1 at 10–11, n. 3 at 11.)

## Competing readings of the final qualification

Servomation argued that Mansfield's status as a recent client sufficed regardless of Englert's contacts. Its reading effectively attached both final qualifications solely to the category of firms with which the employer had negotiated, leaving the category of actual clients unqualified. The court separated the sentence into four components: recent clients, firms in negotiations, employee participation in negotiations, and employee contact. If both participation and contact qualified only firms in negotiations, participation became redundant: negotiation is a particular form of contact. The court applied the interpretive preference to give each term effect where possible consistently with the language and the parties' purpose. Its more reasonable construction linked negotiation participation to the negotiation category and employee contact to the actual-client category. That construction protected business relationships generated through the employee's dealings, rather than forbidding work for every former client wherever located. Since neither relevant participation nor contact was shown, Englert had not violated the covenant under this interpretation. (12; 13 n. 7.)

The court immediately preserved the provisional nature of its analysis. It need not decide at this stage that Servomation's interpretation was wholly without merit; failure to demonstrate likely success was sufficient. With the ultimate right substantially in dispute, a preliminary injunction effectively providing full relief before trial was unwarranted. The interpretive discussion therefore explains why this application failed, without converting an interlocutory ruling into an unconditional final declaration resolving every possible future evidentiary record. (13.)

## Even the employer's reading raised enforceability doubts

The agreement selected Maryland law. Under the cited Maryland decisions, a postemployment restraint had to be reasonable in the circumstances, no broader in area or duration than needed to protect the business, and consistent with employee hardship and the public interest. Protectable interests included customer relationships and confidential business information. On Servomation's broad reading, the covenant reached a former client with whom Englert had never dealt. That went beyond the customer relationship at risk. The likelihood of losing customers depended on the employee's functions and the frequency and place of contacts; infrequent or irregular contact might not support a restraint, and no contact made the justification particularly doubtful. This reinforced denial even assuming the plaintiff's interpretation. (13–14 & nn. 5–8.)

The employer's confidential recipes presented a separate, fact-sensitive inquiry. A food research specialist devised recipes combining culinary quality with efficiencies in cost and preparation. They were not common industry knowledge. But Englert received the manuals in 1966 and was not required to sign the confidentiality covenant until three years later; he returned the manuals when he resigned. Other employees with access had never signed comparable covenants, and some now worked for Mark Four. The court did not equate every disclosure with loss of protection: Maryland required substantial rather than absolute secrecy. Relevant factors included knowledge inside and outside the business, protective measures, competitive value, development investment, and ease of legitimate duplication. Lax safeguards and existing knowledge at the competitor created substantial doubt about ultimate success on this preliminary record. The opinion does not finally adjudicate the recipes worthless or all such information freely usable. (14–15 & n. 10.)

## The separate corporations supplied a third defect

Mansfield had been the Pennsylvania subsidiary's client, while Englert's agreement ran to the Maryland parent. Ownership did not eliminate their legal distinction. The court accepted the defense that working at Mansfield therefore was not work for a client of the contracting employer within this covenant. The final denial rested on the stated interpretive, enforceability, and corporate-identity grounds. It awarded neither damages nor a permanent prohibition and did not merge the subsidiary into the parent merely because both carried the Servomation name. (11 n. 2; 15.)

## Assigned exercise and source scope

C&M 351–700, PDF 259, printed 566, adapts the covenant into a drafting exercise about fictional Tastings, a two-year cafeteria-manager arrangement, and a former client bringing service in-house. The real case involved Mark Four taking over Mansfield's service, not Mansfield itself employing Englert after internalizing the operation. The exercise preserves the qualifying-clause issue but its hypothetical facts are not findings in the opinion. A drafting solution should state expressly whether former clients require employee contact and separately define the negotiation category; the opinion's preferred reading attaches a contact limit rather than eliminating it.
