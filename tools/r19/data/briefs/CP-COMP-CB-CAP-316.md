# Earle M. Jorgenson Co. v. T.I. United States, Ltd.

**133 F.R.D. 472 (E.D. Pa. 1991) · United States District Court, Eastern District of Pennsylvania · January 22, 1991 · Civil Procedure**

**Assigned account:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 386, printed page 739: complete Note 3, before Note 4. Ludwig, District Judge, decided a narrow crossclaim motion; the opinion did not adjudicate ultimate responsibility for the environmental contamination.

**Decision in one sentence.** An original defendant could crossclaim for contribution and indemnity against a third-party defendant whom another defendant had impleaded, because parties not already formally opposing each other on a pleaded claim could be coparties under Rule 13(g). 133 F.R.D. at 474–475.

## Facts and procedural path

Earle M. Jorgenson sought cleanup costs, other damages, and declaratory relief concerning contaminated property in Bristol, Pennsylvania. Its complaint included CERCLA claims against seven defendants. T.I. United States, Ltd. had been A.B. Murray Corporation's sole shareholder until 1984; A.B. Murray formerly owned part of the site. Leonard V. Reed owned 81 percent of Reed Management, which was A.B. Murray's sole shareholder from 1984 to 1990. Jorgenson alleged that active participation, control, operation, and management during hazardous-substance releases left it, as A.B. Murray's successor, with response costs. Those were complaint allegations, not findings establishing each defendant's liability. Id. at 472–473.

On October 9, 1990, T.I. United States impleaded Robert T. Tosti for indemnity and contribution. Tosti had served as A.B. Murray's vice-president of operations from 1983 to 1986. Three days later, original defendant Reed crossclaimed against Tosti on the same types of derivative liability; Reed also crossclaimed against other original defendants. Tosti moved to dismiss November 16, arguing that an original defendant and a third-party defendant could not be coparties. Id. at 473 & n.2.

The question was therefore not whether T.I. United States could crossclaim against the very person it had sued by third-party complaint. It concerned **Reed**, who had not impleaded Tosti and did not already formally oppose him on a pleaded claim. That party relationship supplied the opinion's central distinction.

## Competing approaches to coparty status

The rules did not define coparty, and the relevant appellate and district decisions did not yield one uncontested answer. A Third Circuit statement that crossclaims involved parties on the same side of the main litigation arose in a different setting. Schwab classified a third-party plaintiff and the third-party defendants it had actually sued as opposing parties; that did not necessarily settle the relationship between a third-party defendant and a different original defendant. Id. at 473.

District precedent also diverged. Hansen rejected a crossclaim between a third-party defendant and a codefendant of the third-party plaintiff. Capital Care permitted such a claim if the relationship was sufficiently nonadverse. Ludwig thought the latter formulation helped but could not be decisive in every case: crossclaims themselves necessarily introduced adversity between the parties asserting and resisting them. Treating factual alignment or generalized absence of conflict as the definition would therefore obscure the procedural relationship. Id. at 473–474, including nn.3–4.

## Structure and practical consequences

Ludwig examined the categories of defendant claims together. A **counterclaim** could run against an opposing party. A **third-party claim** under Rule 14(a) ran against a person not already a party. A **crossclaim** ran against a coparty. Reed and Tosti needed to fit within that structure; Tosti's prior presence in the action prevented the straightforward use of Rule 14 to implead him again. Id. at 473–475 & n.5.

Under Tosti's proposed restriction, Reed would generally need an additional action to litigate related contribution or indemnity rights. That would frustrate the rules' policy of resolving related disputes together and arbitrarily favor whichever defendant first impleaded the additional person. Ludwig considered that result inconsistent with Rule 1's goal of just, speedy, and inexpensive adjudication. Id. at 474.

The court nevertheless acknowledged qualifications. Note 6 discusses a possible Rule 13(h) additional-party route if an appropriate counterclaim or crossclaim already existed, and a cumbersome severance, impleader, and reconsolidation route. Those possibilities did not make Tosti's interpretation sensible. Notes 7 and 8 explain the structural and economy concerns; note 9 points to separate trials as a response if multiple issues would become confusing. The court was not ordering any of these procedural devices in this case. Id. at 474 nn.6–9.

It adopted Georgia Ports Authority's approach: a coparty was a party who was **not an opposing party**. Parties formally opposing each other on an existing pleaded claim—an original plaintiff and defendant, or a third-party plaintiff and the third-party defendant it had sued—were opposing parties. Reed and Tosti lacked that existing relationship. Reed could therefore assert the crossclaim. Id. at 474–475.

## Consequence of pleading and judgment

The tenth note makes the definition dynamic. Once Reed pleaded the crossclaim against Tosti, Reed became an opposing party to him. A responsive claim arising from the same transaction or occurrence then had to be assessed as a **counterclaim**, including Rule 13(a)'s compulsory requirement, rather than simply as another permissive crossclaim between permanently fixed coparties. Id. at 475 n.10. Procedural labels can change because a party actually asserts a claim, even if the original caption remains unchanged.

The court **denied Tosti's motion to dismiss** Reed's crossclaim. Id. at 475. This permitted litigation of contribution and indemnity; it did not order Tosti to pay either, decide CERCLA responsibility, or award cleanup damages. One district judge issued the memorandum; there was no separate writing or numerical appellate vote.
