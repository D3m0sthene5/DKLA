# Granfinanciera, S.A. v. Nordberg

**492 U.S. 33 (1989) · Supreme Court of the United States · June 23, 1989**  
**Civil Procedure · Jury trial, fraudulent transfers, and public rights**  
**Assigned:** CivPro PDF 545–546, printed 1072–1074, complete own-caption account and both attached notes.

## Facts and procedural history

Chase & Sanborn Corporation filed for Chapter 11 reorganization in 1983. Its approved plan vested fraudulent-conveyance causes of action in Nordberg, the bankruptcy trustee. In 1985, Nordberg sued Granfinanciera, S.A., and Medex, Ltda., in federal district court, alleging that the debtor's corporate predecessor had transferred approximately $1.7 million to them within a year before bankruptcy without adequate consideration. He alleged both actual and constructive fraud and sought recovery of the transfers, damages, expenses, costs, and interest under the Bankruptcy Code. The distinction between alleged and adjudicated fraud matters: the later bench trial rejected actual fraud but imposed liability for constructive fraud. 492 U.S. at 36–37.

The district court referred the case to the bankruptcy court. Around the time of service, Colombia nationalized Granfinanciera. Both defendants requested a jury in their answer, but the bankruptcy judge denied the request because the action was a bankruptcy “core” proceeding that he understood historically to be a nonjury matter. After trial, he dismissed the actual-fraud claim with prejudice and awarded the trustee $1.5 million against Granfinanciera and $180,000 against Medex on constructive fraud. The district court affirmed without discussing the jury issue. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed as well, treating fraudulent-conveyance recovery and bankruptcy proceedings as equitable and Congress's core-proceeding designation as displacing any jury entitlement. Id. at 37–38.

Neither defendant had filed a claim against the bankruptcy estate. That fact was central: this was the estate's affirmative recovery action against outsiders, rather than a determination of their right to share in estate assets. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to determine whether the Seventh Amendment nevertheless entitled them to a jury. Id. at 36, 57–59.

## Preliminary issue: Granfinanciera's nationalization

Nordberg offered a new alternative ground for affirmance as to Granfinanciera alone. He argued that its status as an instrumentality of Colombia foreclosed jury trial under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and historical sovereign-immunity principles. The Court declined to decide that question. It had not been raised below or adequately briefed and argued, and the circumstances were not exceptional enough to excuse that omission. Petitioners also plausibly asserted, without rebuttal, that affirmance on that ground would enlarge the trustee's enforcement rights beyond the judgment below. The Court left both the statutory jury question for foreign states and the significance of the timing of nationalization unresolved. Id. at 38–40 & nn.1–2.

## The Seventh Amendment framework

Petitioners relied on the Constitution rather than a statutory jury right. Footnote 3 explains the ambiguity of the bankruptcy jury provisions and the transition problem: this bankruptcy had been pending when the 1984 amendments were enacted, so the provision on which the lower court focused did not supply petitioners a statutory jury right. The Court did not settle that statute's comprehensive meaning. Id. at 40–41 n.3.

The constitutional inquiry had two initial steps: compare the statutory action with eighteenth-century English actions, then determine whether the remedy sought is legal or equitable. The remedy inquiry is more important. If those steps establish a jury entitlement, a further question remains: may Congress validly assign the claim to a non-Article III tribunal without juries? Footnote 4 ties the practical-abilities language in *Ross v. Bernhard* to this latter inquiry, rather than announcing that any difficult legal case loses its jury right. A legal claim involving public rights may permissibly receive nonjury administrative treatment; the legal/private-right combination is what carries the constitutional protection here. Id. at 41–42 & n.4.

## Why this action was legal

The Court found that eighteenth-century trustees could recover fraudulent money transfers through actions at law, such as money had and received. Equity sometimes entertained fraudulent-conveyance disputes, but that did not make this particular fixed-money recovery equitable. The historical cases Nordberg cited involved prospective receivables or land held in trust, remedies and subject matter different from recovery of a definite sum without an accounting. The Court's conclusion concerned the money action before it, not an assertion that every fraudulent-transfer dispute involving any property or requested relief must always be legal. Id. at 43–47 & nn.5–6.

The remedy reinforced the historical comparison. Nordberg sought definite monetary sums, and a legal recovery would fully compensate the estate. Calling the request “avoidance” or “restitution” did not change that substance. The transferred dollars were fungible, no accounting or other distinctively equitable relief was required, and the trustee was entitled to the transferred amount rather than an award subject to equitable discretion. *Schoenthal v. Irving Trust Co.* supported treating such fixed-money recovery as legal. Id. at 47–49 & n.7.

## Why the public-rights exception did not defeat a jury

Congress cannot remove jury rights merely by labeling a traditional legal action a core bankruptcy proceeding and placing it before a specialized court. The majority nevertheless recognized a genuine public-rights exception. Unlike a rule confined to litigation with the United States as a party, its formulation could include rights between private parties when they are closely integrated into a federal regulatory program appropriate for administrative resolution. The majority connected that distinction with Article III limits on assigning adjudication to non-Article III tribunals. Id. at 49–55 & nn.8–10.

The trustee's action fell outside that exception. It resembled an ordinary suit to augment the estate, not the ordering and distribution of existing assets among claimants. Footnote 11 expressly avoided deciding whether restructuring debtor-creditor relations itself is a public right: petitioners would win even on that assumption. A blanket statement that the decision declared all bankruptcy adjudication private would therefore be inaccurate. Id. at 55–56 & nn.11–12.

*Katchen v. Landy* supplied the key contrast. There, a creditor had filed claims against the estate, and the trustee's preference objection had to be resolved in allowing or disallowing those claims. The bankruptcy court could decide that matter in equity and order return of the preference without a jury. Here, petitioners submitted no claims, so the recovery action was not part of that process. Footnotes 13–14 explain that the difference is substantive claims allowance, not a broad proposition that any appearance automatically waives every jury right. Footnote 14 also distinguishes the voluntary alternative-forum waiver reasoning used in *Schor*. Id. at 57–60 & nn.13–14.

Congress's reclassification did not change the underlying action's character. Nor did the Court accept administrative convenience as sufficient to eliminate the right. The recovery suit here began after approval of the reorganization plan, and previous schemes had accommodated juries in comparable actions. Potential delay and expense did not overcome the constitutional command. Id. at 60–64 & nn.15–18.

## Separate opinions and precise result

Justice Brennan wrote for Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Marshall, Stevens, and Kennedy throughout. Justice Scalia joined Parts I, II, III, and V and the judgment, producing a **6–3 result**, while the public-rights discussion in Part IV had five votes. The Court **reversed the Eleventh Circuit and remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion**. Petitioners were entitled to their requested jury trial; the decision did not exonerate them from transfer liability or itself decide the merits anew. Id. at 35, 64–65.

Scalia's concurrence disagreed with Part IV's expanded public-rights theory. In his view, the United States had to be a party for the doctrine to apply. He traced the exception to sovereign-immunity and revenue-collection history and rejected flexible balancing as insufficiently protective of the separation of powers. Because this dispute was between private parties, his narrower rule reached the same jury-trial result. Id. at 65–71.

Justice White dissented alone. He understood *Katchen* and the bankruptcy court's equitable character to support affirmance. For him, congressional reform integrating avoidance actions into bankruptcy was substantive, not merely relabeling. He considered the historical evidence ambiguous, emphasized both forum and claim, objected to insufficient deference to Congress's Bankruptcy Clause judgment, and warned that juries could undermine the integrated statutory scheme. His note 9 also argued that monetary restitution can be equitable, contesting the majority's characterization of the requested remedy. Id. at 71–91 & nn.1–14.

Justice Blackmun, joined by Justice O'Connor, dissented separately. He would give jury rights the benefit of historical doubt and initially treat this claim as legal. He nevertheless concluded that Congress could assign it to an equitable bankruptcy forum because fraudulent-transfer recovery was integral to fair distribution and the federal regulatory scheme. He acknowledged constitutional limits on core designations, including ordinary state-law contract suits, but found no violation here. Id. at 91–95.

## Reservations and the assigned notes

The Court did **not** decide whether bankruptcy judges had statutory authority to conduct these jury trials, whether non-Article III judges constitutionally could preside over them, or how district-court review would interact with the Amendment's restriction on reexamining jury-found facts. Those express reservations appear at pages 50 and 64 and in note 19. Entitlement to a jury was resolved; the complete forum architecture was not.

The assignment's first note reproduces the practical-abilities discussion and asks how complexity relates to public rights. Its second describes subsequent lower-court disagreement and a 1994 legislative response, citing §157(e), and describes claim filing in waiver terms. Those later developments are editorial context, not part of this 1989 judgment or independently certified current law. The opinion's own account more precisely links a filed claim to equitable allowance and disallowance. Both complete notes are included in the reading scope; the following *Terry* passage is a separate assignment.
