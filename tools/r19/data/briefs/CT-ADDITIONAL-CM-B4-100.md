# Disciplinary Counsel v. Young
113 Ohio St.3d 36, 862 N.E.2d 504, 2007-Ohio-975 | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

The Supreme Court of Ohio issued this per curiam decision on March 21, 2007, on the certified report of its disciplinary board. The court found four professional-responsibility violations and imposed a second indefinite suspension, consecutive to the indefinite suspension already in effect, with specific reinstatement and probation conditions and costs taxed to James Cornell Young. Six justices concurred in the court’s judgment. Justice Lundberg Stratton concurred in the findings and the indefinite suspension but would have made it concurrent with the existing suspension. This was attorney discipline, not an ordinary action awarding contract damages to the ward. (¶¶ 27–35; official slip 12–13.)

## Background and guardian’s responsibilities

Young, admitted in 1980, had already received a stayed one-year suspension and two-year probation in 1993. A February 2002 interim suspension followed a felony conviction, and an April 2004 indefinite suspension concerned paying a witness falsely to exonerate a drug defendant and soliciting payments relating to whether another client would testify for the prosecution. These earlier disciplinary matters supplied history and aggravation; they were not the guardianship charges adjudicated in this proceeding. Disciplinary Counsel brought the additional guardianship charges on June 13, 2005. (¶¶ 1–3.)

In May 1992 the probate court appointed Young guardian for Michael Gordon, whom the Department of Veterans Affairs had declared incompetent and awarded benefits two years earlier. Young had to arrange medical care and use the monthly benefits for bills and necessities. He opened a guardianship checking account on which he alone was the authorized signatory, and the probate court required biennial financial reports. His secretary, LaTonya Jackson, initially handled many daily tasks, including meeting Gordon and purchasing necessities. As Young became impatient with the demands, he delegated all daily responsibilities to her and authorized her, before 1996, to sign his name, including on checks. (¶¶ 5–8.)

In January 1996 Young decided to relinquish the guardianship, and Jackson agreed to succeed him. But the withdrawal motion was never signed or filed, and Young did not confirm a successor’s appointment. He later dismissed Gordon’s concerns by saying Jackson was guardian and he was no longer responsible. Jackson was never formally appointed. She continued performing tasks and submitting reports in his name, changed the account address so statements no longer reached his office, and eventually stopped informing him of probate notices. That handling continued after his February 2002 suspension. Thus informal delegation and the purported succession did not terminate his court-appointed role. (¶¶ 9–10.)

## Probate proceedings and disciplinary findings

The probate court removed Young on December 5, 2002, for failure to file an account and appointed Gregory Thomas successor guardian on March 20, 2003. Thomas took control of the account, inventoried the assets, and sought a surcharge against Young and the sureties. An agreed judgment found willful and wanton neglect that made the successor’s protective work necessary. The sureties paid $40,000 for which Young had failed to account and $4,101.06 for the successor’s services, with judgment against Young in their favor for $44,101.06. He had not attempted to satisfy it. His separate suit alleging Jackson’s embezzlement and concealment against her and the bank was dismissed; that allegation should not be treated as a finding in this disciplinary case that she committed a particular crime. Young admitted his own failure to complete withdrawal and supervise her. (¶¶ 11–13.)

Young stipulated to conduct reflecting adversely on fitness, neglect of an entrusted legal matter, and failure properly to account for client funds under DR 1-102(A)(6), 6-101(A)(3), and 9-102(B)(3). He initially denied intentional failure to carry out a professional-services contract under DR 7-101(A)(2). Disciplinary Counsel withdrew a separate allegation under DR 7-101(A)(3). The panel and board nevertheless found clear and convincing evidence of all four remaining violations, including intentional abandonment of his employment obligation: he knowingly handed control to his secretary, failed to verify succession, and disclaimed responsibility when Gordon sought help. By the Supreme Court review, he did not object to those misconduct findings; the court accepted them and emphasized a lawyer’s duty to ensure that delegated legal responsibilities are completed properly. (¶¶ 4, 14–15, 27.)

## Mitigation, aggravation, and competing sanctions

The panel and board credited Young’s full disclosure, cooperation, and favorable character evidence. A former judge, a pastor, and a retired physician described his sustained Alcoholics Anonymous participation, church leadership, recovery work, and personal change. Young admitted drug and alcohol dependency and sought mitigation on that basis. The governing mitigation provision required diagnosis, a causal contribution to misconduct, successful sustained treatment, and professional assurance of a competent and ethical return to practice. Although an assistance-program witness supported causation, Young’s own testimony attributed his abandonment to not wanting the burdensome guardianship. The board commended recovery but did not give chemical dependency mitigating effect as a cause of these acts. (¶¶ 16–21.)

Aggravation included prior discipline, a self-centered abandonment even without personal financial gain, repeated unsupervised delegation and multiple violations, harm to a particularly vulnerable client, and failure to repay the sureties. Disciplinary Counsel proposed a consecutive one-year suspension; Young proposed a concurrent two-year suspension. The panel proposed a concurrent two-year actual suspension with a conditional stay and substantial treatment, restitution, and probation requirements. It reasoned that the court might have imposed the same earlier indefinite sanction had these contemporaneous charges been heard together. With the panel’s approval, the board changed the recommendation to a consecutive indefinite suspension subject to the proposed reinstatement conditions. These recommendations must be distinguished from the court’s final order. (¶¶ 22–26.)

## Why the court imposed a consecutive indefinite suspension

Young argued that relatively contemporaneous misconduct did not justify a second consecutive indefinite suspension, that addiction caused the misconduct, that the sureties’ reimbursement removed harm to the vulnerable ward, and that inability to pay should not increase punishment. The court overruled those objections. It accepted the panel’s credibility and evidentiary judgments that disinterest caused the neglect, that a $40,000 estate loss harmed the ward despite later compensation, and that Young had made no restitution. Each disciplinary action required an independently justified sanction based on duties, injury, mental state, aggravation, mitigation, and comparable cases; concurrency was not automatic. (¶¶ 28–32.)

The court expressly acknowledged that Young had not breached the earlier suspension order or treatment regime. Nevertheless, abandoning an incompetent ward, combined with his grave prior disciplinary history, outweighed cooperation and good character and warranted further protection of the public by delaying possible return to practice. The result rested on this combination, not a finding that every instance of unsupervised delegation requires the same sanction. (¶¶ 32–33.)

## Exact order and separate writing

The second indefinite suspension was ordered consecutive to the existing indefinite suspension. Beyond the general reinstatement requirements, Young had to show successful completion of an approved substance-abuse program and ability to practice competently and ethically, and compliance with a monitor-approved plan to repay $44,101.06 to the insurers. If reinstated, he would serve three years’ probation with continued treatment, testing, quarterly reports on treatment and repayments, adherence to the monitor’s recommendations on practice management and financial responsibility, and no further misconduct. Costs were taxed to him. These were conditions for possible reinstatement, not an order reinstating him or a guaranteed release after two years. (¶ 34.)

Justice Lundberg Stratton agreed with the majority except for concurrency: because this misconduct occurred during the same period as the prior misconduct, she considered consecutive time too severe. The official report also records Judge Richard Rogers’s assignment for Justice Resnick, whose term ended January 1, 2007, and Justice Cupp’s nonparticipation after his term began January 2. Neither is an unmentioned eighth vote. (¶ 35; slip 12–13.)

## Contracts connection and reading scope

The assigned account uses the case as an example of a status-based duty that does not disappear upon delegation. The court’s actual reasoning concerns professional obligations, appointment as guardian, and failure to carry out the professional-services undertaking. It does not hold that assistants can never perform tasks or that every permissible contractual delegation is a disciplinary offense. Delegating tasks, completing an authorized change of guardian, and remaining responsible for supervision are different matters. (¶¶ 8–15, 27; C&M printed 1217.)
