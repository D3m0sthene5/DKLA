Parties
Shady Grove Orthopedic Associates provided medical care to Sonia Galvez after an automobile accident.
Galvez assigned part of her rights under an Allstate insurance policy to Shady Grove as payment for that care.
Shady Grove was the plaintiff and petitioner, seeking unpaid statutory interest for itself and a proposed class.
Allstate was the defendant insurer and respondent. Galvez's assignment explains why the medical provider,
rather than the insured patient, pursued the insurance-related claim. Scalia opinion, Part I.
Material Facts
The complaint alleged that Shady Grove submitted its assigned benefits claim under a policy issued in New
York. New York law required Allstate to pay or deny it within 30 days and imposed interest at two percent per
month on overdue benefits. Allstate apparently paid the benefits late but refused to pay the accrued interest.
Shady Grove alleged that refusing to pay such interest was a recurring practice affecting other claimants. These
were allegations at the dismissal stage, not Supreme Court findings that Allstate owed the proposed class
money. Id.
Shady Grove's own interest claim was worth roughly $500. It invoked the Class Action Fairness Act's diversity
provision, 28 U.S.C. § 1332(d)(2), on the basis of proposed class claims exceeding $5 million. The class
mechanism thus mattered both to practical enforcement of many small claims and to the asserted federal
jurisdictional basis. The Court did not enlarge Shady Grove's individual entitlement to $5 million; the larger
number referred to the proposed aggregate recovery. Id., Part I & n.3; Stevens concurrence, n.18.
New York CPLR § 901(a) provided ordinary class-certification criteria. Section 901(b) separately prohibited
maintaining a class action for a statutory penalty or minimum recovery unless the statute creating it specifically
authorized class recovery. The lower courts classified the interest sought here as a penalty within that
provision. Rule 23 also supplied conditions for maintaining a class action, including the prerequisites in
subdivision (a) and one of the categories in subdivision (b). The dispute concerned whether the additional New
York prohibition could apply in federal court. Scalia opinion, Parts I, II-A & nn.1–2.

Procedural History
The Eastern District of New York dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. It applied § 901(b) despite Rule 23, ruled
that the interest claim could not proceed as a class action, and observed that the approximately $500 individual
claim did not meet ordinary diversity's amount requirement. This was a rejection of the asserted class vehicle
and resulting federal jurisdiction, not a merits determination that Allstate had paid interest. Scalia opinion, Part
I.
The Second Circuit affirmed. It distinguished eligibility for class treatment under § 901(b) from certification of
an eligible class under Rule 23, found no direct conflict, and then treated the New York provision as
substantive under Erie. The Supreme Court rejected that distinction and reversed. It remanded for further
proceedings without deciding that the proposed class actually satisfied every Rule 23 requirement or awarding
damages. Id., Parts I, II-A and concluding disposition.
Issue
Does Rule 23 govern whether this proposed class action may proceed despite New York's prohibition on class
actions seeking statutory penalties? If the provisions answer the same question, does applying Rule 23 exceed
the Rules Enabling Act's restriction against altering substantive rights? The second question produced different
rationales among the five Justices who agreed with the judgment. Id., Parts II-A–C; Stevens concurrence, Parts
II–III.
Rule and Holding
Five Justices agreed that Rule 23 and § 901(b) answer the same question: whether this suit may be maintained
as a class action. Rule 23 governs that question if its requirements are satisfied and its application is valid. The
Court held that the New York provision did not bar this federal class action. A valid, applicable Federal Rule is
assessed under its statutory and constitutional authorization; ordinary Erie outcome analysis does not simply
override it. Scalia opinion for the Court, Parts I and II-A; Stevens concurrence, Part III.
The judgment does not make Scalia's entire Enabling Act analysis a majority holding. His plurality focused on
whether the Federal Rule regulates the process for enforcing rights rather than the rights themselves. Stevens
agreed with applying Rule 23 here but would preserve an ordinarily procedural state rule when it actually
defines the scope of a state-created substantive right or remedy. He found § 901(b) did not perform that
function. These distinct approaches, and Ginsburg's disagreement at the initial conflict stage, must be kept
separate. Scalia plurality, Parts II-B and II-D; Stevens concurrence, Parts I–III; Ginsburg dissent, Part I-D.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Second Circuit and remanded. The dismissal based on the New York
class-action bar could not stand. The Court did not certify the class, resolve the insurer's alleged liability,
determine the full amount owed, or declare every state limitation concerning class remedies inapplicable.
Scalia opinion, concluding disposition; Part II-A & n.4.
Reasoning
The majority framed the disputed question at the level of permission to maintain a class action. Rule 23
authorizes the class vehicle when its stated conditions are met; § 901(b) denies that vehicle for specified
claims. Calling the state restriction an antecedent eligibility rule did not remove the collision. Rule 23's own
requirements could also be called eligibility conditions, and its text did not wait for state permission before
operating. The lower court's terminology consequently failed to identify two genuinely different questions.
Scalia opinion for the Court, Part II-A.

The statutory structure did not change the analysis. Section 901(a) could parallel Rule 23 while § 901(b)
imposed an additional conflicting condition; placing the latter in another subsection did not narrow Rule 23.
Nor did federal statutes creating exceptions to Rule 23 show that states possessed equivalent authority.
Congress could override the Federal Rules, whereas a state statute remained subject to a valid controlling
federal rule. Id.
The majority also rejected a remedy-only reading of § 901(b). The provision prohibited maintaining the action
in class form, rather than limiting the judgment after a class had properly been certified. An asserted legislative
purpose to prevent large aggregate penalties could not eliminate that textual conflict. The Court expressly left
open the treatment of a different statute that actually capped recovery in an existing action. Avoiding an
unavoidable textual collision by rewriting either provision would obscure, rather than resolve, the Enabling Act
question. Id. & n.4.
In Part II-B, which lacked Stevens's vote, Scalia applied Sibbach's test asking whether the Federal Rule
genuinely regulates procedure. He analogized class actions to joinder and consolidation: they allow multiple
claims to be adjudicated together while leaving the elements of liability and each claimant's entitlement
unchanged. Allstate might face more claims in practice because small claims became economical to litigate,
but that practical incentive was an incidental effect of procedure. The larger aggregate amount did not itself
establish alteration of substantive rights. Scalia plurality, Part II-B.
The plurality accordingly rejected an application-by-application validity test based on the substantive character
or purpose of the particular displaced state rule. Uniform federal procedure would become unstable if the same
Federal Rule operated differently whenever a state expressed a different regulatory purpose. In Part II-C, joined
only by Roberts and Thomas, Scalia defended that approach as required by Sibbach even while acknowledging
textual difficulties in evaluating whether a right has been abridged without examining the right. The narrower
joinder pattern matters: Sotomayor did not join that response to Stevens. Id., Parts II-B–C.
Stevens accepted the direct conflict but insisted on a distinct Enabling Act inquiry. Congress had authorized
rules of practice and procedure while separately forbidding alteration of substantive rights. A state might define
a substantive entitlement through an apparently procedural device, such as a limitation on proof or recovery.
The federal rule's ordinary procedural character would not authorize it to destroy that feature of the state right.
A reasonable saving construction should be considered; if none were possible and application altered the right,
the Federal Rule could not govern that application. Stevens concurrence, Parts I–II.
Stevens nevertheless found the required showing absent here. Section 901(b) was framed as a general rule for
New York courts and applied to claims arising under any source of law, not specifically New York-created
rights. Its legislative history plausibly reflected a procedural judgment about when class treatment was needed
to motivate litigation. Concerns about excessive aggregate recoveries did not conclusively establish that the
statute defined the underlying entitlement. The high threshold for denying effect to a facially applicable
Federal Rule was not met. Id., Part III.
The Justices supporting the judgment recognized that allowing federal class litigation barred in state court
could encourage forum shopping. For the plurality, that difference followed from Congress's authorized system
of uniform federal procedure. For Stevens, ordinary Erie's forum-choice concerns would become relevant if the
Federal Rule did not govern, but could not replace the specific Enabling Act test once it did. The judgment
therefore did not rest on a finding that forum choice had no practical consequences. Scalia plurality, Part II-D;
Stevens concurrence, Part III.

Separate Opinions
Stevens's separate concurrence supplied the fifth vote for the judgment and for the finding of a direct conflict,
but not for Scalia's general Enabling Act theory. He distinguished the ordinary policy consequences of
procedural rules from rules that define the right being enforced. He also read Sibbach as having decided a
facial challenge to medical-examination rules, rather than every possible application of a Federal Rule to a
state-created entitlement. The case thus requires identifying both what five Justices agreed upon and where
their routes to that result diverged. Stevens concurrence, Parts I–III.
Ginsburg, joined by Kennedy, Breyer, and Alito, would have affirmed. She would read Rule 23 as governing
class litigation procedure and § 901(b) as defining available statutory remedies, permitting both to operate. On
that account, plaintiffs could pursue permissible class relief while forgoing statutory penalties, and individuals
could pursue the penalty separately. She considered the majority's focus on the phrase governing maintenance
of an action too formal because the state could have expressed the same remedial limitation claim by claim.
Ginsburg dissent, Part I-C–D.
The dissent emphasized New York's legislative compromise: broader class procedures were accompanied by a
restriction intended to prevent excessive aggregation of statutory penalties. Ginsburg compared the restriction
to the damages limitation respected in Gasperini and invoked decisions interpreting Federal Rules narrowly
enough to avoid unnecessary conflicts with state policy. Having found no controlling conflict, she would apply
Erie's forum-shopping and unequal-outcome analysis and enforce the New York limitation. Placement in a
procedural code and possible application to other sources of law did not, in her view, negate its substantive
character. Id., Parts I-B–D and II.
Ginsburg also discussed the Class Action Fairness Act as the jurisdictional reason the case could reach federal
court in class form. She regarded using it to facilitate class relief disallowed by the state as inconsistent with
the legislative concerns that prompted expanded federal jurisdiction. This was the dissent's interpretation of
that statute's policy background; it was not an independent holding restricting CAFA jurisdiction. Id., Part III.
Notes
A useful analysis separates three questions: whether the Federal Rule covers the disputed point, whether its
application satisfies the Enabling Act, and what Erie requires if no valid Federal Rule governs. The majority
and Ginsburg divided at the first question; Scalia's plurality and Stevens divided over the second despite
reaching the same result. Collapsing those disagreements into a single statement that federal procedure always
wins loses the case's central qualification. Scalia opinion, Part II-A; plurality, Parts II-B–C; Stevens
concurrence, Part II; Ginsburg dissent, Part I-D.
The decision concerned the availability of the class vehicle, not an adjudicated class recovery. It also expressly
reserved differently framed state damages ceilings. Any claim about which Shady Grove rationale a later court
treats as controlling requires checking that court's subsequent decisions; the fractured 2010 opinions alone do
not establish a universal answer for every other state statute. Scalia opinion, Part II-A & n.4; Stevens
concurrence, Part III.
