# Curtis v. Loether

**Course:** Civil Procedure
**ID:** CP-110
**Review:** Complete source comparison; official argument dates repaired. Independent source review passed.

## Citation

Curtis v. Loether, 415 U.S. 189 (1974). Supreme Court of the United States; argued December 4–5, 1973, as stated in the official report; decided February 20, 1974. Justice Marshall delivered the opinion for a unanimous Court. Casebook pp. 1067–1070; full judicial opinion: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-415/pdf/USREPORTS-415-189.pdf. The report’s footnotes supply additional details about the compensatory-damages claim, the changed caption, and questions left open. The statutory provisions and monetary limit described here are those in the 1974 opinion, not a statement of current Fair Housing Act remedies.

## Parties

Curtis, a Black woman who had sought to rent an apartment, was the plaintiff below and petitioner in the Supreme Court. The white landlords, the Loethers, were defendants and respondents. They demanded the jury trial that the district judge denied. Curtis sought to preserve the judge’s disposition against their jury objection. The lower-court decisions used the name Rogers v. Loether; the Supreme Court changed the caption after the petitioner married while review was pending. 415 U.S. at 190–191 & n.3.

## Material Facts

Curtis alleged that the landlords refused to rent an apartment to her because of her race, violating the fair housing provisions of Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968. Section 812 then authorized private enforcement through civil actions and permitted injunctions, actual damages, and punitive damages up to $1,000. Her complaint requested injunctive and punitive relief. A compensatory claim was later included in the issues to be tried, although the pleadings were not formally amended. Id. at 189–190 & n.1; casebook pp. 1067–1068. After an evidentiary hearing, the district court temporarily prohibited the landlords from renting the apartment to someone else. Approximately five months later, Curtis obtained other housing and consented to dissolving the injunction. The remaining trial concerned actual and punitive damages. Her proposed proof of actual loss was excluded because she had not complied with a pretrial discovery order. The district judge ultimately found discrimination but no proven actual damages and awarded $250 in punitive damages. These were findings and an award from a bench trial whose procedure the landlords challenged. Id. at 190–191 & n.1.

## Procedural History

The landlords timely requested a jury in their answer. The District Court for the Eastern District of Wisconsin concluded that neither Title VIII nor the Seventh Amendment required one and proceeded without a jury. In addition to the punitive award, the judge denied Curtis’s request for attorney’s fees and costs. Id. at 190–191. The Seventh Circuit reversed on the jury-trial issue. It concluded that the Seventh Amendment entitled the landlords to a jury and construed the statute consistently with that requirement. Curtis sought Supreme Court review. The Supreme Court affirmed the appellate judgment on the constitutional ground, holding that either party could demand a jury in this federal damages action. The affirmance therefore sustained the reversal of the bench-trial disposition, not the district court’s $250 award. Id. at 191–192, 198.

## Issue

Does the Seventh Amendment apply to a newly created federal statutory cause of action seeking damages for housing discrimination? In particular, does it entitle either party to a jury when the statute authorizes ordinary federal-court actions for actual and punitive damages, even though the action also involved a request for equitable relief? Id. at 190–196 & n.11.

## Rule and Holding

The Seventh Amendment reaches federal statutory actions that enforce legal rights and seek legal remedies in ordinary courts of law. Its protection extends beyond causes of action bearing forms or names recognized in 1791. A private Title VIII claim for compensatory and punitive damages is sufficiently analogous to a tort action, and its remedies are traditional legal relief. Either party is therefore entitled to a jury upon proper demand in federal court. Id. at 192–196. The Court did not hold that every monetary award is legal relief, decide the jury status of Title VII backpay, or decide whether the same statute required juries in state court. Joining an equitable claim does not eliminate the jury right on a legal claim or on factual issues common to both. Id. at 192 n.6, 196–197 & n.11.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court affirmed the Seventh Circuit. The landlords had been improperly denied the jury trial they timely demanded. The Court did not affirm the district judge’s punitive award or independently adjudicate whether discrimination occurred. It resolved the form of trial required for the damages action. Id. at 191, 198.

## Reasoning

The Court began with the distinction between common-law and equitable rights, rather than asking whether Congress had used a historically familiar cause-of-action label. The Seventh Amendment preserves jury adjudication of legal rights. A legislature’s creation of a new duty does not make an ordinary suit enforcing that duty categorically exempt from the Amendment. Prior cases had repeatedly applied the jury guarantee to statutory actions, and the Court made the principle explicit here. Id. at 192–194. The housing claim had the character of a tort action. The statute imposed a duty against discriminatory refusal to rent and authorized compensation for harm caused by breach. The Court noted analogies to duties of innkeepers and to actions redressing dignitary injuries. Those analogies helped characterize the right; they did not require proof that the precise modern housing claim had existed in eighteenth-century England. Id. at 195–196 & n.10. The requested remedy supplied particularly strong support. Actual damages compensate a plaintiff for injury, and punitive damages punish the defendant’s wrongful conduct. Both are traditional forms of relief in courts of law. Curtis sought money for injury caused by discrimination, rather than restoration of a specific fund the landlords held for her or disgorgement of her money. The nature of that award was central to its legal classification. Id. at 196–197. Curtis relied on decisions allowing the National Labor Relations Board to award backpay and a bankruptcy court to order surrender of a preference without a jury. The Court distinguished their institutional settings. Those decisions concerned administrative enforcement or a specialized equitable tribunal, where jury adjudication could conflict with the structure and function of the statutory process. Congress had instead authorized an ordinary civil action in federal district court for the Title VIII claim. No comparable institutional reason justified denying a jury there. Id. at 194–195. The Court treated Title VII backpay with additional care. Courts of appeals had characterized that remedy as part of an equitable reinstatement or restitution scheme, and its statutory language differed from Title VIII’s straightforward damages authorization. Marshall expressly declined to endorse or reject those Title VII holdings. The comparison showed why monetary relief requires examination of its nature and statutory setting, not an automatic equation of every payment with damages at law. Id. at 196–197. The former injunction request did not change the result. The Court found it unnecessary to decide whether the jury question should be assessed at filing or by reference to the claims remaining at trial. If only damages remained, the right was straightforward. If the lawsuit was treated as combining legal and equitable claims, Beacon Theatres and Dairy Queen preserved the jury right on the legal claim and shared factual issues. Calling damages incidental to an injunction could not remove that protection. Id. at 196 n.11. Policy concerns about delay and racial prejudice on juries did not displace the constitutional requirement. The Court acknowledged both concerns. It pointed to expedition requirements, preliminary injunctions, and the trial judge’s powers to direct a verdict, set aside an unsupported verdict, or order a new trial. More fundamentally, it held that these concerns could not overcome a jury right otherwise required by the Seventh Amendment. Its discussion did not deny that prejudice could occur or guarantee that procedural safeguards would prevent every unfair result. Id. at 198. Finally, the Court explained why it chose the constitutional ground despite the usual preference to avoid unnecessary constitutional questions. The requirement was settled by its existing Seventh Amendment decisions, and the decision did not invalidate the civil rights statute. Constitutional reasoning also supplied a narrower geographic and institutional holding: it directed federal-court procedure without deciding the statute’s independent effect on state-court jury practice. Id. at 192 n.6.

## Separate Opinions

Justice Marshall wrote for a unanimous Court. No concurrence, dissent, or nonparticipation is reported. The different statutory and constitutional approaches described in the opinion concern the courts below and the parties’ arguments; they should not be converted into a division among the Supreme Court Justices. Id. at 189, 191–192.

## Notes

The party claiming the jury right here was the defendant landlord, rather than the discrimination plaintiff. The Court’s formulation protects either party upon demand. A description saying that Curtis successfully obtained a jury or that the Supreme Court upheld her bench-trial damages award would reverse the parties’ procedural positions. Id. at 190–192, 198. The discussion of administrative and bankruptcy proceedings states the distinctions used in this 1974 decision. It does not establish that Congress can avoid a jury for every private-rights dispute by assigning it a different forum. Likewise, the reserved Title VII and state-court questions must remain reserved in a brief of Curtis. Later developments appearing in the casebook’s notes require separate analysis and are not holdings of this opinion. Id. at 192 n.6, 194–197.
