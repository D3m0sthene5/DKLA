198 Md. App. 452, 18 A.3d 110 (2011)
Citation, court, and assigned scope
The Court of Special Appeals of Maryland decided this case on April 28, 2011. Judge Deborah
S. Eyler wrote for a panel with Judges Kehoe and Sharer. The assigned excerpt, printed
pages 263–267, focuses on the mailbox rule and reinstatement. The full opinion additionally
decides the effect of an extended grace period, the meaning of payment by a postdated
check, preservation of a factual argument, and the administrator's independent contractual
liability. Pinpoints below refer to the printed pages of the court's 36-page opinion, excluding
its cover, rather than to PDF-viewer page numbers.
Parties and requested relief
Elizabeth Wilson was the primary beneficiary of a life policy on her husband, Dr. John G.
Griffith. US Life underwrote the policy and promised its death benefits. AMA Insurance
Agency, Inc. (AMAIA), administered the policy, billed and collected premiums, and was
authorized to receive payments for US Life. Wilson sought $400,000 in ordinary death
benefits plus $250,000 in accidental-death benefits after both companies denied payment.
Whether the policy was effective at death and whether both defendants had promised to pay
were separate questions. Slip op. at 1–3, 32–35.
Material facts and the three relevant clocks
The ten-year policy became effective November 15, 1998. Griffith paid premiums
semiannually, on May 15 and November 15, through AMAIA's Chicago office. The policy
ordinarily allowed a 31-day grace period after a due date, during which coverage continued.
It authorized US Life to extend that period by written notice stating when coverage would
end. A separate reinstatement clause permitted reinstatement within 90 days after the first
unpaid premium's due date. Evidence of insurability and written approval were generally
required, but the evidence requirement did not apply within 31 days after the grace period
ended. Id. at 2–4.
Griffith missed the May 15, 2007 payment. A reminder notice said payment must be received
within sixty days of that due date to assure active coverage. A later lapse notice referred to a
31-day grace period and instructed him to submit a good-health application. The dates of
those notices were not fully established. Their wording mattered because US Life later
denied that the reminder had extended the grace period and relied on the lapse notice to
demand evidence of insurability. Id. at 4–5, 9–12.
On Monday, July 23, Griffith electronically instructed his bank to pay $369.46 to AMAIA. The
bank generated a paper check bearing his authorized signature. Its records showed dispatch
on Wednesday, July 25, and delivery on Monday, July 30. The check itself was dated July 30.
Griffith died in a bicycle-related automobile accident on Saturday, July 28, between dispatch
and receipt. He had not supplied a good-health application. AMAIA returned the check on
August 2, before learning of his death, and subsequently denied Wilson's claim. Id. at 5–6.

The court's actual calendar calculation is important. The reminder extended the grace period
through July 14; coverage therefore lapsed July 15. Thirty-one days after July 14 was August
14, but the independent ninety-day outside limit measured from May 15 expired August 13.
Thus August 13 was the last permissible reinstatement date under the policy. Payment also
had to occur while Griffith was alive. Neither the longer calendar window nor an eventual
tender after death could insure an already realized loss. Id. at 15–16.
Procedural history and review
The parties filed cross-motions for summary judgment. The circuit court ruled that the policy
had been reinstated and entered judgment against both defendants for $650,000. On
postjudgment motions it added prejudgment interest at six percent from the date of death to
judgment. Both defendants appealed. The appellate court reviewed the summary-judgment
legal determinations de novo and explained that a legally erroneous refusal to reconsider
also constitutes an abuse of discretion. Id. at 6–8.
Maryland's choice-of-law rule looked to the place of contracting. The policy was delivered
and premiums were paid in Illinois, and the parties agreed that Illinois substantive law
governed. The opinion also noted that Maryland and Illinois law agreed on the dispositive
principles. This is a Maryland court's application and, on reinstatement theory, prediction of
Illinois law; it is not an Illinois Supreme Court decision. Id. at 7–8, 22–24.
Issues
Did the reminder extend the grace period notwithstanding its failure to use the words “grace
period”? Did the governing policy permit reinstatement through payment alone in the
extended window? Was payment made when Griffith instructed his bank, when the bank
dispatched the check, when AMAIA received it, or when it could negotiate it? Did the future
date printed on the check prevent effective payment before death? Finally, was AMAIA liable
for the insurer's contractual benefits because it independently handled and denied the claim?
Rules and holdings
The reminder was an effective written extension. Stating sixty days from an expressly
identified due date adequately identified the termination date. The policy consequently
allowed reinstatement without new evidence of insurability within the relevant period. The
later notice could not unilaterally replace the policy's reinstatement conditions. Id. at 14–17.
The policy's reference to payment did not require the insurer's receipt or negotiation of a
check. Applying the mailbox rule to an authorized mailed payment, the court held that
reinstatement occurred July 25, when the bank dispatched the paper check. July 23 merely
initiated action by Griffith's own agent. A validly payable postdated check did not delay the
effective payment until the date on its face under the UCC provisions applied to this record.
Id. at 18–31.
AMAIA had no independent contractual duty to pay the policy benefits. An agent acting for a
disclosed principal does not assume the principal's contract obligations absent an agreement
to personal liability. AMAIA's own involvement in denying the claim did not supply that
missing promise. Id. at 32–35.

Actual judgment
The judgment for Wilson against US Life was affirmed, including the $650,000 benefit
recovery and prejudgment interest. The judgment against AMAIA was reversed, and the case
was remanded with instructions to enter judgment for AMAIA. Appellate costs were divided
equally between US Life and Wilson. The insurer lost; its administrator won. Describing the
result simply as “affirmed for the beneficiary” would omit a substantial portion of the
disposition. Id. at 32, 35–36.
Reasoning
The policy controlled both the extension and reinstatement mechanisms
The insurer had reserved power to extend the grace period through written notice and
exercised that power in ordinary language. A reasonable insured could calculate July 14 from
the reminder without requiring a legal label or a second express calendar date. Once the
grace period changed, the reinstatement clause's reference to the end of that period
operated on the extended date. Id. at 14–16.
The insurer's notices nevertheless could not change the reinstatement bargain by adding a
receipt condition or demanding a health application where the policy excused one. The
extension was authorized by the contract; unilateral replacement of reinstatement terms was
not. The court thus gave the reminder legal effect without granting either defendant
unrestricted power to rewrite the policy. Id. at 16–17.
Payment was an insured's act, not a later act chosen by the insurer
US Life argued that payment meant negotiation of the check. The court rejected that as an
unreasonable reading rather than finding two equally plausible readings and automatically
construing an ambiguity against the insurer. The policy described payment as something the
insured makes to the insurer or its agent. Negotiating or depositing a check is an act of the
payee. Treating that act as the condition would allow the date of coverage to depend on when
the administrator happened to deposit a check. Id. at 18–21.
Customary practice and the notices permitted payment by check. The relevant inquiry
therefore became the timing of an authorized check payment. The court discussed contra
proferentem as a last-resort doctrine but did not need it to reach its conclusion. Id. at 19–21.
Reinstatement was exercise of an existing contractual right
The court distinguished jurisdictions treating reinstatement as revival of the existing policy
from those requiring a new insurance contract accepted by the insurer. It predicted that
Illinois would follow the majority revival approach, particularly where timely payment alone
satisfied the reinstatement clause. In that situation the insurer already had promised revival
upon performance and had no new discretionary underwriting decision to make. Id. at 22–24.
The opinion used unilateral-contract analysis to explain that offer and performance
relationship. It expressly left room for a different analysis where a later reinstatement
request requires evidence of insurability and affirmative insurer approval. The holding
cannot be extended to every lapsed policy merely because a premium check was eventually
mailed. Id. at 24 n.4.

Dispatch occurred after the instruction to the bank and before receipt
The mailbox rule can apply to performance consisting of an authorized remittance, as well as
to a mailed promise. It requires a permitted medium and effective dispatch. Giving
instructions to one's own messenger is not the same as putting the acceptance into an
independent delivery channel. Id. at 24–27.
Viewed favorably to the defendants, Griffith could still have countermanded the July 23
electronic instruction. On July 25, however, the bank sent the paper check to AMAIA. At that
point the authorized means of acceptance was in motion within the dispatch rule. The
electronic origin of the bank instruction did not convert the transaction into substantially
instantaneous communication with the insurer. The writing that reached the insurer traveled
later through a delivery process. Id. at 27–30.
This distinction prevents two inaccurate summaries: the court did not hold that clicking an
online bill-payment button always accepts an offer immediately, and it did not hold that every
electronic acceptance follows the mailbox rule. The transaction here ultimately involved a
dispatched paper instrument. The electronic-transactions statutes mentioned in a footnote
were not invoked by the parties as governing this appeal. Id. at 28–29 n.5.
The postdated check and late factual objection did not defeat reinstatement
The court considered UCC §§ 3-113 and 4-401(c). The latter permits a bank to charge an
otherwise payable check before its printed date unless the customer supplies the specified
notice of postdating. Nothing in the record suggested Griffith had given such notice. The
check was accordingly treated as valid and properly payable on July 25 despite the July 30
date. This was a record-specific application of the statutory exception, not an assertion that
dates on all negotiable instruments are irrelevant. Id. at 30–31.
US Life's alternative suggestion of a genuine factual dispute appeared for the first time in its
appellate reply after it had taken the opposite position below. The court declined to entertain
that untimely argument and also observed that the material record facts sufficed to
determine the payment date. Id. at 31–32.
Agency authority did not create an additional promisor
AMAIA could receive payments and deny claims as the insurer's administrator without
becoming a party to the insurance promise. The policy named US Life as the entity owing
benefits, and no evidence established that AMAIA assumed that obligation personally.
Because the issue was squarely presented by cross-motions on undisputed facts, the proper
disposition included judgment for AMAIA, not merely vacatur of the judgment against it. Id.
at 32–35.

Study use and limits
Work through the three time periods before applying the mailbox rule: the grace period kept
existing coverage alive; the reinstatement period allowed revival after lapse; the insured's
lifetime imposed an independent boundary. Then distinguish instructions to an agent from
dispatch to the offeror and distinguish a disclosed agent's authority from its personal
contractual liability. The August 13 outside limit and the split judgment are full-opinion
details that the abbreviated mailbox-rule treatment can obscure.
