# Cyberchron Corp. v. Calldata Systems Development, Inc.
47 F.3d 39 (2d Cir. 1995) | Contracts

## The unfinished equipment contract
Cyberchron made specialized computer equipment. Calldata, a Grumman subsidiary, was developing an Advanced Tactical Air Command Central system for the Marine Corps and wanted Cyberchron to supply workstation components. The proposed transaction involved three components and a maximum combined weight of 145 pounds, with penalties for excess weight. The weight specification and corresponding penalties were disputed throughout the negotiations. Cyberchron never accepted those terms, the parties never formed the contemplated purchase contract, and no equipment was delivered or payment made under it. The absence of that final contract did not, however, resolve whether Calldata had induced a separate, compensable change in position. 47 F.3d at 41–43.

A May 15, 1990 purchase order included a termination-liability provision (TLP) that limited Calldata's exposure to $200,000, including termination expenses and changes in costs. Cyberchron sought a larger ceiling. A June modification increased the limit to $700,000; Cyberchron later asked for $1 million, which Calldata did not approve. The parties disagreed about what the provision covered. Cyberchron's representative Paul understood it to assure reimbursement if the transaction ended; Calldata understood it to concern termination for convenience of a valid purchase order, rather than termination for default. The provision reserved to Grumman the power to delete it unilaterally. These events mattered both to Cyberchron's proposed independent TLP recovery and to the reasonableness of reliance on later assurances. Id. at 41–43.

The critical assurances began in mid-July 1990. Grumman's Wilhelm told Cyberchron to proceed as if the weights were approved and that the parties would resolve or finalize their differences later. He repeatedly pressed Cyberchron to continue performance and meet the delivery deadline despite the unresolved weight issue. When Cyberchron asked for written confirmation, it did not obtain it, but received further directions to proceed. After an August 8 conversation, Drost continued to reassure Cyberchron that matters were fine and that work should continue. The trial court found that Cyberchron reasonably understood these representations as assurances that it would be paid for work undertaken while negotiations continued. Id. at 42, 44–45.

Cyberchron requested a substantial progress payment on July 30, based on costs through July 20, but received none. Meanwhile, Calldata explored an alternative supplier, Codar, during August while continuing to press Cyberchron to perform. Calldata issued a September 6 show-cause notice, Cyberchron replied, and Calldata terminated the arrangement for asserted default on September 25. It entered a contract with Codar the next day. The record indicated that Codar's equipment was heavier and inferior, making Calldata's continuing insistence on Cyberchron's unresolved weight requirements particularly significant to the trial court's assessment. Id. at 42–43, 45.

## Trial, cross-appeals, and the surviving theory
After a bench trial, Judge Arthur Spatt in the Eastern District of New York rejected Cyberchron's contract claim, Calldata's contractual counterclaim, and Cyberchron's quantum-meruit claim. The missing agreement on weight and penalties prevented contractual recovery. Restitution failed because Cyberchron had not fully performed and Calldata had obtained no benefit producing unjust enrichment. Neither side appealed those dismissals. The appeal therefore did not reopen the question whether an ordinary equipment contract existed or whether Calldata owed restitution. Id. at 43.

The trial court instead awarded Cyberchron reliance damages for materials and direct labor incurred from July 15 to September 25, 1990. The September 1, 1993 judgment was $162,824.19, consisting of $53,588.40 for materials and $109,235.79 for labor, with prejudgment interest from the filing of the action on December 18, 1990. One background passage in the appellate opinion renders the total as $162,824.10; the opening description of the judgment and the component amounts support $162,824.19. The trial court excluded overhead and lost profits, and did not address posttermination shutdown expenses. Id. at 40–41, 43, 46.

Cyberchron appealed to seek recovery under the TLP and fuller reliance damages. Calldata cross-appealed the availability and scope of promissory estoppel. Judge Mahoney wrote for a panel also consisting of Judges Walker and Sprouse, the latter sitting by designation from the Fourth Circuit. There was no separate opinion. The court applied New York substantive law and reviewed the relevant trial findings for clear error under Rule 52(a).

## Why the termination-liability provision did not supply an independent recovery
Calldata argued that Cyberchron had abandoned its independent TLP theory at trial. The court rejected that procedural contention: Paul's testimony sufficiently preserved the claim. It nevertheless rejected the theory on its merits. The TLP contemplated termination of a valid purchase order, and no such agreement had arisen because essential terms remained unsettled. The reserved unilateral power to delete the clause also prevented treating the disputed provision itself as a clear promise upon which Cyberchron reasonably relied. Thus the same provision that indicated continuing negotiations did not become a freestanding assurance of reimbursement under every possible form of termination. Id. at 43–44.

That holding must be separated from the later oral assurances. Cyberchron could fail to prove contractual rights under the purchase order or TLP while still establishing a distinct promise that induced further expenditures. The promissory-estoppel theory rested on the mid-July directions to proceed and the assurance that the unresolved matters would be worked out, rather than on treating the entire unaccepted purchase order as binding.

## Promissory estoppel and Calldata's objections
The court stated New York's requirements as a clear and unambiguous promise, reasonable and foreseeable reliance, and injury caused by that reliance. It also discussed authorities requiring unconscionable injury or emphasizing injustice. Rather than resolve every difference among those formulations, the court concluded that the trial findings satisfied both the ordinary elements and the additional concern with unconscionability. Id. at 44–45.

The specific directions to proceed as though the weight issue were resolved, combined with repeated pressure to meet the deadline, supported a clear promise even though the final equipment contract remained incomplete. Cyberchron incurred labor and material expenses because of those assurances. Calldata's continued pressure while seeking another supplier, followed by termination, supplied a sufficient basis for the finding of unconscionable injury. The appellate court upheld the selection of July 15 as the beginning of the relevant reliance period; expenses before those assurances could not be attributed to them. Id. at 44–45.

Calldata urged a narrow reading of Swerdloff under which promissory estoppel would apply only to limited categories such as charitable contributions, marriage settlements, and promises associated with improvements to gifted land. The court treated that restrictive formulation as dictum, found it inconsistent with later New York authority and Second Circuit decisions, and declined to make it controlling. Calldata also sought to restrict the damages period to the August 28 delivery date. The trial findings did not make delivery by that date a condition of every assurance to pay for continued work. The court therefore upheld the July 15–September 25 reliance period instead. Id. at 44–45.

## Why the damages award had to be reconsidered
The appellate court agreed with a reliance measure, but rejected the trial court's categorical exclusion of overhead. A business can incur real costs through indirect expenses as well as direct labor and materials. If Cyberchron could establish an appropriate share of actual overhead attributable to the work induced by Calldata's promises, those costs could be part of its out-of-pocket reliance loss. Past operating history and normal, rational allocation methods could provide proof. Cyberchron did not necessarily need to identify a particular alternative project it had lost. The court distinguished an approach in the Seventh Circuit's Autotrol decision under Texas law that emphasized that sort of alternative-use evidence. Id. at 45–47 & n.1.

The court also directed consideration of shutdown expenses after September 25. The timing of payment was not dispositive: an expense incurred after negotiations ended could still result from work undertaken in reliance on the earlier promise. But Cyberchron could recover only shutdown costs caused by the reliance beginning in July, not costs associated with its earlier, unprotected work. This was permission to establish causal loss, not an award of all shutdown costs or all overhead. Lost profits were not the basis of the appellate relief; the remand concerned appropriate reliance damages. Id. at 46–47.

## Holding and exact disposition
The Second Circuit affirmed the determination that Cyberchron was entitled to recover on promissory estoppel, rejected independent TLP recovery, and vacated the judgment for a redetermination of damages. The district court could receive additional evidence or use the existing record. The opinion did not set a replacement dollar amount, restore the rejected contract claims, or order Calldata to purchase the equipment. 47 F.3d at 47.

The case illustrates why the absence of a final bargain and the existence of an actionable reliance promise are separate questions. It also makes causation do real work: the recoverable period starts with the proven assurances, and the recoverable categories include only expenditures properly attributable to them. Preserving that distinction explains both the affirmance of liability and the remand to reconsider a damages award that excluded potentially compensable costs too broadly.
