# In re Howmedica Osteonics Corp.

**867 F.3d 390 (3d Cir. 2017) — August 15, 2017.** Judge Krause wrote for the panel of Krause, Scirica, and Fuentes. No separate opinion appears. ## Facts and procedural setting

Howmedica Osteonics, a New Jersey medical-device company and Stryker subsidiary, sued five former California sales representatives: Keegan Freeman, Michael Nordyke, Brett Sarkisian, Taylor Smith, and Bryan Wyatt. Their employment agreements restricted competition and disclosure of confidential information. Four agreements selected New Jersey for litigation; Nordyke’s selected Michigan. Following disagreements about management and compensation, the representatives resigned and became independent contractors working for competitor DePuy and its regional distributor, Golden State. Customers followed them. Howmedica alleged that the representatives and corporations had conspired before the resignations to divert business. Those were allegations underlying the contract and tort claims, not adjudicated findings of misconduct. (Slip op. 4–6.)

Howmedica filed in New Jersey against the representatives and DePuy, later joining Golden State as an allegedly necessary party. All defendants sought transfer to the Northern District of California. A magistrate judge granted transfer on April 20, 2015; the district judge affirmed on August 26, 2016. The magistrate also found that New Jersey lacked personal jurisdiction over Golden State, although the district judge did not resolve that issue. Meanwhile, Golden State pursued a California declaratory action attacking the noncompetition agreements. The California court related the actions and entered scheduling orders; proceedings were later stayed pending the mandamus petition. (Slip op. 5–8.)

Several qualifications narrowed the dispute. Defendants waived their appellate objections based on improper statutory venue. The representatives also waived their personal-jurisdiction objections on appeal, four having contractually consented to New Jersey jurisdiction, and DePuy had not raised that defense below. Golden State’s jurisdictional objection remained. No party challenged the validity of the forum clauses themselves: California objections to the substantive noncompetition restrictions did not invalidate the severable forum terms. A prior standing objection concerning Howmedica’s enforcement of Stryker contracts was not renewed. (Slip op. 4–8 nn.1–4.)

## Questions and holding

How should § 1404(a) operate when a lawsuit combines defendants bound by a forum clause with defendants who never agreed to it? And could mandamus correct the transfer after the California court had begun managing the case?

The Third Circuit retained mandamus jurisdiction and found clear legal error. Atlantic Marine’s treatment of contracting parties does not remove noncontracting parties’ ordinary private-interest arguments. Courts must analyze the groups separately, consider jurisdiction and joinder constraints, and then assess severance and transfer. Applying that framework, the court directed transfer of the claims against DePuy and Golden State to California, while retaining claims against all five representatives in New Jersey. (Slip op. 8–12, 17–22, 33–34.)

## Mandamus jurisdiction and standard

The All Writs Act permitted review in aid of possible eventual appellate jurisdiction over a final judgment. Transfer orders can be reviewed by mandamus for legal and procedural error. Nevertheless, a transferor circuit does not retain that authority indefinitely once the transferee court proceeds with the litigation. Here, the related-case and scheduling orders did not advance the California case beyond the level previously held compatible with transferor review. Howmedica filed its petition twenty-seven days after the district court’s order, promptly enough under a precedent allowing thirty-three days. The court expressly declined to define the precise event that ultimately divests transferor jurisdiction. (Slip op. 8–11 & n.6.)

Mandamus ordinarily requires clear and indisputable legal error or abuse of discretion, no adequate alternative remedy, and likely irreparable injury; issuance remains discretionary even then. Transfer orders as a class satisfied the latter two requirements under circuit precedent because eventual review in the transferee forum would not adequately repair the procedural injury. The decisive question was therefore whether this transfer involved clear error warranting extraordinary relief. (Slip op. 11–12.)

## Governing transfer principles

Without a forum clause, § 1404(a) balances private and public interests. Private factors include the parties’ preferred fora, where claims arose, relative financial circumstances, witness convenience, access to records, and practical trial burdens. Public factors include enforceability of a judgment, congestion, local interests, public policies, familiarity with governing law, and judicial economy. The court carefully distinguished practical burdens on litigants from the public interest in judicial economy: describing all practical considerations as public would wrongly allow contractual private-interest concessions to reappear under another name. (Slip op. 13–15 & n.7.)

Atlantic Marine changes the analysis for parties who agreed to a valid clause. A court disregards the forum preference of the party resisting its contractual commitment, deems the contracting parties’ private interests to favor the selected forum, and examines the remaining public factors. Ordinarily the clause controls. But nonsignatories have not surrendered their private interests. Treating the clause as either controlling the entire case or irrelevant to the entire case would erase that difference. (Slip op. 15–16, 22–24.)

## The four-step framework

First, identify the contracting parties and presume that claims involving them belong in their agreed forum, subject to the unusual-case exception. Second, independently balance the noncontracting parties’ private and public interests. If both inquiries select the same forum, no further conflict remains. (Slip op. 18–19.)

Third, examine threshold constraints on severance, including personal and subject-matter jurisdiction, venue, joinder, and indispensable parties. Severance may be required to cure a defect, prohibited because an indispensable party cannot be removed, or left to discretion. If only one outcome satisfies the legal constraints, adopt it. If several remain possible, proceed to the fourth step. The framework permits convenience-based sequencing under Sinochem; it does not make threshold defects disappear. (Slip op. 19–21 & nn.8–9.)

Fourth, compare litigation efficiency, available coordination techniques, other public interests, and prejudice to noncontracting parties’ private interests. Courts may retain the whole action, transfer it, or sever claims. They consider the number and resources of noncontracting parties and the burdens of duplicate proceedings. But contractual settled expectations may be displaced only when countervailing interests overwhelmingly outweigh the strong interest in enforcing them. (Slip op. 21–22.)

## Applying the framework

Freeman, Sarkisian, Smith, and Wyatt had agreed to New Jersey litigation. Nordyke’s Michigan clause did not select New Jersey, and neither he nor Howmedica sought to enforce it; the court therefore did not consider Michigan as an alternative forum. DePuy and Golden State had signed no applicable clause. Howmedica’s attempt to bind them through a “closely related parties” theory failed under circuit precedent. The California court’s different conclusion had no preclusive effect because it was not essential to a judgment and rested on a different circuit’s doctrine. Even under other circuits’ foreseeability approaches, the record did not establish that the corporations knew of or participated in the representatives’ negotiations, and Nordyke’s separate Michigan clause did not make New Jersey foreseeable for claims concerning his own conduct. (Slip op. 25–27 & nn.11–13.)

The ordinary interests of DePuy, Golden State, and Nordyke favored California, where the alleged conduct, witnesses, and representatives were located. But Golden State could not remain in New Jersey because it lacked the necessary contacts. Calling it a joint tortfeasor or claiming that a judgment might be persuasive against it did not make it required under Rule 19(a), much less indispensable under Rule 19(b). Severance was therefore permissible, and transfer or dismissal of at least its claims was mandatory. (Slip op. 28–30.)

At the final step, DePuy belonged with Golden State because the two corporations faced the same alleged wrongdoing. Nordyke belonged with the four other representatives because their alleged misconduct was alike. Coordinated pretrial procedures, video depositions, stipulations, and appropriate joint proceedings could reduce duplication between the courts. Comparable congestion and judgment-enforcement considerations did not override the clauses. California’s opposition to noncompetition agreements concerned substantive enforceability, distinct from where litigation should occur. Its local interest was offset by New Jersey’s interest in employment agreements prepared and executed there. (Slip op. 30–33 & nn.14–17.)

Nordyke’s comparatively limited finances counted against New Jersey, but he had already agreed to a similarly inconvenient Michigan forum and shared counsel with the other representatives. The additional burden did not overwhelmingly outweigh the four representatives’ contractual expectations and the efficiency of litigating the representative claims together. (Slip op. 33.)

## Judgment and assigned significance

The court issued a writ vacating the whole-case transfer order. It instructed the district court on remand to sever the claims against DePuy and Golden State under Rule 21, transfer those claims to the Northern District of California under § 1404(a), and retain jurisdiction over claims against all five representatives. It did not decide the underlying contract or conspiracy merits. (Slip op. 33–34.)

The assigned editorial note accurately emphasizes that the Atlantic Marine presumption applies to agreeing parties rather than an entire lawsuit. The complete opinion adds the necessary qualification: noncontracting parties’ interests remain cognizable, yet their presence does not automatically defeat contractual enforcement. Severance, jurisdictional limits, and practical coordination permit different groups to proceed in different fora.
