# Johnson v. Honeywell Information Systems, Inc.
955 F.2d 409 (6th Cir. 1992) | Contracts

## Decision and precise result
Judge Ryan wrote for a Sixth Circuit panel consisting of Ryan, Boggs, and Senior Judge Godbold, sitting by designation. The court decided the diversity appeal January 30, 1992; rehearing was denied March 9. Predicting Michigan law, it held that sufficiently material application fraud could provide just cause even though the employer discovered it after discharge. It reversed denial of Honeywell’s summary-judgment motion on the contract claim, affirmed a directed verdict on the state civil-rights claim, and remanded with instructions to enter judgment for Honeywell on all counts. This is the reported 1992 decision, not a certification of current after-acquired-evidence law. (409–411, 416.)

## Employment, stated discharge grounds, and competing allegations
Mildred Johnson worked as a field relations manager from November 1976 until November 2, 1984. She assisted branch managers with affirmative-action programs, EEOC charges, hiring, and other personnel matters. Her reviews were generally favorable until mid-1983, when supervisors and internal clients complained about availability, cooperation, effectiveness, flexibility, and knowledge. Later reviews were mixed. Honeywell discharged her after she declined to resign, citing refusal to follow reasonable supervisory directions. (411.)

Johnson admitted not changing her work habits but maintained that her performance was satisfactory. She alleged that discharge instead reflected conflict with managers over affirmative-action goals. Nearly a year before discharge she had sent supervisors a memorandum complaining of discrimination against her, exclusion from meetings and opportunities, and unfair criticism intended to make her abandon those efforts. A supervisor investigated and found the complaint without merit. These were competing accounts of the original motivation, distinct from the subsequently discovered application fraud. (411.)

Johnson sued in state court for breach of a just-cause employment contract and retaliation prohibited by Michigan’s Elliott-Larsen Civil Rights Act. Honeywell removed the action. During discovery it learned that Johnson had falsely claimed a bachelor’s degree from the University of Detroit, though she had completed only four courses and audited two; it also discovered false or exaggerated representations about Wayne State studies, prior jobs, and property-management work during a period of unemployment. The advertisement required a college degree and four to six years of relevant experience. Her signed application warned that false information could cause immediate discharge later. (411–412.)

## Proceedings below
Honeywell conceded a just-cause contract but sought summary judgment on two theories: the newly discovered fraud constituted just cause, and fraudulent inducement prevented formation of the claimed contract. The district court rejected the first theory because the fraud had not actually motivated discharge and found factual disputes about materiality and reliance on the second. Later it directed a verdict for Honeywell on the statutory claim because Johnson had not established retaliatory pretext. The contract claim produced a jury verdict for Johnson, and Honeywell’s directed-verdict and postverdict motions on that claim were denied. Honeywell appealed; Johnson cross-appealed the statutory ruling. (410–412.)

## Unknown facts can supply contractual just cause
The Sixth Circuit found no controlling Michigan Supreme Court decision directly addressing after-acquired misconduct. It relied principally on the Michigan Court of Appeals’ unpublished Bradley decision, which allowed misconduct predating termination to be substantive evidence even if the employer discovered it later. General contract doctrine, the rule’s practical rationale, and the absence of contrary Michigan authority also supported the prediction. The court distinguished Toussaint’s discussion of the jury’s role in identifying an employer’s true motive: that inquiry mattered in relevant factual settings, but it did not eliminate an independent defense based on application fraud. The court expressly recognized that unknown facts were neither the actual nor inducing cause of discharge. (412–413.)

The defense was bounded. In résumé-fraud cases, the misrepresentation must be material, directly relevant to evaluating an employment candidate, and relied upon in hiring. These requirements prevented employers from defeating otherwise impermissible discharges by searching for trivial inaccuracies. Thus, the court did not hold that every false application statement automatically constituted just cause. (414–415.)

## Application of materiality and reliance
Johnson admitted misrepresenting her education to obtain consideration for the job. The hiring manager stated that he relied on her claimed degree, would neither have hired nor interviewed her had he known the truth, and hired four other field relations managers on similar degree representations. Her earlier personnel experience, the interviewers’ focus on experience, and a later supervisor’s lack of a degree did not create a genuine dispute about this hiring decision. The question was whether Honeywell would have hired Johnson when she applied without the claimed degree. On this evidence, the court found reliance and materiality established as a matter of law and held the fraud sufficient just cause. (414–415.)

## State statutory claim and independent protected-activity analysis
The court also treated after-acquired fraud as barring relief under Elliott-Larsen, relying on Summers’ reasoning about after-discovered employee misconduct. Even assuming retaliatory discharge, Honeywell had established that it would not have hired Johnson and would have discharged her upon discovering the fraud. This was the opinion’s historical complete-defense reasoning, rather than a finding that the later-discovered fraud actually caused the 1984 decision. Its numbered footnote explained the statute’s retaliation provision and the court’s reliance on analogous Title VII interpretation. (415 & n. 1.)

Separately, the court concluded that Johnson had not established protected opposition to a violation of Elliott-Larsen. Complaints about resistance to affirmative-action goals did not identify practices required by that Act. The memorandum’s attributed comments about not wanting Black workers or hiring minorities and women demonstrated, in the court’s analysis, at most one employee’s alleged intolerance rather than an allegation of Honeywell’s unlawful employment practice. Working on discrimination complaints or affirmative-action matters itself did not create statutory protection. The court therefore sustained the statutory directed verdict on additional grounds, not solely the contract defense. (415–416.)
