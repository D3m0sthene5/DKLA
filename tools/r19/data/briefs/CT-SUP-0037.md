# Gianni v. R. Russel & Co., Inc.
281 Pa. 320, 126 A. 791 (1924) | Contracts

## Court, decision, and assigned scope
The Pennsylvania Supreme Court decided this appeal on November 24, 1924. Justice Schaffer is identified as the author in the assigned report; no separate judicial opinion appears. The FA26 Contracts Supplement prints the decision at PDF pages 37-39. The complete Pennsylvania Reports reproduction supplies the procedural report and the opinion at pages 321-325. Its OCR spells the judge's name Schaefer and the defendant's name Russell; the assigned report spells them Schaffer and Russel. These are transcription and caption variants of the same decision, not additional cases.

## The lease and the disputed promise
Gianni already operated a small store in a Pittsburgh office building, selling tobacco, fruit, candy, and soft drinks. After acquiring the property, R. Russel & Co. negotiated a new three-year lease with him. The written lease described the permitted merchandise, including fruit, candy, and soda water, and expressly prohibited selling tobacco under penalty of immediate forfeiture. It was prepared after the negotiations, left in Gianni's possession, and read to him by two people, including his daughter, before he signed. (281 Pa. at 321-322.)

Gianni claimed that the landlord's agent had also promised him the exclusive right to sell soft drinks in the building. He said that exclusivity was consideration for accepting a higher rent, giving up tobacco sales, and entering the lease. The signed lease contained no exclusivity term. Shortly afterward, the landlord leased the adjoining room to a drug company without restricting that company's right to sell soda water and other soft drinks. Gianni alleged that the competition substantially reduced his receipts and profits. (Id. at 322.)

The alleged oral promise was contested. Gianni testified that it had been made at least two days before signing and repeated when the lease was signed. One witness corroborated a conversation several days before execution, but no witness who attended the signing corroborated the asserted repetition. The landlord's agent denied making the promise at either stage. The appellate decision did not resolve that credibility dispute by finding that the agent had lied or that the promise never occurred. Its legal point was that the claimed promise could not support this recovery alongside the integrated writing. (Id. at 322-323.)

## Procedure and the competing positions
Gianni sued for damages for breach of the alleged oral agreement. The reporter's procedural statement records a jury verdict and judgment for $3,694 in the Allegheny County Court of Common Pleas. The landlord appealed, challenging, among other things, the refusal to enter judgment in its favor notwithstanding the verdict. (Id. at 321.)

Gianni's lawyer expressly rejected reformation based on fraud, accident, or mistake. Instead, he argued that the oral undertaking was an independent agreement, unrelated to what belonged in the written lease. That theory sought to preserve the promise without changing the document. The landlord relied on the parol evidence rule: negotiations about the same subject were superseded by the written agreement. The Supreme Court rejected the tenant's characterization of the promise as genuinely independent. (Id. at 323-325.)

## Issue, rule, and holding
The issue was whether a tenant could enforce an alleged prior or contemporaneous oral promise of exclusive soft-drink sales when the written lease regulated what he could sell but omitted exclusivity. The court held that he could not. The lease was the complete agreement on that subject, so evidence of the alleged oral undertaking was inadmissible and the undertaking could not be enforced by relabeling it as a separate contract. (Id. at 323-325.)

The court did not begin with a rule that every signed writing automatically contains every conceivable agreement between its signers. It first required that the writing be the entire contract, then compared the written and oral arrangements. Its test asked whether similarly situated parties would naturally and normally include the alleged oral term in the same written transaction. If the terms concern the same subject and are sufficiently interrelated to be executed together, the writing embraces the subsidiary agreement. The court, rather than the jury, determines that question of scope. (Id. at 323-324.)

## Reasoning: the missing term belongs to the written subject
The written lease already addressed the store's permitted uses and merchandise. The claimed exclusivity term addressed precisely that commercial arrangement: which occupant could sell soft drinks in this building. More specifically, Gianni claimed that his written promise not to sell tobacco was part of the consideration for the unwritten promise of exclusivity. The relationship between the two promises made inclusion in a common writing especially natural. The document preserved the restriction on Gianni while supposedly omitting its directly corresponding benefit. The court considered that claimed benefit more pertinent to the written provisions than almost any other term could be. (Id. at 324.)

This explains why mere consistency was insufficient. An exclusive right to sell soft drinks did not necessarily contradict the lease's permission to sell them. But the parol evidence rule, as the court applied it to a complete agreement, barred adding related terms as well as directly contradicting existing ones. Once the writing was complete on this subject, silence about exclusivity was not an invitation to prove an additional bargain through oral evidence. (Id. at 323-325.)

The court also drew on the idea that a writing's treatment of an element is a strong indication that it was meant to embody the whole transaction concerning that element. If a subject is entirely absent from the writing, a separate oral arrangement may stand on a different footing. Here, however, sales restrictions and store use were expressly present. Gianni therefore could not move the exclusivity promise outside the lease merely by alleging a separate oral contract. (Id. at 324-325.)

## Why the alternative theories did not save the verdict
The opinion treated the rule as more than a restriction on a particular trial presentation. It stated that an otherwise adequate subsidiary agreement within the writing's scope could not gain validity simply because a plaintiff sued on it separately. Without that consequence, a party could evade integration by dividing a transaction into written and oral causes of action. The allegedly independent contract was still part of the same field covered by the lease. (Id. at 325.)

The court recognized fraud, accident, and mistake as possible grounds for relief concerning omitted terms in the historical doctrine it was applying. Such a showing would support equitable reformation, including reformation administered through Pennsylvania's common-law forms. But Gianni expressly disavowed that route. The court did not find a fraudulent inducement claim factually disproved; that was not the claim submitted to it. Nor did it decide whether every oral promise by a landlord is unenforceable. Its conclusion rested on this complete writing, the close relationship of the promised exclusivity to the written sales terms, and the absence of the invoked exceptions. (Id.)

## Exact judgment and practical result
The Supreme Court reversed the tenant's judgment and entered judgment for the landlord. It did not order another trial to decide whose account of the conversation was more credible, and it did not send the case back for the tenant to recover a smaller amount. The integration ruling eliminated the legal basis for this verdict. (Id. at 325.)

For contract analysis, the useful sequence is to identify the alleged term, determine whether the writing is complete on its subject, compare the relationship of the two arrangements, and then consider a properly asserted exception. The court's insistence on written-contract integrity should be understood through those steps rather than reduced to an unsupported statement that oral agreements never matter.
