# Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Association v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co.

State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co., 463 U.S. 29 (1983), decided June 24, 1983, with the companion petitions identified at 463 U.S. 29 n.*. Justice White wrote the Court's opinion. ## Parties

The Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Association and the parties in the companion petitions, including the Department of Transportation, defended the rescission of a federal automobile occupant-protection requirement. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Company and the National Association of Independent Insurers had petitioned for review of that rescission. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, exercising authority delegated by the Secretary of Transportation, was the agency responsible for the challenged decision. The litigation concerned the legality of NHTSA's explanation for withdrawing the passive-restraint requirement, rather than liability for an individual automobile accident. 463 U.S. at 29 n.*, 34 n.3, 39–40.

## Material Facts

The National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act directed the Secretary to establish practicable, objective safety standards meeting the need for motor-vehicle safety. Relevant considerations included available safety data, the reasonableness and practicability of a proposed standard, and its contribution to the Act's purposes. The statute expressly made orders establishing, amending, and revoking standards subject to APA review. Id. at 33–34.

Standard 208 had developed through repeated proceedings. The original requirement that cars contain manual seatbelts proved insufficient because many occupants did not use them. Passive systems sought to provide protection without repeated affirmative action by the occupant. Airbags inflated automatically in a crash; automatic belts deployed when occupants entered or operated the car. Congress later prohibited reliance on an ignition interlock that prevented operation of a car unless belts were connected, as well as the specified continuous warning buzzer. That history mattered because NHTSA ultimately treated dissatisfaction with interlocks as a reason to question other use-compelling devices. Id. at 34–37.

The 1977 Modified Standard 208 required a phased introduction of passive restraints and let manufacturers choose between airbags and automatic belts. NHTSA expected approximately sixty percent airbags and forty percent automatic belts. By 1981, manufacturers instead planned to use automatic belts in approximately ninety-nine percent of new cars, predominantly belts occupants could detach and leave disconnected. The agency had not repudiated the effectiveness of airbags or the protective value of belts when worn. Its concern was whether the systems manufacturers actually planned to install would increase restraint use sufficiently to justify their cost. Id. at 37–39.

NHTSA rescinded the passive-restraint requirement in 1981. It reasoned that detachable belts might provide little increase in use, that compliance would cost approximately one billion dollars, and that consumers might react adversely to an expensive and ineffective regulation. It did not discuss an airbags-only requirement. Its treatment of continuous, nondetachable automatic belts grouped them with other use-compelling devices instead of examining their distinct operating features. Id. at 38–39, 46–48, 55–57.

## Procedural History

After notice, written comments, and public hearings, NHTSA issued its final rescission rule. State Farm and the other insurer association sought review in the D.C. Circuit. That court found the rescission arbitrary and capricious on grounds involving the predicted use of detachable belts, the treatment of nondetachable belts, and the failure to consider airbags. It also inferred from intervening congressional activity a commitment to passive restraints that warranted increasingly demanding reasons for rescission. Id. at 38–40, 44.

The Court of Appeals directed further administrative proceedings and addressed the implementation schedule through subsequent orders. The Supreme Court granted review and examined both the proper scope of review and the adequacy of NHTSA's actual explanation. It agreed that the rescission could not stand, but rejected part of the lower court's approach, including the heightened standard inferred from congressional reactions and any suggestion that uncertainty could never support rescission. Id. at 40–46, 51–52. The different reasoning explains why the Supreme Court vacated and remanded rather than simply affirming the judgment below.

## Issue

Does rescission of an existing motor-vehicle safety standard receive ordinary arbitrary-and-capricious review, or the more limited scrutiny urged for an agency's refusal to initiate regulation? Under the proper standard, did NHTSA adequately explain abandonment of the passive-restraint requirement when it failed to address an airbags-only alternative, discounted the prospective benefits of detachable automatic belts, and did not separately analyze continuous automatic belts? The case also asks whether subsequent congressional actions and inaction could intensify the reviewing court's scrutiny beyond the statutory standard. Id. at 40–46, 46–57.

## Rule and Holding

APA § 706(2)(A)'s arbitrary-and-capricious standard governed the rescission, just as it governed promulgation or amendment under this statute. An agency must examine the relevant material and explain the connection between its findings and its decision. Typical defects include reliance on unauthorized considerations, omission of an important aspect of the problem, an explanation contrary to the evidence, or an explanation so implausible that it cannot reasonably be attributed to agency expertise or a permissible difference in judgment. A court reviews those matters without substituting its preferred policy and may uphold an imperfect explanation whose path is reasonably discernible. Id. at 41–43.

NHTSA's rescission was arbitrary and capricious. All nine Justices joined the Court's discussion of the failure to consider the airbags alternative. A five-Justice majority also found the treatment of automatic seatbelts insufficient. The Court rejected an intensified standard based on postenactment congressional activity. It recognized that uncertainty can support rescission when the agency reasonably explains the evidence and its judgment. The duty to explain a changed policy did not make the existing standard permanent or dictate an airbags mandate as the only lawful outcome. Id. at 42–46, 48–52, 57–59.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court vacated the D.C. Circuit's judgment and remanded with instructions to return the matter to NHTSA for further consideration consistent with the opinion. The agency could reconsider, retain, or amend the standard along lines supported by an adequate analysis. The Court did not itself write a replacement safety rule or order airbags installed in every vehicle. It also recognized sufficient justification for temporarily suspending the requirement while the necessary reconsideration occurred, without deciding the parties' competing descriptions of the lower court's implementation order. Id. at 34, 57 & n.21.

## Reasoning

1. Revocation was expressly reviewable and had to account for the agency's existing judgment. The Motor Vehicle Safety Act treated the establishment, amendment, and revocation of standards alike for procedural and judicial-review purposes. Giving rescission the exceptionally narrow treatment urged for a refusal to act would undermine that express provision. Rescission also reversed a previously adopted regulatory position. The agency therefore had to explain why it was abandoning that position, while retaining latitude to adapt policy to changed circumstances and judgments. Id. at 41–42.

2. The standard concerned reasoned agency decision-making within the statute. Judicial restraint did not permit the court to imagine a rationale the agency had never supplied. NHTSA had to engage with relevant data and explain how its choice followed. Conversely, the court did not demand flawless drafting or agreement with the policy choice. The opinion distinguished this inquiry from the minimal rationality used to review economic legislation under the Due Process Clause: a presumption favoring constitutional validity of legislation is not the same as review of an agency's discharge of a statutory mandate. Id. at 42–43 & n.9.

3. Congressional reactions did not authorize a heightened version of APA review. The D.C. Circuit relied on Congress's prohibition of interlocks, its failure to veto the 1977 standard, and unenacted proposals. Those events did not establish that a subsequent rescission was unlawful or deserved a different standard of scrutiny. Even ratification of a former agency interpretation would not, without statutory incorporation, necessarily approve or condemn a later exercise of regulatory discretion. The Court also found the inferences from the particular legislative episodes uncertain. Id. at 44–46.

4. The failure to address airbags omitted an important aspect of the existing regulatory problem. NHTSA's concern about manufacturers choosing detachable belts could undermine that means of compliance without undermining airbags. The agency continued to recognize airbag technology as effective. It therefore needed to explain why it was eliminating the passive-restraint obligation altogether instead of considering a requirement satisfied by airbags. The manufacturers' preference for another technology supplied no agency analysis of that option. Congress had authorized safety regulation partly because the industry had not itself made all the necessary safety improvements. Id. at 46–49.

5. Counsel's proposed defenses could not fill the gap. Petitioners suggested difficulties involving small cars, public reaction, and implementation of an airbags mandate. Those were not reasons NHTSA had given for rejecting an option it had never considered in its rescission explanation. The Court applied the Chenery principle requiring review of the agency's actual grounds. It left open whether an airbags-only requirement would require additional notice and recognized that temporary suspension could accommodate study of that option. Id. at 49–50 & n.15.

6. Requiring consideration of this alternative did not prescribe an extra hearing procedure. Petitioners invoked Vermont Yankee's restriction on judicially invented administrative procedures. The Court required no specific new hearing mechanism and imposed no duty to discuss every conceivable policy alternative. Airbags were an established technological means of satisfying the very standard being withdrawn, previously found effective and cost-beneficial. A demand for a reasoned treatment of that option was therefore a substantive review of the agency's explanation. Id. at 50–51.

7. Uncertainty was permissible, but the agency had to explain what followed from it. The Court allowed that serious uncertainty could justify rescission even without direct evidence proving the regulation would fail. Complex regulatory judgments often require inferences beyond conclusive data. Nevertheless, an assertion of uncertainty had to engage with the evidence and explain the choice, including ordinarily why further information was not sought before rescission. The majority thus declined to adopt the lower court's reasoning wholesale while still finding NHTSA's analysis insufficient. Id. at 51–52.

8. The evidence about detachable belts left an unaddressed behavioral mechanism. NHTSA questioned whether field studies of voluntarily purchased vehicles, often using interlocks, could be generalized to all cars. The Court accepted that evaluating the studies' generalizability lay within agency expertise. But even accepting their limitations, the agency had not addressed the role of inertia. Once reconnected, an automatic belt would keep functioning without a fresh decision on every trip. That feature could increase use among occasional belt users and required analysis before treating detachable automatic belts as comparable to manual ones. The Court did not itself decide the magnitude of the increase. Id. at 52–54.

9. The deficient benefit analysis also affected the cost and public-reaction analysis. NHTSA's concern about consumers paying more for negligible improvement depended on its estimate that safety benefits would be minimal. Reexamining the expected benefits required reconsidering that cost judgment. Costs and practicability remained relevant, but the statute made safety the principal objective. Id. at 54–55.

10. Continuous belts required their own explanation. NHTSA grouped nondetachable belts with interlocks under use-compelling features and referred to emergency escape and public resistance. The agency previously had accepted spool-release designs as allowing effective escape, and a continuous belt did not prevent the vehicle from operating as an interlock did. It was entitled to change its assessment but had to explain why the prior distinctions no longer mattered. The unexplained equation of these systems failed to connect the facts and the decision. Id. at 55–57.

## Separate Opinions

Justice Rehnquist concurred in part and dissented in part, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justices Powell and O'Connor. They joined Parts I–IV and V-A, including the governing standard of review, rejection of the heightened congressional-history approach, and the airbag analysis. Rehnquist also expressly agreed that NHTSA needed to explain why it abandoned the previously approved airbag and continuous-spool options. His disagreement concerned the majority's conclusion that the agency had inadequately analyzed detachable automatic belts. Id. at 57–58 (Rehnquist, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part).

For Rehnquist, the agency reasonably declined to extrapolate from studies of vehicles voluntarily bought with automatic belts and interlocks to a universal standard using belts without interlocks. He read the agency as acknowledging some increased use but concluding that the likely benefit was too small to justify the cost. Although its explanation could have been clearer, he regarded the requisite rational connection as present. Id. at 58–59.

Rehnquist further observed that a change in presidential administration could reasonably lead officials to reassess costs, uncertainties, public resistance, and policy priorities. That was his separate opinion's account of permissible political reappraisal. It included an express limitation: a new administration could not disregard Congress's statutory standards or refuse enforcement merely because it disliked a law. The majority did not adopt an election alone as a sufficient explanation for this rescission. Id. at 59 & n.*.

## Notes

For an exam, keep the decision's layers separate: the legal standard of review; the agency's actual reasons; omitted alternatives; treatment of uncertainty and contrary considerations; the appropriate remedy; and the separate opinion's view of political change. The Supreme Court's agreement that NHTSA needed further consideration coexisted with its rejection of part of the D.C. Circuit's analysis. The actual disposition was vacatur and remand, not a judicial airbag mandate. Id. at 41–57.

The assigned casebook omits portions of the general formulation at reporter p. 43 and the congressional-history analysis, and abbreviates the Vermont Yankee discussion. Those points above come from the full opinion. All eight following notes at casebook pp. 608–624 are editorial material; they neither expand the Supreme Court’s 1983 holding nor certify the separate later cases.

1. Political explanations (608–609): the editors ask whether an expressly stated change in electoral ideology could justify rescission, alongside competing scholarship. Their hypothetical is not the rationale NHTSA supplied or one the majority approved.

2. Statutory discretion (609–610): Secretary Coleman’s 1976 proposal discusses individual choice, the market, costs and equity. The editors ask how such considerations fit the Safety Act’s purposes; the quoted agency policy and questions are not additional judicial holdings.

3. Proceedings on remand (610–613): the editors describe the 1984 rule’s renewed passive-protection requirement and its conditional withdrawal if states covering two-thirds of the population adopted qualifying belt-use laws before April 1, 1989. They report that condition was never triggered. The 1986 D.C. Circuit decision treated the withdrawal challenge as unripe and sustained the reasoned treatment of the remaining alternatives. This later judicial proceeding has its own brief and audit status.

4. Technology forcing and substantive review (613–616): Mashaw and Harfst argue that demands for a documented rational process can favor established technology and delay regulation; the note contrasts rulemaking with recalls and later guidance. These are attributed scholarly critiques, not a ruling that agencies are exempt from reasoned analysis.

5. Other technology-forcing statutes (616–619): the editors contrast the diesel-particulate decision in NRDC v. EPA, which allowed a reasoned prediction of future trap-oxidizer development. The excerpt requires a plausible methodology, remaining development steps and timing; it does not demand that every engineering problem already be solved. That separate case retains its independent source-review status.

6. Organic statutes (619–620): analytical obligations may arise from an agency’s authorizing statute and operate through APA review. The editors illustrate health, harassment and economic-cost requirements in later cases. Distinguish a statute-specific omitted consideration from an additional procedure invented by a court.

7. Remand without vacatur (620–622): the editors discuss the seriousness of a defect and the disruption caused by vacatur, possible agency delay illustrated by Core Communications, and competing views of the remedy’s legality. State Farm itself vacated the Court of Appeals’ judgment and remanded; this note does not change that disposition.

8. Continued application (622–624): the editors use Ohio v. EPA’s 2024 stay decision to illustrate searching review and disagreement over the agency’s assumptions, responses to comments and the challengers’ presentation. The majority’s likelihood-of-success analysis and Barrett’s dissent are separate from State Farm’s holding. The note also refers to a separate writing in Department of Commerce v. New York. These later developments remain subject to their own audits and are not presented as a current-law citator certification.

The technological and usage figures describe the historical administrative record. They should not be used as contemporary safety statistics. This brief does not address the present text of Standard 208 or independently certify later developments in arbitrary-and-capricious review.
