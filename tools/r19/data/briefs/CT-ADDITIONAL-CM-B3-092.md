# Diamond v. University of Southern California
11 Cal. App. 3d 49, 89 Cal. Rptr. 302 (1970) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

California’s Court of Appeal, Second District, Division Five, affirmed summary judgment for USC on September 3, 1970. Presiding Justice Kaus wrote; Justices Stephens and Reppy concurred, with no separate opinion. Roger Diamond’s appeal expressly sought vindication of his asserted attorney-fee entitlement in the class action he brought as an attorney representing himself and other first-time economy season-ticket holders. The court resolved the case through the historical exception to anticipatory breach for contracts that had become unilateral through the promisees’ complete performance. It declined to decide the broader class-action questions. (11 Cal. App. 3d at 49, 51–56 & n.3.)

## Promised tickets, initial refusal, and eventual performance

The complaint alleged that before the 1968 football season USC sold economy season tickets with a promise that each purchaser could obtain an option to buy a Rose Bowl ticket if USC’s team was selected. Diamond and the proposed class had paid for such season tickets for the first time. After selection for the January 1, 1969 game, he received a December 4 notice that reasons beyond USC’s control prevented first-time economy holders from receiving the promised applications. He sued on December 9. The complaint alleged a $12 difference between a Rose Bowl ticket’s market value and the university’s price, and $36,000 in aggregate damages for 3,000 tickets purchased by approximately 600 persons, plus costs and fees. These were allegations, not a damages award. (Id. at 51.)

USC supported its January 23 summary-judgment motion with ticket-committee chairman Elton Phillips’s declaration. He stated that 46,052 season tickets had been sold with the option representation, that the Rose Bowl allotment included reservations for specified groups, and that USC accordingly ranked first-time economy purchasers last. Applications mailed to other season-ticket holders required orders by December 4. Between December 8 and 16, enough holders failed to exercise their options that applications could be sent to the previously excluded group; USC mailed those on December 17. The report states 53,003 allotted tickets, 10,590 reserved, and 42,513 remaining, figures whose subtraction does not reconcile; that numerical inconsistency is not necessary to the holding. (Id. at 52.)

Diamond’s counterdeclaration described calls with USC’s attorney: on December 16 he was not told of the newly available tickets; on December 19 he was informed that applications had already been mailed. He asserted that USC could not proceed without judicial approval but offered to assist in obtaining it. Counsel said USC would proceed without that approval. The trial court granted summary judgment on February 10, and judgment was entered March 4. (Id. at 52.)

## Diamond’s theory and the contract holding

Diamond argued that the December 4 refusal anticipatorily repudiated USC’s obligation; filing suit materially changed the class’s position and ended USC’s power to retract; subsequently supplying applications improperly settled matured claims directly with class members and bypassed their representative; and the court should award him fees even without a common fund. He requested reversal for determination of class damages and a reasonable fee reflecting the achieved result. The appellate court accepted only for argument’s sake that filing an action could end the power to retract an otherwise actionable anticipatory repudiation. It did not hold that filing this action had that effect. (Id. at 53.)

The decisive antecedent question was whether the December notice could produce an immediately actionable anticipatory breach. Under the California rule the court applied, anticipatory breach did not apply to contracts unilateral at inception or made unilateral by one party’s complete performance. The explanation was that a plaintiff with no remaining performance obligation need not be released from future duties and could wait until the defendant’s performance became due. The ticket purchasers had performed everything required of them by paying for their season tickets; the remaining promise was USC’s provision of Rose Bowl applications. Diamond’s suit was consequently premature, and the later offer to sell him a ticket defeated the proposed cause of action just as it would have had he sued individually. Bringing a class action for himself and 599 others did not change that contract rule. (Id. at 53–54.)

This was not a holding that insufficient initial ticket supply excused the promise through impossibility. Note 2 expressly says the declaration failed to explain why USC made obligations to all season-ticket purchasers that could not be fulfilled unless roughly 3,500 refrained from ordering. Nor did the judgment rest on deciding that the lawsuit played no causal role in the eventual ticket availability. Note 5 records the trial court’s extraneous-cause finding and Diamond’s failure to supply evidence overcoming it, but expressly disclaims making the appellate decision depend on acceptance of Phillips’s declaration. The contract ground supplied the result. (Id. at 52 n.2, 54 n.5.)

## Questions reserved and the opinion’s qualifications

The court left open the proposed class-action questions about fees outside a common fund, fees imposed on opposing parties rather than beneficiaries, direct dealings with members, and presuming that litigation caused a favorable result. Its discussion of a corporate-reorganization fee precedent explains Diamond’s proposed extension, not a general rejection or adoption of that extension. Note 6’s suggestion that a preliminary injunction against the game might have been effective is a hypothetical observation; no such injunction was requested or granted. (Id. at 55 & n.6.)

The opinion itself acknowledged criticism of the unilateral-contract exception. Note 4 reports Corbin’s objection that it permits suit by a party who has not performed while denying it to one who has, and note 7 refers to discussion of its social utility. The concluding passage contemplated a future class case presenting public-policy considerations sufficient to question the technical exception and confine this decision to its peculiar facts. That qualification is not an overruling within this judgment. Note 1 likewise suggests only tentatively that the proper damage measure would compare the value of an economy season ticket with and without the option, rather than Diamond’s alleged Rose Bowl resale spread. (Id. at 51 n.1, 54 n.4, 55–56 & n.7.)
