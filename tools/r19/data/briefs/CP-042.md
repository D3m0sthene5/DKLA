Citation
Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335 (1960), decided with Sullivan v. Behimer. Supreme Court of the United
States. Argued April 19–20, 1960; decided June 13, 1960. Justice Whittaker delivered the Court’s opinion.
Justice Stewart concurred separately in Blaski. Justice Frankfurter, joined by Justices Harlan and Brennan,
filed separate dissents addressing the two cases. Assigned excerpt: casebook pp. 410–415. Full opinion: Full
judicial opinion.
Parties
Blaski and other Illinois residents were patent-owner plaintiffs in the underlying infringement action. Howell
and a Texas corporation he controlled were the defendants seeking transfer. Judge Hoffman, the federal district
judge in Illinois, appears in the Supreme Court caption because the plaintiffs obtained mandamus directing him
to return the action to Texas. He was the judicial officer against whom that procedural remedy ran, rather than a
party accused of patent infringement. 363 U.S. at 336–338.
The companion case concerned minority shareholders Behimer and Roberts, who sued Standard Oil Company
and Standard Oil Foundation over their alleged acquisition of Utah Oil Refining Corporation’s assets at an
inadequate price. Those defendants sought transfer from Illinois to Utah. The companion case provides a
second application of the same statutory issue; its facts should not be merged with the patent dispute. Id. at
338–339. [Companion-case details supplement the assigned excerpt.]
Material Facts
The patent defendants lived and maintained their only place of business in Dallas, where the alleged
infringement occurred. Other litigation involving the patents was pending in the Northern District of Illinois,
and discovery there had produced evidence potentially useful in this case. The defendants therefore preferred
Illinois and offered to waive venue objections and appear there if the action was transferred. The plaintiffs
objected because Illinois was not a district in which the defendants resided, maintained a business, infringed
the patents, or could originally have been served. Id. at 336–337 & nn.2–4.
The companion defendants were Indiana corporations doing business in Illinois, but they were not
incorporated, licensed, doing business, or amenable to service in Utah. Utah nevertheless offered practical
advantages: corporate officers, records, and many shareholders were there; Utah law governed; and its court’s
calendar was less congested. Those defendants likewise offered to appear and waive venue objections in Utah.
Id. at 338–339 & n.5. [Full-opinion supplement.]
Procedural History
The patent owners properly filed in the Northern District of Texas. After answering, the defendants moved to
transfer under § 1404(a). The Texas district court granted transfer based on convenience and justice without
addressing the plaintiffs’ threshold objection. The Fifth Circuit denied leave to seek mandamus, reasoning that
defendants’ willingness to waive venue supported transfer. When the case arrived in Illinois, Judge Hoffman
denied the plaintiffs’ motion to return it to Texas, although he expressed doubts about the original transfer. The
Seventh Circuit then granted mandamus directing retransfer. Id. at 336–338.
In the companion case, the Illinois district court granted the defendants’ motion to transfer to Utah. The
Seventh Circuit granted mandamus to undo that transfer as well. The Supreme Court reviewed both judgments

Issue
Under the version of § 1404(a) then in force, could a court transfer an action from a proper forum to a district
where the plaintiff could not have sued the defendants at the outset, solely because the defendants later
requested that destination and offered to waive venue and personal-jurisdiction objections? At what time, and
from whose position, must the availability of the proposed forum be assessed? Id. at 342–344.
Rule and Holding
The transferee district had to be one in which the plaintiff had a right to bring the action when suit began,
independently of the defendants’ subsequent wishes or waiver. A later offer to submit to the destination court
did not establish that prerequisite. Because neither set of plaintiffs could originally have sued as of right in the
proposed transferee district, the transfers were unauthorized under the statutory language before the Court. Id.
at 343–344.
This was a limitation on transfer under that enactment. The Court acknowledged that venue and personal
jurisdiction can ordinarily be waived, and that the proposed transferee courts had subject-matter competence
over cases of the relevant kind. Those propositions did not themselves satisfy the additional statutory condition
on moving these pending actions. Id. at 343–344.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed both Seventh Circuit judgments. The writs requiring the district courts to undo the
transfers therefore stood. The decision neither resolved patent infringement nor decided the merits of the
shareholders’ claims; it determined where the pending litigation could proceed under § 1404(a). Id. at 344.
Reasoning
Section 1404(a) was enacted to relieve litigants from an inconvenient federal forum without requiring the
outright dismissal associated with forum non conveniens. It permitted transfer for convenience and in the
interest of justice, but also limited the destination to a district where the action could have been brought. The
majority treated the destination requirement as a separate condition that must be satisfied before convenience
can justify transfer. A favorable balance of practical considerations could not cure failure of that condition. Id.
at 335–336, 342–344.
The defendants urged a flexible reading tied to the time of transfer. They argued that their later residence,
business activity, or express consent could make another district capable of hearing the suit. The majority
instead read the statute’s past-tense formulation as directing attention to the circumstances at commencement.
A district that became available because the defendants subsequently consented was not thereby a district
available to the plaintiff when the action began. Id. at 342–343.
That distinction explained why ordinary waiver principles did not resolve the case. A defendant sued in a court
possessing subject-matter jurisdiction may waive objections to venue or personal jurisdiction. But these actions
had been filed elsewhere. Their arrival in the new district depended on a valid transfer order, and the statute
specified the conditions for such an order. Defendants could waive their own objections without expanding the
transferor court’s statutory authority. Id. at 343–344.

The majority also identified an asymmetry in the defendants’ proposed rule. A defendant could obtain transfer
to a district of its choosing by consenting to it, while a plaintiff with an equally strong convenience argument
could not obtain transfer to the same district without the defendant’s agreement. The Court found no indication
that Congress intended availability of a transfer destination to depend on that unilateral choice. It consequently
adopted the plaintiff’s original right to sue as the common reference point. Id. at 344.
Application did not require the Supreme Court to reconsider factual convenience. The parties conceded that
venue and reach of process were lacking in the proposed districts at the outset. The Illinois patent litigation and
the Utah witnesses and records explained why transfer was attractive, but they did not supply the missing
statutory eligibility. The judgments preventing those transfers followed from the threshold defect. Id. at
340–344 & nn.2, 5.
The majority separately rejected the suggestion that the Fifth Circuit’s earlier action prevented reconsideration
in Blaski. Its footnote explained that the orders were interlocutory, arose within the same action, and did not
adjudicate the merits of the underlying case. The denial of leave to seek mandamus had not finally established
the receiving court’s authority. The Seventh Circuit could address that question rather than permit a trial that it
would later regard as unauthorized. This aspect of the opinion concerned the effect of prior procedural rulings,
not the substantive interpretation of the transfer phrase. Id. at 340–341 n.9. [Full-opinion supplement.]
Separate Opinions
Justice Stewart agreed with the statutory result and the majority’s treatment of the earlier Fifth Circuit
proceedings. He acknowledged that the litigation’s movement between courts had been inefficient, but
emphasized that no preclusion claim was being pressed before the Supreme Court and that ordinary res judicata
principles did not bar the later examination. Id. at 345. [The casebook omits this concurrence.]
Frankfurter’s Blaski dissent, joined by Harlan and Brennan, concentrated on judicial administration. In his
view, the Fifth Circuit had already considered and decided the same transfer question between the same parties.
Allowing another circuit to reconsider it generated duplicative appellate litigation, expense, and an impasse in
which neither district would try the case until the Supreme Court intervened. He would have required respect
for the earlier appellate decision, independently of the disagreement over statutory meaning. Id. at 345–350.
[This separate dissent is not developed in the assigned excerpt.]
Frankfurter’s companion dissent attacked the majority’s statutory interpretation. He regarded the language as
ambiguous and emphasized that venue regulates the locality of litigation and normally protects a waivable
interest. Where defendants consented to proceed in the transferee court, the plaintiff would retain an available
forum, and the court would still have to find convenience and justice before transferring. That judicial inquiry
could protect a plaintiff’s legitimate interests without a categorical commencement-time prohibition. Id. at
351–363; CB 412–414.
The dissent also invoked the statute’s connection to forum non conveniens, under which dismissal could be
conditioned on a defendant’s submission to another forum. It disputed the majority’s discrimination argument
because plaintiffs already selected the original forum and defendants still bore the burden of showing that
transfer served the statutory purposes. Its limiting principles were federal adjudicatory power, effective
submission to the new forum, and judicial assessment of convenience and justice. Id. at 363–369; CB 414–415.

Notes
The holding interprets the earlier wording of § 1404(a). Atlantic Marine later quotes statutory language
expressly permitting transfer to a district to which all parties have consented. That later alternative must be
distinguished from the defendants’ unilateral consent in Hoffman. Do not apply the 1960 formulation as though
the subsequently expanded statutory text did not exist. 363 U.S. at 343–344; Atlantic Marine, 571 U.S. 49, 52,
59 (2013).
For analysis, separate three questions: whether the original forum is proper, whether the destination qualifies
under the applicable statute, and whether convenience and justice favor moving the case. Hoffman resolved the
second question on conceded facts. Its discussion of waivable venue does not turn subject-matter jurisdiction
into a matter of party consent. 363 U.S. at 340–344.
