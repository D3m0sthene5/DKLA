# Mennonite Board of Missions v. Adams

**462 U.S. 791 (1983) · Supreme Court of the United States · June 22, 1983 · Civil Procedure**

Justice Marshall wrote for six Justices: Chief Justice Burger and Justices Brennan, White, Blackmun, and Stevens joined him. Justice O’Connor dissented, joined by Justices Powell and Rehnquist. The Court reversed the Indiana Court of Appeals and remanded. The assigned account is in *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 148, printed page 246. This brief concerns the historical decision and its assigned treatment.

## Facts and procedural history

Alfred Jean Moore purchased property in Elkhart, Indiana, from the Mennonite Board of Missions (MBM). To secure a $14,000 obligation, she gave MBM a mortgage recorded on March 1, 1973. Their agreement required Moore to pay the property taxes, but she fell behind without MBM’s knowledge. The county commenced a tax sale in 1977. It posted an announcement in the courthouse, published notice, and mailed certified notice to Moore, as Indiana law then required. Neither the auditor nor Moore told MBM about the impending sale. (792–794.)

Richard Adams bought the property at the August 8, 1977 sale for $1,167.75. Moore continued making monthly payments to MBM afterward, leaving MBM unaware that its security had been sold. MBM learned of the sale on August 16, 1979, after the two-year redemption period had expired. Moore still owed it $8,237.19. (794.)

Indiana’s statutory sequence explained the danger. A tax-sale purchaser initially acquired a certificate creating a lien superior to existing liens. Interested owners and lienholders could redeem by paying the purchase price, subsequent taxes and assessments, and the statutory additional amount. If no one redeemed during the two-year period, the purchaser could apply for a deed. The auditor then had to notify the former owner and afford another 30 days to redeem, but no corresponding mortgagee notice was required. A tax deed ultimately conveyed title free of existing liens; the avenues for attacking that title were limited. A mortgagee therefore faced both an immediate reduction of its lien’s priority and potential destruction of its security. (793–794, 798.)

Adams sued to quiet title in November 1979. MBM resisted his summary-judgment motion, arguing that it had not received constitutionally adequate notice of the tax sale or its opportunity to redeem. The trial court upheld the statute, and the Indiana Court of Appeals affirmed. The Supreme Court took the appeal and reversed on the pre-sale notice issue. (795, 800.)

## Issue and holding

Did courthouse posting and newspaper publication, supplemented by mailed notice to the property owner but no notice to the recorded mortgagee, satisfy the Fourteenth Amendment before a tax sale adversely affected the mortgagee’s interest?

The Court held that they did not. A mortgagee has a protected property interest. When its identity is reasonably ascertainable from a publicly recorded mortgage, constructive publication must be supplemented by notice mailed to its last known available address or by personal service. A creditor’s ability to monitor the property does not remove the state’s obligation to provide a reasonably effective method of notice. (798–800.)

## Majority’s reasoning

Marshall applied *Mullane*’s requirement of notice reasonably calculated, in the circumstances, to inform interested persons of the proceeding and permit objections. Neither labeling a proceeding in rem nor relying on traditional constructive-service practice answered whether the selected notice was reasonably likely to reach the person whose property interest would be impaired. The majority’s historical discussion rejected the earlier sharp distinction between resident and nonresident parties, and between proceedings against people and proceedings against property, as a justification for leaving identifiable interests to indirect notice. (795–797 and n.3.)

MBM’s mortgage was constitutionally significant even though it did not give MBM title to the land. The tax-sale certificate subordinated its security, and the ensuing deed could erase it altogether. Publication was directed largely toward attracting buyers and was unlikely to inform creditors who did not regularly search sale announcements. Mailing to Moore was also inadequate as notice to MBM: a defaulting owner could not reliably be expected to protect the creditor’s interest or forward the warning. Mailing directly to the mortgagee was an inexpensive, more effective alternative. (793 n.1, 798–799.)

The Court distinguished learning that taxes were delinquent from learning that the state was about to conduct a particular tax sale. The latter information was what the county had to communicate. A sophisticated lender could inspect records, require tax-payment receipts, or arrange other contractual protections; those possibilities did not excuse the government’s failure to mail notice. Nor did the constitutional protection depend on whether the creditor was a professional lender. The majority observed that mailing might actually reduce administrative burdens if the creditor paid the taxes before sale. (799–800 and n.5.)

The ascertainability point was qualified. The recorded mortgage identified MBM as a corporation of Wayne County, Ohio, without a full mailing address. The Court **assumed** that reasonably diligent efforts could locate its address and suggested that even an imperfectly addressed letter might reach it through ordinary postal practice. It did not demand extraordinary efforts to discover a mortgagee whose identity was absent from public records. This is an assumption about reasonable discoverability, not a finding that the auditor already possessed MBM’s complete street address. (798–799 n.4.)

## O’Connor’s dissent

O’Connor regarded the majority as replacing *Mullane*’s contextual balancing with a broad rule requiring direct notice whenever an affected person could reasonably be identified. She believed that the state should have primary responsibility for balancing notice costs against the relevant class’s ability to protect itself, subject to correction of an irrational balance. Earlier cases involving unexpected condemnations or accounting proceedings did not, in her view, control predictable tax assessments and annual sales. She also disputed the majority’s characterization of historical in-rem notice practice and questioned how extensive the new identity-and-address search duty would become. (800–805 and dissent nn.1–3.)

Her application weighed Indiana’s revenue collection and record-search burdens against mortgagees’ practical resources. Institutional lenders held most outstanding mortgage debt, and even MBM had voluntarily undertaken a transaction allocating tax responsibility to Moore. MBM could have demanded paid assessments, escrowed tax funds, or checked public records. The dissent considered those precautions modest and the recurring tax-sale process predictable. It would have upheld the notice provisions because due process did not require the state to rescue a party from an unreasonable failure to protect its own interest. The majority, by contrast, refused to turn creditor vigilance into a substitute for the county’s notice duty. (806–809 and dissent nn.4–6.)

## Judgment and limits

The judgment was **reversed and the case remanded** for proceedings consistent with the opinion. The Court did not separately decide whether a mortgagee must receive notice of its redemption right before issuance of the tax deed: inadequate notice of the sale already required reversal. It also did not decide the constitutionality of Indiana’s 1980 amendment allowing mortgagees to request annual certified notice for a fee; the relevant events preceded that amendment. Those two reservations prevent treating this decision as a resolution of every later stage or version of the Indiana procedure. (793 n.2, 800 n.6.)

The assigned quotation captures the direct-notice rule and the dissent’s objection to it. The developed brief adds the statutory sequence, the address assumption, and the questions expressly left undecided. The majority itself called for notice by mail or other means “as certain to ensure actual notice” when names and addresses are reasonably ascertainable. That language concerns the required method, rather than a finding that MBM received notice; it did not. O’Connor’s objection to a categorical rule remains the dissent’s criticism. (800–801.) The textbook’s quotation says “lettered,” while the original opinion says “unlettered”; the majority protected inexperienced creditors as well as those versed in commercial practice. (800.)
