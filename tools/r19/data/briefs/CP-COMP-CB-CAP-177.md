# Kalb v. Feuerstein

**308 U.S. 433 (1940) · Supreme Court of the United States · January 2, 1940**  
**Civil Procedure — statutory ouster of foreclosure jurisdiction and collateral attack**

Under the specific farmer-bankruptcy statute at issue, a pending bankruptcy petition automatically deprived the state court of power to confirm a foreclosure sale and dispossess the farmer without bankruptcy-court consent. The resulting state actions were void and open to collateral attack even though the farmer had not obtained a separate stay or challenged jurisdiction in the foreclosure proceeding. Justice Black wrote the Court’s opinion in two consolidated appeals. CivPro assigns the developed comparison with *Chicot* in PDF page 218, printed pages 392–393, through the reconciliation questions before *Durfee*.

## Foreclosure continued while bankruptcy was pending

The mortgagees began foreclosure of the Kalbs’ Wisconsin farm on March 7, 1933, and obtained a foreclosure judgment on April 21. The sheriff sold the property on July 20, 1935. Ernest Newton Kalb’s farmer-bankruptcy petition had initially been filed and approved in October 1934, dismissed in June 1935, and reinstated on September 6, 1935, under the second Frazier-Lemke Act, with the dismissal vacated. Thus the petition was duly pending when the state county court confirmed the sale on September 16, 1935. **435–436 & nn.1–3.**

Neither the state nor the bankruptcy court had been asked for, or granted, a separate stay. The mortgagees, who bought the farm at the sheriff’s sale, obtained a writ of assistance on December 16. The sheriff enforced it on March 12, 1936, ejecting the Kalbs and their family. The decisive jurisdictional question concerned the confirmation and subsequent enforcement while the petition was pending; the Court did not simply hold that the original 1933 foreclosure judgment was invalid when entered. **436.**

In No. 120, the Kalbs brought an equitable action against the purchasers seeking restoration of possession, cancellation of the sheriff’s deed, and removal of the purchasers. The complaint was dismissed on demurrer, and Wisconsin’s Supreme Court affirmed. In No. 121, Ernest Kalb sued the mortgagees, sheriff, and county judge for damages alleging conspiracy, assault and battery, and false imprisonment. That action also ultimately reached a final dismissal affirmed by the state supreme court. A prior appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court had been dismissed for lack of final judgment before the final state dismissals at issue here. **436–437 & nn.4–5.**

## The federal issue was not avoided by a state-law finality label

Wisconsin initially reasoned that §75 of the Bankruptcy Act did not operate as an automatic statutory stay. Without a judicial stay, the confirmation and writ were permissible. On rehearing, the state court also described the order as at most erroneous, not void, and emphasized the absence of an appeal or a jurisdictional showing in the foreclosure court. **437–438.**

The appellees argued that this latter explanation supplied an adequate nonfederal ground barring Supreme Court review. Black disagreed. Whether the state court’s actions were merely erroneous or beyond its power depended on the meaning of the federal statute. If Congress itself ousted jurisdiction while bankruptcy was pending, the lack of a direct appeal could not convert the prohibited actions into valid ones. Statutory interpretation remained a federal question. **437–438 & n.6.**

## The statute created an exception to ordinary finality

The Court acknowledged the normal presumption of regularity and protection against collateral attack for judgments of competent courts, citing *Chicot*, among other decisions. But Congress’s bankruptcy power allowed it to enact a specific exception, making judicial actions concerning protected debtors or property nullities. State control of local procedure could not authorize a court to disregard controlling federal bankruptcy law. **438–439 & nn.7–11.**

The language of §75 supplied that exception. Subsection (n) immediately subjected the farmer and property, including unexpired redemption rights and unconfirmed sales, to the bankruptcy court’s exclusive jurisdiction. It extended redemption or withheld confirmation for the time needed to carry out the statutory process. Subsection (o) prohibited commencement or maintenance of specified proceedings, including mortgage foreclosure and recovery of possession, unless the bankruptcy judge consented after the prescribed petition, hearing, and commissioner’s report. Subsection (p) applied those prohibitions to judicial and official proceedings, creditors, and the debtor’s property wherever located. **440–441 & n.12.**

The 1935 amendments and committee reports reinforced the transfer of exclusive control to the bankruptcy court. The statutory scheme sought rehabilitation of financially distressed farmers and supplied local conciliation commissioners who could assist with petitions; farmers did not have to hire counsel. Requiring them to seek separate stays or litigate the same protection in additional courts would undermine that accessible, automatic safeguard. **441–443 & nn.13–17.**

Because the statute itself withdrew power to proceed, the confirmation, sheriff’s deed, writ, and resulting ejection—so far as based on the county court’s actions—lacked legal authority. Whether the farmer had actually contested jurisdiction, or could have done so, was immaterial under this specific congressional arrangement. **443–444 & nn.18–19.**

## The Court did not decide personal tort liability

The conclusion that the proceedings lacked jurisdiction did not itself establish damages liability for every person involved. Responsibility for unlawful conduct depended on state law. The Court expressly declined to resolve other state-law contentions offered in support of the judgments, including the appellees’ arguments concerning immunity. **443–444.** The cases therefore returned to Wisconsin with the federal jurisdictional premise corrected, rather than with a final damages award or a Supreme Court resolution of all state remedial questions.

The Supreme Court **reversed the judgments in both cases and remanded to the Supreme Court of Wisconsin for proceedings consistent with its opinion**, **444**. No separate writing, appendix, or numerical voting tally is reported.

## Reading the textbook comparison

The assignment correctly contrasts *Kalb* with *Chicot*. *Chicot* protected a federal court’s final debt-readjustment decree against parties who could have challenged the enabling statute. *Kalb* concerned a particular congressional prohibition on another court’s continued proceedings while exclusive bankruptcy protection was in force. The actual opinion expressly discussed both the ordinary finality principle and its statutory exception; it was not an unqualified rule permitting collateral attack whenever jurisdiction is disputed.

The entire twelve-page official file was read, including all nineteen judicial notes, their continuations, the consolidated-case designation, and the final mandate. The prior opinion’s conclusion at the beginning and *Avery* syllabus at the end are separate-case context, not part of Kalb’s judicial reasoning.
