# Pennsy Supply, Inc. v. American Ash Recycling Corp. of Pennsylvania
895 A.2d 595, 2006 PA Super 54 (2006) | Contracts

## Court and posture
Judge Orie Melvin delivered the Pennsylvania Superior Court's March 17, 2006 opinion before Judges Joyce and Tamilia. No separate writing appears. Pennsy appealed an order sustaining demurrers to all five counts of its complaint against American Ash and dismissing the action. The appellate court reversed and remanded for further proceedings, relinquishing jurisdiction. Its holding concerns the complaint's sufficiency: well-pleaded facts and fair inferences were accepted as true, and dismissal was proper only if recovery was clearly precluded. The decision did not enter a damages award or finally determine product defects, a contract breach, or reliance. (¶¶1, 3–5, 22.)

## Alleged transaction and losses
Northern York County School District engaged general contractor Lobar to construct a high school. Lobar subcontracted paving of driveways and a parking lot to Pennsy. The project specifications required specified aggregates but allowed substitution of AggRite, a treated-ash aggregate. They included notice that American Ash offered AggRite free and a letter to the architect confirming availability on a first-come basis. Pennsy told American Ash it needed about 11,000 tons, collected the material, and used it in the work. (¶2; 895 A.2d at 598–599.)

Pennsy completed paving in December 2001. Extensive cracking appeared in February 2002. After notice through the District and Lobar, Pennsy performed remedial work in summer 2003 without charging the District. Removing and properly disposing of the AggRite formed part of that work because the material was classified as hazardous waste. American Ash did not arrange removal and disposal when asked, and Pennsy gave notice that it would seek reimbursement. Pennsy alleged $251,940.20 in remedial work and an additional $133,777.48 in disposal expenditures. Those figures were pleaded losses, rather than judicial valuations or amounts awarded. (¶2.)

The November 18, 2004 complaint asserted breach of contract, implied warranty of merchantability, express warranty, warranty of fitness for a particular purpose, and promissory estoppel. The court's initial description calls Count III an express warranty of merchantability, while note 1 identifies its governing provision as 13 Pa. C.S.A. § 2313; the distinction does not turn it into a second implied-warranty count. Pennsy requested leave to amend if any demurrer were sustained. The trial court dismissed all counts, and Pennsy preserved the appealed issues through a Rule 1925(b) statement. (¶3 & nn.1–2.)

## Contract claim and consideration
The trial court regarded the free material as a conditional gift and found no pleaded bargaining over American Ash's avoidance of disposal costs. Pennsy argued that its acquisition relieved American Ash of an expensive disposal burden, supplying consideration even though no money changed hands. The appellate court distinguished a detriment merely incidental to receiving a gift from a detriment sought in exchange for the promise. Benefit to the promisor can indicate an exchange, but is not a conclusive test. What matters is reciprocal inducement: the promise induces the detriment and that detriment induces the promise. (¶¶4, 6–9; 895 A.2d at 599–601.)

Pennsy expressly alleged that American Ash promoted AggRite for use in construction and supplied it free to induce others to dispose of it, avoiding American Ash's own costs. Accepting that allegation, the promise of free supply induced Pennsy to collect and take title, while the resulting transfer and relief from disposal obligations induced American Ash to offer the material. The transaction therefore could be an exchange instead of a gift with a collection condition. Note 4 recognizes that the specifications formally permitted an alternative, while explaining why the complaint fairly supported an inference that the successful paving bidder realistically would use the authorized free material. The court did not find that the formal specification itself compelled use of AggRite. (¶10 & n.4; id. at 601–602.)

The complaint did not expressly allege that the parties discussed disposal costs or that Pennsy understood American Ash's motive when accepting the material. The court held that actual negotiation over the terms was unnecessary: facts showing reciprocal inducement could establish bargained consideration without such discussions. Note 5 observes that the structure of the complaint arguably implied Pennsy's awareness and records counsel's assertion of a shared understanding, but the main holding did not make that inference essential. The court also rejected a possible alternative ground that the oral agreement lacked sufficiently pleaded terms; the complaint identified supply for the project in accordance with its specifications and alleged breach of that term. Count I was restored. (¶11 & nn.3, 5; id. at 602.)

## Article 2 warranty claims
The trial court dismissed the warranty counts because a gift was not a sale of goods. AggRite was a movable good, and Article 2 defined a sale as transfer of title for a price. The price could be payable in money or otherwise; relief from disposal costs could supply the nonmonetary consideration for a sale. A genuine gift, by contrast, had no price even if title passed. (¶¶12–14 & n.6; id. at 602–603.)

The court did not hold that every nongift transfer automatically carried every Article 2 warranty. The statutory comment reproduced in note 7 required attention to the purposes of the particular provisions. Here American Ash allegedly promoted AggRite as a building material, supplied technical sheets describing compliance with roadbed specifications, and represented it as a substitute for the designated aggregate base. That affirmative commercial promotion differed from abandoning garbage for anyone to retrieve. The court therefore could not say the warranty laws clearly barred recovery and reversed dismissal of Counts II–IV to the extent dismissal rested on absence of a sale. It did not find that all substantive requirements for each warranty or its breach had been proven. (¶14 & n.7; id. at 603–604.)

## Promissory estoppel and timing of reliance
The trial court dismissed promissory estoppel because Pennsy received American Ash's promotional literature only after cracking occurred and because it viewed Pennsy as relying on project specifications rather than a direct promise. The appellate court addressed the alternative theory in case the contract claim later failed. Estoppel required a promise reasonably expected to induce action or forbearance, actual reliance, and necessity of enforcement to avoid injustice. (¶¶15–16 & n.8; id. at 604.)

The complaint also alleged direct assurances of suitability at an August 2, 2001 project meeting, documented in minutes dated August 15. That meeting preceded the alleged agreement around August 21, although Pennsy had already obtained the subcontract through a bid assuming use of free AggRite. Earlier reliance on the specifications did not eliminate the possibility that the later direct assurance induced a further action or forbearance. Those issues required discovery. Note 9 acknowledges that the record did not identify the minutes' author, John Page; the court assumed from Pennsy's brief that someone directly connected to Pennsy attended, rather than finding Page's identity or agency. (¶¶17–18 & n.9; id. at 604–605.)

There was a second viable reliance route: American Ash allegedly supplied representations to the project architect, who used them in issuing specifications on which Pennsy relied. Restatement (Second) § 90, adopted in Pennsylvania, extended to expected action by a promisee or a third person. Clear justified third-party reliance was not categorically confined to intended beneficiaries. The fact that Pennsy itself received literature late therefore did not foreclose the alleged reliance through the architect. Count V was restored for further proceedings. (¶¶19–21; id. at 605–606.)

## Result and assigned account
The exact result was reversal of the order granting all demurrers and dismissing the complaint, remand for further proceedings, and relinquishment of appellate jurisdiction. (¶22; id. at 606.)
