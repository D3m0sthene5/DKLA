# RaDec Construction, Inc. v. School District No. 17
248 Neb. 338, 535 N.W.2d 408 (1995) | Contracts

## Court, issue, and result

On July 7, 1995, the Nebraska Supreme Court affirmed an $80,269 judgment for the school contractor against the Millard school district. Justice Connolly wrote for a court listing Chief Justice White and Justices Caporale, Fahrnbruch, Lanphier, Wright and Connolly. No separate writing or numerical vote statement accompanies the opinion. The architect's contractual decision was ordinarily binding absent fraud, gross mistake necessarily implying bad faith, or failure to exercise honest judgment. Although the district correctly argued for that demanding standard, the architect's reduction was patently erroneous and thus legally equivalent to bad faith for this dispute. (248 Neb. at 338–339, 342–344.)

## School contract and changed earthwork

RaDec became general contractor on a $5,275,800 bid, including $404,269 allocated to earthwork. The original plan required importing 100,000 cubic yards of fill. The parties incorporated American Institute of Architects general conditions. Section 12.1.4 authorized the architect to determine the costs of a change when the parties could not agree, based on reasonable expenditures and savings attributable to it. The contractor had to supply an itemized accounting and supporting data; credits for a net decrease were to reflect actual net cost confirmed by the architect. The provision also addressed overhead, profit, specified cost categories and payments pending final determination. (Id. at 339–340.)

After contracting, architect Wilscam, Mullins, Birge revised the plan so no imported fill was necessary. It requested an itemized new quotation from RaDec, which sought reductions from earthwork subcontractor Wiekhorst Brothers. Wilscam rejected proposed reductions of $30,100 and then $80,000 as unfair to the district. The district invoked section 12.1.4. At a meeting including the district, contractor, subcontractors and architect, grading subcontractor Negus-Sweenie proposed a $203,500 cost for earthwork without imported fill. (Id. at 340–341.)

Two months later, only district and architect representatives attended a further meeting. Wilscam issued a $189,018 contract deduction. The meeting notes showed removal of the $407,888 earthwork figure in the payment schedules and substitution of Negus-Sweenie's $203,500 plus profit. Although the architect's representative said other figures were considered, the calculation matched those supplied by Negus-Sweenie. The distinction between the original bid allocation and the payment-schedule figure appears in the opinion; they should not be silently treated as the same amount. (Id. at 341.)

RaDec objected that rebuilding the entire earthwork price from the bottom up ignored costs already incurred and exceeded the architect's authority to price the change. It refused to sign the change order but finished the school. During construction, it replaced Wiekhorst with Negus-Sweenie. Wiekhorst sued the bonding company and settled for $75,000; the evidence described that payment as compensation for completed work. (Id. at 341–342.)

## Trial and competing review standards

In February 1991, RaDec sued for additional earthwork payment, alleging the district had paid $248,138 while expenses were $332,492.45. After a bench trial, the judge awarded $80,269 based on testimony from a former RaDec officer. The judge explained that the appropriate reduction compared importing dirt with using on-site dirt and found the architect's repricing arbitrary and a gross mistake. The opinion reports the award as entered; it does not equate it with simple subtraction of those two pleaded payment-and-expense figures. (Id. at 342.)

The district argued that the judge used an insufficiently strict standard, that the evidence failed even that standard, and that it did not support the award. The Supreme Court agreed about the governing contractual rule: a designated third person's decision on construction disputes could be binding, subject to fraud, a gross mistake necessarily implying bad faith, or failure of honest judgment. A court could not merely substitute what it regarded as a better calculation. But decisions without a reasonable basis, or patently erroneous ones, could constitute the equivalent of bad faith and the necessary gross mistake. (Id. at 342–343.)

## Application and affirmance

The evidence supported the trial court's finding that Wilscam used Negus-Sweenie's figures without verifying a reasonable relationship to actual costs. RaDec had supplied an accounting accurately reflecting the savings from eliminating imported fill, but Wilscam did not use it. That supported more than disagreement over professional discretion: the method was patently erroneous for the contract change being evaluated. (Id. at 343–344.)

Because this was a law action tried without a jury, the trial court's factual findings received the effect of a jury verdict. The appellate court did not reweigh conflicting evidence; it viewed the judgment favorably to the successful party and afforded reasonable inferences from the record, reversing findings only when clearly wrong. Under that review, the trial judge's gross-mistake conclusion stood. The Supreme Court additionally held the determination legally equivalent to bad faith because it was patently erroneous. The district's correct statement of the stricter legal rule therefore did not require reversal of the supported judgment. The final disposition was affirmed. (Id. at 344.)
