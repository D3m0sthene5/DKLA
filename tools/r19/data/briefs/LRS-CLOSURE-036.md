# Kixmiller v. Securities and Exchange Commission
492 F.2d 641 (D.C. Cir. 1974) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and administrative proceedings

Bruce Kixmiller owned 1,000 Class B shares of the Washington Post Company. On November 19, 1971, he advised its general counsel that he intended to present three proposals at the 1972 annual meeting and requested inclusion of them and supporting statements in management's proxy materials. Proposal A sought charter and bylaw changes giving Class B shareholders the rights of Class A shareholders. Proposal B requested a $75,000 ceiling on employee compensation. Proposal C requested a fairness doctrine for the company's newspaper-publishing activities. These were shareholder proposals, not SEC orders imposing compensation or editorial rules. (643 & nn. 1–3.)

Rule 14a-8 required inclusion of qualifying proposals submitted by a security holder entitled to vote at the meeting, subject to specified exclusions. The company asserted that Class B holders could vote on thirty percent of directors and certain other matters but not general business matters; accordingly, Kixmiller could not submit proposals on which he had no vote. Following the rule's procedures, it notified the SEC's Division of Corporate Finance that it intended to omit the proposals and sought a staff no-action response. Kixmiller submitted opposing memoranda and materials. (643 & nn. 4–7.)

On March 8, 1972, the Division advised that it would not recommend enforcement action. It reasoned that requiring a proposal in proxy materials when the proponent could not legally present it at the meeting would be inconsistent with preventing misleading solicitations, and that some basis existed for the company's position. Kixmiller requested Commission reconsideration. The full Commission declined to review the staff's position, hold an oral hearing, or issue its own informal statement. He petitioned for direct appellate review; the SEC moved to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction. (643 & nn. 8–9.)

## Whether the staff response was a Commission order

The unanimous Tamm–Robinson–Robb panel issued a per curiam opinion on January 30, 1974. Exchange Act § 25(a) supplied its direct-review authority and required an order issued by the Commission. Staff members, individually or collectively, lacked authority to make such Commission orders. Here the Commission expressly declined to consider the merits; it did not adopt the staff's position as its own. The staff response therefore did not satisfy the statutory requirement. This was a limit on direct review under the particular statute, rather than a determination that every form of administrative inaction falls outside the broader APA concept of agency action. (641–644 & nn. 10–14; 645 n. 24.)

Kixmiller relied on Medical Committee for Human Rights v. SEC, where the court had reviewed a staff no-action position approved by the Commission. The panel distinguished the agency's role in the two proceedings. In Medical Committee, the Commission had actually examined the controversy and affirmed the staff's recommendation. Here it had declined any examination or adjudication. Medical Committee itself made reviewability of the staff position depend on the Commission's initial determination to review it. That prerequisite was absent. The court's citation also preserved Medical Committee's later Supreme Court vacatur as moot; it did not describe that judgment as a merits affirmance. (644 & nn. 15–21.)

## Whether the court could compel Commission consideration

A distinct question concerned the Commission's refusal to take up the claim. The panel assumed, without deciding, that this refusal otherwise fell within § 25(a), then held that it could not override the refusal. The Act expressly committed necessary investigations and institution of enforcement suits to Commission discretion. Decisions to refrain from investigation or enforcement were generally unreviewable, and these statutory provisions made that allocation particularly clear. The court did not decide that the staff's substantive voting-rights interpretation was correct. (644–645 & nn. 22–28.)

The SEC's regulation assigned ordinary informal advice to staff and reserved possible Commission consideration for special circumstances. Questions of substantial importance or novel or highly complex issues could be presented to the Commission, but obtaining its informal statement remained discretionary. The court found nothing arbitrary or abusive in applying that arrangement here. Proxy review involved heavy volume and limited resources: the Commission reported that staff had processed 7,023 proxy statements and 141 information statements during fiscal 1973 and commented on substantially all of them. Requiring full Commission review in every instance would undermine this informal assistance. The court treated priority-setting and internal deployment of resources as matters properly left to the agency. (645–646 & nn. 29–35.)

The remaining avenue mattered. A dissatisfied shareholder could litigate proxy-solicitation questions directly, with or without first resorting to SEC staff or the Commission. Thus declining Commission review did not itself foreclose a judicial contest over corporate compliance. The court held that the SEC could initially draw the line for its review of staff advice and had not drawn it unreasonably in this regulation or legally erred in its discretionary choice here. (646 & nn. 36–39.)

## Judgment and assigned significance

The court **granted the motion to dismiss the petition**. It neither upheld the three proposals' exclusion on the merits nor directed the Commission to reconsider or enforce. There was no separate writing or appendix. The decision distinguishes an agency's adoption of a staff determination from its refusal to take up that determination, and separately recognizes the discretion governing the latter choice. (643, 646.)
