Parties
Myrtle Nell Catrett, administratrix of her husband Louis H. Catrett’s estate, sued fifteen corporations that
allegedly manufactured or distributed asbestos products responsible for his death. Celotex Corporation was one
defendant and the petitioner in the Supreme Court. Catrett was the respondent defending the appellate reversal
of Celotex’s summary judgment. The Supreme Court proceeding concerned the sufficiency of Celotex’s
motion and the required response, rather than a finding that any particular corporation had caused the death.
477 U.S. at 319–322; casebook pp. 1017–1018.
Material Facts
Louis Catrett died in 1979. His widow’s September 1980 complaint alleged negligence, breach of warranty,
and strict liability arising from asbestos exposure. To recover against Celotex, she needed proof connecting
him to its products. Celotex argued that she had failed in discovery to identify a witness who could testify to
that exposure. Its motion did not include an affidavit affirmatively establishing that no exposure had occurred.
Id. at 319–320.
Catrett identified three documents supporting exposure in Chicago in 1970–1971: her husband’s deposition
from a workers’ compensation proceeding, a letter from an official of a former employer, and an insurer’s letter
to her attorney. The casebook identifies the product as Firebar, manufactured by a Celotex predecessor, and the
employer as Anning-Johnson. The employer official, T. R. Hoff, was a potential trial witness. Celotex
contended that the documents were inadmissible hearsay. That objection raised a question about the usable
proof behind the documents, rather than establishing that the record contained literally nothing referring to
exposure. Id. at 320, 334–336, 338 nn.2–3; casebook p. 1018.
Brennan’s dissent details an earlier, withdrawn summary-judgment motion. Catrett had supplied the three
documents in responding to that motion and had indicated an intention to call Hoff. According to Brennan,
Celotex knew of that support when it renewed its no-evidence motion. He considered the renewed motion’s
failure to address the support decisive. This procedural detail helps explain why the dispute persisted even
though the opinions broadly agreed that a movant need not always produce affirmative evidence disproving the
claim. Id. at 334–337 & nn.5–7 (Brennan, J., dissenting).
Procedural History
Celotex first sought summary judgment in September 1981, about a year after filing of the action. In July 1982,
the District Court for the District of Columbia granted the defendants’ pending motions, including Celotex’s,
stating that exposure to Celotex products had not been shown in the District of Columbia or elsewhere within
the statutory period. Catrett appealed only the judgment favoring Celotex. Id. at 319–321.

A divided D.C. Circuit panel reversed. The panel majority read Rule 56 and Adickes v. S. H. Kress & Co. to
require Celotex to support its motion with evidence negating exposure before Catrett had a duty to respond.
Because Celotex had not made that affirmative evidentiary showing, the panel did not reach the adequacy or
admissibility of Catrett’s materials. Judge Bork dissented. The Supreme Court granted review to resolve a
disagreement among circuits about the moving party’s burden. Id. at 319–322.
The Supreme Court rejected the D.C. Circuit’s categorical requirement and reversed and remanded. It left to
that court the questions whether Catrett’s actual showing was adequate and whether, reduced to admissible
evidence, it would support her burden at trial. The decision therefore did not itself resolve the disputed
evidence or finally establish Celotex’s entitlement to judgment. Id. at 326–328.
Issue
When the opposing party will bear the trial burden on an essential element, must a summary-judgment movant
submit affidavits or other affirmative evidence disproving that element? What must the movant identify, what
response is required, and should the Supreme Court itself decide whether Catrett’s particular showing of
exposure sufficed? Id. at 322–328.
Rule and Holding
After adequate time for discovery, a party lacking a sufficient showing on an essential element for which it
bears the trial burden may face summary judgment. The movant must identify the basis of the motion and the
relevant record showing the absence of a genuine material dispute. It need not invariably submit affidavits or
affirmative evidence negating the opponent’s claim; it may point out the absence of evidence supporting that
claim. Once the motion is properly made and supported, the opponent must identify specific supporting facts
beyond the allegations in the pleadings. Id. at 322–325.
The D.C. Circuit erred by treating an affirmative evidentiary negation as invariably necessary. The Court did
not decide whether Catrett’s actual showing met the applicable standard. It also expressly distinguished the
form of materials submitted on summary judgment from whether their substance could be presented through
admissible proof at trial. Id. at 324, 326–328.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the D.C. Circuit and remanded for further proceedings. The appellate court was to
address the adequacy of the exposure showing and its capacity to support admissible proof. This was not a final
holding that Catrett’s three documents were inadmissible, that no exposure occurred, or that Celotex had won
the wrongful-death case on the merits. Id. at 326–328.
Reasoning
The trial burden explains why an absence of proof can be dispositive. If Catrett ultimately had to establish
exposure to a Celotex product, and could produce no sufficient evidence of that connection after discovery,
disputes concerning other matters would not make a trial of the claim necessary. Summary judgment tests
whether the evidentiary record supports a triable issue; the defendant need not always undertake the additional
task of proving the opposite of a fact the plaintiff must establish. Id. at 322–323.
Rule 56’s text supported that conclusion. The rule referred to affidavits if any and expressly allowed motions
with or without supporting affidavits. Other record materials—depositions, interrogatory answers, and
admissions—could demonstrate that the opposing party lacked a necessary evidentiary basis. Requiring an
affidavit denying exposure in every case would add a requirement absent from the rule and impede the
procedure’s function of isolating unsupported claims. Id. at 323–324.

The Court nevertheless retained an initial responsibility for the moving party. Celotex had to identify what its
motion challenged and what portions of the record supported its position. Permission to rely on an evidentiary
deficiency was not permission to obtain judgment simply by filing an unsupported demand. The majority’s
initial-responsibility language and White’s concurrence are important qualifications to the common shorthand
that a defendant can merely point to a lack of proof. Id. at 323, 328–329.
Catrett argued that the requirement to respond to a motion properly supported under Rule 56(e) meant that
Celotex first had to submit affidavits. The Court rejected that equation: a motion can be properly supported by
the existing record without a new affidavit. Once such a motion identifies a fatal evidentiary gap on an issue
the nonmovant must prove, repeating the complaint’s assertion does not fill the gap. The nonmovant must
designate specific facts that could support a genuine issue. Id. at 324–325.
The qualification concerning admissible form is separate. A party need not depose every favorable witness or
present every item in the identical format later used at trial to resist summary judgment. The proper inquiry
includes whether the identified material can support admissible proof of the disputed element. The Court
therefore declined to treat the mere form of Catrett’s documents as a conclusive reason to enter judgment, and
remanded the actual sufficiency question. This does not license reliance on allegations with no evidentiary
basis or facts incapable of admissible presentation. Id. at 324, 326–327.
Adickes did not require the categorical rule adopted below. Celotex reaffirmed that the moving party must first
show the absence of a genuine material issue and accepted Adickes’s result on its record. But it rejected
reading that responsibility as an obligation in every case to produce evidence disproving the opposing party’s
essential element. The 1963 amendment preventing reliance solely on pleadings after a supported motion did
not enlarge the movant’s initial obligation into an invariable affirmative-negation requirement. Id. at 325–326.
Adequate opportunity for discovery mattered. The majority observed that the motion came a year after the
action began and that discovery had occurred. It also identified then-Rule 56(f) as protection where a party
needed more discovery to oppose a premature motion. The holding accordingly cannot be applied by stripping
away a meaningful opportunity to obtain evidence and then faulting a party for not already possessing it. Id. at
326 & n.6.
The Court treated summary judgment as part of the Federal Rules’ allocation of litigation costs and trial rights.
Factually supported claims should reach trial; opposing parties should also be able to demonstrate before trial
that an essential claim or defense has no factual support. Reversal corrected the standard used by the D.C.
Circuit, while remand preserved the distinct task of applying the proper standard to the record. Id. at 327–328.
Separate Opinions
Justice White joined the Court but emphasized that a conclusory no-evidence assertion is insufficient. A
plaintiff need not voluntarily conduct discovery, depose her own witnesses, or supply evidence absent a
discovery obligation or court order. Where she has identified a supporting witness, a movant cannot ignore the
witness’s possible testimony. White understood the parties to disagree about whether Catrett had already
identified sufficient support and agreed that the appellate court should examine that question. Id. at 328–329
(White, J., concurring).
Justice Brennan, joined by Burger and Blackmun, also agreed that a defendant need not always submit
affirmative evidence disproving the plaintiff’s case. He distinguished the movant’s initial production burden
from its ultimate burden of establishing entitlement to summary judgment. When the nonmovant bears the trial
burden, the movant may either negate an element or demonstrate insufficiency of the existing proof. Under the

latter approach, it must engage with supporting evidence already in the record rather than announce that none

Brennan would have affirmed the reversal of summary judgment because Celotex’s renewed motion
overlooked the documents and Hoff’s potential testimony. Catrett had already called those materials to the
court’s attention. In his view, Celotex had to explain their inadequacy before shifting the production burden,
and remand was unnecessary because that failure was apparent. His dissent concerned application and clarity,
rather than a defense of the D.C. Circuit’s absolute affidavit requirement. Id. at 334–337.
Justice Stevens would have affirmed on a narrower basis. He read the motion and argument as focused on
absence of exposure within the District of Columbia, despite materials supporting exposure in Illinois. He
regarded the district judge’s brief addition of exposure elsewhere as inadequate to justify the broader judgment.
The majority disagreed with that reading of the district court’s ruling, emphasizing its express reference to
other locations. This disagreement about the record should be attributed to the respective opinions rather than
resolved by silently adopting either account. Id. at 320 n.2; id. at 337–339 (Stevens, J., dissenting).
Notes
Identify who bears the trial burden on the particular element before applying the no-evidence method. A
claimant seeking judgment on an element it must prove cannot establish that element merely by saying the
opponent lacks contrary evidence. Brennan expressly explains that distinction; the majority’s holding addresses
the situation in which the nonmovant bears the dispositive trial burden. Id. at 323–325, 331–332.
Separate the legal holding from the unresolved application. Celotex authorized a method of making a motion
and rejected a categorical affirmative-negation requirement. It remanded whether Catrett’s actual evidence
sufficed. A summary saying the Supreme Court ruled that her documents were inadmissible or that Celotex
definitively owed nothing would contradict that disposition. Historical Rule 56 subsection references should be
checked against any current assigned rule text before use in present-day litigation. Id. at 326–328.
