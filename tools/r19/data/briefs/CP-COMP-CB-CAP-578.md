# Parker v. Hoefer

**2 N.Y.2d 612, 162 N.Y.S.2d 13, 142 N.E.2d 194 (N.Y. 1957).** New York Court of Appeals, April 12, 1957. Dye wrote the five-judge majority; Froessel dissented, joined by Van Voorhis. Assigned account: CivPro PDF page 687, printed page 1365, complete Note 2.

## The dispute and procedural route

Parker, a Vermont resident, held a final Vermont judgment against Hoefer, a New York resident, for alienation of Parker's husband's affections and criminal conversation. Parker could not collect the judgment in Vermont and brought an action to enforce it in New York. Hoefer invoked article 2-A of New York's Civil Practice Act, which had abolished the underlying heart-balm actions as contrary to state public policy. Parker moved to strike that defense under Rule 109 and for judgment under Rule 112. The dispute concerned enforcement of a judgment already obtained, rather than the initial availability of a tort action in New York. **Dye opinion, opening discussion, through the reproduced 615 marker.**

Special Term granted judgment on the pleadings, and the Appellate Division affirmed. Majority note 1 records an unusually extended appellate route: an initial direct appeal from Special Term was dismissed; Hoefer then obtained the Appellate Division's affirmance; an appeal as of right asserting a constitutional question was dismissed; the Appellate Division denied leave; and the Court of Appeals granted leave when the application was renewed there. Those earlier appeal rulings were procedural steps, not additional merits holdings in this April 1957 opinion. **Majority n.1; Froessel dissent, opening.**

## What Hoefer argued

Hoefer's answer invoked all of article 2-A and alleged that many acts charged in the Vermont litigation had been alleged to occur in New York. Section 61-d provided that an act later done within New York would not generate, inside or outside the state, one of the causes of action abolished by the article. Section 61-f addressed contracts concerning such abolished claims. Hoefer argued that those restrictions and New York's public policy barred the enforcement action. She also invoked authority describing full faith and credit as something less than an invariably unqualified command. **Dye discussion at reproduced 615–617; Froessel at 617–618.**

The majority rejected that defense as insufficient as a matter of law. The dissent thought the asserted New York connection required factual inquiry and that the state statute might bar enforcement if the judgment rested on acts occurring in New York.

## Statutory interpretation: the tort and the judgment are different claims

Dye's opinion first emphasized that article 2-A abolished specified underlying actions, but did not expressly abolish actions on judgments rendered by sister-state courts with jurisdiction over the parties and subject matter. Majority note 2 identifies the abolished actions as alienation of affections, criminal conversation, seduction, and breach of a promise to marry. **Majority statutory discussion and n.2.**

The majority declined to expand the statute beyond its clear language. Liberal construction under § 61-h did not permit adding an unmentioned prohibition on enforcing foreign judgments. It also found Hoefer's pleading about alleged New York acts insufficient: Parker had not alleged, Hoefer had not admitted, and the record did not establish that the Vermont judgment actually rested on transactions occurring in New York. The majority did not make an affirmative finding that every relevant act occurred in Vermont. **Reproduced 615–616 discussion.**

The tort claim had merged into the final Vermont judgment. That judgment constituted a debt, and the New York enforcement action was a different cause of action from the abolished tort underlying it. New York therefore was being asked to enforce an adjudicated obligation, not to entertain an original alienation-of-affection or criminal-conversation claim. **617; assigned quotation at printed 1365.**

## Full faith and credit: local policy does not supply an appeal from the Vermont judgment

The majority supported its reading with the Full Faith and Credit Clause and its implementing federal statute. It explained that a sister-state judgment rendered with the requisite jurisdiction ordinarily receives the validity and effect it has in the rendering state. The opinion mentioned qualifications involving jurisdiction and fraud or collusion, and recognized that some exceptions existed, while stating that they were not relevant to this case. It did not declare every document labeled a judgment enforceable regardless of those conditions. **616; majority n.3 reproduces the constitutional provision.**

The majority distinguished Pink v. A.A.A. Highway Express. That case concerned insurance assessments said to arise from mutual membership and obligations extrinsically imposed by another state's law. Here, the obligation arose from a final judgment between the litigating parties. Treating the two settings as interchangeable would erase the important difference between applying another state's substantive law and respecting another state's adjudication. **616–617.**

New York could not retry the underlying Vermont dispute or sit as an appellate court reviewing that judgment merely because its own law would not recognize the original tort. That distinction explains both the constitutional reasoning and the statutory conclusion. Hoefer's answer raised no legally sufficient factual issue requiring trial. **617.**

## Froessel's dissent and the exact disposition

Froessel, joined by Van Voorhis, read § 61-d differently. In his view, the language forbidding New York acts from generating abolished claims either within or outside New York could reach a judgment based on such acts. Otherwise, the statutory provision would not perform its intended function in the circumstances exemplified by this case. The allegation of New York acts therefore warranted a trial to determine where the judgment-producing conduct occurred. **Dissent, reproduced 617–618.**

The dissent also believed that the state's strong public-policy declaration could relieve it from enforcing a judgment violating the statute. It would reverse both Special Term and the Appellate Division and deny Parker's motion, with costs; it did not propose a present merits finding about where every act had occurred. **Dissent ending, 618.**

The Court of Appeals instead **affirmed the judgment, with costs**, by a **5–2 vote**. Conway, Desmond, Fuld, and Burke joined Dye; Froessel and Van Voorhis dissented. There was no other separate writing in the recovered complete reproduction. **Majority ending and final judicial lineup.**
