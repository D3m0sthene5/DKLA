# Veale v. Warner
1 Wms. Saund. 323, 326, 85 Eng. Rep. 463, 468 (K.B.; assigned as 1670) | Civil Procedure | Historical common-law pleading

## The bond and the sequence of pleadings

Thomas Veale brought debt against William Warner on a sealed £2,000 bond conditioned on performance of an arbitration award concerning their disputes. The formal declaration demanded the bond debt and alleged £100 damage from its detention. Those are pleading amounts, not an adjudicated damages award. Warner demanded oyer: the bond and its condition were read and set out in the pleading record. The condition named John Coggs and John Foxwell as arbitrators, with an umpire if they failed to resolve the differences within the stated time. Preserved Williams edition, printed 323e–324.

Warner pleaded that the arbitrators had ordered him to pay Veale £3,169 16s. 3d. and deliver a general release, and that he had performed both obligations. His account deliberately omitted the award's reciprocal requirement that Veale give Warner a general release. The omission mattered under the court's historical understanding of mutuality: an award imposing everything on one party and nothing on the other appeared invalid. Printed 324a; 326a–326b.

Veale's replication denied payment and offered that factual issue for determination by the country, meaning a jury. He did not add the omitted reciprocal release. Warner declined that issue and rejoined that Veale had acknowledged payment in writing and should not be allowed to deny his acknowledgment. Veale demurred to the rejoinder, and Warner joined in demurrer. This shifted the immediate dispute from whether money had actually been paid to the legal sufficiency of the pleadings. Printed 325–326; assigned PDF 295.

## Why the bad rejoinder did not secure Veale's judgment

On Veale's motion for judgment, Saunders, Warner's counsel and the reporter, acknowledged a legally insufficient rejoinder but pointed to the earlier defect in the award as pleaded. If that award was invalid, whether Warner had performed it did not establish Veale's entitlement to judgment on the bond. Saunders also treated Warner's refusal of the offered payment issue and his bad rejoinder as acknowledging nonperformance for this purpose. He nevertheless argued that Veale could not obtain judgment because the record described only an invalid award. Printed 326b–327; assigned PDF 296.

The court agreed with that legal objection. Veale needed to set out the omitted part that made the award valid before assigning its breach. A demurrer to the later rejoinder did not repair his failure to supply that earlier necessary matter. The case illustrates examination of the existing pleading record for an earlier substantive defect, rather than a rule that a plaintiff automatically wins whenever the defendant's last response is defective. Printed 327.

It is essential to distinguish the actual award from the award appearing in the record. The full reporter account expressly states that Veale really was required to release Warner. The judges were not finding that the arbitrators had in fact issued only a one-sided award. Their reasoning depended on the version both parties had allowed the pleadings to present. This was a pleading trap rather than an evidentiary finding that no reciprocal undertaking ever existed. Printed 326a–327.

## The court refused to reward the trap

Agreement with Saunders's legal point did not produce judgment for Warner. The whole court thought the technique a trick and refused to give the defendant judgment. It instead gave Veale leave to discontinue upon payment of costs. Chief Justice Kelynge reprimanded Saunders for deliberately omitting the reciprocal provision to deceive the plaintiff's pleader. The report identifies agreement of the whole court but gives no complete participating list or numerical vote; it records no separate dissent. Printed 327–327a.

Accordingly, the disposition was neither a judgment awarding Veale the bond debt nor a final merits judgment declaring Warner the prevailing party on the bond. The reported action ended with permission for the plaintiff to discontinue on the stated condition. The report also says that Veale's counsel, Winnington and Sympton, did not see their pleading defect until it was raised in court. Printed 327b.

## Saunders's account and the later editors' qualification

Saunders appended an explanation sympathetic to Warner: he described the arbitration as imposing more than the bond's penalty, asserted that Warner actually was the creditor rather than debtor, and reported that Warner subsequently obtained relief against the bond through an English bill in the Exchequer exposing bad practice between Veale and the arbitrators. Those are the reporter's account of hardship and a later proceeding, not findings adjudicated by King's Bench on this demurrer. The later proceeding has no separately reproduced judgment or detailed remedy in this report. Printed 327a–327b.

The report uses £3,169 16s. 3d. in the pleadings but £3,100 in Saunders's hardship explanation, with an excess of £1,100 over the £2,000 penalty. These formulations should remain distinct rather than silently changing the pleaded sum to fit the later arithmetic. The historical result also should not be generalized into current arbitration doctrine. A later editor's note expressly doubts the supposed invalidity of the award as pleaded, reasoning that a final money award might itself bar further litigation without an express reciprocal release. That editorial qualification is not a separate judicial opinion in Veale. Printed 327, editor's note 2.
