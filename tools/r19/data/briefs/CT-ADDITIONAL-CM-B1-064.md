# Newman v. Schiff

778 F.2d 460 (8th Cir. Nov. 27, 1985) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B1-064

Newman lost his claim for a $100,000 televised reward because he had not responded to the only offer the court found: an invitation to call the live Nightwatch program before it ended. Replaying the challenge on the morning news reported the earlier offer; it did not renew it. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the judgment for Schiff on that ground, rejecting the district court's later ratification analysis and declining to decide the timeliness of acceptance of a supposed renewed offer. The final direction expressly imposed no costs against Newman. Senior Judge Bright wrote for a panel including Judges Heaney and Arnold; the report contains no separate opinion. (778 F.2d at 460–461, 464, 466–468.)

## The broadcast, research, and correspondence

Schiff promoted books and lectures asserting that federal income taxation was voluntary. The court described extensive media appearances and treated his tax contention as meritless. On February 7, 1983, he appeared live on CBS News Nightwatch, interviewed by Karen Stone from approximately 3 to 4 a.m. Eastern Time. The program displayed its call-in number and invited viewers to speak with him on air. Against that setting, he promised $100,000 to anyone who called “this show” and identified a Code section requiring an individual to file a tax return. This was a potentially binding reward offer, rather than an unenforceable boast merely because it was public or televised. (Id. at 461–462 & nn.2–4, 466.)

Newman, a Missouri lawyer, did not watch the live program. He saw a two-minute recorded excerpt several hours later on CBS Morning News. Note 4 reproduces the relevant segment, including Diane Sawyer's explanation that Schiff had discussed his views earlier that morning on Nightwatch. Newman researched the Code at work and called CBS Morning News the next day, February 8, using a general CBS number supplied by the long-distance operator rather than the Nightwatch call-in number. He cited statutory provisions and sent a letter stating that his research performed the requested consideration. Note 5 reproduces the full letter, including his request for a transcript and Schiff's address and his assurance that he looked solely to Schiff for payment. (Id. at 462–463 & nn.4–5.)

CBS replied on March 3 that it had forwarded the letter to Schiff at Freedom Press. On April 13 Newman wrote to Schiff directly, repeating his authorities and claim. Schiff's April 20 response acknowledged making an offer but denied that Newman had satisfied it. He asserted that § 6012 did not establish a filing requirement and that his offer went to the first qualifying respondent, with earlier claimants having already invoked that section. Those were Schiff's assertions in a letter, not findings that another person had earned the reward. The appellate court rejected his underlying tax premise and did not use his alleged priority condition as its ground of decision. (Id. at 463 & n.6, 466–467.)

## The district court's two explanations

The district court initially found that the live offer ended with Nightwatch, that the rebroadcast did not extend it, and that Newman therefore had not accepted in time. After Newman requested additional findings and amendment of the judgment, the court retained the judgment but changed part of its explanation. It concluded that Schiff had ratified the morning rebroadcast by failing to object, accepting publicity, and writing the April 20 letter. It regarded that ratification as renewing the offer, yet held that Newman still responded too late because he had not done so on the morning of the rebroadcast. (Id. at 463–464.)

Newman challenged the standard for timing a response to that supposed renewed offer. The appellate court did not resolve that timing dispute. It instead rejected the premise that a second offer existed, affirming because Newman had failed to accept the initial and only Nightwatch offer. Missouri law governed the diversity action, as note 9 expressly records. The report identifies both Newman's appeal and Schiff's cross-appeal; it notes that Schiff did not challenge the district court's rejection of his tax theory. It does not provide a separate developed disposition of some additional cross-appeal issue. (Id. at 460, 464 & n.9, 467.)

## Objective assent and the bounded reward offer

The court explained formation through manifested assent and offer and acceptance. The relevant question was how the words and conduct would reasonably be understood, rather than whether the parties privately occupied an identical mental state. Its discussion of Embry illustrates why a speaker's undisclosed contrary intention need not defeat a reasonable understanding of commitment. The court nevertheless cautioned against completely disregarding actual, proven assent; the objective approach was not an instruction to ignore every relevant fact about intention. Notes 7–11 supply the historical discussion, the criticism of the meeting-of-minds metaphor, Lucy, and Learned Hand's contrasting formulation. (Id. at 464–465 & nn.7–11.)

Public reward cases such as Carlill and James v. Turilli showed that the broadcast format did not prevent formation. The court expressly said a person who called Nightwatch with the Code sections Newman found would have formed a contract and called Schiff's bluff. The failure here concerned the invitation's temporal and program-specific scope. In context, “this show” meant the live call-in broadcast, and the offer remained open only until that program concluded. A reasonable viewer of the later news segment would understand it as a report of what Schiff had offered, not another invitation to earn $100,000 by calling a different program. (Id. at 465–466.)

## Why ratification could not create the missing offer

Schiff's later conduct and letter could be considered as evidence bearing on whether the rebroadcast objectively appeared to be an offer. The court acknowledged that a clearly demonstrated belief by Schiff might make a similar understanding by Newman more reasonable. But the actual conduct and letter were indefinite, continued to deny obligation, and did not change the news report's character. (Id. at 466–467.)

Even under an agency ratification analysis, the result was unchanged. Ratification adopts an unauthorized act performed on another's behalf with knowledge of the material facts and relates back as authorization of that act. It does not supply legal significance missing from the act itself. The court illustrated the point with an unauthorized contract lacking consideration: later authorization alone could not cure that defect. Schiff might have authorized CBS's decision to replay his interview, but authorization of a news report did not turn that report into a renewed reward offer. (Id. at 467.)

The court separately explained that § 6012 required individuals above specified income thresholds to file returns and commended Newman for exposing Schiff's false claim. That approval did not award Newman contractual relief or replace the required acceptance. The judgment remained affirmed, with the express no-costs direction appearing on reporter page 468. (Id. at 467–468.)
