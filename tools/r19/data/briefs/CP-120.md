# Magnani v. Trogi

**CP-120 · Civil Procedure · 70 Ill. App. 2d 216, 218 N.E.2d 21 (1966) · May 4, 1966 · Coryn, joined by Alloy; Stouder dissenting**

The court upheld a new trial where one undifferentiated verdict left liability and damages uncertain on two distinct causes of action. It did so despite both parties’ failure to object to the verdict form before the verdict. The holding is expressly confined to the situation presented; it does not require a new trial whenever multiple claims lack separate verdict forms.

## Facts and procedural posture

M. Helen Magnani sued Arnold Trogi following her husband Raymond’s injury and death. She proceeded in two legal capacities. As administratrix, she sought $30,000 under Illinois’s Wrongful Death Act. Individually, she sought reimbursement for medical and funeral expenses under the Family Expense Statute. The opinion does not narrate the underlying injury event; its factual account centers on the different recoveries and their beneficiaries. A wrongful-death award would be apportioned between the widow and minor son according to their dependency on the decedent. An award of family expenses would not undergo that apportionment. Thus determining which claim produced which recovery had practical consequences for distributing the money. 70 Ill. App. 2d at 218–219.

Neither party tendered separate forms for the two claims. The court submitted one form without objection, and the jury returned a verdict for the plaintiff against the defendant with $19,000 in damages. The trial court could not determine whether that amount applied to both causes of action, only to wrongful death, or reflected nonliability on the expense claim. It therefore set aside the verdict and judgment and granted a new trial on liability and damages. Magnani obtained leave to appeal that order. Trogi cross-appealed the denial of his alternative request for judgment notwithstanding the verdict. Id. at 218–220.

## Issue and governing principles

Did the trial court abuse its discretion by ordering a new trial to resolve the ambiguity, even though Trogi first challenged the verdict form in a post-trial motion?

The Civil Practice Act provided for separate verdicts on a party’s motion when complaint counts asserted different demands on which separate recoveries could be obtained. The majority regarded these statutory claims as separate causes of action. More broadly, a new-trial motion allowed the trial court, before losing jurisdiction, to correct errors committed by itself or the jury. A reviewing court would disturb that decision only upon an affirmative showing of clear abuse of discretion. The trial judge’s observation of the whole trial placed it in a better position to assess the effect of errors, fairness, and substantial justice; the court allowed greater latitude for granting than denying a new trial. Id. at 219–220.

## Majority reasoning

Magnani argued that Trogi waived any objection by remaining silent when the form was submitted. Coryn acknowledged that this would be a valid argument in most circumstances. Here, however, the central difficulty was that the jury had not made its conclusions on each cause of action ascertainable. A wrongful-death liability finding seemed apparent, but any inference about liability for family expenses was conjectural. No wording allocated the $19,000 between the claims. The jury had to make those determinations in the first instance; the court could not supply an allocation of its own. Id. at 220–221.

The timing also foreclosed immediate correction. The jury had returned its verdict on December 21, 1962, and the assigned excerpt identifies the post-trial motion as filed January 15, 1963. By the time the defect was raised, the court could not reassemble the jury and direct correction. The resulting uncertainty furnished a substantial basis for starting over. The majority expressly declined a universal rule requiring retrial for every omission of separate forms. It held only that this judge had not abused discretion on this record. Id. at 221; assigned printed page 1154. The CAP transcription’s “1968” for the motion date conflicts with the assigned scan and the decision’s 1966 date; the chronology here follows the assigned text rather than reproducing that apparent transcription error.

## Stouder’s dissent

Stouder disputed both prejudice and preservation. The full dissent states that Magnani had waived her individual interest in the verdict, eliminating the trial judge’s allocation dilemma, and that the verdict was within the evidence and law. It also emphasized that broad new-trial discretion has limits when the order depends on a legal ruling about the propriety of verdict forms. Deference cannot replace examination of that ruling. Id. at 222.

Trogi had neither objected at the instructions conference nor before his post-trial motion. In Stouder’s view, a party could not obtain relief from an error it invited or accepted. Trogi’s failure to object, combined with his failure to establish actual prejudice, required rejection of this basis for a new trial. Because the majority addressed only the form issue, Stouder would next examine other asserted trial errors, including the cross-appeal issues, to see whether another ground could support the order. He did not purport to decide all those issues in the dissent. Id. at 222–223.

## Disposition and study point

By a two-to-one division, the court affirmed the order granting a new trial, vacating the verdict and judgment, and denying Trogi’s judgment-notwithstanding-the-verdict motion. It did not replace the jury’s $19,000 verdict with a judicial allocation or direct judgment for the defendant. The useful comparison is between uncertainty requiring fresh jury determinations and preservation rules requiring prompt objections. The majority found sufficient uncertainty to sustain this particular grant of a new trial; the dissent found waiver and the absence of demonstrated prejudice decisive. The complete report contains no judicial footnotes and includes the dissent in full. This brief addresses the historical ruling, without asserting that its procedural doctrine remains unchanged today.
