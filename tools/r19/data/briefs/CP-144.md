# Hanover Logansport, Inc. v. Robert C. Anderson, Inc.

**Civil Procedure · CP-144 · 512 N.E.2d 465 (Ind. Ct. App. 1987) · September 10, 1987**

Hanover adopts party intent as the measure of a consent judgment’s preclusive scope, but demands an explicit, mutual reservation grounded in the existing complaint. Anderson’s unilateral acceptance language did not preserve a previously unpleaded delay-damages claim. The court reversed denial of Hanover’s dismissal motion and directed entry of judgment on the offer and dismissal of the remaining action.

Hanover owned premises in a South Bend shopping center; Spatz & Company was its leasing agent. Anderson planned a liquor store there. The parties signed a lease on February 23, 1984 for nine years and eleven months beginning May 1. Hanover agreed to remove the existing tenant, Discount Liquors, but that tenant remained. Anderson alleged that occupancy was necessary to transfer its liquor license and that failure to remove the tenant exposed it to a one-year bar on a renewed license application. Its July complaint requested occupancy under the lease or, alternatively, $500,000 in profits over the lease term. It also alleged that reliance on the lease had cost it another location. (512 N.E.2d at 466–67.)

Before trial, Hanover tendered a Rule 68 offer of the leased premises. Anderson accepted subject to the lease’s licensing conditions and added that acceptance was only for mitigation, not settlement of breach damages. Hanover requested judgment in accordance with the accepted offer, and both parties filed an agreed entry directing that the stipulated judgment be recorded. Anderson took possession around July 1, 1985. Hanover later sought dismissal; the trial court denied it. A critical qualification appears in judicial note 1: the record did not show that judgment actually had been entered. Because entry was required and the appellate disposition would direct it, the court proceeded as though judgment had been entered. (Id. at 467–68 & nn.1–2.)

Hanover argued that Anderson’s complaint offered alternative remedies, its offer supplied specific performance, and Anderson’s acceptance and possession ended litigation. Anderson maintained that Rule 68 permitted a partial judgment and that its reservation notified everyone of remaining damages. Its appellate brief clarified that it wanted profits lost during the delay in delivery, while Hanover understood the continuing demand as profits over the entire lease term. The appellate court expressly addressed the broader additional-damages question to avoid another appeal, even though it was not the precise issue Hanover framed. (Id. at 468–69 & n.4.)

Judge Staton explained the consent judgment’s dual character. It embodies an agreement and also invokes judicial authority. A purely judgment-centered approach stresses finality, avoidance of harassment, and conservation of resources. An agreement-centered approach asks what the parties actually settled; requiring settlement of every possible issue could deter compromise because a party might inadvertently surrender something. After considering divergent federal decisions and distinguishing Indiana cases attacking the validity of earlier consent judgments, the court adopted the agreement-centered approach. Rule 68’s structure also supported reserving issues: settlement can resolve part of a controversy while other matters await adjudication. (Id. at 469–71 & nn.5–8.)

That approach did not make Anderson’s stated private intention controlling. Both parties had to agree, and the reserved matter had to be precise. The court required incorporation of the reservation into the offer of judgment itself and inclusion of the reserved claim as an inherent part of the original complaint. Here, the complaint requested specific performance or profits over the lease term; it did not state a claim for profits lost during delayed delivery. The acceptance’s general reference to breach damages did not establish Hanover’s assent to that particular residual claim. Clear terms in both the complaint and agreed disposition would permit settlement while telling each side exactly what litigation remained. (Id. at 471–72.)

Judicial note 9 extends the same reservation requirement to an issue or another cause of action, not just damages. Note 10 prevents curing the deficiency by amending after dismissal and then asserting compliance: the claim had to be in the complaint when the parties reached the agreement and entered the consent judgment. Those retained notes appear after the casebook’s editorial material in its appended notes pages and form part of the judicial reasoning. (Id. at 471–72 nn.9–10; assigned PDF pages 695–696.)

The court **reversed and remanded with instructions to enter judgment on the offer and grant Hanover’s motion to dismiss**. Robertson concurred. Hoffman separately concurred on the authority of General Discount Corp. v. Weiss Machinery, without a developed alternative analysis. The complete reported corpus includes all ten judicial notes and that separate writing. The assigned principal, printed pages 1311–1314, abbreviates the underlying documents and omits the missing-entry qualification; the full-source qualification therefore matters when explaining why the court ordered entry rather than merely enforcing an already documented judgment. This brief describes the 1987 Indiana rule, without treating it as an unchecked current rule for every state or federal consent judgment.
