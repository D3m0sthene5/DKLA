# Thompson v. Thompson

## Citation

Thompson v. Thompson, 484 U.S. 174 (1988). Supreme Court of the United States. Argued October 6, 1987; decided January 12, 1988. Justice Marshall delivered the Court's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Brennan, White, Blackmun, and Stevens. Justice O'Connor joined except for the first full paragraph of Part II and concurred in part and in the judgment. Justice Scalia concurred in the judgment. Full judicial opinion: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-484/pdf/USREPORTS-484-174.pdf. 

## Parties

David Thompson, the father of Matthew Thompson, was the federal plaintiff and Supreme Court petitioner. The child's mother, Susan Clay, formerly Susan Thompson, was the defendant and respondent. Each parent had obtained a custody decree favorable to that parent in a different state. The father sought federal declaratory and injunctive relief resolving which decree controlled. The dispute before the Supreme Court concerned the availability of that federal cause of action, not a direct federal determination of the child's best interests. 484 U.S. at 176–178. 

## Material Facts

In July 1978, the mother petitioned a California court to dissolve the marriage and sought custody of Matthew. The opinion dates the petition, not the final dissolution decree. The court initially awarded joint custody. The California court provided that she would have sole custody once she left for Louisiana, pending its investigator's report. She moved with Matthew in December 1980. Her relocation and provisional award were part of the described custody proceedings; the Supreme Court did not find that she had unlawfully abducted the child. Id. at 177–178. 

On April 7, 1981, a Louisiana court enforced the then-existing California award in favor of the mother and modified visitation. After receiving the investigative report, the California court later awarded sole custody to the father. The resulting decrees appeared to give conflicting custody rights. The father's proposed federal suit asked a district court to declare the Louisiana decree invalid, require recognition of the later California decree, and grant corresponding injunctive relief. Id. at 177–178. 

The Parental Kidnaping Prevention Act of 1980, codified at 28 U.S.C. § 1738A, directs states to enforce qualifying sister-state custody determinations and limits modification and competing jurisdiction. The statute was intended to address interstate conflict over custody orders. The father relied on that federal enactment as the source of a private action in federal district court. He had not first sought enforcement of the later California decree in Louisiana. Id. at 178–180, 186–187. 

## Procedural History

The father filed the federal action in 1983. The district court dismissed for lack of subject-matter and personal jurisdiction. The Ninth Circuit disagreed with the district court's jurisdictional analysis but affirmed because the complaint failed to state a claim: the PKPA did not create the private federal cause of action the father asserted. This distinction matters because the Supreme Court's disposition did not simply endorse every jurisdictional ground stated below. Id. at 178–179. 

The Supreme Court granted review to resolve disagreement over whether the PKPA supplied an implied federal remedy for competing state custody decrees. It affirmed the Ninth Circuit. The father's requested district-court action was unavailable under that statute, but the Court did not decide which custody decree was valid or who should have custody. State enforcement proceedings and ultimate Supreme Court review of appropriate federal questions were distinct avenues discussed in the opinion. Id. at 178–179, 186–187. 

## Issue

Does the PKPA impliedly create a private federal cause of action permitting a parent to obtain a district-court judgment determining which of two conflicting state custody decrees is valid? In assessing that question, do the statute's text, context, and legislative history show that Congress intended a new federal remedy, rather than a rule governing interstate recognition in state proceedings? Id. at 178–180. 

## Rule and Holding

The PKPA does not supply the asserted private federal cause of action. The controlling inquiry was whether Congress intended to create that remedy. The Act's language and structure direct states to recognize qualifying custody determinations, while its history shows that Congress rejected a proposed federal-jurisdiction solution in favor of an interstate full-faith-and-credit approach. The Court therefore declined to infer authority for district courts to adjudicate the competing decrees in the father's proposed suit. Id. at 179–187. 

The absence of that federal district-court remedy does not make the statute unenforceable. Its requirements govern state custody proceedings, and federal questions can ultimately receive Supreme Court review through the appropriate route. The holding concerns the particular implied cause of action; it is not a ruling that the PKPA has no legal effect or that state courts may disregard it. Id. at 186–187. 

## Judgment

The Supreme Court affirmed the Ninth Circuit's judgment that the father had failed to state a claim under the PKPA. It did not validate the Louisiana decree, invalidate the California decree, or award custody. Nor did it hold that exhausting additional state proceedings would itself create the unavailable federal cause of action. Id. at 186–187. 

## Reasoning

The Court examined congressional intent rather than assuming that a federal statute necessarily gives every affected person a district-court action. The PKPA did not expressly authorize the remedy asserted. The question therefore required attention to the legislation's operation and history. Although the opening methodological discussion referred to familiar considerations from implied-right cases, the Court treated them as evidence bearing on legislative intent, not as a license to devise whatever remedy might further statutory purposes. Id. at 179–180. 

Custody judgments historically presented special full-faith-and-credit difficulties because they were modifiable as circumstances changed. A second state might conclude that the first state's order remained open to reconsideration and issue a competing determination. Differences among state jurisdictional rules encouraged forum selection and instability in custody arrangements. The Court described these problems as the background Congress addressed, rather than treating each interstate move as unlawful conduct. Id. at 180–182. 

The PKPA responded by prescribing which state determinations qualify for recognition, restricting simultaneous proceedings, and limiting another state's authority to modify an existing determination. A qualifying order requires appropriate jurisdiction under state law and compliance with specified federal conditions. The statute also addresses continuing jurisdiction and the circumstances under which modification becomes permissible. These provisions establish obligations for adjudicating states. They do not, by their terms, identify a district court as a new custody-review forum. Id. at 175–177 & nn.1–2, 182–183. 

The statutory placement reinforced that reading. Congress added § 1738A alongside the general full-faith-and-credit provision in § 1738. That neighboring provision directs recognition of judgments without ordinarily supplying an independent federal cause of action to relitigate their effect. The Act's language and position thus fit a rule of decision for interstate recognition more readily than a jurisdictional transfer of custody disputes to federal district courts. Id. at 182–183. 

The legislative history was particularly significant because Congress considered a different approach. A proposal associated with Representative Fish would have created federal diversity jurisdiction for specified custody-enforcement disputes. The legislative materials reflected objections that federal courts lacked domestic-relations expertise and that a new forum would add complexity and burden. Congress instead adopted a state-court enforcement solution. The Court regarded that choice as evidence against implying a federal remedy omitted from the enacted scheme. Id. at 183–186. 

The father argued that the cause of action he sought was narrower than the rejected proposal because it would apply only when two states had already issued conflicting decrees. The Court found the distinction insufficient. Even such cases could require federal judges to examine traditional state-law jurisdiction and custody questions, including the child's best interests and whether the child had been abandoned or abused. The supposedly limited remedy would still entangle district courts in the field Congress had chosen to leave primarily to state adjudication. Id. at 185–187 & n.4. 

The father's account also risked placing district courts in the role of reviewing state judgments directly. The PKPA's purpose of resolving interstate conflict did not compel that institutional arrangement, especially where the father had not attempted to invoke the statute in Louisiana to enforce the later California order. The Court treated ordinary state enforcement as a meaningful mechanism, not as a futile formality preceding a federal action. Id. at 178, 187 & n.5. 

Finally, the Court rejected the claim that refusing an implied remedy would make the Act ineffective. That argument assumed state courts would be unable or unwilling to implement federal full-faith-and-credit duties. The Court declined that premise and noted the availability of Supreme Court review for genuinely intractable conflicts presenting federal questions. The possibility that a litigant would prefer immediate district-court review did not establish that Congress intended it. Id. at 186–187. 

## Separate Opinions

Justice O'Connor joined the opinion except for the first full paragraph of Part II. Her short separate opinion expressly adopted Scalia's Part I reasons. Those reasons insisted that actual congressional intent controls the existence of an implied private action and that the PKPA did not create this one. She emphasized that several factors identified in earlier implied-right cases are tools for discerning intent, rather than independent grounds permitting a court to create a remedy after finding no legislative intent to do so. Id. at 188 (O'Connor, J., concurring in part and in the judgment). 

Justice Scalia likewise agreed with the result. He objected that the majority's statement that Congress need not actually have had the creation of a remedy in mind weakened the requirement of actual legislative intent. In the first part of his concurrence, he criticized reliance on the statutory context if detached from an actual indication in the text or history that Congress created a private remedy. His concern was that a broad contextual approach could allow judicial judgments about desirable remedies to substitute for legislative choice. Id. at 188–191 (Scalia, J., concurring in the judgment). 

In the second part, Scalia proposed a stronger prospective approach: the Court should announce that private causes of action would not be implied in statutes enacted thereafter. He associated that proposal with the constitutional allocation of legislative authority and the uncertainty of searching for unexpressed intent. The Court did not adopt that categorical proposal in Thompson. The majority applied its congressional-intent framework to the particular statute, while Scalia separately urged a broader change in doctrine. Id. at 191–192. 

## Notes

The case distinguishes a federal rule governing the effect of state judgments from a federal cause of action to enforce that rule in district court. A statute can bind state judges, support a federal argument in state proceedings, and permit ultimate Supreme Court review without creating the district-court suit a particular litigant seeks. That distinction explains why a conclusion about the remedy does not resolve the underlying custody conflict. Id. at 179–187. 

Procedural labels also require care. The district court invoked jurisdictional grounds, while the Ninth Circuit affirmed for failure to state a claim. The Supreme Court's affirmance concerned the latter absence of a statutory cause of action. Treating the decision as a general holding that federal law cannot govern custody recognition would collapse distinct questions about governing law, jurisdiction, remedy, and review. Id. at 177–180.

Id. at 176–192.
