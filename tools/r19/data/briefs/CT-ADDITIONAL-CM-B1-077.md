# Minneapolis & St. Louis Railway Co. v. Columbus Rolling-Mill Co.

119 U.S. 149 (1886) | Contracts

## Decision and posture

The Supreme Court affirmed a judgment for the rolling mill because the railway rejected the seller’s offer by attempting to order a quantity below the stated minimum. Its later attempt to accept the original quantity range could not revive the terminated offer. Justice Gray delivered the November 29, 1886 opinion of the Court. The complete report includes the admitted correspondence, the jury-trial posture, and an additional ruling about submitting a legal question to the jury. It records no separate opinion, judicial footnote, or numerical vote. 149–152.

## The admitted correspondence

The railway, established in Minneapolis, asked the Columbus, Ohio manufacturer on December 5, 1879, for prices on specified quantities of steel and iron rails for March 1880 delivery. On December 8 the mill replied that it did not make steel rails, but would sell **2,000 to 5,000 tons** of fifty-pound iron rails at $54 per gross ton, for cash, free on board cars at its mill. It disclaimed damages for strikes, serious damage to the works, or delays beyond its control, and expected notice of acceptance before December 20. 149–150.

On December 16 the railway telegraphed an order for **1,200 tons**, referring to the seller’s December 8 offer. Its same-day letter repeated that quantity and the price, requested a written contract and a rail template, and asked about splice prices. On December 18 the mill replied that it could not book the order at that price. The next day the railway requested **2,000 tons** under the original letter and asked for a written contract. Its mistaken reference to the “sixth” was admitted to mean the eighth. After further inquiries, the mill denied on January 19, 1880 that any contract existed. The parties admitted that the letters and telegrams were sent on the specified dates and received in due course. 150.

## Litigation and issue

The railway alleged a December 19 contract for 2,000 tons and sued for breach in the federal Circuit Court for the Southern District of Ohio. The mill denied formation. A jury returned a verdict for the mill; the railway took exceptions and sought a writ of error. The Supreme Court had to determine the legal effect of the railway’s earlier 1,200-ton response and its later attempt to return to the original terms. 149–150, 151–152.

## Why the later acceptance failed

Gray stated the common-law requirement of mutual assent: an ordinary offer imposes no obligation until accepted according to its terms. A response proposing different terms rejects the original offer unless the offeror renews it or assents to the proposed change. An offeree cannot subsequently restore the rejected offer simply by tendering an acceptance. An acceptance deadline does not itself keep an ordinary offer irrevocably open; while it remains open, it can be accepted, rejected, or withdrawn before the deadline. 151.

The mill’s original offer gave the railway a choice of any quantity within the 2,000–5,000-ton range. The 1,200-ton order was outside that range. Crucially, both the telegram and letter referred to the original offer and its terms. The court therefore treated them as a qualified response to that offer, not an independent proposal that left the original offer undisturbed. That response rejected the original terms. The mill then declined the reduced order. The railway’s December 19 request for 2,000 tons came before the originally stated deadline, but after the earlier negotiation had closed, and created no contract. 151–152.

## Jury-instruction issue and exact disposition

Given that legal effect of the writings, the Court did not need to decide whether the trial instructions had treated rejection as a legal ruling or submitted it as a factual question. Submitting a question of law to the jury furnished no ground for reversal when the jury reached the correct result. The Court **affirmed the judgment** for the mill. It ordered no new trial and awarded the railway no damages. 152.
