# Standard Fire Insurance Co. v. Knowles

**Course:** Civil Procedure

## Citation

Standard Fire Insurance Co. v. Knowles, 568 U.S. 588–596 (2013). Supreme Court of the United States, No. 11-1450. Argued January 7, 2013; decided March 19, 2013. Justice Breyer delivered the opinion for a unanimous Court. No separate judicial writing was filed. Assigned reference: Civil Procedure casebook printed pp. 870–871, repository PDF p. 449, the amount-in-controversy paragraph of the CAFA discussion. The explicit DKLA Atlas case node supplies the separate brief assignment.

## Parties and material facts

Greg Knowles claimed Standard Fire unlawfully omitted a general contractor fee from certain homeowners' insurance loss payments. In April 2011 he filed a proposed class action in Arkansas state court for hundreds or possibly thousands of Arkansas policyholders allegedly injured in the same way. The merits of that insurance practice were not resolved by this opinion. 568 U.S. at 590–591.

The complaint stipulated that the plaintiff and class would seek aggregate damages of less than $5 million. An attached affidavit stated that Knowles would never seek class damages exceeding $5 million in the aggregate. The difference between his authority over his own recovery and his lack of authority over absent persons before certification was central. The class was proposed, not certified, when the jurisdictional question arose. Id. at 591–593.

## Procedural history

Standard Fire removed on May 18, 2011 under the Class Action Fairness Act. On the company's evidence, the federal District Court found that the aggregate amount in controversy would be just above $5 million without the stipulation. It nevertheless treated the stipulation as reducing that amount and remanded to state court. The Eighth Circuit declined discretionary review of the remand order. The Supreme Court granted certiorari amid conflicting lower-court approaches. Id. at 591–592.

## Issue

Can a proposed class representative defeat CAFA's amount-in-controversy requirement by stipulating before certification that the representative and the class will seek less than the jurisdictional amount, although the aggregated claims would otherwise exceed that amount? Id. at 590–593.

## Rule and holding

A stipulation cannot reduce absent proposed class members' claims for CAFA jurisdictional purposes unless it legally binds them. Before certification, Knowles lacked authority to bind those persons. His stipulation could constrain him personally but could not override the District Court's finding that the proposed class's aggregated claims exceeded $5 million. Id. at 592–593, 596.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court vacated the judgment below and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion. The District Court should have ignored this nonbinding class-wide limitation when aggregating the proposed members' claims. The Court did not award insurance benefits, determine liability, or certify the class. Id. at 596.

## Reasoning

CAFA directs aggregation of the individual claims and defines class members to include named or unnamed people falling within a proposed or certified class definition. The jurisdictional inquiry therefore encompassed those proposed members' claims rather than only the amount Knowles personally chose to recover. The District Court had already found that this aggregation passed the threshold absent the purported cap. Id. at 592.

The essential feature of a stipulation is its binding effect on the party making it. Knowles could make a concession for himself; he could not make it for people who were not yet parties through a certified class. Jurisdiction was examined as of filing in state court, when that authority was absent. A limitation that might become effective after certification did not presently reduce the others' claims. Id. at 592–593.

Knowles invoked the prohibition on jurisdiction depending on contingent future events. The Court answered that the cap itself was contingent. A state court might certify only after requiring its removal, reject Knowles as an inadequate representative because of the cap, or allow a new representative to intervene. Those were illustrations of possible developments, not findings that any had occurred in this case. Id. at 593–594.

The Court considered the argument that any later change in the cap would produce a different case that CAFA could not consider at removal. It rejected that characterization: recognizing a stipulation's present lack of binding effect does not substitute a future complaint for the existing one. Treating an ineffective concession as binding would elevate its wording over its legal operation and conflict with CAFA's purpose of allowing federal consideration of significant interstate cases. The Court's example of splitting a $100 million action into 21 smaller actions was a hypothetical consequence, not the history of Knowles' lawsuit. Id. at 594–595.

Administrative simplicity did not justify relying on the cap. Ignoring it required the same aggregation the statute demands in cases containing no stipulation. Nor did the ability of an individual plaintiff to limit an individual claim answer the class problem: an individual limitation binds all relevant plaintiffs in a single-plaintiff case, while Knowles' limitation could not bind his proposed class. Id. at 595–596.

## Reserved issue and notes

The Court declined Knowles' alternative argument concerning a stipulation limited to attorneys' fees because his stipulation did not provide that option. The opinion therefore does not settle whether a differently framed fee limitation would affect the jurisdictional calculation. Id. at 596.

The assigned page gives the citation as “568 U.S. 558.” The official report begins at page 588; the verified citation is 568 U.S. 588. The textbook's short account of the unbound absent members matches the holding, but its reporter page is a typographical error. Casebook p. 871; official report pp. 588–589.

The complete report has no judicial footnotes, separate writings, or appendix. Its asterisk note lists amici and continues from page 589 to page 590. The unanimity concerns this jurisdictional issue; the opinion does not resolve unrelated CAFA exceptions or the insurance claim's merits.
