# Lamps Plus, Inc. v. Varela
587 U.S. 176, 139 S. Ct. 1407 (2019) | Contracts | Class arbitration, ambiguity, and contra proferentem

## Facts and procedural history

In 2016 a hacker impersonating a company official persuaded a Lamps Plus employee to disclose tax information for approximately 1,300 employees. A fraudulent federal income-tax return was then filed in employee Frank Varela's name. Varela brought state and federal claims in a California federal district court on behalf of a proposed class of affected employees. His employment arbitration agreement was drafted by Lamps Plus. The breach and resulting suit supplied the underlying dispute; the Supreme Court did not determine the company's liability for the breach. Court slip op. 1–2.

Lamps Plus moved to compel individual arbitration and dismiss the suit. In one order the district court compelled arbitration, authorized class proceedings, and dismissed Varela's claims without prejudice. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, interpreting the agreement as ambiguous about class arbitration and construing it against Lamps Plus under California law. The agreement lacked an express reference to class proceedings, but broadly sent employment disputes and legal proceedings to arbitration. The Ninth Circuit distinguished Stolt-Nielsen because the parties there stipulated that they had reached no agreement on class proceedings; no equivalent stipulation existed here. Judge Fernandez dissented, finding no ambiguity and viewing the result as an evasion of Stolt-Nielsen. Court slip op. 2–3.

The Supreme Court granted review and, on April 24, 2019, reversed and remanded. Roberts wrote for Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh, producing a five-to-four decision. Thomas also concurred separately. Ginsburg dissented with Breyer and Sotomayor; Breyer and Sotomayor each wrote separately. Kagan dissented with Ginsburg and Breyer joining in full and Sotomayor joining Part II only. The different joins matter because the dissenters did not uniformly agree about jurisdiction or whether the agreement was unambiguously class-inclusive. Official PDF p. 3; Court slip op. 13.

## Questions and jurisdictional holding

The merits question was whether an ambiguous arbitration agreement provides the contractual basis the FAA requires for compelled class arbitration, and whether California's rule construing ambiguity against the drafter can supply that basis. The Court accepted the Ninth Circuit's conclusion of ambiguity out of deference to its state-law interpretation; it did not independently hold that the text clearly barred class arbitration. Court slip op. 5–6 & n.3.

Varela also challenged appellate jurisdiction. Section 16 generally bars interlocutory appeals from orders compelling arbitration but permits appeals from final decisions concerning arbitration under § 16(a)(3). Under Randolph, an order both directing arbitration and dismissing all underlying claims is final within that provision. The majority applied that rule here. It rejected the argument that Lamps Plus had received everything it requested and therefore lacked a stake in appeal: class arbitration materially differs from the individual arbitration it sought and imposes additional costs and risks. Court slip op. 3–5.

The jurisdiction notes answer Breyer's objections. No party requested a stay, so the majority did not decide a hypothetical refusal of a requested FAA § 3 stay. Randolph had reserved whether the district court should have stayed the action in that case, rather than reserving finality of the actual dismissal order. Microsoft v. Baker involved plaintiffs manufacturing an appeal by voluntary dismissal, whereas Lamps Plus was a defendant objecting to compelled class arbitration. Court slip op. 4–5 & nn.1–2. These are the reasons for exercising jurisdiction in this procedural posture, not a general authorization to appeal every unfavorable feature of an arbitration order.

## Majority's rule and reasoning

The FAA rests on consensual arbitration and ordinarily requires enforcement according to the parties' terms. State contract principles often identify those terms, but federal law displaces state rules that obstruct the FAA's purposes. Arbitrators derive their authority from the agreement, and parties can specify participants, issues, procedures, and decision-makers. Court slip op. 6–7.

Stolt-Nielsen required a contractual basis for class arbitration and held silence inadequate. The majority extended that reasoning to ambiguity. Individual arbitration offers informality, speed, reduced expense, specialist adjudication, and limited judicial review. Class proceedings involve procedural complexity and larger stakes and may determine absent class members' rights without the appellate safeguards available in court. Because class arbitration changes those fundamental features, an uncertain agreement cannot establish the required affirmative consent. Court slip op. 7–9.

The Court analogized to its refusal to infer delegation of gateway arbitrability questions from silence or ambiguity. But it expressly left undecided whether the availability of class arbitration is itself a gateway question: the parties agreed that a court should decide it here. The decision therefore should not be cited as definitively allocating every class-arbitration question between judge and arbitrator. Court slip op. 9 & n.4.

Contra proferentem did not solve the consent problem in the majority's view. It is a last-resort default after ordinary interpretive methods leave meaning unresolved. The majority characterized it as assigning uncertainty against the drafter on equitable and public-policy grounds, rather than discovering the parties' actual shared intent. It therefore could not create the affirmative contractual basis required for class arbitration. Court slip op. 9–11.

Varela and Kagan argued that the rule treats every contract equally. The majority answered that general applicability does not prevent FAA preemption when a rule nevertheless interferes with arbitration's fundamental attributes. It relied on Concepcion's treatment of a general unconscionability rule that enabled class procedures without the requisite consent. The majority did not abolish the anti-drafter rule throughout contract law; it held that this rule cannot impose class arbitration when the contractual basis for that procedure is absent. Court slip op. 11–13.

The Court further explained that federal arbitration law supplies defaults for some ambiguities, such as ambiguities about arbitrable scope, without asking who drafted the language. Footnote 5 distinguished Mastrobuono's use of contra proferentem because both the FAA's preference and the anti-drafter canon led to the same outcome there; the Court had not faced the conflict presented here. Court slip op. 12 & n.5.

## Thomas's concurrence

Thomas joined the majority as an application of existing FAA precedents, while reiterating skepticism about implied obstacle preemption. He would also reverse directly under Stolt-Nielsen: he read this agreement as silent on class arbitration and its repeated references to the company and the employee's own claims as suggesting bilateral proceedings. His single note cited California intermediate decisions interpreting similar language that way. This separate textual route does not change the majority's acceptance of ambiguity for its analysis. Thomas slip op. 1–2 & note.

## Kagan's dissent

Kagan offered two grounds for permitting class arbitration. In Part I, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer, she read the agreement's comprehensive language to include class disputes. It sent any and all employment-related disputes to binding arbitration, substituted arbitration for any and all lawsuits, allowed any remedy under applicable law, and incorporated providers' rules allowing class proceedings. Singular first-person pronouns identified the contracting employee; they did not limit the types of disputes he could bring. Her notes address Mastrobuono's broad dispute/remedy language and Lamps Plus's distinction between claims and lawsuits, observing that this agreement defined claims to include controversies. Kagan slip op. 2–4 & nn.1–2.

In Part II, also joined by Sotomayor, Kagan assumed ambiguity and applied the anti-drafter canon. Lamps Plus controlled its form and could have expressly excluded class arbitration. California's rule applies to all types of contracts and can favor or disfavor class proceedings depending on who drafted the language and who seeks them. It therefore differs from a rule targeting collective-action waivers. The FAA's equal-treatment principle, in her view, left this impartial state rule intact. Kagan slip op. 5–9 & nn.3–6.

Kagan distinguished Stolt-Nielsen's stipulation that no agreement on class arbitration existed and its arbitrators' resort to their own policy preferences. That ruling had reserved what contractual basis would suffice and recognized the possible relevance of background contract law. Here a neutral rule gave legal meaning to an ambiguous agreement. Contra proferentem also promotes shared understanding: before contracting it encourages clear drafting, and after contracting it protects the recipient's reasonable understanding of unresolved wording. It could not fairly be dismissed as concerned only with policy. Kagan slip op. 9–12.

Her seventh note identified uncertainty in the majority's explanation: its broad language rejects ambiguous agreements generally, while its narrower discussion distinguishes policy-oriented defaults from tools identifying meaning. She anticipated difficulty classifying other interpretive rules; she did not purport to resolve their future application. Her final note criticized Thomas's willingness to join implied preemption despite his stated doubts. Kagan concluded that the majority substituted its policy objection to class proceedings for the agreement and authoritative state law. Kagan slip op. 11–14 & nn.7–8.

## Other dissents

Ginsburg, joined by Breyer and Sotomayor, emphasized bargaining power and effective enforcement. In her account the FAA began as a means of enforcing commercial arbitration between merchants, but the Court expanded it to employment and consumer forms with little real choice. Compulsory solo proceedings can make low-value claims impractical. Calling class arbitration nonconsensual while imposing individual arbitration on employees who would prefer collective relief inverted the consent principle. Her discussion of contemporary company and state responses is a historical description in a dissent, not this brief's statement of present law. Ginsburg slip op. 1–5.

Breyer separately would find no appellate jurisdiction. The FAA's structure permits early appeals when arbitration is denied and generally postpones review when arbitration is ordered. He viewed dismissal as an impermissible means of converting an unappealable arbitration order into a final appeal, relied on Microsoft, and read Randolph's reservation differently. Class arbitration remains arbitration, and disagreement with its form should not permit immediate appeal. He also joined Ginsburg and Kagan on the merits. Breyer slip op. 1–9.

Sotomayor did not join Breyer's jurisdiction dissent, explaining in her first note that she was not persuaded of a jurisdictional defect, though his questions warranted consideration in an appropriate case. She regarded class actions as an ordinary procedural device, found this agreement at least ambiguous given its broad terms and incorporated arbitral rules, and would apply neutral California law. She criticized preempting state law without independently considering whether a narrower contractual interpretation would avoid the issue. Sotomayor slip op. 1–3 & nn.1–2.

## Disposition and source scope

The Court held that an ambiguous agreement cannot supply the necessary contractual basis for compelled class arbitration and that contra proferentem cannot substitute for that basis. It reversed the Ninth Circuit's judgment and remanded for proceedings consistent with the opinion. It did not adjudicate data-breach liability or issue a final class award. Court slip op. 13.
