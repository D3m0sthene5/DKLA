# Scott, an Infant, by His Next Friend v. Shepherd, an Infant, by Guardian
2 Wm. Bl. 892, 96 Eng. Rep. 525 (C.P. 1773) | Civil Procedure | Historical trespass and case

## A squib passed through a crowded market

On the evening of the fair at Milborne Port on October 28, 1770, Shepherd threw a lighted squib, a small gunpowder firework, from the street into the crowded market-house. It landed on the stall of Yates, a gingerbread seller. Willis immediately picked it up and threw it across the market-house to protect himself and Yates's goods. It landed on Ryal's stall. Ryal, acting to protect his own goods, immediately threw it again. This throw struck Scott in the face; the firework then exploded and destroyed the sight of one eye. The problem was whether Shepherd could be treated as the immediate author of Scott's injury even though two other people had physically moved the firework. 96 Eng. Rep. 525–526.

The caption identifies both parties as infants and their representative capacities, but the report does not make an infancy defense the issue. Scott sued in trespass and assault, alleging that Shepherd had thrown the squib against him and struck him with it. Shepherd pleaded not guilty. At the summer assizes at Bridgwater, before Justice Nares, the jury found for Scott with £100 damages, subject to the court's opinion on the stated case. The Common Pleas judges therefore had to determine whether these established facts supported this particular form of action. This was not an ordinary appeal affirming an unconditional final judgment: the verdict remained dependent on the reserved legal question. Id. at 525–526, 527–528.

## Why the form of action mattered

The historical distinction was between trespass, which addressed a direct or immediate injury inflicted with force, and an action on the case, which addressed an injury consequential upon an act. The dispute did not turn simply on whether Shepherd had acted badly, whether Scott was seriously injured, or whether some remedy should exist. It concerned whether Scott could recover through trespass against Shepherd on the facts the jury had stated. A court could recognize a possible action on the case while rejecting the action actually brought. Id. at 526–528.

The judges delivered their opinions individually. Nares, Gould, and Chief Justice De Grey supported the action; Blackstone disagreed. Their reasoning should not be compressed into a single unanimous rule that every consequence of an unlawful act is a trespass. Nares emphasized that proposition; De Grey expressly agreed with Blackstone that the controlling distinction concerned immediacy, then disagreed about how to characterize the defensive throws. Id. at 526–529.

## Nares: the continuing dangerous agency

Nares regarded injury to someone as the natural and probable consequence of throwing the lighted squib into the crowd. The act was unlawful, and he reasoned that Shepherd must answer for its consequences whether mediated or immediate. Shepherd had supplied the firework's dangerous capacity; Willis and Ryal had not created a new power of mischief. That capacity persisted until the explosion. Nares compared the situation to releasing a mad ox into a crowd: the person who releases the danger can be answerable although he does not personally touch the eventual victim. Id. at 526.

For Nares, the intervening acts did not cleanse Shepherd's original wrong. He also invoked the court's reluctance to defeat a deserving damages verdict by overly exact scrutiny of whether the evidence fitted the pleaded form. That consideration supplemented his account of the dangerous instrument, rather than replacing the need to decide whether trespass lay. Blackstone would directly challenge this procedural argument because the jury had deliberately reserved the very question of fit between facts and action. Id. at 526–528.

## Blackstone: each new throw was a new immediate act

Blackstone rejected lawfulness as the criterion for choosing the form of action. Someone who unlawfully throws a log into the highway may cause a later pedestrian's fall, but that later injury is consequential and calls for case; if the thrown log immediately hits the person, trespass lies. Conversely, lawful activity can produce an immediate trespass. These examples separated the wrongfulness of conduct from the causal relation relevant to the historical writ. Id. at 526–527.

He considered Shepherd's direct act complete when the squib came to rest on Yates's stall. Willis and Ryal were rational agents who gave it new motion and direction. They differed from an animal, an inanimate object deflected by a tree, or another instrument still moving under the original force. A dangerous capacity to cause future harm did not establish that every subsequent motion was Shepherd's immediate act. Otherwise the original participant in a long chain of people kicking a football might face trespass for damage eventually caused by someone else. Id. at 527.

Blackstone recognized a right to remove danger but believed the two men had to protect others while doing so. They could have brushed the squib down or thrown it out through an open side instead of across the market-house. He tentatively thought case could lie against Shepherd for consequential damage, without finally deciding it, and thought trespass would lie against Ryal as the immediate actor. His position was therefore not that Scott necessarily had no remedy. It was that Scott's chosen action against this defendant did not fit the facts. Id. at 527–528.

He also distinguished a court's tolerance of variance after an ordinary verdict from this case's expressly reserved question. Here the court was asked whether proof of a consequential injury could sustain a declaration alleging an immediate trespass. Although proof supporting trespass might also support case, the reverse did not necessarily follow. Blackstone would have entered judgment for Shepherd in this action. Id. at 527–528.

## Gould and De Grey: defensive necessity preserved the first act

Gould agreed with Nares that the action could be maintained, while emphasizing that the difficulty concerned the form of remedy. Independently of the broad unlawful-act proposition, he treated the terror imposed on Willis and Ryal as depriving them of reflective choice: the defensive response was an inevitable consequence of the danger Shepherd had created. It could therefore be considered as though Shepherd had personally thrown the squib into Scott's face. His discussion of people acting in concert in the football example was an additional illustration, not a factual finding that these market participants had agreed to a common plan. Id. at 528.

De Grey accepted Blackstone's general distinction between immediate and consequential injury. He expressly said that unlawfulness alone did not answer the question: trespass can lie after a lawful act, and case can be appropriate after an unlawful one. The question was whether the injury proceeded from Shepherd's first force or a genuinely new force supplied by a third person. Id. at 528–529.

He treated the subsequent movements as a continuation of the original force until the squib burst. Immediate did not mean instantaneous; a connected chain of effects could suffice. Willis and Ryal were acting under the necessity of self-preservation imposed on them, rather than as free agents initiating new trespasses. Innocently moving this danger away did not shift the blame from the first thrower. On that characterization, the reserved facts supported trespass against Shepherd. Id. at 529.

## Judgment, assigned reading, and limits

The result was **postea to the plaintiff**, by three judges to one. Scott's conditional £100 verdict could proceed on the court's determination that the action was maintainable. The report does not give a new damages assessment, an adjudication of separate liability against Willis or Ryal, or a judgment in a separately filed action on the case. Id. at 529.
