# Hertzog v. Hertzog
29 Pa. 465 (1857) | Contracts

## The son's work and the claimed obligation
John Hertzog sued his deceased father's estate for the value of services and for money lent. He reached majority around1825 but continued to live with his farmer father and work on his farms, apart from a temporary absence in Virginia. In1842 John married and brought his wife into his parents' home. His father later placed him on another farm the father owned, then moved there with his own wife. They lived together until the father's death in1849. The father managed the farm in his own name; no accounts were kept between father and son. The reviewing court presumed the son and his family received their living from the father during the association. (29 Pa. at 465,469–470.)

Witness Adam Stamm described everyone working together, the father receiving proceeds that went toward the farm, and the value of John's and his wife's services. He heard the father say he would pay John for his labor. Daniel Roderick testified that John had repeatedly asked his father for payment and been put off; John asked Roderick to intervene. Roderick then approached the father, who said he intended to make John safe. The significance of these statements was disputed: did they show an existing agreement for wages, or only an intention to reward family services? (Id. at 465–466,470.)

There was also a separate $500 loan claim. Evidence indicated that the father had received money belonging to John's wife when buying a farm in1847. The estate challenged John's ability to recover that money without joining her, invoking the1848 married-women's legislation. The reviewing court treated the evidence of the loan and its date as indistinct rather than resolving a proven loan amount in John's favor. (Id. at 466,471.)

## Trial and review
The estate requested an instruction requiring proof that the relationship was one of master and servant rather than ordinary parent and child. The trial court agreed that wages could not be recovered without a mutual understanding to compensate, but told the jury it could infer such an agreement from Stamm's and Roderick's testimony. It also permitted addition of a quantum-meruit count and ruled that John could sue for the wife's money received in1847 without joining her. The estate pleaded limitations, although the published reviewing opinion did not separately decide that defense. (Id. at 466.)

The jury returned a $2,203.97 verdict for John, and judgment followed. The estate obtained review by writ of error, challenging the trial court's answers. Justice Lowrie delivered the Pennsylvania Supreme Court's opinion. The complete report identifies no numerical division, concurrence, dissent, or judicial footnote. (Id. at 467,471.)

## Three meanings of contract
Lowrie began by separating relationships commonly gathered under the label implied contract. A true contract rested on the parties' mutual intention. When the terms were openly expressed it was express; when actual intention was inferred from circumstances it was implied. By contrast, a constructive contract was a legal fiction used to enforce a duty through a contract remedy even when the parties had no intention to contract. Its duty came from law rather than agreement. The court criticized the use of one label for these materially different sources of obligation. (Id. at 467–468.)

The court said this case asserted a proper express or implied agreement, not a constructive obligation. Thus the addition of a quantum-meruit count did not authorize the jury to replace proof of the agreement being claimed with a general belief that payment would be fair. That distinction limits the assigned note's description of refusal of restitution: Lowrie expressly identified the theory before him as one requiring a real contract, rather than deciding every possible modern unjust-enrichment theory. (Id. at 468,470–471.)

## Family relations explained the services without implying hiring
Ordinary work for a stranger may support an inference of hiring because the relationship otherwise calls for an explanation. But an existing relationship may supply that explanation. Lowrie contrasted a stranger's compensated work with neighbors helping save a burning house, where customary kindness explained their conduct. Likewise, family association could account for a son's work for his father without an employment bargain. A party claiming a contract had to prove it; merely proving circumstances equally explained by the existing family relationship was insufficient. (Id. at 468–469.)

Here the son's continued residence, shared household, work on the father's farm under the father's management, absence of accounts, and presumed receipt of support supplied that family explanation. The court's statements about affection, filial duties, and sons remaining at home expressed its historical view of customary family relations. They did not establish that relatives were legally incapable of making a hiring agreement. Lowrie expressly allowed proof of an express contract through circumstantial evidence: accounts, periodic wages, or, in an appropriate case without creditor objections, a settlement of wages and an agreed balance might show one. None was present here. (Id. at 469–470.)

John also urged on review that the farm had been operated for father and son jointly with profits divided. Lowrie found no evidence of that arrangement, no evidence of annual products, and no such theory in the jury presentation, which concerned wages for John's and his wife's services. That unsupported profit-sharing characterization could not sustain the judgment. (Id. at 470.)

## An intention to reward did not establish an existing wage agreement
The father's reported statements shortly before death were not the making or admission of a contract. In Lowrie's reading, they acknowledged that the son deserved a reward, while leaving unproved any existing agreement to pay wages. John's repeated unsuccessful requests for payment likewise suggested that he wanted an agreement rather than that one had already been made. The trial court therefore erred by authorizing the jury to infer a contract from this testimony. (Id.)

Lowrie rejected allowing jurors to decide simply by their sense of honesty or fairness when the claim rested on contract. The parties' proven agreement had to supply the rule of decision. The opinion's criticism concerned this verdict and proof, rather than a finding that the services lacked value or that family affection could never coexist with compensation. (Id. at 470–471.)

## Loan issue and exact judgment
The court stated conditionally that, if the $500 was lent by John's wife, John, or both before the1848 law, John could sue without joining his wife. It did not enter judgment for that sum or conclusively find the transaction's date. (Id. at 471.)

The judgment was reversed and a new trial awarded. This was not an affirmance of the wage verdict or a final dismissal of every claim against the estate. The faulty inference instruction required further proceedings under the court's distinction between family services and proof of an actual compensation agreement. (Id.)
