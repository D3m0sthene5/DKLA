# Corcoran v. City of Chicago

**Civil Procedure · CP-134 · 373 Ill. 567, 27 N.E.2d 451 (1940) · April 10, 1940; rehearing denied June 5, 1940**

Corcoran holds that Illinois’s statutory authorization for appellate review of a verdict’s evidentiary weight did not violate the state constitutional right to jury trial. The power permitted a reviewing court to require another jury to consider the case, subject to strict limits protecting the first jury’s role. The Illinois Supreme Court affirmed the appellate judgment but expressly declined to decide whether the appellate court had correctly weighed the evidence in this particular negligence case.

John Corcoran sued Chicago for personal injuries. He alleged that depressions, holes, obstructions, and uneven places in a city street made travel unsafe and caused the automobile in which he was riding to overturn. Some evidence supported the alleged dangerous condition; other evidence indicated that the street was reasonably well maintained. The jury awarded Corcoran $5,000. The superior court denied a new trial and entered judgment. The Appellate Court reversed, finding the verdict against the manifest weight of the evidence, and initially remanded for another trial. (373 Ill. at 568–69.)

Corcoran then asked the Appellate Court to strike the remand provision. His affidavit stated that another trial would involve the same issues and that he had no additional or different evidence. Striking the remand made the order final and allowed him to seek Supreme Court review by a common-law writ of error. He challenged § 92(3b) of the Illinois Civil Practice Act, which authorized review when a judgment was unsupported by the evidence or against its weight. In his view, allowing appellate judges to set aside a jury’s resolution of conflicting evidence deprived the prevailing litigant of constitutionally protected jury trial. (Id. at 569–70.)

The city first sought dismissal of the writ. It argued that Corcoran had brought the loss of a second jury trial upon himself by seeking removal of the remand, had raised the statute’s validity too late, and had not submitted the constitutional issue before the Appellate Court decided the appeal. Justice Murphy rejected those objections. The removal of the remand did not itself prevent a constitutional challenge to the power exercised. Corcoran first raised the issue on rehearing, which was his first opportunity to know that the Appellate Court would reverse for factual error under this provision rather than decide another legal ground. The state constitution independently supplied Supreme Court review where statutory validity was involved, and the common-law writ remained available despite changes in the Practice Act’s wording. The motion to dismiss was denied. (Id. at 570–71.)

On the merits, the court traced Illinois’s statutory practice to an 1837 enactment allowing exceptions to denials of new trials and appellate assignments of error based on those rulings. Earlier Illinois decisions had exercised this power, including reversal of verdicts manifestly against the evidence. The modern provision substantially continued that practice. Cases invalidating a different power—appellate substitution of contrary factual findings followed by final reversal without remand—did not control. Section 92(3b), as relevant here, did not authorize that disposition on conflicting evidence. Corcoran’s own request, rather than the statutory power, had removed the remand. (Id. at 571–75.)

The constitutional guarantee preserved jury trial as historically understood, so the court also examined common-law practice. A judge at nisi prius tried the case locally and returned the verdict and record to the court at Westminster. New-trial motions were addressed to that court, whose judges could act on the record without having personally heard the witnesses. Authorities describing the new trial as a safeguard against unjust verdicts contradicted the claim that only the judge who conducted the trial could correct evidentiary error. The mechanism allowed another jury to reconsider the dispute rather than making reviewing judges the final factfinders. (Id. at 575–79.)

That justification required restraint. Slight differences in the balance of testimony were insufficient. The opinion collected precedents requiring a clearly wrong verdict, a strong preponderance of contrary evidence, or circumstances in which justice demanded intervention. It presented these formulations as illustrations of strict limits, not an exhaustive new test. Unrestricted appellate substitution would itself deprive parties of jury trial. (Id. at 579–80.)

The Supreme Court **affirmed** the Appellate Court’s judgment. It held the statute constitutional and stated that it lacked jurisdiction to determine whether the Appellate Court had correctly analyzed the evidence. The reported opinion contains no separate concurrence or dissent and no judicial footnotes. The assigned principal passage, printed pages 1249–1250, emphasizes the historical foundation but omits much of the jurisdictional analysis and the limiting discussion. Its subsequent federal-law notes are editorial comparisons, not holdings about the Seventh Amendment in this Illinois case. This brief reports the 1940 decision without an unchecked claim about current Illinois or federal review standards.
