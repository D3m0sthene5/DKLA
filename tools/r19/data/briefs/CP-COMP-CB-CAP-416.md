# Atlas Roofing Co., Inc. v. Occupational Safety and Health Review Commission

**430 U.S. 442 (1977) · Supreme Court of the United States · March 23, 1977**  
**Civil Procedure · Seventh Amendment and administrative adjudication**  
**Assigned:** CivPro PDF 544–545, printed 1070–1072, own-caption account and accompanying questions. This judgment also decides *Frank Irey, Jr., Inc. v. Occupational Safety and Health Review Commission*, the consolidated companion petition.

## What happened and how the cases reached the Court

Congress enacted the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 after finding that existing state statutory and common-law remedies did not adequately protect workers against unsafe workplaces. Those existing remedies generally addressed actual injuries or deaths. OSHA added a federal duty to maintain safe working conditions and allowed the Government to seek abatement orders and civil penalties even when no employee had yet suffered injury. State remedies for actual injury remained available. This distinction matters because the proceedings concerned enforcement of the new federal duties, rather than employees' private damages suits. 430 U.S. at 444–445 & nn.1–2.

The statutory process began with a Labor Department inspection and citation. An employer could contest the proposed penalty or abatement order within 15 days and obtain a hearing before an administrative law judge of the Occupational Safety and Health Review Commission. The Secretary bore the burden of establishing the violation and the propriety of the proposed order and penalty. The judge could affirm, modify, or vacate them and had to consider factors including seriousness, business size, good faith, and prior violations. Unless a commissioner directed review within 30 days, the judge's decision became the Commission's final order. A reviewing court of appeals could examine legal issues but had to accept factual findings supported by substantial evidence on the whole record. A later district-court collection action did not reopen the violation or penalty determination. Consequently, employers could be made to pay without any jury deciding the underlying facts. Id. at 446–447.

Both employers here were cited after 1972 inspections of worksites where employees had died. Frank Irey, Jr., Inc. allegedly failed to shore, slope, or otherwise support a trench in unstable or soft material as a safety standard required. The Secretary proposed a $7,500 penalty for a willful violation; the administrative process ultimately assessed $5,000. Atlas Roofing allegedly failed to secure roof-opening covers against accidental displacement. The Secretary proposed, and the administrative process assessed, a $600 penalty for a serious violation. The administrative judges and Commission sustained the violations and abatement requirements. Id. at 447–448.

Irey petitioned the Third Circuit; Atlas petitioned the Fifth Circuit. Both challenged the factual findings and the constitutionality of OSHA's enforcement machinery. The Third Circuit affirmed, first through a divided panel and then en banc over four dissents. The Fifth Circuit also affirmed. The Supreme Court granted review limited to whether the Seventh Amendment prevented this administrative adjudication of OSHA violations. Although the employers had also raised criminal-jury arguments below and in their petitions, that was not the question on which review was granted. Id. at 448–449 & nn.4–5.

## The issue and the competing arguments

The employers argued that a Government suit for a money penalty resembles a traditional action at law. If such a suit were brought in federal court, they maintained, disputed facts would have to be tried to a jury. Congress therefore should not be able to eliminate that protection simply by choosing an administrative forum. Otherwise, legislative control over the forum could become legislative control over a constitutional right. Id. at 449–450, 457–458.

The Government defended Congress's ability to create public regulatory duties and commit enforcement to an administrative process. It also invited the Court to resolve a broader historical question: whether the Seventh Amendment applies to Government litigation for penalties at all. The Court declined that invitation because its administrative-forum reasoning disposed of these cases. Thus, the decision does not establish that every federal Government civil-penalty action in court lacks a jury right. Id. at 449–450 n.6.

## Holding and reasoning

The Court held that the Seventh Amendment did not prevent Congress from assigning these OSHA proceedings to an administrative tribunal. When Congress creates statutory public rights and the Government acts in its sovereign capacity to enforce them, Congress may place initial adjudication and factfinding in an administrative forum with which a jury would be incompatible. Id. at 450, 455, 460–461.

First, the Court examined established administrative practices. Taxation, customs, immigration, and other regulatory schemes had allowed agencies to find facts and impose monetary obligations without a jury. Some precedents had not expressly discussed the Seventh Amendment, but the Court considered their acceptance of nonjudicial factfinding relevant. Other decisions addressed the jury issue more directly, including administrative rent regulation and National Labor Relations Board proceedings. Together, those authorities showed that monetary consequences did not invariably require adjudication by a court and jury. Id. at 450–453 & nn.8–10.

The Court rejected the proposed limitation of those precedents to powers essential to the Government's existence, such as taxation and immigration. The precedents' explanations also reached congressional regulation of interstate and foreign commerce, the setting in which OSHA operated. Congress's authority to use administrative enforcement therefore did not depend on placing workplace safety below some specially privileged list of sovereign subjects. Id. at 456–457.

Second, creating a statutory cause of action does not itself eliminate jury rights. The Court distinguished administrative enforcement from statutory legal actions filed in courts that ordinarily employ juries. Its discussion of *Curtis v. Loether* and *Pernell v. Southall Realty* emphasized that Congress must preserve jury trial when it chooses an ordinary judicial action involving traditionally legal rights and remedies. The difference lay in the character of the right and permissible enforcement forum, not the bare fact that legislation created the claim. Id. at 454–455 & nn.11–12.

Third, history did not make juries the exclusive civil factfinders. Equity, admiralty, and some common-law proceedings used other arrangements. Historically, even similar factual questions could be decided by a jury in a damages action and by a judge in an action for specific performance. Equity also developed remedies when common-law relief was inadequate. The Court used that history to explain why the Amendment's preservation of jury trial did not freeze every possible future federal regulatory remedy into an ordinary action at law. Id. at 458–460 & n.14.

The employers' strongest objection was that selecting a forum could otherwise destroy jury trial altogether. The Court responded with an express boundary: wholly private tort, contract, and property disputes were not implicated by the public-rights holding. Footnote 7 distinguished private-right administrative factfinding conducted as an adjunct to an Article III court. Footnote 16 further confirmed that the Supreme Court retained the final constitutional decision about whether a jury was required in a legal action before a forum ordinarily using juries. Congress's forum choice was therefore not described as an unrestricted power to abolish jury trial. Id. at 450 n.7, 458, 461 n.16.

## Limits, notes, and exact judgment

Judicial review remained part of OSHA's actual scheme. Footnote 13 expressly reserved whether Congress could commit public-right adjudication and fines to an agency without any judicial intervention at any stage. The employers also did not challenge the absence of mandatory Commission review of an administrative judge's factual findings, as footnote 3 records. Neither reservation should be converted into an affirmative holding approving every conceivable administrative arrangement. Id. at 446 n.3, 455 n.13.

The Court's historical discussion of criminal fines in footnote 15 drew on its prior Sixth Amendment precedent to explain the anomaly it saw in requiring juries for these civil administrative penalties. It did not expand the grant of review into a general decision about every criminal sanction. Likewise, footnote 10 distinguishes two grounds discussed in the NLRB precedent: statutory administrative enforcement and monetary relief incidental to equitable relief. OSHA's justification was not merely that every monetary penalty was equitable. Id. at 453 n.10, 460 n.15.

Justice White wrote for all eight participating Justices. Justice Blackmun did not participate; there are no separate opinions. The Court **affirmed both courts of appeals' judgments**, leaving the Commission's orders and assessed penalties intact. It did not remand or retry the underlying safety violations. Id. at 443, 461.

## How the assignment uses the decision

The assigned account accurately identifies the two administrative proceedings and appellate affirmances, then reproduces the public-rights and historical-forum reasoning. Its questions ask whether ordinary personal-injury claims could be reassigned to agencies and what limits constrain congressional replacement of common-law claims. Those are editorial questions, not additional holdings. The full opinion's express private-rights limitation and judicial-review reservation provide the necessary qualifications to the excerpt.
