# Seaver v. Ransom
224 N.Y. 233, 120 N.E. 639 (N.Y. October 1, 1918) | Contracts

## Facts and promised provision
Mrs. Beman was dying and owned a small estate, principally a house and lot in Malone. Her husband, Judge Beman, prepared a will giving her niece Marion Seaver $1,000, her niece's mother $500, another sister and that sister's son $100 each, and her husband lifetime use of the house, with remainder to the American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals. He was also named residuary legatee and executor. Seaver was thirty-four, in ill health, and sometimes lived with the Bemans. Upon hearing the will read, Mrs. Beman said she wanted the house to go to Seaver. She feared she would not survive long enough to execute a rewritten will, although her husband offered to prepare one. (235–236.)

Judge Beman solemnly promised that, if she signed the existing will, he would provide enough for Seaver in his own will to make up the difference. She then executed it. His eventual will made no provision for Seaver. The promise was supported by Mrs. Beman's execution of the will in the form he requested; this was not simply a gratuitous statement directly to the niece. (235–236.)

## Proceedings and competing theories
Seaver recovered after a trial without a jury. The trial court reasoned that Beman obtained property through his promise to provide the niece $6,000, the house's value, and equity impressed his property with a trust. The Appellate Division affirmed on the alternative ground that Seaver could enforce the contract made for her benefit. The executors appealed, arguing that she lacked the necessary relationship or obligation to enforce a promise made to another, that specific performance was unavailable, and that no trust arose. (234–237.)

## Trust distinction and holding
Pound rejected the trial court's particular trust theory but affirmed contractual enforcement. A legatee who obtains property by promising to use it for a testator's specified purpose can hold that property subject to a trust. Here Beman received only lifetime use of the house. No property obtained by his promise could be identified as a fund bound for Seaver. He was personally bound by the promise, but his general property was not automatically impressed with that original trust. An action for contract damages, or relief making the executors trustees to perform the agreement, rested on a different basis. (236–237.)

The contract was made on valid consideration for Seaver's direct benefit. The court held that the childless aunt's wish to provide for her dependent, favored niece fell within the principle supporting enforceable promises for close family beneficiaries. Recovery did not require an existing pecuniary debt from the aunt to Seaver or a legally mandated testamentary provision. (238–242.)

## Development of the beneficiary rule
Pound reviewed the historical privity rule and the American movement toward enforcement by intended beneficiaries. He acknowledged that New York had not expressed a simple unlimited rule. The existing cases chiefly fell into four groups: promises discharging a pecuniary obligation owed by the promisee to the beneficiary; promises benefiting the promisee's wife, intended wife, or child; public agreements whose covenants protected inhabitants; and promises addressed directly to the beneficiary at another person's request, with consideration furnished by that other person. Those groups did not justify excluding a case within their principles merely because its beneficiary was a niece. (237–239.)

The family cases depended in substantial part on affection and moral responsibility rather than a legal duty to make a will. A child could enforce an equivalent promise even though the parent had no legal duty to devise property to that child. A dependent niece could have a stronger moral claim than an affluent or unworthy child. The court therefore refused to treat degree of kinship as an absolute limit. It distinguished authorities involving different instruments, relationships, or arrangements rather than treating their statements as controlling every beneficiary claim. (239–240.)

Seaver alone was substantially harmed by this breach, while representatives of Mrs. Beman's estate had no interest in specific enforcement. Those circumstances reinforced allowing the person the promise was intended to benefit to enforce it. Pound traced both broader and narrower interpretations of Lawrence v. Fox and stressed the practical and equitable reason for enforcing this particular bargain. (239–241.)

As an analogy, a devise of the house to Beman conditioned on his paying Seaver $6,000 would have made him personally liable upon acceptance, even if the house proved worth less. Such liability would follow because the testatrix effectively bequeathed a promise to the beneficiary. The difference between that implied undertaking and Beman's express undertaking on valuable consideration to provide by will was insufficient to defeat recovery. The court allowed the equities to be enforced as contract damages or specific performance under the agreement, without restoring the rejected theory that an identifiable trust res had originally passed to Beman for Seaver. (241–242.)

## Judgment, votes, and scope
The Court of Appeals affirmed with costs. Pound, Hogan, Cardozo, and Crane formed the four-judge majority. Hiscock C.J., Collin, and Andrews dissented; the report supplies no separate dissenting reasons. No numbered judicial footnotes appear.
