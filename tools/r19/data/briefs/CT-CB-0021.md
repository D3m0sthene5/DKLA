United States Naval Institute v. Charter

936 F.2d 692 (2d Cir. 1991)
Citation, court, and assigned scope
The United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit decided this remedial appeal on
June 18, 1991. Judge Kearse wrote for a panel also comprising Judges Winter and Altimari.
The casebook excerpt appears at printed pages 21–24. The full opinion also resolves
copyright ownership, an asserted abandonment of the contract claim, prejudgment interest,
and attorney's fees. Reporter-page pinpoints were checked against OpenJurist's star
pagination as well as the judicial text on Justia.
Parties and their objectives
The United States Naval Institute held the author's copyright in The Hunt for Red October
and marketed the hardcover edition. It licensed paperback rights to Charter Communications
and Berkley Publishing Group, treated collectively as Berkley in the opinion. Naval sought
relief for premature paperback retail sales that allegedly displaced more profitable hardcover
sales. On this appeal, Naval wanted a larger recovery of Berkley's profits, additional interest,
and attorney's fees. Berkley wanted the judgment eliminated or reduced. Each side therefore
challenged different elements of a judgment that had awarded Naval both compensation for
its own losses and a portion of the other side's profits. (936 F.2d at 693–95.)
Material facts
The parties made their licensing agreement in September 1984. One provision conveyed an
exclusive right to publish and sell paperback editions; another made the license term begin
on the date of the agreement. A separate provision prohibited publication before October
1985. The beginning of ownership of the licensed rights and the promised first-publication
date were consequently different dates. That distinction became decisive when Naval sought
Copyright Act remedies in addition to contract damages. (Id. at 693, 695.)
Berkley shipped paperbacks to outlets before October, enabling retail sales to begin on
September 15, 1985. Sales were sufficiently extensive for the book to approach the top of
paperback bestseller lists before the end of September. The earlier appellate decision had
accepted that advance shipment itself could be consistent with industry practice, but held
that causing such substantial early retail sales breached the agreement's timing provision.
The 1991 opinion proceeded from that established breach rather than deciding afresh
whether any pre-October shipment was forbidden. (Id. at 693–94.)
The damages problem was one of substitution. Some consumers buying the cheaper
paperback in September would otherwise have bought the hardcover, but others would have
waited for the paperback rather than buying the hardcover at all. Thus Berkley's entire profit
from September paperback sales could not simply be equated with Naval's lost hardcover
profit. Hardcover sales had been declining before the premature release, which complicated
the estimate of what September sales would have been without breach. (Id. at 694, 696–97.)

Procedural history and standard of review
Naval unsuccessfully sought preliminary relief, and the district court initially entered
judgment against it after trial. The Second Circuit's 1989 decision, Naval I, reversed on the
contract-liability question and remanded for appropriate relief. On remand, the district court
treated the conduct as copyright infringement and awarded $35,380.50 in lost hardcover
profits, $7,760.12 in Berkley's profits attributed to displacement of hardcover sales, and
$15,319.27 in prejudgment interest on the compensatory award, plus costs. It denied
attorney's fees and interest on the profit award. (Id. at 693–94.)
Naval appealed the refusal to award a much larger profit figure—its proposed amount was
$724,300—as well as the refusals concerning fees and interest. Berkley cross-appealed,
arguing that an exclusive licensee could not infringe the rights it owned, that Naval had
abandoned contract damages, and that the actual-loss calculation was speculative. The court
reviewed the factual calculation of damages for clear error. The earlier contract-liability
ruling supplied the law of the case. (Id. at 694–97.)
Issues
Did Berkley's early retail publication infringe a copyright interest owned by Naval, or did it
breach a covenant governing rights already licensed to Berkley? Once copyright remedies
were unavailable, could Naval still recover contract damages? If so, could it recover Berkley's
full profits, and was the district court's comparison with August hardcover sales a
permissible estimate of its own loss? The remaining questions concerned interest and
attorney's fees. (Id. at 695–98.)
Rules and holdings
An exclusive license transfers ownership of the rights conveyed. Berkley had acquired the
relevant paperback rights in September 1984; the later restriction on publication was a
contractual covenant, not the starting date of that ownership. Its premature publication
therefore breached the agreement without infringing those already-conveyed rights. The
Copyright Act supplied no basis for the additional profit award or the claimed fees. (Id. at
695–96.)
Contract damages ordinarily compensate the injured party's loss. A breaching party's gains
can sometimes help measure that loss, but gains that do not correspond to the promisee's
injury are not automatically recoverable in an ordinary contract action. Here, Berkley's total
paperback profit did not measure Naval's lost hardcover profit. The compensatory estimate
based on the prior month's hardcover sales was not clearly erroneous, despite inevitable
uncertainty about the counterfactual sales volume. (Id. at 696–98.)
Under the New York law selected by the agreement, prejudgment interest followed the
successful contract claim. An ordinary contract action did not provide an equivalent basis for
an attorney's-fee award. The court also rejected Berkley's attempt to eliminate contract
recovery on abandonment grounds because contract liability already had been resolved and
Naval's prayer for relief and Rule 54(c) supported the relief to which it was entitled. (Id. at
696, 698.)

Actual judgment
The court reversed the $7,760.12 award of Berkley's profits and affirmed the judgment in
other respects. The $35,380.50 compensatory award and $15,319.27 prejudgment-interest
award survived, as did the denial of attorney's fees. The court directed no appellate costs.
This was a mixed remedial disposition; neither “Naval recovered all of Berkley's profits” nor
“Berkley won because there was no infringement” accurately describes it. (Id. at 693, 698.)
Reasoning
The agreement separated the ownership grant from the restriction on exercising it
The district court had treated October 1985 as the moment the license took effect. The actual
language instead tied its beginning to the September 1984 agreement date. The reference to
first publication appeared elsewhere in defining how long the license would last, confirming
that the parties distinguished those events. Because Berkley owned the paperback rights
when the early sales occurred, the contractual restriction did not convert it into an infringer
of its own conveyed interest. The court distinguished precedents in which the plaintiff still
owned the relevant rights. (Id. at 695–96.)
The distinction affected remedies directly. Section 504(b)'s route to infringer's profits
depended on infringement; the court could not preserve that route merely because Berkley
had behaved contrary to the agreement. Conversely, the license did not free Berkley from its
contractual restrictions. Ownership answered the copyright question, while the earlier
promise and adjudicated breach answered the contract question. (Id.)
Procedural history preserved contract relief
Berkley argued that Naval had abandoned the monetary contract claim before the first trial.
The court's earlier decision nevertheless had held that Naval was entitled to relief for the
contractual breach, and Berkley had not sought reargument on abandonment. That ruling
was the law of the case. In addition, Naval had requested other just and proper relief, and
Rule 54(c) generally directs a nondefault judgment to grant the relief warranted even if it
was not specifically demanded. Naval had accepted substantial royalties and renounced
rescission, making monetary compensation the suitable relief for its successful contract
theory. (Id. at 696.)
Defendant's gain and plaintiff's loss were not interchangeable
Naval's proposed $724,300 figure included profits on sales to consumers who would never
have bought the hardcover. Those purchases enriched Berkley but did not deprive Naval of
corresponding hardcover transactions. Recovering all those gains as ordinary compensation
would detach the award from the injury caused by the breach. The court did not prohibit
consideration of defendant's profit in every contract case: it expressly recognized situations
where that profit helps define the plaintiff's loss. The evidentiary relationship was absent
here. (Id. at 696–97.)
The smaller $7,760.12 award likewise rested on the rejected copyright theory. Its removal
did not require removing the separate $35,380.50 estimate, which measured Naval's lost
profits and could be sustained under contract law. The court therefore examined the

Reasonable estimation does not require impossible proof of an unrealized sales
history
The district court used August sales to estimate September hardcover sales absent early
paperbacks. Berkley wanted either no recovery for want of certainty or a projection based
only on the first half of September. The Second Circuit rejected a demand for precision
unavailable by the nature of the problem. There was evidence of actual lost sales: an
exceptionally large paperback distribution produced substantial sales before the contractual
date while Naval continued selling hardcovers. The unresolved question was the amount of
loss, not whether the record supplied any causal basis. (Id. at 697.)
The monthly benchmark could be generous to Naval without being clearly erroneous.
Nothing established that purchases occurred evenly throughout September, so Berkley's
proposed extrapolation was not uniquely compelled. Normal uncertainty attributable to the
wrongdoer's alteration of events could be resolved against it. This reasoning permits a
supported estimate; it does not authorize an invented amount whenever a breach is shown.
The court reviewed the available evidence and the proposed alternative before approving the
estimate. (Id. at 697–98.)
Interest and fees followed their own legal rules
New York contract law entitled the successful plaintiff to prejudgment interest,
compensating for delay in receiving the money owed. Attorney's fees were a different
question: rejection of copyright liability removed the proposed statutory route, and an
ordinary contract claim supplied no comparable entitlement. The court accordingly
preserved interest while affirming the denial of fees. (Id. at 698.)
Separate opinions
No separate concurrence or dissent appears in this panel decision. Kearse's opinion gives the
common disposition for the panel. The earlier Naval I opinion is a different stage of the
litigation, not a concurrence or dissent in this decision.
Use in class and source qualifications
Begin a remedy analysis by identifying both the theory of liability and the interest being
protected. The same conduct can violate a contract without meeting a statutory infringement
requirement, and different remedies cannot be imported merely because they would be more
generous. For expectation damages, explain why a particular sales benchmark reasonably
approximates the promisee's loss before invoking uncertainty against the breacher.
The textbook editors flag a possible inconsistency in the opinion's reference to an average of
3,000 hardcover copies per month. This brief does not replace that judicial figure with a
reconstructed one or use it to recompute damages. The dispositive dollar amounts, the
competing measurement arguments, and the court's standard of review are independently
stated in the opinion. The book's note and the court's factual recitation remain separate
sources of information. (Casebook p. 23 n.c; 936 F.2d at 697.)
