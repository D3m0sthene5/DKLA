# Garland v. Cargill

602 U.S. 406 (2024); No. 22-976, decided June 14, 2024

## Court, opinion, and citation convention
Justice Thomas wrote the Supreme Court's six-Justice opinion, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett. Justice Alito also concurred separately. Justice Sotomayor dissented, joined by Justices Kagan and Jackson. The Court affirmed the Fifth Circuit's judgment in favor of Michael Cargill.

## Parties and material facts
Cargill owned two bump stocks. The federal defendants were the Attorney General and other officials defending a Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives rule that classified the devices as machineguns. Cargill surrendered his devices under protest and challenged the rule under the Administrative Procedure Act. The dispute was about statutory authority, not whether the Second Amendment independently protected a right to possess the devices. Thomas, slip op. at 3–6.

The National Firearms Act defines a machinegun by its ability to fire automatically more than one shot, without manual reloading, through a single function of the trigger. The definition also reaches a part designed and intended to convert a weapon into a machinegun. A semiautomatic firearm normally fires once for each separate trigger engagement. A bump stock assists rapid repeated firing, but the parties disagreed about whether that process involves a single statutory trigger function and whether the resulting fire is automatic in the required sense. Id. at 1–3, 6–7.

The devices in this case were nonmechanical bump stocks. The majority expressly excluded spring-operated mechanical devices from the question presented. That limitation matters: the opinion should not be summarized as resolving every accessory that accelerates firing, every device called a bump stock, or every later classification involving other mechanisms. Id. at 3 n.1.

Before the challenged rule, ATF had repeatedly taken the position that the devices at issue did not fall within the statutory definition. After the 2017 Las Vegas mass shooting, the agency reconsidered that position. Proposed legislation did not become law, and ATF issued a final rule in 2018 that interpreted a single trigger function to include a single pull and analogous motions and treated the devices as self-regulating mechanisms producing continuous fire. Owners were directed to destroy or surrender covered bump stocks within ninety days, with criminal consequences for noncompliance. Id. at 3–5.

Those events explain the rule's origin and the significance of the challenge. The Court's holding nevertheless turns on the enacted definition. It does not rest on a finding that an agency can never change its interpretation or that the rulemaking process lacked notice and comment.

## Procedural history
After a bench trial, the Western District of Texas entered judgment for the government, concluding that a bump stock fit the definition. A Fifth Circuit panel affirmed. The en banc court later reversed. A majority agreed at least that the statute was ambiguous and that lenity required resolving that ambiguity in Cargill's favor. An eight-judge plurality went further and found the devices unambiguously excluded. Id. at 5–6.

The Supreme Court granted review to resolve a conflict among the courts of appeals. It affirmed the en banc judgment. Its own majority adopted a statutory reading that excluded the devices; it did not make lenity the basis of its decision. Distinguishing the Fifth Circuit's route from the Supreme Court's route is essential to stating the holding accurately. Id. at 6–7, 19.

## Issues
Does a semiautomatic rifle equipped with the bump stock at issue fire more than one shot through a single function of the trigger? Even assuming that requirement were satisfied, does the weapon fire those shots automatically through that trigger function? Finally, can a purpose-based presumption against rendering a statute ineffective expand the definition to cover the devices despite the majority's reading of its operative words?

The parties' disagreement was not simply about speed. It concerned the legal unit of analysis: a mechanical trigger engagement, or the shooter's initiating action that begins an assisted sequence of shots. Thomas, slip op. at 7–19; Sotomayor, slip op. at 6–18.

## Rule and holding
ATF exceeded its statutory authority by classifying the nonmechanical bump stocks at issue as machineguns. The majority gave two grounds. First, each shot still requires a separate trigger function, so the device does not produce multiple shots through a single function. Second, even on the assumption that the sequence could be attributed to one trigger function, the additional ongoing input required from the shooter prevents it from occurring automatically through that function. Thomas, slip op. at 6, 14–17.

The two grounds are logically distinct. A brief that says only “the trigger moves between shots” omits the alternative automatic-fire analysis. A brief that says only “human input is required” obscures the majority's recognition that conventional machineguns also involve human input and its narrower inquiry into input additional to the trigger's function.

## Actual judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the Fifth Circuit's judgment. Cargill prevailed on the statutory-authority question presented. The Court did not direct Congress to enact a replacement prohibition, decide a constitutional right to the devices, or settle the legal classification of every rapid-fire accessory. Id. at 19; id. at 3 n.1.

## Majority reasoning
### The statutory unit is a function of the trigger
The majority started with the statutory words and dictionary definitions of “function” and “trigger.” It understood the phrase to identify the action by which the trigger activates the firing mechanism. Under that reading, each reengagement after reset is a distinct trigger function. The number of shots produced by that individual engagement controls. Id. at 7–12.

The diagrams explain the factual premise: the semiautomatic mechanism releases, fires, and resets for each shot. The bump stock does not transform those separate engagements into one mechanical function; it facilitates their rapid succession. Rate of fire therefore cannot substitute for the elements Congress chose. Id. at 8–12.

### The shooter's initial action does not absorb later trigger functions
ATF emphasized that the shooter can initiate a sequence and then maintain a relatively stable position while the device assists repetition. The majority responded that the statute does not distinguish a finger pull from an analogous engagement produced through movement of the firearm. Either way, the trigger must operate separately for each shot. Id. at 12–14.

The Court also tested ATF's account for consistency. The rule treated analogous motions as trigger functions. If that is correct, a later analogous engagement cannot simply disappear from the statutory count because it follows the first one. The statute does not define the relevant event as the beginning of an entire firing sequence. Id.

The majority further observed that rapid repeated firing can occur without the accessory. It regarded ATF's willingness to exclude an unmodified semiautomatic firearm, while relying on a broadly framed account of one initiating action to include the equipped firearm, as a weakness in the agency's proposed distinction. Id. at 14.

### “Automatically” is tied to the single trigger function
The alternative reasoning concerns the complete phrase, not the word “automatically” in isolation. The statute requires multiple shots to occur automatically through a single trigger function. For the majority, the relevant device still requires ongoing input beyond that function. A firearm is not a machinegun merely because some part of its operation is automatic. Id. at 14–17.

ATF and the dissent answered that a conventional machinegun also requires continuous pressure. Thomas distinguished pressure that constitutes the continuing function of the trigger from additional input elsewhere that must accompany it. The distinction may be contested, but it is the distinction the majority makes; its reasoning is not that any sustained human involvement rules out automatic operation. Id. at 15–16.

The majority used the Ithaca Model 37 shotgun as an analogy because ATF conceded that it does not fire automatically in the statutory sense, even though multiple shots can follow a continuously held trigger. The additional manual operation explained that concession. The majority considered the need for additional input in the bump-stock setting legally comparable. The dissent rejected the analogy because it involved manual reloading, a separate statutory condition: the shotgun does not automate the loading process that a semiautomatic rifle already automates. Majority footnote 8 responds that ATF conceded the shotgun also fails the independent automatic-fire requirement, not merely the manual-reloading condition. Id. at 16–17 & n.8; Sotomayor, slip op. at 12–13 n.5.

### Statutory purpose cannot replace the specified elements
ATF invoked the presumption against ineffectiveness: courts should resist a construction that permits easy evasion of a law's evident object. The majority recognized the principle but rejected its application. Excluding these devices does not make the statute useless, because it still regulates conventional machineguns. Congress may draw a line narrower than every conceivable formulation of a law's purpose. Thomas, slip op. at 17–19.

The majority also pointed to ATF's own earlier classifications as inconsistent with the claim that exclusion would defeat the statutory scheme. It did not convert those classifications into binding law or hold that the agency was estopped from changing course. They supported its response to the purposive argument. Id.

The majority also invoked the rule that an express statutory definition ordinarily controls even if it differs from ordinary usage. Its footnote 9 therefore answers the dissent's broader ordinary-meaning argument through the enacted definition, rather than treating the familiar label “machinegun” as dispositive. Id. at 19 n.9.

The disagreement consequently involves both the meaning of the operative language and the permissible role of purpose. A broader concern about dangerous firing speed does not itself establish that Congress selected speed as the statutory criterion.

## Alito's concurrence
Alito joined the majority in full. He acknowledged that the Congress enacting the definition likely would not have viewed the devices as materially different from conventional machineguns in lethal effect. Nevertheless, he considered the statutory language clear and binding. A later tragedy may strengthen the case for amending a law without changing what the existing law means. Alito, slip op. at 1.

He therefore identified legislation as the remedy for the disparity. That observation is a separate concurrence's institutional point, not a condition attached to the Court's judgment. It also prevents an inaccurate description of the majority coalition as necessarily approving the devices as a matter of public policy.

## Sotomayor's dissent, joined by Kagan and Jackson
### The ordinary phrase concerns the shooter's initiating action
Sotomayor read a single function of the trigger as a single act initiating the firing sequence. The relevant question was what the shooter must do to generate continuous fire, rather than how many times internal components move during the sequence. On that reading, the accessory converts the firearm into a machinegun because one initiating act, followed by sustained pressure, produces repeated shots. Sotomayor, slip op. at 6–10.

She disputed the majority's claim to ordinary meaning. Dictionary definitions of function and trigger, in her view, describe the force or mechanism set in action by the shooter and do not compel the majority's component-by-component approach. The fact that the Court needed a detailed mechanical explanation did not itself make that explanation the ordinary legal meaning of the phrase. Id. at 7–8.

### Contemporary usage and precedent
The dissent relied on evidence from the 1934 congressional hearings and reports using trigger function and trigger pull in connection with the shooter's action. It characterized these materials as evidence of contemporary usage, not merely a general statement of legislative policy. That distinction matters in a textualist dispute: historical legislative materials can be offered to establish how words were used even where a court rejects free-ranging reconstruction of subjective intentions. Id. at 8–10 & n.2.

She also relied on the Court's description of machineguns and semiautomatic firearms in Staples. The dissent regarded the prior distinction between repeated fire from one pull and one shot from each pull as consistent with its reading. Id. at 9–10.

### Automatic operation need not eliminate continuing pressure
Sotomayor emphasized that ordinary machineguns do not fire without continuing human participation. Sustained pressure can coexist with automatic fire. The accessory externalizes a process that other weapons perform internally, but she found no textual basis for making that difference dispositive. Id. at 10–14.

She distinguished assisted firing from unassisted bump firing: the device controls the direction and distance of recoil and provides a place for a stationary finger, replacing coordination the shooter otherwise must supply. Her footnotes 3–4 accordingly challenge the majority's suggestion that the same account must classify an unmodified rifle identically and explain why her trigger-function reading makes those mechanical differences relevant to both statutory requirements. Id. at 11–13 & nn.3–4.

She also argued that the majority shifted perspectives: it focused on the internal mechanism to count trigger functions, then on the shooter's conduct to reject automatic operation. In her account, a consistent functional reading would treat the continuing pressure in both settings similarly. Id.

### Anti-evasion and the reach of the definition
The dissent viewed the presumption against ineffectiveness as a reason to reject a reading that permits devices to reproduce the regulated firing capability through a different mechanism. Congress included conversion parts and broadly framed the definition to avoid easy circumvention. She discussed ATF's classifications of other devices as evidence that the law addresses functional conversion rather than one traditional design. Id. at 14–17 & nn.6–7.

Those discussions do not constitute holdings approving every other classification. Cargill did not challenge those devices in this case. The dissent's claim was that the majority's approach would make regulation vulnerable to evasion and produce harmful consequences. It would have upheld the challenged classification rather than affirmed the Fifth Circuit. Her concluding comparison with an M16 and her warning about the consequences appear on page 18, after the discussion of other devices; neither is an additional holding. Id. at 17–18.

## Study synthesis and limits
The case is useful for distinguishing text, mechanism, function, purpose, and institutional authority. The majority and dissent both claim to apply the statute, but identify the legally relevant action differently. Their disagreement about the presumption against ineffectiveness follows from, and reinforces, that disagreement over the text.

State precisely what the Court decided: the statutory definition excludes the devices before it for the two reasons given; the judgment is an affirmance; the constitutional right to possess them is not adjudicated; mechanical spring-operated devices are expressly outside the case; and the Supreme Court's reasoning should not be replaced with the en banc court's lenity analysis. The brief records the 2024 decision, not the current status of all federal or state firearms regulation.
