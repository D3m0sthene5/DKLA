# Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons

**470 U.S. 373 (1985) · Supreme Court of the United States · March 4, 1985**

Justice O’Connor wrote for six Justices, joined by Justices Brennan, White, Marshall, Powell, and Rehnquist. Chief Justice Burger concurred in the judgment. Justices Blackmun and Stevens did not participate. All seven participating Justices agreed to reverse and remand; Burger disagreed with aspects of the majority’s guidance. This brief concerns the Supreme Court preclusion stage, distinct from the earlier circuit discovery decisions. The assigned account is Note 2 on CivPro PDF 690, printed 1371, with additional editorial comparisons continuing onto printed 1372.

## Facts and successive proceedings

Drs. Treister and Marrese were board-certified orthopaedic surgeons whose applications to join the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons were denied without a hearing or explanation. Treister sued in an Illinois circuit court in November 1976, alleging interference with associational rights protected by Illinois common law. Marrese filed a similar action separately. Neither alleged a state antitrust violation, and neither simultaneously brought a federal antitrust case. The Illinois Appellate Court held that Treister’s complaint failed to state a cause of action; the Illinois Supreme Court declined review. Following that appellate decision, the circuit court dismissed Marrese’s complaint as well. 470 U.S. at 375.

In March 1980, the doctors filed a federal action in the Northern District of Illinois. Their amended complaint alleged that the Academy possessed monopoly power and excluded them to discourage competition, constituting a boycott forbidden by §1 of the Sherman Act. These were allegations; the Supreme Court did not determine that the Academy possessed monopoly power or committed an unlawful boycott. The Academy moved to dismiss on claim-preclusion grounds because the state actions involved the same events and ended in dismissals with prejudice. The district court denied the motion, reasoning that state courts lacked jurisdiction over federal antitrust claims and therefore their judgments could not bar those claims. Id. at 376.

Discovery produced a second controversy. The Academy refused access to membership-application files despite a court order, and the district court entered a criminal-contempt judgment. The Seventh Circuit’s first panel decision reversed the contempt judgment, criticized the discovery order, and suggested both preclusion and lack of antitrust merit. After further appellate proceedings, the court sat en banc and held the federal claims barred. No opinion commanded a majority on the claim-preclusion reasoning. A plurality stressed the availability of a materially similar Illinois antitrust cause of action; another judge favored requiring the doctors to bring their entire dispute within a reasonable time, either through concurrent actions or a federal action with pendent state claims. The contempt judgment was also reversed on differing explanations concerning the discovery order. Id. at 376–378.

## Appellate jurisdiction and the scope of Supreme Court review

The Supreme Court examined jurisdiction even though the parties had not raised that objection before it. The district court initially declined to certify its denial of the dismissal motion under §1292(b). While the Academy’s appeal from criminal contempt was pending, however, the district court amended the order to certify the preclusion question. The Seventh Circuit accepted interlocutory review and consolidated the appeals. Id. at 378.

An appeal generally transfers control over the aspects involved in that appeal, but criminal contempt is a separate proceeding vindicating judicial authority. Its appeal did not transfer the entire underlying antitrust litigation to the court of appeals. The district court could therefore certify the dismissal question, and the Seventh Circuit properly exercised jurisdiction over that additional matter. Id. at 378–379.

The Supreme Court granted review only of the preclusion ruling. It expressly declined to address whether the discovery order was valid or whether the contempt judgment should be reversed. Reversal of the preclusion ruling must not be described as a substantive vindication of the doctors’ discovery demands. Id. at 378, 387.

## Holding: state law first, a statutory exception second

Under §1738, federal courts ordinarily give a state-court judgment the preclusive effect it receives under the rendering state’s law. The lower courts erred by determining the effect of these Illinois judgments through independent federal preclusion rules without first examining Illinois law. The exclusive federal jurisdiction over Sherman Act claims did not remove that first step. Id. at 379–382.

The Court prescribed a sequence. First, determine whether the rendering state’s preclusion law would bar the particular claim or issue. If it would not, there is no need to decide whether a federal statute creates an exception to §1738. Only if state law would produce a bar should the federal court consider an express or implied statutory exception. That second inquiry depends on congressional intent, the particular federal statute, and the nature of the claim or issue—not simply on a generalized preference for federal adjudication. Id. at 381–383, 386.

Exclusive jurisdiction does not make state judgments irrelevant. A state court may resolve an issue that becomes important in a later exclusively federal action. Issue preclusion may respect that determination even though the state court could not entertain the federal cause of action itself. Claim preclusion presents a different problem where the earlier tribunal lacked jurisdiction over the omitted theory or remedy. Most state rules recognize that jurisdictional limitation. But the federal court must examine the actual state rule and its application rather than replace it with a categorical federal answer. Id. at 380–383.

## Why efficiency did not justify stronger federal preclusion

The Seventh Circuit had suggested that federal courts could give the state judgments greater effect than Illinois itself would. Migra foreclosed that approach. If greater federal preclusion were independently permissible, there would have been no reason for Migra’s remand to determine the state rule. The Court refused to create a special antitrust rule that would treat an initial state-law action as a forfeiture of later federal rights when the rendering state did not impose that consequence. Id. at 384–386.

Efficiency and repose were legitimate interests, but they did not erase the parallel state and federal judicial systems or §1738’s comity interests. A rule steering all related litigation into the broadest forum might be attractive in a single judicial system. Congress instead directed respect for the scope states give their own judgments. Moitie did not establish a contrary rule because it addressed a prior federal judgment, not §1738’s command governing a state judgment. Id. at 385–386.

The majority’s notes preserve important limits. It did not decide whether Nash County’s similar outcome would remain correct under a properly performed state-law inquiry. It also distinguished rules preventing claim-splitting between limited and general courts within the same system from this case’s separate state and federal systems. A state-law cause of action analogous to a federal claim does not automatically supply jurisdiction over the federal claim itself. Id. at 382–384 nn.2–3.

## Burger’s concurrence

Burger agreed that §1738 required consultation of general state preclusion principles and that federal courts could not exceed a clear state limitation. He thought the majority gave insufficient guidance for indeterminate state law. Where a virtually identical state cause of action offered the same theory and remedy, he questioned whether the jurisdictional-competency principle necessarily saved the later federal claim. Id. at 387–389.

His notes distinguished narrow and broad conceptions of an issue that “could have been raised,” examined within-state claim-splitting examples and their limits, and distinguished the stronger state interest in defining substantive rights in diversity litigation. If Illinois law remained ambiguous, he favored consideration of a federal rule addressing whether the plaintiff had a full and fair opportunity to pursue the equivalent rights. He regarded that rule as protecting efficient federal adjudication and avoiding uncertainty, and would have resolved the question immediately. Those proposals did not command the Court. Id. at 389–391 & nn.1–4.

## Disposition and assigned significance

The Court reversed the Seventh Circuit’s judgment and remanded. It left application of Illinois preclusion law to the district court in the first instance. It did not determine that Illinois law barred the doctors’ claims, decide whether the Sherman Act creates a §1738 exception, or adjudicate their antitrust allegations. The discovery and contempt issues remained outside the grant of review. Id. at 386–387.

The case’s central lesson is methodological: identify the state judgment’s actual state-law effect before deciding whether federal law requires a departure.
