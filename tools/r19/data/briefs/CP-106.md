Parties
Walter Peterson, receiver of the Interstate Coal Company, was the plaintiff in the underlying action for the
unpaid balance on coal deliveries. Arthur Sidney Davison was the defendant and counterclaimant. Peterson
petitioned the Supreme Court for mandamus or prohibition against District Judge Augustus N. Hand after Hand
appointed an auditor at Davison’s request and over Peterson’s objection. Hand was the respondent because the
requested relief would control the district court’s management of the case, not because the judge owed the
alleged coal debt. 253 U.S. at 303–305.
Material Facts
Peterson sought $21,014.43 for coal sold and delivered. Davison substantially admitted the listed transactions
but asserted that Peterson’s schedule was incomplete: it omitted additional deliveries, payments, and
allowances. Davison also counterclaimed for $9,999.10 for failure to perform coal contracts. The competing
accounts were extensive. Peterson’s account contained 298 items; Davison’s contained 402, involving 123
deliveries on ninety-one days over eleven months. Prices varied, and the accounts raised penalties,
commissions, cash discounts, freight allowances, and uncredited payments. These figures explain why a
preliminary accounting could materially clarify the issues for trial. Id. at 304–307.
Judge Hand appointed an auditor to hear witnesses, examine the accounts, and report which items were
conceded and which disputed. The auditor could also express opinions on contested deductions and the net
amounts due. The order expressly left final determination of all factual issues to the jury. It did not specify the
report’s evidentiary effect, so the Supreme Court analyzed the appointment on the assumption that a report
accepted by the court would be admissible as prima facie evidence of its factual statements and conclusions. Id.
at 304–307.
The order provided that the auditor’s and stenographer’s expenses could be charged to either or both parties as
the trial judge determined. Appointment, admissibility, and payment were therefore distinct questions. An
auditor who only organized accounts could still require substantial compensation, and authority to obtain that
assistance did not necessarily entail discretion to allocate its cost without regard to the outcome. Id. at 304,
314–319.

Procedural History
After the appointment, Peterson sought extraordinary relief from the Supreme Court to prohibit proceedings
before the auditor and require restoration of the action to the ordinary trial calendar. The Court granted leave to
file the petition and issued an order to show cause on January 12, 1920. The underlying accounts had not yet
been finally adjudicated, and the proceeding was not an appeal from a jury verdict or a final money judgment.
Id. at 305.
The respondent objected that the district court had jurisdiction over the parties and action and that Peterson
could preserve objections for review after judgment. The Court nevertheless considered the asserted
deprivation of jury trial immediately: if the required preliminary process itself unlawfully displaced that right,
forcing the parties through it could create avoidable difficulty and expense. After addressing the merits, the
Court denied the requested writs. It distinguished the fundamental objection to the auditor’s appointment from
a correctable error in the provision for costs. Id. at 305–306, 319.
Issue
May a federal district court, without the parties’ consent or an express authorizing statute, appoint an auditor to
simplify complicated accounts and give tentative opinions admissible as prima facie evidence while reserving
final factfinding to the jury? May the court tax the resulting expenses as costs, including against a prevailing
party at the judge’s discretion, and does an error in allocating those costs warrant mandamus or prohibition? Id.
at 306–319.
Rule and Holding
In the absence of contrary legislation, a federal trial court has inherent authority to obtain appropriate
assistance necessary to perform its judicial duties. A compulsory preliminary reference that clarifies issues and
produces tentative findings can be consistent with the Seventh Amendment when the jury retains final
determination and the parties remain free to present, examine, and cross-examine witnesses. Giving the report
prima facie evidentiary effect does not itself transfer ultimate factfinding to the auditor. The complex accounts
justified the appointment here. Id. at 306–314.
The auditor’s and stenographer’s court-ordered expenses could be taxed as costs under the law and practice
then applicable. But the provision permitting assessment against either party at the judge’s unrestricted
discretion was erroneous: the prevailing party in a federal action at law ordinarily had a right to the entire
taxable costs. That error could be addressed through the district court or ordinary review and did not justify
either extraordinary writ. Id. at 314–319.
Judgment
The Supreme Court denied the petition for mandamus or prohibition. The auditor’s appointment and prescribed
duties remained authorized, notwithstanding the identified error in the expense-allocation clause. Peterson
could request modification of that clause or seek review after final judgment. No amount due on the coal
accounts was decided, and denial of the writs did not approve every term of the district court’s order. Id. at 319.
Reasoning
The Court first separated power from discretionary necessity. The question presented was whether this kind of
nonconsensual assistance could lawfully be used at all. If it could, the hundreds of accounting entries, changing
prices, and disputed allowances amply justified the district court’s conclusion that an auditor was necessary for
intelligent consideration. The auditor could isolate genuine disagreements and identify the detailed facts
supporting each charge, thereby reducing the matters the jury actually needed to resolve. Id. at 306–307.

An auditor’s assessment of conflicting testimony did not necessarily make the appointment an unconstitutional
substitute for trial. A preliminary hearing could expose mistakes, misunderstandings, and abandoned positions
before the jury phase. Although the report might suffice as evidence unless challenged, its conclusions
remained tentative. The order reserved ultimate factual decisions to the jury, and the parties retained the ability
to introduce their own proof and challenge the opposing evidence. Those retained rights were central to the
constitutional analysis. Id. at 307, 310–312.
The Court acknowledged that this specific form of auditor was not an established feature of English or colonial
jury practice before the Constitution. Earlier officials called auditors had different functions, while the closest
model developed in Massachusetts after 1818. That historical absence did not end the inquiry. The Seventh
Amendment preserves the jury-trial right without freezing every rule of pleading, evidence, or trial preparation.
New procedures can clarify the issues and make jury adjudication effective, provided they do not obstruct the
right or interfere with the jury’s ultimate factual authority. Id. at 307–310.
The report’s assumed evidentiary effect was also compatible with that allocation. Rules may give an official
finding a rebuttable evidentiary status without conclusively binding a jury. Here the auditor was an officer of
the appointing court, proceedings remained under its supervision, and the report could be used only to the
extent the court accepted it. The Court therefore treated the device as regulated assistance to adjudication rather
than an independent adjudicator whose findings displaced trial. Id. at 310–312.
Constitutional permission was only one step. No act of Congress expressly authorized the appointment, so the
Court separately examined inherent authority. Courts had long appointed masters, examiners, and similar
assistants to organize complicated evidence in equity. The need to understand voluminous accounts and frame
the issues could also arise in an action at law. With no congressional prohibition, the institutional power to
acquire appropriate assistance did not depend on the historical division between law and equity. Id. at 312–314.
That reasoning had a substantive limit: a compulsory reference that finally determined the disputed facts would
interfere with the jury right. The order before the Court was permissible because it called for clarification and
tentative findings. The opinion thus supports supervised assistance that leaves judicial and jury responsibilities
intact, rather than a general power to assign the entire decision to someone else. Id. at 313–314.
The expense issue required a different analysis. Congress had provided no public funding for the auditor or
stenographer. The Court reviewed the historical practice of treating necessary, court-ordered litigation
expenses as taxable costs and distinguished those expenditures from expenses incurred merely for a party’s
convenience. Because no applicable statute or rule excluded these fees, they could be included in costs. This
conclusion made the lawful appointment practically workable without determining which party would
ultimately pay. Id. at 314–317.
The further authority to allocate those costs was narrower. In actions at law, federal practice entitled the
prevailing party to taxable costs as a rule, rather than giving the judge the flexibility characteristic of equity.
The order’s authorization to charge either or both parties departed from that rule. But an ordinary corrective
application or later review could address the clause; this error did not establish that the preliminary reference
itself had to be stopped by mandamus or prohibition. Id. at 317–319.
Separate Opinions
Justices McKenna, Pitney, and McReynolds are recorded as dissenting. The report supplies no written
explanation of their disagreement. Accordingly, it would be unsupported to assign them a particular objection
to the Seventh Amendment analysis, inherent power, costs, or the extraordinary-writ disposition. Id. at 319.

Notes
Distinguish four propositions: authority to appoint assistance, protection of the jury’s final role, admissibility
of a tentative report, and allocation of the expense. Peterson approved the appointment and analyzed limited
evidentiary use while finding the cost-allocation clause erroneous. Treating the case as an unqualified approval
of an auditor’s authority would erase that structure. Id. at 306–319.
The original casebook excerpt substantially compresses the factual setting and omits the final section
explaining the error in charging costs. The full opinion is necessary to understand why the petition was denied
despite that error. The ruling predates the Federal Rules and should not be read as a substitute for examining
later rules governing masters and costs; no current-rule compliance determination is made in this brief. Id. at
317–319; casebook pp. 1004–1006.
