# Howard v. Kerr Glass Manufacturing Co.

**Citation:** 699 F.2d 330 (6th Cir. 1983). **Court/date:** Sixth Circuit, February 9, 1983; argued October 21, 1982. **Opinion:** Per curiam, panel of Krupansky, Wellford, and Senior Judge Brown. **Course:** Civil Procedure—pretrial orders and amendment. **Assigned location:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 511, printed page 1001, Note 2.

## Facts and procedural posture

Howard, a Detroit beverage-plant employee, injured his right hand when a bottle or bottles he was handling exploded. He sued Kerr in diversity, alleging negligent manufacture and failure to inspect. Identification of Kerr as the manufacturer and proof of proximate cause depended essentially on circumstantial evidence. (331–332.)

The parties’ joint pretrial document included implied warranty of merchantability and foreseeability as factual issues, and implied warranty of fitness among Howard’s claims and theories. Counsel assembled and discussed it with the court at a January 1981 conference. Howard did not then amend his complaint. At the jury trial about six weeks later, the judge prohibited the warranty theory. After the first trial day, Howard sought leave to amend, which was denied. The jury rejected negligence, and Howard appealed. (331–333 & n.1.)

A significant complication was that the judge had not signed the joint document at the conference. Nevertheless, both parties and the court knew the warranty theory, the judge treated the document as part of the record, and he later signed it. This was not a case of a previously undisclosed claim suddenly appearing at trial. (332–333 & nn.2–3.)

## Issues and holdings

**Pretrial issue:** Should Howard have been permitted to proceed on the disclosed implied-warranty theory despite omitting it from the original complaint? **Yes.** The pretrial procedure and order, or alternatively leave to amend, should have allowed that theory. (333.)

**Other issues:** Could Howard obtain appellate relief on the rejected negligence theory? **No.** His failure to seek judgment notwithstanding the verdict under Rule 50(b) prevented appellate consideration of his directed-verdict challenge. The other trial rulings did not justify reopening negligence. (332.)

## Reasoning

Issues identified at the pretrial conference and incorporated into its order supersede the pleadings. Rule 16’s purposes include considering desirable amendments. Here, Kerr had ample notice, could not claim surprise, and should have prepared for warranty. Howard received no adverse indication at the conference that warranty would be excluded. Later signature implied approval that the issue had been presented; the court discussed a possible nunc-pro-tunc analogy rather than announcing that every unsigned proposal automatically binds a judge. Pretrial orders should be construed liberally, as should amendments where the adversary suffers no surprise. (332–333.)

The remaining rulings independently explain the narrow remand. The judge limited questioning of the plant’s quality-control manager about Kerr’s earlier visits, but interrogatory answers establishing visits had already been read to the jury. Howard could argue foreseeability, and the restriction did not seriously impair negligence. The court did not definitively decide that the evidentiary ruling was correct; it held that any error did not require remand. (332.)

Kerr’s unused expert was neither its employee nor under its direction or control. Howard failed to show that the testimony necessarily bore on manufacturer identification, the thin-wall theory, or plant procedures at the time of injury. His own listed expert also did not testify. On these circumstances, refusing a requested adverse-inference instruction was not error. (332.)

## Exact disposition

**Remand solely to let Howard proceed on the asserted breach-of-warranty theory.** Negligence issues were expressly foreclosed. The appellate court did not find warranty liability or award damages, and it did not reopen the entire trial. (333–334.)

## Textbook connection and limits

The assigned note accurately uses Howard to show that a pretrial order can prevail over the original pleading. The full opinion adds the unsigned-at-conference complication, subsequent signature, absence of surprise, and the warranty-only remand. Its Rule 50 discussion uses historical directed-verdict/JNOV terminology. The adjacent McLean note concerns exclusion of an omitted defense issue and is a separate holding.
