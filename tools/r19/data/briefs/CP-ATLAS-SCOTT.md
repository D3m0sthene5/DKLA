# Scott v. Harris

**550 U.S. 372 (2007) · Supreme Court of the United States · April 30, 2007**  
**Civil Procedure · Summary judgment, video evidence, and Fourth Amendment reasonableness**

## Facts and the competing accounts

In March 2001, a Georgia deputy recorded Harris driving 73 miles per hour in a 55-mile-per-hour zone. When the deputy activated his lights, Harris fled rather than stopping. Deputy Timothy Scott joined the pursuit after hearing radio communications. The chase reached speeds above 85 miles per hour and continued for approximately six minutes and nearly ten miles. In a shopping-center parking lot, Harris evaded police vehicles trying to contain him, collided with Scott’s car, and returned to the highway. 374–375.

Scott sought permission to use a Precision Intervention Technique to spin Harris’s vehicle to a stop. His supervisor authorized intervention. Scott instead applied his push bumper to the rear of Harris’s car; he said the speed made the planned technique unsafe. Harris lost control, left the road, overturned, and suffered injuries that left him quadriplegic. Whether Scott had authorization for the precise maneuver was irrelevant to the Court’s constitutional analysis. 375 & n.1.

The chase had been recorded. Harris’s account, accepted for summary-judgment purposes by the lower courts, portrayed speeding but controlled driving, with little actual threat to other drivers or pedestrians and a largely clear roadway when Scott intervened. The Supreme Court majority instead described the video as showing an extraordinarily dangerous chase: movement around numerous vehicles, crossing the center line, red-light violations, and substantial risks to motorists and police. Justice Stevens believed the same recordings supported the lower courts’ account. The factual dispute was therefore not simply whether a video existed, but what conclusions a reasonable factfinder could draw from it. 378–380; 389–393.

## Procedure and the issues

Harris sued Scott and others under 42 U.S.C. §1983. The claim before the Supreme Court alleged excessive force in an unreasonable seizure under the Fourth Amendment. Other claims against Scott or other defendants were outside the Court’s review. The district court denied Scott’s motion for summary judgment based on qualified immunity, finding material disputes. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed on interlocutory appeal, reasoning that a jury could find an unconstitutional use of deadly force and that the unlawfulness was sufficiently established to defeat immunity. 375–376 & nn.2–3.

The Court addressed two connected questions. First, did Rule 56 require accepting Harris’s version despite the video? Second, on the facts the Court regarded as established, was Scott’s force objectively reasonable? Applying the qualified-immunity sequence then prescribed by Saucier, the Court decided the constitutional question first. Footnote 4 acknowledged criticism of that sequence but reserved reconsideration because the majority considered the constitutional issue here readily decidable. That historical procedural discussion should not be mistaken for a fresh statement about today’s required sequence. 377–378 & n.4.

## The video and summary judgment

Justice Scalia’s eight-Justice opinion reaffirmed the ordinary rule: evidence and reasonable inferences favor the nonmovant at summary judgment. But that protection applies to a genuine dispute, not every verbal disagreement. A version so plainly contradicted by the record that no reasonable jury could believe it need not be adopted. The Court noted that no party alleged alteration of the recording or disputed that it depicted the actual events. 378–381.

For the majority, the video conclusively defeated the proposition that Harris’s driving posed little danger. The whole record could not support a rational verdict based on that proposition, so the lower courts should have used the factual picture supplied by the recording. This was not announced as a general license to disregard a plaintiff whenever a defendant supplies video. The demanding premise was that the relevant account was untenable for any reasonable jury. 380–381.

The opinion also preserved an important qualification: the video confirmed that no other vehicle or pedestrian was threatened at the precise instant Scott rammed Harris. The majority treated the ongoing pursuit’s broader danger as decisive, rather than asserting that every lower-court factual statement was false. Once the supportable facts and inferences had been determined, it treated constitutional reasonableness as a legal question. 380 n.7; 381 n.8.

## Fourth Amendment holding

Scott conceded that deliberately terminating Harris’s movement by striking his vehicle was a seizure. The governing question was therefore whether the manner of seizure was objectively reasonable. Harris argued that the intervention was deadly force and that Tennessee v. Garner required specified preconditions, including necessity and a warning. The Court rejected using the deadly-force label as a mechanical trigger for a rigid test. Garner concerned shooting an unarmed person fleeing on foot who did not pose the danger present in this chase. Here, it was the flight itself that threatened serious harm, regardless of the relatively minor offense that initially prompted pursuit. 381–383 & n.9.

The Court balanced the serious risk Scott’s maneuver posed to Harris against the imminent danger the majority found to motorists, possible pedestrians, and pursuing officers. It considered both the number of lives endangered and the parties’ relative responsibility: Harris intentionally created the dangerous flight, while potential victims had not. This culpability assessment concerned the reasonableness of the seizure; it was not a reason to deny that a seizure had occurred. 383–385 & n.10.

The majority also rejected a constitutional obligation to end the pursuit. Withdrawal would not reliably communicate that Harris could stop fleeing, and the Court declined to impose a rule under which especially reckless driving secured escape. Footnote 11 characterized this as uncertainty about what withdrawal would accomplish, not a factual finding that Harris necessarily would continue driving dangerously. The Court held Scott’s intervention reasonable in this dangerous pursuit and concluded that he was entitled to summary judgment because there was no Fourth Amendment violation. 385–386 & n.11.

## Ginsburg and Breyer’s concurrences

Justice Ginsburg joined the Court but stressed that the decision was not a mechanical rule authorizing force in every fast chase. Reasonableness remained sensitive to the danger actually posed and to safer means available under the circumstances. She also regarded reconsideration of Saucier as unnecessary in a case where the constitutional question warranted an answer. 386–387.

Justice Breyer likewise joined, explaining that watching the video had changed his assessment. He proposed freeing lower courts from Saucier’s mandatory sequence: deciding constitutional issues unnecessarily could waste resources, produce poorly developed precedent, or insulate constitutional rulings from review when immunity nevertheless protected the defendant. That proposal was not adopted in Scott. Breyer also expressly resisted reading the majority’s concluding formulation as an absolute rule, agreeing with Ginsburg that additional circumstances could matter. 387–389.

## Stevens’s dissent

Justice Stevens would have allowed a jury to decide. He read the recording as showing controlled passing rather than the threatening near-collisions inferred by the majority. Other vehicles might have moved aside because of police sirens and lights; red-light conditions and traffic risks were less conclusive than the majority suggested. The shopping center was closed, the late-night roads lacked pedestrians, and police had blocked intersections. Stevens acknowledged that flight merited punishment, but denied that these circumstances established the necessity of potentially lethal force as a matter of law. 389–393 & nn.1–4.

The dissent particularly challenged the treatment of alternatives. Police had Harris’s plate number and might have arrested him later; ending pursuit, warning him, or using stop sticks could have avoided the catastrophic intervention. Stevens considered the majority’s speculation about continued dangerous flight inconsistent with favorable inferences for Harris. He regarded the disagreement among judges who had seen the recordings as powerful evidence that reasonable jurors could also disagree. 393–397 & nn.5–9.

The disagreement thus encompassed both the evidentiary threshold and constitutional evaluation. The majority regarded the supported factual record as fixing an extreme danger and leaving a legal reasonableness question. Stevens believed danger, necessity, and the adequacy of alternatives remained disputes properly assessed by a jury. His position was a dissent, not the governing disposition. 381 n.8; 395–397.

## Judgment and assigned scope

The Court **reversed the Eleventh Circuit, 8–1**, and held Scott entitled to summary judgment on the Fourth Amendment claim before it. Scalia wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer, and Alito. Ginsburg and Breyer separately concurred while joining the Court; Stevens alone dissented. The judgment rested on the absence of a constitutional violation, rather than a separate determination that an established violation was excused by qualified immunity. 373; 377–378; 386.
