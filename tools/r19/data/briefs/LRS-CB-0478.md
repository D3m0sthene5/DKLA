Citation
Perry v. Sindermann, 408 U.S. 593 (1972). Supreme Court of the United States; argued January 18, 1972;
decided June 29, 1972. Justice Stewart wrote for five Justices. Chief Justice Burger concurred in an opinion
covering Perry and Board of Regents v. Roth. Justice Brennan, joined by Justice Douglas, and Justice Marshall
each dissented in part because they would direct additional procedural relief; they agreed with the First
Amendment analysis. Justice Powell did not participate. Assigned excerpt: casebook pp. 478–482, October
second PDF pp. 49–53. The full report supplies the omitted separate opinions, summary-judgment record, and

Parties
Robert Sindermann was a professor at Odessa Junior College in Texas and the plaintiff challenging the failure
to renew his annual contract. The defendants and Supreme Court petitioners were members of the college’s
Board of Regents and its president. Sindermann alleged retaliation for public criticism and denial of procedural
due process; the officials denied retaliatory motivation and asserted insubordination and the absence of a duty
to provide a hearing. These competing accounts had not been resolved by factfinding. 408 U.S. at 594–596 &
nn.1–3.
The Supreme Court considered two distinct constitutional routes. A First Amendment claim attacked the
reason for denying another contract; a procedural claim asserted an entitlement to continued employment that
required a hearing before nonretention. The absence of formal tenure had different significance for those two
routes. Id. at 596–599.
Material Facts
Sindermann taught within the Texas state college system from 1959 through 1969: two years at the University
of Texas, four at San Antonio Junior College, and four at Odessa Junior College. Beginning in 1965, Odessa
employed him under successive one-year contracts to teach Government and Social Science. He served for a
time as a department cochairman. His long public teaching service and repeated appointments supplied context
for the alleged institutional assurances, although service alone did not establish tenure. Id. at 594, 600–602.
During 1968–1969, Sindermann became president of the Texas Junior College Teachers Association, testified
before legislative committees, and publicly opposed positions taken by the Regents. He supported making
Odessa a four-year institution, while the Regents opposed that change. A newspaper advertisement bearing his
name criticized them. The officials alleged that some legislative appearances occurred despite instructions not
to leave his classes, creating a disputed distinction between protected criticism and asserted insubordinate
conduct. Id. at 594–596 & nn.1–2.
His contract ended in May 1969, and the Regents voted not to renew it. They issued a press release alleging
insubordination but provided no official statement of reasons for nonrenewal and no opportunity to contest its
basis at a hearing. Thus the record included public allegations, while still lacking the official explanation and
responsive procedure Sindermann requested. Id. at 595 & n.1.
Odessa had no formal tenure system, but its Faculty Guide stated that the administration wished faculty to feel
they had permanent tenure while their teaching was satisfactory, their attitude toward colleagues and superiors
cooperative, and they were happy in their work. Sindermann claimed legitimate reliance on this provision and

Procedural History
Sindermann sued in federal district court. The officials moved for summary judgment, relying on expiration of
the contract and the absence of a formal tenure system. The record consisted of pleadings and three short
affidavits from Sindermann substantially repeating his allegations; the officials submitted no affidavits. The
district court nevertheless entered summary judgment against him, concluding that expiration and lack of
formal tenure left him without a cause of action. Id. at 595–596 & nn.2–4.
The Fifth Circuit reversed and remanded. It held that nonrenewal could violate constitutional speech
protections despite lack of tenure and that the disputed reason required a full hearing in court. It also directed
examination of whether Sindermann had an expectancy of continued employment supporting a procedural
claim. The Supreme Court affirmed that remand while rejecting any suggestion that a subjective expectancy
alone was sufficient. Id. at 596, 603.
The judgment therefore reopened factual and legal questions rather than conclusively establishing either
retaliation or tenure. The Court allowed Sindermann to attempt proof under the correct rules. The partial
dissenters agreed that his speech claim should proceed but would also have directed summary judgment
entitling him to reasons and a hearing, instead of requiring further proof of a protected employment interest. Id.
at 598–603, 604–605.
Issue
Does the absence of a contractual or tenure right defeat a claim that public employment was denied because of
protected speech? Separately, can institutional policies and practices establish a property interest in continued
employment despite the absence of a formal tenure clause, and had Sindermann alleged enough to require
further proceedings rather than summary judgment against him? Id. at 596–603.
Rule and Holding
Government may have discretion to deny a benefit for many reasons while remaining prohibited from denying
it on a basis that infringes protected speech. A public employee therefore need not first prove an entitlement to
reemployment to challenge retaliatory nonrenewal. Sindermann’s allegations presented a genuine
constitutional dispute about the actual reason, which lack of tenure did not resolve. Id. at 596–598.
For procedural due process, a property interest must rest on rules or mutually explicit understandings
supporting a legitimate claim of entitlement. Those understandings can arise from institutional words, conduct,
and practices as recognized by state law; they need not appear in a formal tenure clause. A subjective
expectation is insufficient. Sindermann was entitled to attempt to prove the alleged de facto tenure
arrangement. The Court did not hold that he had established one. Id. at 599–603 & n.7.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the Fifth Circuit’s judgment remanding to the district court for further
proceedings, while qualifying its expectancy language. Sindermann could litigate the disputed First
Amendment motive and prove, if possible, a legitimate entitlement under the institution’s policies and
practices. Proof of the latter would require officials to provide a requested hearing on the grounds for
nonretention, but would not itself entitle him to reinstatement. No final damages award, tenure declaration, or
reinstatement order was entered by the Supreme Court. Id. at 598, 602–603.

Reasoning
The district court’s premise conflated discretion over a benefit with freedom to use constitutionally forbidden
reasons. Stewart explained that government cannot use withholding of a benefit to impose a penalty on
protected speech that it could not impose directly. The Court had applied that principle to several kinds of
benefits and repeatedly to public employment, including nonrenewal of nontenured teachers’ annual contracts.
Accordingly, Sindermann’s lack of a guaranteed future job did not answer his speech claim. Id. at 596–598.
That principle still required proof of an unconstitutional basis. The Court did not find that every public
criticism by an employee is protected in every setting or that the Regents’ asserted insubordination was
necessarily a pretext. It identified Sindermann’s legislative testimony and criticism of college policy as
presenting a bona fide constitutional claim, in light of precedent recognizing potential protection for teachers’
speech on public concerns. Because the actual reason was disputed and undeveloped, summary judgment based
only on lack of tenure was improper. Id. at 595–598 & nn.1–3.
The procedural claim required a separate inquiry into protected liberty or property. As in Roth, failure to retain
a person in one job did not by itself establish a deprivation of occupational liberty or a property right to
continued employment. Nor did asserting retaliatory treatment automatically create a right to an administrative
hearing on that assertion. The Court focused instead on whether Sindermann’s alleged institutional assurances
could support an employment entitlement. Id. at 599 & n.5.
The Faculty Guide was material because it combined a disclaimer of a formal tenure system with language
suggesting continued security while specified conditions were satisfied. Sindermann alleged that the
administration had promulgated and fostered reliance on that understanding. The Court did not resolve the
apparent tension by treating the first sentence as conclusive or by automatically converting the remaining
language into tenure. It held that the alleged practices deserved examination under the law governing the
relationship. Id. at 599–601.
The statewide guidelines provided another asserted source. They contemplated a probationary period generally
not exceeding seven years of appropriate full-time service, subject to stated qualifications, and associated
tenure with continued employment absent adequate cause. Sindermann relied on his decade in the state system.
The opinion did not establish that the guidelines automatically bound Odessa or that every year necessarily
counted in his favor; their legal operation and the legitimacy of reliance remained part of what he offered to
prove. Id. at 600–601 & n.6.
The Court used ordinary contract concepts to explain why a formal writing need not exhaust the possible
understanding. Parties’ conduct, surrounding circumstances, and past usage can help give content to a
relationship or imply additional assurances. A university might develop an institutional common law under
which long-serving faculty had the equivalent of tenure, particularly where it used annual contracts without a
formal tenure system even for senior faculty. This was a permissible route to proof, not a federal rule making
repeated annual contracts permanently renewable. Id. at 601–602.
State law remained essential. The Court expressly declined to hold that Sindermann possessed a legitimate
claim, explaining that his property theory would fail if Texas law afforded a teacher in his position no
contractual or other tenure claim. The federal Constitution protects an established entitlement through due
process; it does not independently supply every term that the claimant wishes the employment relationship
contained. Id. at 602 n.7.
The Court accordingly narrowed the Fifth Circuit’s vocabulary. An expectancy based solely on the teacher’s
personal hopes was not protected. The relevant showing was an objectively supported entitlement under

Finally, proof of property and proof of unjustified nonrenewal were different matters. Establishing tenure-like

could challenge their sufficiency. The college might still demonstrate adequate cause. The Court’s explicit
refusal to make reinstatement automatic preserved that difference between entitlement to a fair decision
procedure and entitlement to a favorable substantive decision. Id. at 603.
Separate Opinions
Chief Justice Burger joined the judgments and opinions in Perry and Roth but stressed the state-law source of
any contractual entitlement. He suggested that when the relevant state contract law was uncertain, federal
courts should abstain from deciding the constitutional hearing question until state courts resolved the
underlying entitlement issue. This was an additional view about handling future cases, not an order in Perry
requiring Sindermann to begin a separate state proceeding before the remand could occur. Id. at 603–604.
Justice Brennan, joined by Douglas, agreed with Part I’s holding that lack of tenure did not defeat the speech
claim. Adopting Marshall’s broader due process theory, he would also recognize an immediate right to reasons
and an opportunity to respond, and would modify the Fifth Circuit judgment to direct that relief. In Roth he
would affirm the professor’s favorable partial summary judgment; in Perry he would give the professor more
than an opportunity to prove an independent entitlement. Id. at 604.
Justice Marshall likewise agreed that the First Amendment claim required full consideration, but dissented
from the limited procedural disposition. For the reasons in his Roth dissent, he would direct the district court to
enter summary judgment establishing Sindermann’s right to an explanation and a hearing on disputed facts. His
underlying view was that governmental employment decisions themselves must be reasoned and nonarbitrary,
with procedure protecting that interest even absent a separately proved tenure promise. Id. at 605; Roth, 408
U.S. at 587–592. Justice Powell did not participate.
Notes
Perry and Roth apply the same entitlement concept to different records and opposite summary-judgment
positions. Roth had received judgment requiring process despite no established renewal entitlement or liberty
deprivation. Sindermann had lost without an opportunity to prove allegedly binding institutional
understandings. The Supreme Court reversed the former judgment and sustained reversal of the latter; these
results do not establish that every nontenured professor either always has or never has a property interest.
Perry, 408 U.S. at 599–603; Roth, 408 U.S. at 578–579.
Three propositions must remain separate: protected speech can prohibit a motive regardless of entitlement; an
independent rule or understanding may create an entitlement requiring procedure; and success in establishing a
hearing right does not determine the ultimate validity of nonrenewal. On an examination, the Faculty Guide,
institutional practice, governing state law, disputed reasons, and precise requested remedy belong to different
steps of that analysis. Perry, 408 U.S. at 596–603.
The report’s account of a press release should not be rewritten as an official hearing or as a Supreme Court
holding that a liberty claim had already succeeded. Likewise, the de facto tenure allegation is not a finding that
Sindermann was tenured. Those are significant limits produced by the sparse summary-judgment record and
the Court’s remand. Id. at 595–596, 599–603.
