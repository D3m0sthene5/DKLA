# United States v. Brand Jewelers, Inc.

**318 F. Supp. 1293 (S.D.N.Y. 1970) — October 8, 1970. Civil Procedure.** District Judge Frankel issued the opinion. Assigned account: CivPro, physical page 159, printed pages 270–271, the Note on “Sewer” Service. No separate writing is reported. This is the 1970 ruling denying dismissal, not the later settlement reported by the textbook.

## Alleged scheme and requested remedies

The United States sued Brand Jewelers, its president and sole shareholder Howard Star, its attorney Sol H. Erstein, and corporate and individual process-serving defendants. The complaint alleged a systematic practice of obtaining default judgments against economically disadvantaged customers without proper service. Brand sold watches, jewelry, and other goods through street, workplace, and door-to-door sales, with small installments and little or no down payment. According to the complaint, thousands of collection suits were filed annually in New York City Civil Court, and more than ninety percent produced defaults. Process servers allegedly understood that proper service was neither expected nor desired, failed to deliver process or prepared false affidavits, and supplied those affidavits for entry of judgments later enforced through wage garnishment and other means. [1293–1294, Part I.]

These were allegations accepted for the motion to dismiss. Frankel expressly cautioned that they were not established facts. The government also alleged burdens on interstate commerce and federal bankruptcy policy, improper military-status affidavits, tax and mail consequences, and interference with federal efforts addressing poverty and other public problems. The court ultimately relied on two grounds, rather than adopting every asserted federal interest. [1294–1295.]

Invoking the district court’s jurisdiction over civil actions commenced by the United States under 28 U.S.C. §1345, the government sought an injunction, invalidation of improperly obtained defaults, an accounting, notice to affected debtors, and an opportunity for those debtors to obtain restitution or compensatory or punitive damages and related relief upon establishing entitlement. Section 1345’s jurisdictional text, reproduced in note 1, did not itself settle the distinct issue whether the government could pursue this nonstatutory enforcement suit. [1294–1295; n.1.]

## Issue and defendants’ objections

The central question was whether the United States could maintain the civil action without a statute expressly authorizing this particular suit. Defendants characterized the asserted power as excessive, distinguished the commerce precedents, and argued that Congress’s express grants of enforcement authority in other settings implied exclusion here. They also requested a more definite statement and striking of parts of the complaint. The case reached the court under Rule 12, before an evidentiary determination of the alleged scheme or its effects. [1295–1300.]

## First ground: protecting interstate commerce

Frankel began with In re Debs, which allowed the United States to seek judicial relief against obstruction of interstate rail transportation and the mails. Debs did not confine standing to a governmental proprietary loss. Its broader reasoning permitted the government to seek assistance concerning public wrongs affecting matters constitutionally entrusted to national authority. The Brand court examined that reasoning and later applications involving racial obstruction of interstate travel, navigable waters, railroad agreements, national parks, and military policies. The significance was an asserted public, national responsibility, rather than the government’s ownership of a claim or a private creditor’s demand. [1295–1297.]

The distinction from ordinary private controversy remained important. Note 2 emphasizes a prior limitation: government litigation genuinely brought only for a third party’s benefit, without a public obligation, would not qualify merely because the government filed it. Frankel treated the alleged scale and public effect of the sewer-service scheme as meeting that limitation here. Note 3 candidly identifies the cited Judge Wisdom reasoning as in substantial part his individual view, rather than treating every sentence as a panel holding. [1296–1297; nn.2–3.]

The complaint alleged a substantial burden on commerce through widespread garnishment. Congress’s Consumer Credit Protection Act finding, reproduced in note 4, linked garnishment with job loss and disruptions in employment, production, and consumption. Whether the government could ultimately demonstrate the alleged effect remained open. At the pleading stage, however, the court accepted the substantial-burden allegation and rejected defendants’ attempt to treat it as presumptively false simply because they labeled their challenge one to standing. [1298; n.4.]

Defendants’ proposed distinctions between physical and nonphysical obstruction, or direct and indirect effects, did not justify dismissal. The court understood the precedents as protecting national responsibilities against substantial public interference, not solely against violence or railroad blockades. It acknowledged that an evidentiary record might reveal effects too attenuated or fanciful to suffice. The allegations before it nonetheless described a broad public harm capable of bringing national powers and duties into play. [1298–1299.]

Frankel also considered the danger of placing the federal government’s resources against private defendants. Notes 5 and 6 identify the defense memoranda expressing that concern. He did not deny its importance, but reasoned that Congress could control executive abuses and that the court would evaluate the propriety, scope, and merits of requested relief. Allowing the government to litigate was not the same as allowing it to grant itself every requested remedy. [1299; nn.5–6.]

## Alternative ground: widespread state-action deprivation

The court independently held that the United States could pursue widespread property deprivations without due process through state action. The alleged conduct of licensed process servers and persons authenticating service through official seals and notarial authority amounted to state action in the relevant sense. The alternative theory therefore did not treat every private credit dispute as a constitutional violation: it addressed alleged use of the state-backed service and judgment machinery to deprive many people of property. [1299–1300.]

Frankel acknowledged that the government’s nonstatutory authority in this setting was an open question. Note 7 expressly lists contrary opinions. He nevertheless saw no persuasive constitutional distinction between protecting against large-scale commerce burdens and protecting against large-scale due-process denials. Congress’s failure to provide this exact authorization did not, in his view, establish an affirmative prohibition, particularly where both Congress and courts retained authority to constrain the executive. This was an alternative district-court holding amid acknowledged disagreement, not a declaration that controlling authority had already settled every such federal enforcement action. [1299–1300; n.7.]

## Exact order and limits

The court **denied the motions to dismiss and for other relief in all respects**. Requests to clarify or strike allegations were denied without prejudice to similar contentions during discovery or pretrial proceedings. With the government’s acquiescence, Frankel also recognized a controlling legal question with substantial grounds for disagreement and grounds for interlocutory appeal under §1292(b), subject to the Court of Appeals’ discretion. The opinion itself did not report that an appellate court accepted that appeal or resolved the issue. [1300, Part III.]

This order did not enter an injunction, vacate the collection judgments, award restitution, or establish the allegations after trial. The assigned textbook separately reports a 1972 settlement and consent decree addressing judgments from 1969–1971, notice, merits hearings, and attorney responsibility for future service. That is assigned editorial aftermath, not relief ordered by the fully read 1970 opinion. A complete primary copy of the 1972 decree was not recovered in this author interval; its detailed terms are not independently certified here.
