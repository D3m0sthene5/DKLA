# Mitchell v. Federal Intermediate Credit Bank

**Civil Procedure · CP-139 · 165 S.C. 457, 164 S.E. 136 (1932) · May 5, 1932**

Mitchell holds that a litigant who knowingly uses part of an indivisible claim to defeat an opponent’s suit cannot reserve the remainder for a later affirmative action. Mitchell won the earlier federal action on his notes, but that success did not preserve a second recovery for the excess crop proceeds involved in the same transaction. The South Carolina Supreme Court affirmed the plea barring his accounting action.

Mitchell, a Beaufort County farmer, alleged that arrangements with the bank, South Carolina Agricultural Credit Company, and W. E. Richardson required him to borrow through the credit company and market his crop through a growers’ cooperative, assigning the proceeds as security. He borrowed $6,000 in November 1925 and $3,000 in January 1926, giving two notes and crop mortgages. He alleged that his vegetables yielded at least $18,000 and that one or more defendants received those proceeds without accounting to him. The bank denied the alleged agency relationship and said that it purchased his notes and mortgages for value before maturity. The credit company and Richardson did not appear, were insolvent and in default, and could be disregarded for this appeal. These allegations did not establish a new damages amount in this state proceeding. (165 S.C. at 459–60.)

The bank had sued Mitchell on the notes in federal court in August 1926. Mitchell contested jurisdiction and pleaded the crop-proceeds facts as a complete defense but requested no affirmative judgment by counterclaim. After jurisdiction was resolved for the bank, a January 1930 trial ended in a verdict for Mitchell, affirmed on appeal. His state accounting suit, begun in October 1926, had been held in abeyance by agreement during the federal litigation. When it resumed, the bank obtained permission to plead the completed federal judgment in bar. The state judge separately tried that defense on the federal record, sustained it, and refused to treat Mitchell’s notice of appeal from the preliminary trial order as a stay. (Id. at 460–62.)

Justice Stabler first rejected the procedural challenge. The plea presented legal questions whose resolution could dispose of the whole action, making an immediate separate hearing sensible within the judge’s statutory discretion. An automatic stay would undermine that useful sequencing. Mitchell still could challenge the order on appeal from final judgment, so his objection was preserved rather than lost. (Id. at 462–63.)

On preclusion, Mitchell admitted that both actions concerned the same transaction. The crop proceeds allegedly owed him had offset the bank’s notes; he now sought only the surplus beyond that offset. He argued that pleading facts as a defense did not require him to counterclaim for affirmative recovery and that success in the first action permitted an independent suit for the excess. The court instead asked whether one claim could be divided between defense in the first action and offense in the second. (Id. at 463–64.)

The governing principle required the holder of an entire, indivisible demand to present its whole scope rather than litigate it piecemeal. It applied to the use of a claim as a setoff or defense as well as to successive plaintiff’s suits. Mitchell’s alleged legal injury was the failure to account for approximately $18,000 in crop proceeds, one demand against the bank. When the bank sued for approximately $9,000, he could invoke his demand defensively or seek affirmative judgment for the balance. Once he chose the defensive use and obtained the benefit of defeating the notes, he could not later enforce the remainder of the same demand. The absence of a prior award for the excess was a consequence of the relief he had chosen to request, not a basis for a second action. (Id. at 464–66, 480.)

The court considered Mitchell’s authorities at length. In Kirven, the earlier defendant withdrew the defense concerning fertilizer quality and later sought damages for injury caused by the fertilizer. The Mitchell court distinguished that situation from facts knowingly pleaded and used in the first case. It discussed Greenwood Drug’s later refusal to allow a tort suit based on issues involved in earlier contract litigation and distinguished Cannon’s reliance on Kirven. Ludlow involved uncertain prior pleadings and possible innocent lack of knowledge about an overpayment; it did not establish that a party could knowingly plead a demand defensively and later sue on the same facts. The opinion recognized that the rule requires claims available within the first action and discussed exceptions such as a court’s own limitation of recovery or lack of knowledge. It found no such basis here. (Id. at 465–70.)

Cases from other jurisdictions reinforced that conclusion. O’Connor involved defective construction used first to defeat the contract price and then asserted for affirmative damages. Watkins concerned the portion of a failed conveyance claim exceeding the note already defeated. Other decisions applied the same principle to successful setoffs. The court also explained that inconsistent factual positions after losing the first case provide an additional preclusion reason, but are not necessary to the rule against splitting. Winning on the defense does not create an exception. (Id. at 470–79.)

The circuit-court judgment was **affirmed**. Stabler’s opinion was joined by Chief Justice Blease, Justices Carter and Bonham, and acting Associate Justice Featherstone; the complete report contains no separate writing or judicial footnotes. The assigned excerpt, printed pages 1279–1281, focuses on the defense-to-affirmative-claim problem and omits the procedural ruling and many comparisons. Its later Lucky Brand discussion is editorial treatment of another decision, not part of Mitchell. The holding here should not be restated as a universal modern rule that every unused counterclaim is barred: the case addresses a known indivisible demand actually employed to defeat the earlier suit. No unchecked current-law conclusion is supplied.
