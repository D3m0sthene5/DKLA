# Alexander v. Kramer Bros. Freight Lines, Inc.
273 F.2d 373 (2d Cir. 1959) | Civil Procedure

## Accident, contested testimony, and trial

Two tractor-trailer trucks collided on the Pennsylvania Turnpike near Somerset at about six in the morning on October 19, 1951, amid spotty fog. One truck belonged to the corporate plaintiff and was driven by Alexander, who sustained serious injuries; the corporate plaintiff's truck was badly damaged. Kramer Bros. owned the other truck. It denied negligence and alleged contributory negligence. The drivers were the only eyewitnesses, and their accounts directly conflicted. Alexander said the defendant's truck entered from a right-side ramp in fog that prevented him from avoiding it. The defendant's driver, Holman, said he had already been traveling west with Alexander following before Alexander struck him from behind. Negligence and contributory negligence therefore depended on credibility. 273 F.2d at 374.

In this diversity action, a jury returned a verdict for the plaintiffs. The defendant appealed alleged errors in the instructions and an evidentiary ruling. The central assigned issue concerned the burden of establishing freedom from contributory negligence and whether counsel preserved the instruction error.

## Erroneous instruction and preservation

Near the close of the plaintiffs' case, Judge Inch stated in a colloquy that the defendant bore the burden of proving contributory negligence. Plaintiffs' counsel agreed; defense counsel took an exception, which the judge acknowledged. The defendant then presented its evidence. Several days later, however, defense counsel neither requested a correct burden instruction nor objected to the actual charge on that subject, although counsel submitted numerous requests on other matters. Id. at 374–375.

The panel held that the burden instruction was wrong under the law applicable at that time. Klaxon required the federal diversity court to use New York's conflict rules. Those rules selected Pennsylvania law for substantive tort issues but New York law for procedural issues; New York required plaintiffs in a nonfatal-injury action to prove freedom from contributory negligence. The casebook replaces much of that historical choice-of-law discussion with an editorial reference to applicable law. The complete opinion makes clear which law the court applied and why. Id. at 374–375 & n.2.

The mistake nevertheless did not justify reversal. The then-existing Rule 51 required a distinct objection to an instruction before the jury retired, stating both the matter and grounds. The purpose was to give the trial judge a meaningful chance to reconsider and correct the charge. An exception to an informal statement days before the charge, when no instruction ruling was before the court, did not fulfill that purpose or excuse failure to object to the actual instructions. The defense also never supplied the authorities that might have persuaded Judge Inch to correct his earlier view. Id. at 375 & nn.1, 3.

The panel distinguished cases concerning the specificity of an objection actually made at the charge and cases involving preserved evidentiary rulings. It also distinguished Keen, where additional preserved error justified reversal despite acquiescence in the charge. Alexander was not an exceptional case warranting appellate correction of an unpreserved instruction error. Id. at 375 & n.4. The holding preserves the difference between an erroneous instruction and an error sufficiently preserved to support reversal.

## Additional issues resolved in the complete opinion

The defendant requested categorical instructions based on Pennsylvania highway speed restrictions and then asked the judge to read the statute when those requests were refused. The judge did read it and had already explained that contributory negligence would bar recovery. The requested language was not wholly accurate and too dogmatic in telling the jury it must find for the defendant. A judge need not adopt counsel's particular formulation where the jury receives sufficient law. Id. at 375.

The defendant also sought to rehabilitate Holman after cross-examination by admitting his earlier consistent statements. The proposed rule allowed consistent statements made before a motive to fabricate arose. Holman, as the defendant's driver whose own fault was at issue, already had an interest in explaining the collision favorably when he made those statements. The court contrasted a cited case where the consistent statement concerned ownership before the accident and thus preceded any accident-related motive. Footnote 5 further notes that Holman's excluded statements were neither marked for identification nor otherwise included in the record. The rehabilitation rule did not require admission here. Id. at 375–376 & n.5.

## Disposition, court, and assigned limits

The Second Circuit affirmed the plaintiffs' judgment. Swan wrote for the panel with Lumbard and First Circuit Judge Magruder sitting by designation; no separate opinion appears. The court did not rule the burden instruction correct, retry the conflicting eyewitness accounts, or decide every earlier objection inadequate for all later proceedings.

The assigned excerpt occupies PDF page 55, printed pages 50–51, and concentrates on the Rule 51 preservation issue. The complete opinion, including its designation note and all five numbered judicial notes, was read before drafting and adds the speed-instruction and rehabilitation rulings. The negligence burden, New York conflict characterization, and procedural formulations are historical features of this 1959 decision. Its durable lesson is that counsel should present a concrete, timely instruction objection that gives the trial judge a chance to correct the actual charge; its treatment should not be used as the verbatim current Rule 51 or a statement of current comparative-fault law.
