# Wirtz v. Baldor Electric Co.
337 F.2d 518 (D.C. Cir. 1963), supplemental opinion after remand (June 29, 1964) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Two stages of the same appeal

The reported decision contains a December 31, 1963 opinion and a June 29, 1964 supplemental opinion after remand. Judge Washington wrote the first opinion for a panel consisting of Chief Judge Bazelon, Washington, and Wright; the supplement is per curiam. No dissent or separate concurrence appears in either stage. The first opinion found substantive-evidence and procedural defects but remanded the record to determine plaintiffs’ standing, retained appellate jurisdiction, and kept the existing stay in force. The supplement accepted standing for five companies, rejected proposed rehabilitation of the wage determination, and ordered it struck down, subject to a further stay allowing Supreme Court review. A brief stopping at the first opinion would miss the completed standing inquiry and final remedial order. (518, 520, 535–537.)

## Governing statute and formal hearing requirements

The Walsh-Healey Public Contracts Act required specified government contracts to include minimum wages determined by the Labor Secretary to prevail for comparable work in the relevant industry and locality. After Perkins v. Lukens Steel had denied prospective contractors litigable rights under the earlier statute, Congress’s 1952 Fulbright Amendment made the APA applicable and required wage determinations on the record after an opportunity for a hearing. It also authorized review for persons adversely affected or aggrieved, expressly including qualifying manufacturers and dealers. (520–521 & nn.1–4.)

The then-numbered APA § 7(c) placed the burden on the proponent of a rule or order, required reliable, probative, and substantial evidence, and protected rebuttal and the cross-examination needed for full and true disclosure of the facts. The distinction between rulemaking and adjudication did not eliminate these safeguards because Congress expressly required on-the-record rulemaking. The court applied those statutory requirements rather than declaring that all informal rulemaking must use trial procedures. (521–522 & n.4.)

## Survey, hearing, and the Secretary’s determination

The Department studied two branches of the electric-motor and generator industry: fractional equipment below one horsepower and integral equipment of one horsepower or more. BLS sent questionnaires to 775 establishments, principally identified through confidential state unemployment-insurance lists. The survey sought covered-worker and wage data for the payroll period nearest October 15, 1960. Its pledge restricted access to sworn Bureau employees and promised not to disclose information identified by company name. Six wage tables ultimately summarized 216 firms: 212 responding firms and estimates for four nonrespondents. (522 & nn.5–8.)

The manufacturers’ association, NEMA, received the tables about five weeks before the hearing. It obtained copies of responses from seventy-two establishments and comparative information from sixty-one. Eight days before the hearing, it reported discrepancies; BLS accepted two that reduced its initial estimates. The day before the hearing, NEMA sought a subpoena for the completed questionnaires, lists of recipients and respondents, and related correspondence. It had also offered confidential treatment of information inspected by counsel. The examiner denied access because of the confidentiality pledge and the claimed effect of disclosure on future voluntary surveys. (522–523 & n. 9; 530 n. 20.)

At the hearing, BLS’s supervisor could not answer some questions without consulting the underlying responses, but government counsel successfully objected to that consultation. NEMA sought to offer company affidavits identifying errors and to strike the tables and testimony. The strike request failed; the affidavits were eventually admitted by agreement and produced further revisions. Secretary Goldberg sustained the procedural rulings and found the revised survey reliable, reasoning that established errors had been corrected and that the confidentiality assurance was essential to the Bureau’s work. Neither he nor the examiner consulted the underlying responses. The determination set $1.45 and $1.70 prevailing hourly wages for the two branches, increased by three cents to account for intervening wage changes. (523–525 & nn.10–11.)

## December 1963: disclosure and meaningful testing

The court accepted that statistical tables could be admitted. Admission did not, however, remove the need for access to underlying material necessary to test authenticity, accuracy, and meaning through rebuttal and cross-examination. Legislative materials and the cases discussed allowed convenient summaries subject to adequate opportunities to test them. It was not necessary to place every underlying document into the record when adequate access had already resolved objections. Here access had been denied even under NEMA’s offered confidentiality arrangement. (525–527 & nn.12–14.)

The Secretary’s concern about future information collection could be substantial, but it did not authorize disregard of the statutory hearing safeguards. The court rejected the argument that lack of a vested right to government contracts answered this statutory issue. It also distinguished cases without the same cross-examination right or involving other privilege circumstances. The court nevertheless declined to compel production of BLS’s confidential files in this type of case. Government could withhold information and risk failing to prove its position, or disclose it and permit testing. Thus, the holding should not be recast as an unconditional judicial order to publish every confidential survey response. (527–529 & nn.15–18.)

## December 1963: the unrehabilitated evidence

The procedural defect was accompanied by serious impeachment of the evidence itself. Eight accepted corrections affected a meaningful share of the covered workers and reduced estimated prevailing rates by three and four cents in the respective branches. The evidence also suggested that the covered-worker definition could have been widely misunderstood; government witnesses conceded omitted categories and possible erroneous exclusions. The Secretary supplied no adequate corroboration to counter that challenge. The tables, standing alone, therefore failed the statutory substantial-evidence requirement. (529–530 & nn.19–22.)

The court did not decide that every confidential survey was unusable. It reserved what could follow without impeachment, with rehabilitating evidence, or with different disclosure, and disclaimed any effect on other industries’ determinations. Because of its evidentiary holding, it did not decide whether disclosure would violate the Federal Reports Act. These limits matter when comparing the decision with a general criticism of formal rulemaking. (530–531 & nn.23–24.)

## Standing and the conditional industrywide remedy

The Fulbright Amendment did not confer standing on every industry member without actual adversity. It displaced Perkins’s denial of litigable rights but still required injury. Some plaintiffs allegedly paid at or above the prescribed minimum; speculative future competition for labor did not establish injury, and their immediate competitive position could improve if rivals had to raise wages. Other plaintiffs alleged lower rates and appeared prima facie injured, even if they had not previously obtained government contracts. Because the Secretary disputed the facts, the District Court had to identify which plaintiffs had standing. (531–533 & nn.25–33.)

The court left the Rule 23 dispute unresolved. If at least one plaintiff had standing, it concluded that the entire industry determination should be enjoined. Relief confined to suing firms would give them an inequitable competitive advantage in government bidding and undermine the uniform industrywide wage rule. The court relied on the nature of this quasi-legislative action, statutory review authority, and the consequences of partial relief. It did not announce identical remedial scope for every administrative dispute. The December mandate returned the record for standing findings, retained appellate jurisdiction, and continued the stay. Note 36 also allowed the Secretary to propose record supplementation through the District Court. (533–535 & nn.34–36.)

## June 1964: standing established and supplementation rejected

On remand, affidavits showed that Baldor, Holtzer-Cabot, Westinghouse, Louis Allis, and Northwestern Electric belonged to the covered industry, had obtained or sought government contracts, and actually paid one or more covered workers less than the prescribed minimum. The appellate court accepted that these five faced direct and immediate financial injury, whether through higher wage costs or withdrawal from government contracting. A small increase would not make injury speculative. It reserved the situation where only unused hiring or starting rates fell below the minimum. (535–536 & supplemental nn.1–2.)

The Secretary’s proposals, reproduced in the appendix, sought new plant visits and 1964 wage and job-duty information using a clarified definition of covered workers, while retaining confidentiality and releasing tabulations and descriptions without identifying plants. The first proposal would preserve the existing determination if the new rates were at least as high, or revise rates downward if necessary. The alternative contemplated an entirely new survey and formal hearing while appellate jurisdiction continued. (537–538, Appendix.)

The court rejected both as rehabilitation of the existing order. The Secretary conceded that records and recollections no longer permitted reliable checking of the 1960 survey. Wage information from 1964 could not prove what wages prevailed in 1960, and current rates had been affected by the disputed order itself. Moreover, the proposed disclosure still offered no meaningful opportunity to test the confidential sources in light of the identified weaknesses. The extent of disclosure needed could vary with the strength of the evidence and its survival under impeachment, but no modicum of disclosure was inadequate here. The Secretary could initiate a lawful new determination, not keep this invalid one in force merely until a replacement was ready. (536–537.)

## Final order and assigned scope

The supplemental decision struck down the determination and continued the injunction’s stay for sixty days to permit seeking Supreme Court review. If review was sought, the stay would continue until the Court acted; denial would make the injunction effective immediately. New proceedings consistent with the APA remained permissible. The source does not establish what later review occurred, and this brief does not add a subsequent disposition from assumption. (536–537.)
