# Vines v. Orchard Hills, Inc.
181 Conn. 501, 435 A.2d 1022 (1980) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural result
Justice Peters wrote for the Connecticut Supreme Court on July 15, 1980; Chief Justice Cotter and Justices Bogdanski, Healey, and Parskey concurred. A nonwillfully defaulting land purchaser could pursue restitution of a payment unjustly retained by the seller, but the purchasers had not proved that entitlement here. A contract permitting retention of a 10% down payment as liquidated damages was presumptively reasonable, subject to rebuttal. The court set aside the refund judgment and remanded for further proceedings rather than entering a final judgment conclusively permitting the seller to keep the money. (181 Conn. 501–502, 509–514.)

## Purchase, default, and the refund judgment
On July 11, 1973, Euel and Etta Vines contracted to purchase a New Canaan condominium from Orchard Hills for $78,800. They paid $7,880, exactly 10%, by the time they signed. The default clause provided that if the purchasers failed to perform while the seller performed its obligations, the seller would retain all payments as liquidated damages and the parties' rights and liabilities would end. The buyers later declined to take title because Euel's employer transferred him to New Jersey; they notified the seller in a January 4, 1974 letter. They alleged neither seller nonperformance nor a legal excuse for their own failure. Their breach precipitated the restitution action. (Id. at 502–503 & n. 1.)

The trial court overruled the seller's demurrer, then awarded the buyers $7,880 plus interest after a hearing. Evidence showed that the condominium's fair market value had risen to $160,000 by the 1979 trial. The trial court treated the increase of approximately $80,000 as a windfall justifying repayment. Neither side supplied the property's value at the time of breach or evidence of the seller's breach-related damages. On appeal, the seller challenged the pleading ruling, nonenforcement of the liquidated-damages clause, and use of the trial-date valuation. (Id. at 502–504.)

## A restitution claim despite the purchasers' breach
The court rejected the argument that default alone made the complaint legally insufficient. Whether the seller was unjustly enriched, notwithstanding the agreed damages clause, presented factual issues beyond the pleading's legal sufficiency. The court therefore sustained the initial refusal to dismiss, while separately examining the evidentiary basis for the buyers' judgment. (Id. at 504.)

Older decisions had often denied recovery to a party whose own breach generated the dispute. Peters explained why restitution could nevertheless be appropriate: a categorical bar punished a purchaser who partially performed more severely than one who paid nothing, even though partial performance usually caused the innocent party no greater injury. Contract damages ordinarily compensate the injured party's loss of its bargain. Retention exceeding that loss can create an unjust enrichment, and anti-forfeiture principles elsewhere in contract and property law supported examination of that result. (Id. at 505–509.)

The court did not unreservedly adopt an economic model encouraging breach whenever the breaching party's gain exceeded the other's loss. That model assumed confidence that the injured party could establish and recover its losses; judicial administration might leave it uncompensated. Restitution was legal in form but equitable in nature, allowing the court to balance avoidance of forfeiture, incentives to perform, the character of the breach, and protection of the innocent party. An early Connecticut case allowing repayment after mutual rescission was not directly controlling, but its opposition to forfeiture helped support recognition of the claim. The announced holding covered a purchaser whose breach was not willful. No one alleged that the Vines' employment-transfer default was willful. (Id. at 506–510.)

## The buyers' burden and the agreed 10% damages
Permission to sue did not establish entitlement to repayment. The defaulting purchasers had to show more than termination of the contract and the seller's possession of the payment: they ordinarily had to prove that the seller's damages were less than the money received. The burden of showing a net gain rested with them even if the seller could strategically choose to introduce evidence of its losses. (Id. at 510.)

The contract also supplied an agreed measure of damages. Connecticut recognized that private liquidated-damages agreements could properly replace a judicial damages calculation, although an otherwise valid clause would not be enforced when a particular breach caused no damage. Here the clause was a defense against defaulting buyers' restitution claim. Unlike an injured party affirmatively suing to enforce such a clause, these buyers bore the burden of proving invalidity or unenforceability. Loss of the bargain, brokerage expenses, and less quantifiable costs of holding property beyond the contemplated sale could all matter. A 10% earnest-money allocation was presumptively reasonable in this setting. (Id. at 511–513; n. 2 reproduces the tentative Restatement provision, and n. 3 identifies an analogy to a presumptive 10% cutoff in another commercial context.)

That presumption could be rebutted. The buyers could demonstrate fraud, mistake, or unconscionability, or show that the actual breach caused no damage or damages substantially below the agreed amount. Their nonwillful default and the seller's status as a condominium developer did not themselves displace the clause, although those circumstances could bear on the ultimate unjust-enrichment inquiry. (Id. at 513–514.)

## Why the trial evidence failed, and what the remand allowed
The trial court used the wrong valuation date. The seller's damages had to be measured at the time of breach; subsequent market appreciation properly accrued to the seller. Evidence that the condominium was worth $160,000 in 1979 did not show that the seller suffered no loss when the buyers failed to close years earlier. The record therefore could not sustain the refund. (Id. at 513–514.)

Because Connecticut had not previously clearly stated the availability and limits of this restitution claim, the court afforded the buyers another opportunity to prove it. They might establish that resale at the time of breach could have produced enough to cover lost profits and incidental and consequential damages, or produce evidence of unconscionability or excuse that avoided the clause. Those were possible avenues of proof, not findings that the buyers had already established them. The judgment was set aside and the matter remanded for further proceedings. (Id. at 514.)
