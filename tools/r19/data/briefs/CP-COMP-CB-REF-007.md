# American Institute of Certified Public Accountants v. Affinity Card, Inc.
8 F. Supp. 2d 372 (S.D.N.Y. 1998) | Civil Procedure

## Court, decision, and assignment

District Judge Scheindlin decided this motion on July 16, 1998, in the Southern District of New York. The issue was whether service on an employee of another corporation could support the default judgment against Affinity Card. The complete judicial reproduction contains no judicial footnotes or separate writing. The assigned account appears in Note 1 after Hellenic Challenger, Civil Procedure PDF page 157, printed page 265. It emphasizes that actual notice does not inevitably cure defective service. The full opinion adds that vacatur was conditioned on Affinity's agreement to accept new service through its attorney. [Complete reported opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp2/8/372/2576638/).

## Contract allegations and the attempted delivery

AICPA, Affinity, and Marine Midland Bank entered a contract on November 5, 1992, establishing a Visa program marketed to AICPA members. Marine Midland issued the cards, Affinity assisted with promotion, and AICPA and Affinity shared participant fees. AICPA alleged that Affinity withheld fee information and failed to make required payments. Its March 24, 1998 complaint asserted breach of contract; this service ruling did not decide whether those allegations were true. 8 F. Supp. 2d at 374–375.

On March 30, process server Peter Murphy delivered the summons and complaint to Patrick McDonald at the shared Wellesley, Massachusetts office suite where Affinity conducted business. The affidavit of service identified McDonald as Affinity's assistant vice president. He actually held that title at Primecard Corporation and did not work for Affinity. Four companies shared the suite and reception area; Greg Miller was president, treasurer, and at least a partial owner of all four. At 374–375.

The parties disagreed about the exchange. Murphy said he asked for Miller, explained that he had important legal papers for Affinity and Miller, and asked who could accept them. According to Murphy, McDonald agreed to deliver them to Miller that afternoon, viewed the documents, and described himself as an assistant vice president. Murphy assumed that the title referred to Affinity. McDonald said Murphy asked for Miller but did not identify the papers, show them, or ask whether he could accept service. In his account, Murphy handed him a sealed envelope after hearing his title. McDonald put it in Miller's mailbox that day. At 374–375.

Affinity failed to answer or move in time, and a default judgment was entered on May 11. It then moved under Rules 55(c) and 60(b)(4), arguing that ineffective service deprived the court of personal jurisdiction and rendered that judgment void. At 374–375.

## A void judgment and the treatment of conflicting accounts

The court distinguished this motion from discretionary relief for excusable neglect or other ordinary grounds under Rule 60(b). A judgment entered without personal jurisdiction was a nullity; relief from such a void judgment was mandatory, and the moving party did not need to establish a meritorious defense to the contract claim. Valid service was a prerequisite to exercising personal jurisdiction. At 375.

That did not eliminate factual questions about what happened. Invoking the circuit's preference for decisions on the merits rather than defaults, the court said equally plausible conflicting accounts should be resolved in favor of the party seeking vacatur when the record supplied no basis to choose between them. It thus credited McDonald's version, rather than finding that he affirmatively represented authority to accept service for Affinity. This was the court's approach to this default-vacatur record, not a universal rule that every disputed service affidavit must be rejected. At 375–376.

The federal rules offered three possible routes: service under Rule 4(h)(1), or service authorized by the law of New York, where the district court sat, or Massachusetts, where delivery occurred. The court considered each. At 375–378.

## Federal service: an officer of a different company is not Affinity's agent

Rule 4(h)(1) allowed delivery to an officer, managing or general agent, or another authorized agent. The plaintiff had to supply a basis for inferring authority to accept process. Corporate job labels were not conclusive: service could reach a representative sufficiently integrated into an organization to know what to do with the papers, even without a specified title. But that functional approach still required a meaningful relationship with the defendant. At 376.

McDonald had worked for Primecard only a few months. He had no Affinity responsibilities, had never heard of Affinity, and was not expressly authorized to accept its process. On the credited account, he did not hold himself out as its representative and learned the envelope contained legal documents only weeks later. The court therefore could not infer sufficient integration or service authority. At 376.

Hellenic Challenger was different because its recipient was the defendant's well-integrated employee familiar with receiving summonses. The court also considered Solospar, where delivery to a different corporation's employee had survived a motion to dismiss, with renewed challenge permitted after factual development. The posture mattered: here a default judgment already existed, and uncertainty was resolved toward restoring an opportunity to litigate the merits. The earlier service decisions did not make every employee in a shared office an agent of every corporation located there. At 376–377.

## Actual receipt and the alter-ego argument

AICPA emphasized that Miller actually received the summons and complaint that same day. The court accepted actual receipt as important but held that it could not, by itself, cure ineffective service. Knowledge of the lawsuit and delivery to a legally appropriate recipient were distinct requirements. At 377.

AICPA alternatively argued that Primecard and Affinity were effectively Miller's alter egos, so service on one should reach the other. Applying New York choice-of-law principles, the court used Massachusetts corporate law. That law required substantially more than shared officers and office facilities: pervasive intercorporate control with injurious consequences, or confused intermingling and substantial disregard of separate corporate existence, together with the equitable justification for disregarding the entities. At 377.

The common officers, some shared employees, address, and telephone number did not meet that showing. Without further evidence of the necessary control or intermingling, the court respected the companies' separate identities. Thus Miller's relationship with both corporations did not convert McDonald into an Affinity officer or agent. At 377–378.

## Neither state-law service route cured the defect

New York's long-arm statute made Affinity amenable to service because it had contracted to provide services in New York. But amenability did not establish effective delivery. New York permitted reasonable reliance on an employee's representation of service authority; on the accepted account, McDonald was a nonemployee who made no such representation for Affinity. Service therefore failed under New York law. At 378.

Massachusetts likewise permitted delivery to an officer, managing or general agent, person in charge of the business at its principal office, or another authorized agent. The court described Massachusetts decisions as construing those categories restrictively. McDonald's lack of employment or authority defeated that route as well. At 378.

## Exact order and practical consequence

The court granted the motion to vacate the default judgment. It stated that it lacked jurisdiction over Affinity until proper service occurred. Recognizing AICPA's good-faith belief that it had served the company, however, the court conditioned vacatur on Affinity's agreement to accept service through its attorney. AICPA was to make that service within seven days, and a conference was scheduled for August 12. At 378.

The order removed the defective default and supplied a route toward proper service and merits litigation. It did not dismiss the contract allegations on their merits or vindicate Affinity's conduct in the card program. The assigned parenthetical accurately captures the shared-office and actual-notice problem; the complete opinion explains the credited facts, rejected alter-ego theory, three alternative service rules, and conditional remedy.
