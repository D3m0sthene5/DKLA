# Perttu v. Richards
605 U.S. 460 (2025) | Civil Procedure

## Facts and procedural posture

Michigan prisoner Kyle Richards and two other prisoners sued prison employee Thomas Perttu under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 in 2020. They alleged repeated sexual abuse and interference with their attempts to report it. According to their complaint, Perttu intercepted, ripped up, and discarded grievance forms, threatened to kill prisoners who filed more, and retaliated through administrative segregation and other measures. Richards’s First Amendment claim for damages included the alleged retaliation against his grievance activity. Whether Perttu had destroyed the forms and retaliated was therefore relevant both to that substantive claim and to whether administrative remedies had actually been available. The parties agreed that those issues were intertwined. The Supreme Court did not adjudicate the truth of the allegations. Majority slip opinion at 1–3.

Perttu sought summary judgment for failure to exhaust administrative remedies under the Prison Litigation Reform Act. A grievance coordinator’s affidavit reported no record of the relevant sexual-abuse grievances in 2019 or 2020. The plaintiffs answered that Perttu’s interference explained their inability to complete the grievance process. Finding a genuine factual dispute, a Magistrate Judge conducted a videoconference evidentiary hearing in November 2021. Richards, representing himself, questioned witnesses who said they had seen grievance destruction and retaliation; Perttu denied those acts. The Magistrate Judge discredited Richards’s witnesses because their testimony was substantially guided by his questioning or conclusory, and recommended dismissal without prejudice for nonexhaustion. The District Court adopted that recommendation. Majority slip opinion at 3–4.

Richards appealed and argued that resolving exhaustion without a jury impermissibly resolved facts belonging to the substantive case. After appointing counsel and obtaining further briefing, the Sixth Circuit reversed. It accepted its usual rule that judges could resolve disputed exhaustion facts, but held that the Seventh Amendment required a jury when resolving exhaustion also resolved a genuine material factual dispute on the merits. That exception conflicted with the Seventh Circuit’s approach in *Pavey v. Conley*. The Supreme Court granted review. Majority slip opinion at 4.

## Issue, holding, judgment, and vote

Who determines disputed exhaustion facts when those same facts bear on the merits of a claim entitled to a jury under the Seventh Amendment? Parties have a right to a jury determination of PLRA exhaustion in that intertwined setting. The Court reached that conclusion as a matter of statutory interpretation, applying ordinary federal adjudicatory practice against which Congress enacted the PLRA. It did not decide whether Congress could constitutionally require judges to resolve the dispute instead. Majority slip opinion at 5–6, 16–17.

The Court affirmed the Sixth Circuit, 5–4. Chief Justice Roberts’s majority was joined by Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, and Jackson. Justice Barrett dissented, joined by Justices Thomas, Alito, and Kavanaugh. Affirmance preserved Richards’s opportunity for jury adjudication; it did not establish exhaustion, find Perttu liable, or award damages. The Supreme Court’s statutory basis differs from the Sixth Circuit’s constitutional rationale.

## Exhaustion and the allocation of trial functions

The PLRA bars a prison-conditions action until available administrative remedies have been exhausted. Proper exhaustion normally requires compliance with the prison grievance system’s procedures. Under *Ross v. Blake*, interference or threats preventing use of an otherwise proper process can make that process unavailable, in which event the exhaustion requirement does not bar suit. Majority slip opinion at 2–3.

Exhaustion is an affirmative defense, rather than a jurisdictional requirement or an obligation that prisoners must specially plead and prove in their complaints. *Jones v. Bock* treats it under ordinary Federal Rules practice, and *Woodford v. Ngo* recognizes that a court may dismiss an obviously meritless claim without first resolving a more complex exhaustion dispute. The PLRA says nothing about assigning disputed exhaustion facts to judge or jury. Its introductory prohibition on bringing an action is standard language also used for defenses such as limitations, which can be determined by juries. Congress did not add nonexhaustion to the statute’s judicial screening provisions. The majority therefore treated statutory silence as evidence that Congress retained ordinary practice. Majority slip opinion at 7–8.

Richards argued both that intertwined exhaustion facts required a jury and, more broadly, that historical practice required juries for all disputed PLRA exhaustion facts. The majority did not decide the broader proposition. It first asked whether a fairly possible statutory interpretation would avoid the constitutional question. Finding one, it held only that intertwined exhaustion disputes should go to the jury when the underlying claim carries a Seventh Amendment jury right. Majority slip opinion at 5–6.

## Majority reasoning: protecting jury adjudication of common facts

The majority looked to the adjudicatory background existing when Congress enacted the PLRA in 1996. *Beacon Theatres v. Westover* required sequencing legal and equitable claims to preserve jury resolution of common factual issues wherever possible. In that antitrust litigation, trying the equitable matter first could have prevented full jury adjudication of the damages claim. *Dairy Queen v. Wood* similarly protected jury determination of factual issues common to legal and equitable relief for an alleged contractual breach. For the majority, these precedents demonstrate that judges must sometimes defer matters they ordinarily resolve themselves to preserve jury adjudication of intertwined merits facts. Here, both exhaustion and First Amendment liability depend on Perttu’s alleged interference with grievances. Majority slip opinion at 8–10.

Jurisdictional precedents supplied a further analogy. In *Smithers v. Smith*, a court assessing the amount in controversy improperly decided whether defendants had jointly taken the plaintiff’s land, an essential merits issue. *Land v. Dollar* involved whether disputed stock belonged to the United States, a fact bearing on both sovereign immunity and the substantive claim. Those cases required proceeding to merits determination rather than deciding the common factual question through a preliminary jurisdictional dismissal. The Sixth Circuit reasonably inferred that comparable protection should apply to a nonjurisdictional affirmative defense such as exhaustion. Majority slip opinion at 10–11.

Other pre-PLRA decisions and treatises extended the intertwinement principle to issues including personal jurisdiction, venue, choice of law, and forum non conveniens. The Court cited those applications as evidence of the adjudicatory background; it expressly declined to decide whether each extension was correct. Its holding thus concerns PLRA exhaustion and does not independently settle every threshold issue sharing facts with the merits. Majority slip opinion at 11–12.

## Responses to Perttu’s arguments

Perttu contended that *Beacon Theatres* concerned collateral estoppel, whereas judicial exhaustion findings would not bind a later jury. The majority questioned that premise. Lower-court statements denying preclusion had offered limited analysis, while treatises and the Restatement suggested that findings supporting a threshold dismissal could sometimes have effects in later litigation. Findings favorable to Richards might also implicate law of the case against Perttu. The Court did not conclusively decide those preclusion questions; it held that their possible operation supported protecting jury adjudication. Majority slip opinion at 12–13 & nn.3–4.

More fundamentally, the majority refused to limit *Beacon Theatres* to estoppel. Here a judge’s adverse exhaustion finding dismissed the entire suit and prevented a jury from ever being convened. Dismissal without prejudice did not necessarily supply a practical route back: grievance deadlines commonly last days, and those deadlines ordinarily have expired by the time dismissal occurs. Perttu offered neither a tolling argument nor evidence that discretionary acceptance of late grievances would generally solve the problem. Majority slip opinion at 14–15.

Perttu argued specifically that sexual-abuse grievances could still proceed under Michigan’s Prison Rape Elimination Act policy. Richards disputed whether that policy covered his separate First Amendment claim. The Court took no position on that disagreement or on the effect of Michigan’s later policy revision. It also noted that Perttu did not propose a different intertwinement rule for each case depending on whether this particular prisoner might eventually reach a jury. Majority slip opinion at 15 n.5.

Perttu finally invoked the PLRA’s goal of conserving resources and *Katchen v. Landy*, which allowed bankruptcy adjudication without a jury under a specific statutory scheme. The majority distinguished a specialized bankruptcy scheme contemplating prompt nonjury resolution from the PLRA, which contemplates jury resolution of the substantive damages claim and is silent about exhaustion. A general efficiency objective did not show that Congress displaced the usual practice governing intertwined facts. Majority slip opinion at 15–17.

## Barrett’s dissent

Justice Barrett would have reversed the Sixth Circuit. Under the Seventh Amendment’s historical inquiry, factual overlap with the merits does not itself transform a threshold matter into a jury question. Richards compared exhaustion to a plea in bar or discharge tried to a common-law jury; Perttu compared it to an equitable action to enjoin litigation heard by a chancellor. Those competing accounts could inform the broader question whether all exhaustion disputes require juries, but neither showed a historical distinction turning on intertwinement. Barrett agreed that resolving the broader historical issue was unnecessary and premature. Dissent slip opinion at 3–5 & nn.1–2.

She separately objected to the majority’s statutory route. The parties and courts below had advanced and analyzed constitutional theories, rather than the statutory theory the Court adopted. For Barrett, ordinary party-presentation and review principles counseled against inventing that alternative basis. The majority answered that examining statutory grounds before reaching the Seventh Amendment was a cardinal principle, as in *Tull v. United States*, and that amici had briefed the statutory issue using the same precedents as the parties. Dissent slip opinion at 6–8 & n.3; majority slip opinion at 6 n.1.

Barrett also rejected reading a statutory jury right into silence. *Tull*, *Feltner v. Columbia Pictures Television*, and *Monterey v. Del Monte Dunes* had found no statutory jury right in silent provisions even when the Constitution independently required jury participation. She regarded *Beacon Theatres* as a prudential sequencing rule responsive to estoppel, rather than a general rule of statutory construction. Exhaustion findings, in her view, did not bind the later merits jury; her footnote distinguished preclusion of the same threshold issue from preclusion of merits facts when the plaintiff had prevailed on exhaustion. Dissent slip opinion at 8–14 & n.4.

The dissent viewed *Smithers* as correcting premature determination of damages under ordinary amount-in-controversy principles, and *Land* as a judicial-administration case that did not involve a jury right and ultimately proceeded to a bench trial. Finally, Barrett considered the majority’s rule arbitrary and open to manipulation: a prisoner alleging sexual abuse and destroyed grievances would get no exhaustion jury merely by bringing an Eighth Amendment claim, but could obtain one by adding an intertwined First Amendment retaliation claim. Dissent slip opinion at 11–17.

## Scope and assigned material

The decision requires jury determination in the identified intertwined setting under the PLRA. It leaves unresolved the broader entitlement to a jury for nonintertwined exhaustion disputes, the constitutional validity of an express contrary congressional command, whether Richards actually exhausted or encountered unavailable remedies, and his substantive claims.
