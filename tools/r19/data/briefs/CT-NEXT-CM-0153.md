# Tongish v. Thomas
251 Kan. 728, 840 P.2d 471 (1992) | Contracts

## Facts and procedural posture
Denis Tongish agreed in April 1988 to grow sunflower seeds for Decatur Coop. The acreage was reduced by agreement from 160 to 116.8 acres. Coop promised $13 per hundredweight for large seeds and $8 for small seeds, with deliveries in thirds by December 31, March 31, and May 31. Coop separately arranged to transfer the seeds to Bambino Bean & Seed, passing the purchase price through and anticipating only a fifty-five-cent-per-hundredweight handling return. That resale arrangement protected Coop from market-price fluctuations but did not alter its obligation to buy Tongish’s crop. 251 Kan. at 728–729, 734–735.

Tongish delivered some seeds in October and November. A later dispute about dockage arose because his seeds were better than other growers’ and commingled sampling disadvantaged him. Coop resolved it through an additional payment reflecting reduced dockage. By January, a short crop, weather, and other factors had doubled the market price. Tongish notified Coop on January 13 that he would deliver no more. In May he sold 82,820 pounds to Thomas for $14,714.89, approximately $5,153.13 above what the Coop agreement would have yielded. Id. at 729.

The case began as Tongish’s action against Thomas for the unpaid balance. Thomas deposited $7,359.61 in court and was dismissed. Coop intervened with its breach claim. After a bench trial, the district court found that Tongish breached without a valid basis but awarded only $455.51 in lost handling charges. The Court of Appeals reversed for calculation under the UCC market-price formula. Tongish obtained Kansas Supreme Court review of that damage ruling. The intervention and the parties’ successive positions explain why the grower appears as appellee and Coop as third-party intervenor/appellant in the caption. Id. at 729–730.

## Question and adopted rule
The narrow issue was whether the general compensatory objective in K.S.A. 84-1-106 capped Coop’s recovery at its actual handling-profit loss despite K.S.A. 84-2-713’s specific market-price/contract-price measure for nondelivery or repudiation. The Supreme Court adopted the Court of Appeals’ analysis and upheld the specific statutory market remedy. It did not substitute a final calculated award for the district court’s $455.51 judgment. Id. at 728–731, 735.

Section 2-713 permits the market price when the buyer learned of the breach minus the contract price, with applicable incidental and consequential damages and deductions for expenses saved. A buyer who does not cover may pursue that alternative. General and specific statutes should be harmonized when possible, but the specific provision controls an irreconcilable conflict unless the legislature indicates otherwise. Limiting this buyer to handling profit could not be reconciled with the particular statutory measure on these facts. Id. at 730–731.

## Reasoning and treatment of the resale arrangement
The court considered whether Coop’s protection against fluctuations justified an exception. Earlier Kansas market-damage decisions did not present this precise resale arrangement. It examined Allied Canners, where heavy rains prevented the supplier from delivering raisins, downstream buyers released the middleman from their contracts, and a California court limited recovery to the middleman’s actual expected profit under specified circumstances. That court’s concerns included the seller’s knowledge of the resale contract and whether the breach occurred in bad faith. Id. at 731–733.

Tongish’s case materially differed. He knew the seeds ultimately went to Bambino, although he may not have known the deal’s details. His crop was available for sale, and he sold it to another buyer after prices increased. Although the district court did not need to make a separate express finding of bad faith, it found no valid reason for his breach. The opinion distinguished that opportunistic nondelivery from a supplier with no crop to sell. This distinction should not be converted into an unmade trial finding or a requirement that every § 2-713 claimant prove a distinct bad-faith element. Id. at 733.

The adopted analysis reviewed the debate between actual-profit compensation and market damages, including the argument that the latter values the contractual allocation of risk when the agreement is made. The Supreme Court emphasized that Coop remained bound to buy at the agreed price even if it lacked a buyer or prices fell. If Tongish could pay only the small handling return when prices rose, the contract would operate as a floor protecting him against declines while preserving the profit from repudiation on increases. The market differential preserved the reciprocal allocation of price risk and encouraged contractual stability. The court accepted that the result might exceed the middleman’s realized profit loss. Id. at 733–735.

## Unjust enrichment argument and result
Tongish also argued that the larger award would unjustly enrich Coop. He had not presented that contention below. The Supreme Court additionally rejected it on the merits: the dispute concerned which statutory measure applied, rather than a restitution claim involving an inequitable retention of benefits conferred by one party on another. That additional holding is omitted from the assigned excerpt. Id. at 735.

McFarland delivered the court’s opinion; the complete report contains no separate writing, judicial footnote, or specified numeric division. The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals’ reversal and remand for determining and awarding § 2-713 damages and reversed the district court judgment. It did not award the $5,153.13 resale gain as an automatically equivalent damage figure. Id. at 728, 735.
