# Dial Corp. v. News Corp.

**317 F.R.D. 426 (S.D.N.Y. 2016)** — October 31, 2016. District Judge William H. Pauley III. Civil Procedure. Assigned: CivPro PDF 710, printed 1411–1412, Note 6. This is the final settlement-approval and fee opinion, rather than a trial judgment establishing antitrust liability.

Consumer packaged-goods companies alleged that News Corporation and related defendants monopolized in-store promotion services: advertising and promotional opportunities connecting manufacturers with supermarket shoppers. Plaintiffs challenged the defendants’ arrangements with retailers as barriers to competing providers. The litigation began with a private investigation in 2010, a Michigan filing in December 2012, and transfer to the Southern District of New York in 2013. Discovery produced approximately eleven million documents, numerous depositions, and six experts. The court certified a class, and an attempted interlocutory appeal did not interrupt the case. After the court denied summary-judgment and expert-exclusion motions in January 2016 and addressed twenty-six motions in limine, the parties reached a settlement term sheet at the end of the first trial day, February 29, 2016. Preliminary approval followed on June 2; the final hearing occurred September 21. These steps supported an informed compromise after substantial adversarial testing. They did not constitute judicial findings that defendants had committed the alleged violations. (Original opinion, slip 1–4; 317 F.R.D. 428–430.)

The settlement provided a $244 million fund distributed according to class members’ purchases during the class period. Unclaimed amounts would be redistributed to members who deposited earlier checks until the fund was exhausted. There was no reversion to defendants and no cy-pres disposition. For five years, structural relief restricted exclusive retailer contracts exceeding thirty months, binding renewals more than eighteen months before expiration, and prohibitions on disclosing contract termination dates to prospective competitors. The first two restrictions contained exceptions for competition or specified retailer requests. These provisions sought to address the contractual barriers alleged in the lawsuit, alongside compensation for past conduct. The settlement expressly avoided any admission of liability and offered no additional opt-out opportunity. (Slip 4; 430.)

The settlement also required mediation and, if mediation failed, binding arbitration of covered disputes. Compliance disputes concerning the structural relief during the first three years were covered; those arising more than three years after approval were excluded from arbitration. The opinion separately describes covered antitrust claims using the unusual timing phrase “accrues five years after approval.” The original filing contains that wording too. This brief does not silently substitute a supposed “within five years” term or certify the underlying settlement contract’s exact interpretation. The court’s clear discussion of the first three years of structural-compliance disputes supplies the relevant basis for its arbitration analysis. (Slip 4, 10–11; 430, 432–433.)

Plaintiffs sought final approval and $73.2 million in fees, thirty percent of the fund, together with expenses and incentive awards. No class member objected to the settlement itself. PepsiCo separately objected to the proposed fee percentage. An initial report listed 317 claims, representing 45.35 percent of the class but 87.44 percent of qualifying purchases. A later count increased to 328, although the corresponding purchase percentage could not be determined. The court ordered additional notices and telephone follow-up to potentially eligible nonclaimants, allowing late proofs by December 1, 2016. It treated the participation figures and absence of settlement objections as evidence supporting approval, without treating the settlement fund as automatically distributed to every member. (Slip 5–9, 26; 430–432, 439.)

The governing settlement inquiry considered procedural fairness and the substantive Grinnell factors, including complexity, expense and duration; class reaction; the litigation’s development; risks of proving liability and damages and maintaining the class; defendants’ capacity to pay more; and the recovery’s reasonableness in relation to possible recovery and litigation risks. These factors required an overall judgment, rather than a rule that each consideration independently favored settlement. Experienced counsel negotiated at arm’s length after substantial discovery and trial preparation. Complex antitrust proof and uncertain damages created real risks despite plaintiffs’ favorable pretrial rulings. Defendants’ ability to withstand a larger judgment did not by itself make this compromise inadequate. The $244 million recovery compared favorably with plaintiffs’ expert estimates of $355 million under a core theory and $674 million under an adjusted theory, both single-damage figures; even successful liability proof would not guarantee acceptance of those calculations. Structural changes supplied additional value. (Slip 6–11; 431–433.)

The arbitration term nevertheless troubled the judge. Having supervised the litigation for four years, the court would cede future disputes to a nonpublic forum and lose the clarity supplied by continued public adjudication. Precedent addressing settlement-imposed arbitration was scarce. But this class consisted of sophisticated businesses better equipped than ordinary individuals to litigate in arbitration; none objected after an adequate opportunity to examine the agreement. Defendants insisted that arbitration was necessary to achieve a deal. Taking those circumstances together, the court accepted arbitration as an adequate substitute, although it considered the provision imperfect. This is a contextual approval of a negotiated class settlement, not a general holding that every compulsory arbitration provision is fair or that courts must approve one whenever a defendant demands it. (Slip 10–11; 432–433.)

The court independently scrutinized compensation for counsel under Goldberger. The relevant considerations included time and labor, complexity, litigation risk, quality of representation, the requested fee in relation to the recovery, and public policy. A percentage approach did not excuse an inflated lodestar or establish thirty percent as a presumptively appropriate benchmark for a large fund. Counsel had spent years pursuing the case and obtained valuable relief, but five firms continued operating through an opaque division of responsibilities after the court had appointed only two as class counsel. Subsequent permission to use different firms as trial and settlement counsel did not authorize duplicative billing. Heavy partner time and unusually high associate and paralegal rates also inflated the claimed lodestar. The court’s fiduciary responsibility to the class required rewarding useful work without a windfall. (Slip 12–22; 433–438.)

Counsel proposed a $36,433,985.50 lodestar and approximately a 2.01 multiplier. The court first reduced the lodestar by approximately $2.5 million for staffing and billing anomalies, yielding $33.9 million. It then discounted work billed after the appointment of two firms: approximately 20,190 hours at the blended rate produced $10.627 million, of which it credited two-fifths and treated three-fifths as presumptively duplicative. Rounding that further reduction to approximately $6 million produced a $27.9 million lodestar. A 1.75 multiplier recognized litigation risk and quality without rewarding the inflated original calculation. The resulting fee was **$48,825,000**. The opinion describes that amount as twenty percent of the fund; the exact dollar award, rather than an assumed mathematical equality with twenty percent of $244 million, controls. The court also rejected counsel’s attempts to make their separately negotiated twenty-percent fee on five absent members’ $30 million private settlements a materially different comparison: the same litigation-related Goldberger considerations supported both fees. (Slip 20–22 and n.3; 436–438.)

The court approved **$7,512,915.12 in expenses**. Most expenses concerned experts and document review. Contract attorneys’ approximately twenty-five thousand hours were charged at an average $39 per hour without a markup and treated as expenses rather than added to the lodestar. This treatment avoided the need to determine an appropriate spread between those attorneys’ actual cost and a marked-up billing rate. Six corporate representatives also received **$50,000 each**, totaling $300,000. They had supplied extensive documents and employee time, some had advanced substantial investigation and litigation fees, and their lawsuit risked retaliation from an important business partner. The small proportion of the fund and those contributions supported the discretionary awards. The court did not announce an automatic entitlement to incentive payments. (Slip 23–26; 438–439.)

The exact result was final approval of the settlement, with the fee-and-expense request granted in part and denied in part. The court awarded the amounts above, directed renewed claim outreach and the December 1 deadline, required a revised proposed final judgment, and terminated the pending motions. Pauley issued the sole opinion; there are no separate writings or numerical appellate vote. Footnote 1 addresses a related declaratory action dismissed in 2014; note 2 excludes contract-attorney hours from the main billing figure; note 3 compares the separate private settlements’ fees. (Slip 2 n.1, 5 n.2, 22 n.3, 26.)

**Source scope.** The complete reported judicial text, all three notes, and all twenty-six original filing pages in native extraction were read; selected originals were visually checked. The complete assigned Note 6 was read in text and on the original scan. The original itself contains inconsistent total hours (69,128.95 versus 69,218.95) and an earlier expense figure ending in .20 versus the final award’s .12. Those variants are preserved rather than presented as resolved facts. This brief explains the historical decision and does not certify current antitrust, class-settlement, or arbitration law.
