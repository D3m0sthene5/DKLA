# Conley v. Gibson

**Civil Procedure · CP-ATLAS-CONLEY · 355 U.S. 41 (1957) · November 18, 1957**

Conley allowed Black railroad workers’ fair-representation suit against their union to proceed. The Court rejected exclusive Adjustment Board jurisdiction, found the railroad unnecessary on the pleaded claim and requested relief, and held the complaint sufficient under the Federal Rules’ notice pleading standard. It **reversed and remanded to the district court for further proceedings**. The familiar pleading language is part of that historical decision; the Court did not determine that the discrimination allegations were true or award the requested relief. (355 U.S. at 44–48.)

The petitioners worked at the Texas and New Orleans Railroad’s Houston Freight House. Their bargaining representative was Local Union No. 28 of the Brotherhood of Railway and Steamship Clerks. A collective bargaining agreement protected jobs and seniority. According to the complaint, the railroad purported to abolish forty-five jobs occupied by Black employees in May 1954, discharged or demoted them, and actually gave the jobs to white workers. A few Black workers allegedly regained their former jobs without their seniority. The workers charged that the union deliberately refused their repeated requests for protection comparable to that afforded white workers, and more generally failed to represent them equally and in good faith. (Id. at 42–43.)

The workers brought a federal class suit in Texas against the Brotherhood, the local, and officers of both, requesting declaratory relief, an injunction, and damages for breach of the Railway Labor Act’s duty of fair representation. The defendants sought dismissal because the National Railroad Adjustment Board supposedly had exclusive jurisdiction, the railroad had not been joined as an indispensable defendant, and the complaint failed to state a claim. The district court dismissed on the jurisdictional ground; the Fifth Circuit apparently relied on the same ground in affirming. The Supreme Court granted review and also addressed the two alternative defenses, which both parties had briefed and argued. (Id. at 43–45.)

Section 3 First (i) governs disputes between employees and carriers arising from grievances or interpretation or application of bargaining agreements. This case was instead a dispute between employees and their bargaining agent over its statutory obligation to represent them without racial discrimination. The bargaining agreement would be at most incidentally involved, and the Board lacked authority to protect employees against this asserted union discrimination. Footnote 3 sets out the statute; footnote 4 distinguishes Slocum and rejects the reasoning of Hayes on the relevant point. The Court therefore held dismissal for want of jurisdiction erroneous. (Id. at 44–45 & nn.3–4.)

The railroad was not indispensable to the action as framed. The workers sought no relief from it, and none available on this claim would bind it or affect its rights or interests. If an issue later arose requiring its participation, the railroad or the defendants could request joinder. This was a conclusion about these allegations and this requested relief, not a rule that employers can never be necessary in litigation involving a bargaining representative. (Id. at 45.)

On the merits of pleading, the Court’s earlier fair-representation decisions made the alleged discrimination legally actionable. The representative’s duty continues after negotiation of an agreement into its administration, grievance handling, adjustment of working rules, and protection of existing contractual rights. A facially fair agreement can be administered discriminatorily with the union’s active or tacit consent. The availability of employees’ own grievances or contract suits did not authorize the union’s alleged selective refusal to help them. The Court expressly did not decide whether a union must handle any grievances at all: having undertaken to bargain or present grievances for some represented employees, it could not refuse comparable good-faith action for others because of race. (Id. at 45–47 & nn.5–7.)

The opinion applied the then-stated test that dismissal was inappropriate unless it appeared beyond doubt that no set of supporting facts could entitle the claimant to relief. It separately rejected the demand for detailed factual allegations. Rule 8(a)(2) required a short and plain statement giving fair notice of the claim and its grounds. The allegations identified racial discrimination in the union’s refusal to protect the workers’ jobs and grievances; if proved, they would establish a breach of its statutory duty. (Id. at 45–47 & nn.5,8.)

That approach depended on the rest of the procedural system. The illustrative forms, discovery, amendment, pretrial definition of issues, and other mechanisms could clarify the dispute after the pleading stage. Footnote 9 specifically lists more definite statements, motions to strike or for judgment on the pleadings, pretrial proceedings, discovery, summary judgment, and amendment. The Court applied former Rule 8(f)’s instruction to construe pleadings to do substantial justice and favored resolution on the merits over pleading as a technical contest. These references describe the rules and forms the 1957 opinion used, rather than asserting that every referenced provision retains its historical numbering or content. (Id. at 47–48 & n.9.)
