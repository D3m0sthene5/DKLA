# DeMentas v. Estate of Tallas
764 P.2d 628 (Utah Ct. App. 1988) | Contracts

## Court and result
Judge Orme wrote the Utah Court of Appeals' November 17, 1988 opinion, with Judges Bench and Davidson concurring. The unanimous panel affirmed judgment dismissing Peter Dementas's $50,000 claim against Jack Tallas's estate, with each party bearing its own appeal costs. The claimant adequately notified the estate of his demand, contrary to one trial-court ground, but could not prove consideration or an underlying debt. A notarized acknowledgment of gratitude for services already performed gratuitously did not establish an enforceable contract or account stated. (628–634; final disposition 634.)

## Friendship, services, and the written memorandum
Tallas immigrated from Greece in 1914 and lived nearly seventy years in Salt Lake City. He succeeded in business as an insurance agent and landlord and spent his last years at the Little America Hotel. Over fourteen years his close friend Dementas rendered at least some personal assistance. On December 18, 1982 Tallas dictated a memorandum in Greek stating that he owed Dementas $50,000 for past help, including collecting mail, grocery shopping, and management of rental properties. He also proposed changing his will to make Dementas an heir for that sum. Tallas retained the Greek document, prepared an English version, notarized that version using his own seal, and delivered the documents three days later. He died on February 4, 1983 without making the contemplated testamentary change. (629–630.)

The English memorandum described their father-and-son-like relationship, family dinners, transportation, gasoline expenditures, property assistance, and past services. It acknowledged a supposed $50,000 obligation, referred to an existing mention in the will, and promised a future change making Dementas an heir. The second note explains the actual existing will provision: it requested that Dementas and another person familiar with the properties collect rents and manage the properties for reasonable compensation. That prospective paid-management request was not the promised $50,000 legacy. The third note identifies the translation difference: the Greek contemplated an heir for $50,000, while the English mentioned an heir without specifying that sum in the future-devise language. The court considered the difference inconsequential in this case's posture. (631; nn.2–3.)

## Claim presentation and trial
Dementas timely presented a $50,000 estate claim with copies of both documents. First Security Bank, the personal representative, denied it, and he sued. A pretrial order disposed of quantum meruit, a contract to make or modify a will, and gift causa mortis. The case proceeded on the memorandum as, at best, an acknowledgment of an asserted existing debt from an earlier oral contract. After extensive testimony and exhibits, the trial court found that the documents were executed without fraud, duress, or undue influence. It nevertheless found that the memorandum expressed appreciation for gratuitous services and an intention to compensate Dementas later, rather than an enforceable contractual commitment supported by consideration. (629–630.)

The trial court also rejected account stated because the presented claim had not expressly named that legal theory. Alternatively, it found that no account stated had been proved. The first note confirms that a will-contract theory was procedurally precluded and adds that lack of consideration would defeat it in any event. This is not a categorical decision that contracts to benefit a person in a will are unenforceable; the note expressly recognizes enforceable examples and the relevant statutory requirements. (630; n.1.)

## An estate claim need not name every legal theory
The appellate court rejected the trial court's restrictive notice rationale. Utah's claim-presentation statute required a written statement identifying the claim's basis, claimant, and amount, but disclaimed excessive precision concerning security, uncertainties, and due dates. The Probate Code called for liberal construction. A claimant could alternatively begin an action whose complaint would satisfy ordinary notice pleading; it would be anomalous to demand greater detail in the less formal presentation to a personal representative. (630–631.)

The stated amount, assertion of indebtedness for services, and attached acknowledgment gave the representative enough information to investigate and choose among payment, contest, or settlement. Failure to articulate account stated as a particular theory did not invalidate the claim. That procedural conclusion opened consideration of the merits; it did not supply an underlying obligation or entitle Dementas to payment. (630–631.)

## Extrinsic evidence and the difference between existence and value
The parties disputed whether the memorandum was ambiguous and whether it was integrated, invoking the parol evidence rule. The court did not decide those disputes. Even a writing appearing completely integrated can be accompanied by evidence showing whether its promise has consideration. Under Restatement § 218(2), admission of that evidence was proper independently of the writing's interpretation or integration. The plaintiff bore the burden of establishing consideration or a legally sufficient substitute as part of his contract case. The opinion distinguished the rule governing certain negotiable instruments. (631–632.)

The fourth note distinguishes lack of consideration from failure of consideration. Lack means no contract arose; failure presupposes a contract whose promised performance later fails. The distinction also affects pleading under Rules 8(b) and 8(c). A signed agreement may generate a presumption of consideration, but evidence of its absence makes the issue one for the factfinder rather than conclusively establishing an exchange. (632; n.4.)

Existence of consideration was also different from adequacy. The estate offered extensive testimony contesting Dementas's asserted services. Postal employees and neighbors supplied some of that evidence, as the fifth note details. The trial judge admitted it insofar as it affected credibility, not to establish that services were worth less than $50,000. Any genuinely bargained-for detriment could suffice even if economically slight. Since Dementas had performed some services, their value alone was not the decisive problem. The sixth note reproduces the trial judge's colorful remarks but does not convert the litigation into a finding that every claimed service occurred or that the court valued them at $50,000. (632–633; nn.5–6.)

## Past services and moral obligation
The decisive finding was that the services were performed gratuitously, without expectation of payment. The later promise therefore responded to completed events rather than inducing their performance through an exchange. That is the reason past consideration is ordinarily equivalent to no consideration: the promise was made because the assistance had occurred, not to procure it. The trial finding had not been shown erroneous. (633.)

The court acknowledged criticism of the rule and efforts elsewhere to enforce some promises through moral obligation. Utah had not embraced that exception. Even if it applied, the finding that these services were gratuitous would defeat Dementas's claim under the approach discussed by the court. The seventh note gives a pension illustration of the rule's potentially harsh operation. The eighth reproduces Manwill's concern that treating morality alone as consideration would dissolve the distinction between enforceable contracts and naked promises. The ninth observes that services furnished with an expectation of payment may already create an implied contract, and discusses limitations difficulties and the memoranda's potential effect on that issue. These observations do not establish payment expectations or an implied debt here. (633; nn.7–9.)

## Account stated and final disposition
An account stated requires prior monetary transactions creating indebtedness, agreement on the correct balance, and an express or implied promise to pay it. The trial court found no existing monetary accounts or liquidated debts due when these documents were executed. Nor was there an enforceable promise to pay the $50,000. Because account stated itself is contractual, labeling the memorandum an agreed account could not manufacture an obligation from gratuitous assistance where none previously existed. (633–634.)

The appellate court thus affirmed the judgment on the merits despite correcting the claim-notice reasoning. It did not order testamentary changes, award restitution, or treat notarization as a substitute for consideration. Each party was to bear its own appellate costs. (634.)
