1978; decided January 9, 1979. Justice Stewart delivered the Court's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Burger
and Justices Brennan, White, Marshall, Blackmun, Powell, and Stevens. Justice Rehnquist dissented. Assigned
excerpt: Civil Procedure casebook pp. 1334–1341. Full judicial opinion: Full judicial opinion.
Parties
Shore, a Parklane shareholder, brought a private securities class action against Parklane Hosiery Company and
thirteen officers, directors, and shareholders. He was the plaintiff below and respondent in the Supreme Court.
The defendants, petitioners in the Supreme Court, opposed his effort to establish an element of his claim
through a judgment obtained by the Securities and Exchange Commission in separate litigation. The SEC was
the plaintiff in that earlier adjudication, not the private plaintiff seeking damages in this case. 439 U.S. at
324–325.
Shore requested damages, rescission, and costs arising from an allegedly misleading proxy statement issued in
connection with a merger that would take Parklane private. The SEC sought equitable relief for securities-law
violations involving the same statement. The differing plaintiffs and remedies produced the question whether
Shore could use the SEC's favorable factual determination against defendants who had already litigated that
issue. Id. at 324–326.
Material Facts
Parklane issued a proxy statement soliciting shareholder approval of a merger. Shore alleged that the statement
contained material falsehoods and omissions and sued before the SEC commenced its own action. Thus, the
private plaintiff had not waited for the government to win before deciding whether to sue. The SEC
subsequently charged that the statement violated federal securities law. After a four-day bench trial, the district
court found the statement materially false and misleading and entered a declaratory judgment. Although the
SEC had requested injunctive relief, the resulting judgment relevant here was declaratory. Id. at 324–325.
The defendants contested the SEC case and appealed, but the Second Circuit affirmed. Shore then sought
partial summary judgment in his private action on the already-decided issue of the proxy statement's
misleading character. The requested preclusion would not determine every issue in the private case. Injury and
damages, among other matters not settled by the SEC judgment, remained for adjudication. Id. at 325 & n.2.
The defendants had no Seventh Amendment right to a jury in the SEC's equitable action. Their argument
therefore combined two objections: Shore had not been a party to the first case and would not have been bound
had the SEC lost, and applying the bench-trial determination in his damages action would prevent a jury from
redetermining the issue. Id. at 325–326, 333–334.
Procedural History
After the SEC judgment became final, Shore moved for partial summary judgment based on collateral estoppel,
the doctrine now commonly called issue preclusion. The district court denied the motion because it believed
preclusion would deprive the defendants of their constitutional jury right in the private action. The Second
Circuit reversed, holding that the defendants had fully and fairly litigated the issue and that its earlier resolution
could bind them notwithstanding the absence of a jury in the first action. Id. at 325–326.

The Supreme Court granted certiorari to address offensive nonmutual issue preclusion and the Seventh
Amendment objection. It affirmed the Second Circuit. That disposition allowed the established
proxy-statement issue to be treated as conclusive in Shore's suit. It did not itself enter a damages award, decide
injury, or resolve all elements of private securities liability. Id. at 326, 337–338 & n.24.
Issue
May a private plaintiff who was not a party to an earlier SEC action use the judgment in that action to prevent
the same defendants from relitigating an issue they fully contested and lost? What limits should govern this
offensive use of nonmutual issue preclusion? Does the Seventh Amendment require a fresh jury determination
merely because the first proceeding was equitable and tried to a judge? Id. at 326–327, 333.
Rule and Holding
Federal courts may permit offensive nonmutual issue preclusion, subject to broad trial-court discretion to
prevent unfairness. Offensive use means that a plaintiff seeks to establish an issue against a defendant who
previously litigated and lost it against another plaintiff. A court ordinarily should refuse that use when the new
plaintiff could easily have joined the earlier action or when application would be unfair to the defendant. The
decision authorizes a context-sensitive practice; it does not make every prior adverse judgment automatically
conclusive for every later claimant. Id. at 329–332.
Relevant unfairness includes a weak incentive to defend the first suit, inconsistency with earlier judgments
favorable to the defendant, or procedural opportunities in the second suit that were unavailable in the first and
could readily produce a different result. None justified refusal here. Shore probably could not have joined the
SEC action, the defendants had ample incentive to defend it, the judgment conflicted with no prior favorable
decision, and the later jury proceeding supplied no material procedural advantage requiring relitigation. Id. at
330–333.
The Seventh Amendment did not require the defendants to retry the settled issue before a jury. A valid
equitable adjudication can have preclusive consequences in a later legal action. The Court held that applying
that principle here preserved the jury's role on genuinely unresolved factual questions rather than violating the
constitutional jury guarantee. Id. at 333–337.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the Second Circuit's judgment. Shore could rely on the SEC adjudication to
establish the proxy statement's materially false and misleading character, while the remaining issues in his
private action still required resolution. The Court neither awarded Shore damages nor held that the SEC
judgment resolved every issue presented by his complaint. Id. at 325 n.2, 337–338.
Reasoning
Traditional mutuality limited estoppel to persons who would have been bound had the first judgment gone the
other way. The Court distinguished that reciprocal requirement from the fundamental protection owed to the
person against whom preclusion is asserted. Due process prevents binding someone who lacked the appropriate
opportunity to be heard. Parklane and its officers, however, had themselves defended the SEC action. Shore's
absence did not mean that the defendants lacked their own opportunity to litigate. Id. at 326–328.
The Court nevertheless explained why offensive use creates concerns different from defensive use. Defensive
estoppel ordinarily encourages a plaintiff to join available defendants in one action: losing against one may
defeat later attempts against others. Offensive estoppel may reward a potential plaintiff who waits to see
whether another claimant wins. The waiting plaintiff can benefit from victory without risking preclusion from

Fairness to the defendant supplies an additional limitation. A defendant sued for a modest amount might
rationally devote few resources to the case without anticipating a much larger later action. A single adverse
judgment also should not automatically outweigh a series of prior favorable judgments. And a meaningful
procedural difference, such as access to evidence previously unavailable, might justify renewed litigation
because it could change the result. The Court used these examples to define circumstances for rejecting
estoppel, rather than treating repeated litigation as inherently forbidden. Id. at 330–331.
Those concerns did not fit Shore's case. His action was already pending when the SEC sued. Federal law
restricted consolidation of private litigation with an SEC enforcement action without the Commission's
consent, making easy joinder an unrealistic premise. The government action also involved serious allegations
and potential relief, and the existing private suit made the consequences of defeat foreseeable. The defendants
litigated through trial and appeal. Id. at 331–332 & n.17.
There was no inconsistent prior judgment in the defendants' favor. Nor did the defendants identify a missing
procedural opportunity likely to produce a different result. The Court did not regard the change from judge to
jury as itself enough to establish such an advantage. Its conclusion was specific to the asserted distinction; it
did not eliminate the separate inquiry into genuinely consequential procedural differences in other cases. Id. at
332–333.
On the constitutional issue, the defendants emphasized that in 1791 a nonparty ordinarily could not obtain
estoppel because mutuality was required. The Court answered that the Seventh Amendment preserves the
substance of the jury institution without freezing every procedural incident of eighteenth-century practice.
Changes in procedural rules can alter which questions remain open for trial without necessarily infringing the
jury right. The relevant historical tradition also recognized the preclusive effect of an equitable decree in a
subsequent legal action. Id. at 333–336.
Beacon Theatres did not require the opposite result. That case protected jury determination of common legal
issues when legal and equitable claims were joined in the same action and the district court proposed trying the
equitable issues first. Its concern arose precisely because an equitable adjudication could otherwise have
preclusive force. Parklane involved a completed, separate SEC proceeding, rather than an avoidable
sequencing choice within Shore's suit. The Court also relied on decisions recognizing that prior nonjury
determinations may resolve issues material to later legal claims. Id. at 334–336.
The Court rejected one part of the Second Circuit's reasoning: the defendants had not waived their jury right by
failing to request an advisory jury in the SEC case or to accelerate the private case. An advisory jury would not
supply the constitutional right, and the defendants could not insist that the SEC postpone enforcement to
accommodate their litigation preference. The result rested on preclusion's permissible operation, not an
invented waiver. Id. at 337–338 n.24.

Separate Opinions
Justice Rehnquist dissented on both constitutional and discretionary grounds. He considered the historical
mutuality requirement important because it meant that these defendants would have received a jury
determination in the comparable 1791 legal action. Abandoning mutuality in this setting did more than change
a minor procedural detail: it removed a contested factual question from the jury entirely. He distinguished
summary judgment and directed verdict, which operate when a legally sufficient factual controversy is absent,
from estoppel based on another tribunal's resolution of a genuine dispute. Id. at 338–350 (Rehnquist, J.,
dissenting).
Rehnquist also challenged the majority's treatment of judge and jury as effectively neutral alternatives for this
purpose. Differences in decisionmaking, community participation, and evaluation of evidence could matter,
and the Constitution's protection of the jury made those differences legally significant. He did not accept that
the equitable origin of the first judgment answered whether a stranger to that proceeding could use it
offensively to extinguish the later jury opportunity. Id. at 343–351.
Even assuming constitutional permission, he would have refused offensive estoppel under the discretion the
Court recognized. The private action still required a jury to decide injury and damages, so the practical
economy was limited. In his assessment, preserving the jury opportunity outweighed the incremental savings.
His dissent opposed the use approved in these circumstances; it should not be converted into a holding that all
nonmutual preclusion is impermissible. Id. at 351–356.
Notes
The direction of preclusion must be identified precisely. Shore sought to bind defendants who participated in
the first litigation. He did not seek to bind an absent person through someone else's representation. Martin and
Taylor, briefed separately in this continuation, address that different problem. The reason Shore himself would
not have been bound by an SEC defeat is the absence of mutuality; it does not show that Parklane lacked a full
and fair opportunity to litigate its own position. 439 U.S. at 327–332.
The opinion also requires separating preclusion from trial sequencing. Beacon Theatres protects a jury
opportunity when a court can organize a combined action accordingly; Parklane recognizes the consequences
of an independent judgment already entered after adequate litigation. In applying the cases, identify the first
proceeding, the exact issue necessarily determined, the party to be bound, the opportunities actually available,
and the questions that remain for the jury. Id. at 333–338 & n.24.
The assigned excerpt covers the principal preclusion and jury analysis. The full opinion supplies the complete
