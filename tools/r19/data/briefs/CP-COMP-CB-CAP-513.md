# Smith v. Barry

**502 U.S. 244 (1992) — Supreme Court of the United States, January 14, 1992. Civil Procedure.** Justice O’Connor wrote for eight Justices; Justice Scalia concurred in the judgment. All nine agreed to reverse and remand. This brief covers the Supreme Court’s notice-of-appeal decision, rather than the distinct Fourth Circuit decision on remand, 985 F.2d 180 (1993).

## Facts and procedural history

William Smith was an inmate at the Maryland State Penitentiary. He brought a pro se action under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 against prison administrators, corrections officers, two state psychologists, and a private physician, Dr. Wayne Barry. Smith alleged that he had a psychogenic pain disorder and that defendants’ refusal to provide a wheelchair violated the Eighth Amendment. He separately alleged excessive force by corrections officers. These were allegations underlying the civil-rights litigation, rather than findings the Supreme Court made about unconstitutional treatment. 502 U.S. at 245.

The district court dismissed Barry because it concluded that his treatment of Smith was not action under color of state law. After appointment of trial counsel, the remaining case went to trial in 1988. The court directed verdicts for administrators and officers on the wheelchair claim and for the administrators and three officers on the excessive-force claim. The jury rejected excessive-force claims against the other four officers, but found the two psychologists deliberately indifferent to Smith’s medical needs and awarded $15,000. Id. at 245–246.

The psychologists timely moved for judgment notwithstanding the verdict. While their motion was pending, Smith filed a notice of appeal without consulting his attorney. Under the version of Appellate Rule 4(a)(4) governing the case, that notice was without effect. The district court denied the psychologists’ motion on April 13, 1988. Trial counsel later warned Smith that the earlier notice was premature and that a new appeal had to be filed by May 13; counsel also urged him not to appeal, or at least to obtain another legal opinion. Id. at 246.

Meanwhile, the Fourth Circuit clerk had responded to the ineffective notice by sending the parties the court’s informal briefing forms for pro se appeals and instructions about its procedures. The forms asked six questions about the parties’ legal positions. Smith returned his informal brief to the court of appeals on May 4, within the period for a valid notice of appeal. The clerk’s initiation of briefing did not itself cure the earlier notice, but it produced a different, timely document whose legal effect became the issue. Id. at 246–247.

After appointing appellate counsel, the Fourth Circuit dismissed the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. It concluded that an informal brief could not be the functional equivalent of a notice of appeal because Smith submitted it in response to a briefing order, and because the rules contemplated a notice and a brief as separate documents. The circuit also identified potential omissions from Smith’s brief, but did not decide whether those omissions independently made it inadequate. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve disagreement among circuits about whether an appellate brief could serve as the required notice. Id. at 247.

## Issue and holding

May a document submitted as an appellate brief satisfy the notice-of-appeal requirement? Yes. If it is filed within Rule 4’s time limit and supplies the notice required by Rule 3(c), its title or intended role as a brief does not disqualify it. The Court rejected the Fourth Circuit’s categorical rule, while leaving the sufficiency of this particular brief’s contents for decision on remand. Id. at 248–250.

## Rules, arguments, and reasoning

Rule 3(a), as reproduced in the opinion, required an appeal as of right to be taken by filing a notice with the district court clerk within Rule 4’s time. Rule 3(c) required identification of the appealing parties, designation of the judgment or order being appealed, and identification of the court to which the appeal was taken. The Court treated compliance as jurisdictional, but distinguished compliance in substance from adherence to a particular form. Liberal construction could recognize a filing that performed the required function; it could not excuse the absence of what the rule required. Id. at 247–248.

The Fourth Circuit’s focus on why Smith completed the informal brief applied the wrong inquiry. A document must communicate an intention to seek appellate review, but the relevant question is what notice it gives the other parties and the courts, rather than the filer’s subjective motivation. Smith had received the briefing form because he had already attempted an appeal. More fundamentally, a timely document supplying the required notice could qualify even though it was submitted in response to instructions about briefing. Id. at 248–249.

The ordinary sequence of a notice followed by a brief likewise did not establish that one paper could never perform both functions. Rule 3(c) expressly protected an appeal against dismissal for informality of form or title. The rules contemplated separate documents without forbidding recognition of a sufficiently informative, timely brief as a notice. The appellate court could then require another document satisfying its briefing standards, or make appropriate adjustments to preserve the rules’ transcript and briefing sequence. Proper briefing was not itself a jurisdictional condition; Rule 3(a) distinguished failure to file a timely notice from later procedural omissions. Id. at 249.

Respondents argued that Smith had filed his brief in the wrong court: Rule 3(a) directed notices to the district court, whereas he had sent his form to the court of appeals. The Court answered with Rule 4(a)(1)’s express procedure for notices mistakenly filed in an appellate court, under which the filing was deemed made in the district court on the appellate court’s receipt date. Recognition of the brief therefore did not require inventing an exception to the place-of-filing rule. Id.

Respondents also maintained that the informal brief omitted required information. The Court did not resolve that objection. Because the Fourth Circuit had relied on a categorical prohibition, it had not undertaken the necessary content analysis. Remand required that analysis under Rule 3(c), informed by the Court’s notice decisions. Consequently, the holding did not establish jurisdiction over every defendant or every issue in Smith’s litigation. Id. at 250.

## Separate opinion and judgment

Justice Scalia agreed that the appeal could not be rejected merely because the paper was a brief. His reason was Rule 3(c)’s express prohibition on dismissal for informality of form or title. He declined to rely on a theory of functional equivalence under liberal construction, preferring interpretation according to the rules’ apparent intent without either generosity or stinginess. His separate reasoning did not change the unanimous judgment. Id. at 250.

The Supreme Court **reversed the Fourth Circuit’s judgment and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion**. It neither adjudicated Smith’s constitutional claims nor determined that the informal brief adequately notified every prospective appellee. Id.
