# Williams v. Rank & Son Buick, Inc.
44 Wis.2d 239, 170 N.W.2d 807 (1969) | Contracts

## Holding and disagreement
The Wisconsin Supreme Court reversed the circuit court's order supporting a buyer's fraud claim concerning a car falsely represented as air-conditioned. The majority accepted sufficient evidence that the salesman made the representation, but held reliance unjustified as a matter of law because its falsity was readily discoverable through ordinary observation during the buyer's extensive inspection opportunity. Hanley wrote the October 3, 1969 opinion. Wilkie dissented, joined by Beilfuss and Heffernan, arguing that the majority improperly displaced the trial judge's contextual finding of justifiable reliance. The assigned account identifies the result as four to three; the original expressly records those three dissenters. (239, 242–250.)

## Purchase and conflicting representations
Williams bought a used Chrysler Imperial on March 19, 1968. He maintained that a newspaper advertisement and the dealer's salesman represented it as equipped with air conditioning and that this feature was a main reason for purchase. The car in fact lacked air conditioning. The dealer disputed the salesman’s words and fraudulent intent, while the trial court credited the buyer. The majority found sufficient evidence for that credibility determination and for finding the oral statement intentionally deceptive. The claim did not fail because the car actually had the promised feature or because the salesman conclusively prevailed on credibility. (243–245.)

Before purchase Williams inspected the car on the lot and was allowed to drive it away without a salesman for approximately an hour and a half, an opportunity customers normally did not receive. He did not test the feature on which he said he principally relied. The majority considered a simple operation of a knob sufficient to discover the lack of an air-conditioning unit. The assigned problem describes knobs labeled air as ventilation controls, but the judicial majority's dispositive analysis is the opportunity for ordinary observation. (246–247; assigned printed 517.)

## Fraud burden, review, and advertisement chronology
Fraud had to be proved by clear and convincing evidence; trial findings ordinarily stood unless contrary to the great weight and clear preponderance of the evidence. The court described false fact, intent to induce, actual reliance, and resulting injury as the pertinent requirements. It also quoted earlier authority that material false representations relied on and causing harm could matter regardless of bad faith, while finding sufficient evidence of intentional oral deception here. Its decision did not require resolving every debate over the manager's good faith in advertising. (242–244 & majority nn. 1–2.)

The only advertisement introduced was dated March 21, two days after the signed contract. Williams first testified that he visited in response to the March 21 ad, but acknowledged on cross-examination that he read the contract and its date was inserted before signature. The dealer argued that he seized on a later ad to reduce the purchase price. Williams asked the court to infer an earlier publication from his account of seeing it before buying. The majority found that inference unreasonable and insufficiently clear and convincing; an actual earlier ad could readily have been produced. Thus advertising reliance was evidentially deficient independently of the oral-reliance analysis. (244–245.)

The manager described dictating ads by telephone and receiving proofs only after publication, leaving no advance opportunity to correct dictation or printing mistakes. Highlighting the factory-air-conditioning words could permit an inference of intent, but the majority considered it unpersuasive given routine advertising practices. None of this excused a false oral statement if otherwise actionable; the majority moved instead to whether reliance could legally be justified. (243–245.)

## Ordinary observation and the buyer's circumstances
A fraud victim ordinarily need not distrust a positive representation simply because investigation might expose it. But Wisconsin decisions withheld relief when the person ignored known falsity or facts that ordinary observation, rather than a substantial search, would have revealed. The court assessed discoverability through intelligence, experience, relationship, and practical inspection opportunity, acknowledging that those circumstances sometimes justified reliance despite a possible inspection. (245–246.)

Here Williams was a literate high-school graduate and the court inferred some business acumen because he and his brother ran a business. The parties dealt at arm's length without a fiduciary relationship, and the dealer had not interfered with examination. Those circumstances, combined with the unusually long unaccompanied drive and ease of testing, led the majority to deem reliance unjustified as a matter of law. Although obviousness was generally factual, the cited cases permitted legal resolution in appropriate circumstances. (246–247.)

The majority stressed that the action sounded in fraud rather than breach of warranty. It therefore did not decide whether a warranty theory would have supported a different result, nor did it adjudicate the assigned hypothetical rescission remedy simply because the problem asks whether the sale was voidable. Because it reversed the order, it declined to decide Williams's request for attorney fees above the statutory allowances. (247–248.)

## Wilkie's dissent
Wilkie considered the decision a revival of caveat emptor inconsistent with consumer protection. He distinguished the majority's precedents involving experienced businesses intimately familiar with their products or patent rights, and transactions in which circumstances called for checking financial backing before extending credit. Those cases did not establish that an individual car buyer understood factory-installed cooling systems. (248–250 & dissent nn. 1–6.)

The buyer bargained with a large dealership, and the record did not show familiarity with automobile air conditioning. Factory equipment might be concealed under the hood rather than visibly mounted beneath the dashboard. A March purchase, rather than one during hot weather, also made failure to test cooling less telling. These were contextual reasons the dissent thought falsity insufficiently obvious for a legal bar to reliance, not findings adopted by the majority. (249.)

Wilkie would leave the issue to the factfinder. By finding reliance, the trial judge implicitly found a right to rely, and that conclusion was not against the great weight and clear preponderance of evidence. He would affirm. Beilfuss and Heffernan expressly joined. (250.)

## Exact disposition and complete-source scope
The controlling judgment reversed the circuit court's order; it did not remand for a new fraud trial or award a different purchase-price reduction. The opinion left the additional attorney-fee request unaddressed in light of that reversal. Its holding turns on legally unjustified oral reliance and the independently unproved advertising sequence, while the dissent contests whether the former should have been removed from factual judgment. (244–248.)
