# Bucquet v. Livingston
57 Cal. App. 3d 914, 129 Cal. Rptr. 514 (1976) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
The California Court of Appeal, First District, Division Two, reversed a judgment on the pleadings dismissing the remaining claims of beneficiaries against the attorney who prepared their parents' inter vivos trust. Presiding Justice Taylor wrote the May 3, 1976 opinion; Justices Kane and Rouse concurred. The court held that the allegations stated a malpractice cause of action, not that negligence or damages had been proved. Rehearing was denied June 2 and Supreme Court hearing July 1, 1976. (914, 916–917, 924, 926.)

## Estate plan and alleged drafting error
In July 1961 George engaged Livingston to plan his and his wife Ruby's estates with the object of minimizing federal estate and California inheritance taxes. The intended structure made one-half of George's trust available to Ruby with the marital deduction and made the other half available to her for life without inclusion in her taxable estate at death. Ultimately, income would pass to their daughter Barbara; after Barbara's death, the corpus would be distributed among her children following a $175,000 payment to her husband Howard. The beneficiaries were specifically identified. (918.)

Article IX, however, gave George and, after his death or adjudicated incompetency, Ruby the power to alter, revoke, or terminate the agreement. The complaint alleged that retaining this general power of appointment defeated the intended treatment of the nonmarital half. George died July 27, 1964. Livingston then represented Ruby as coexecutrix in probate and related tax matters and allegedly failed to explain the power's consequences or her ability to disclaim it. Their professional relationship continued until her death in September 1969. (918–919.)

Ruby incurred California inheritance taxes on the nonmarital half because the power treated her as owner rather than simply life tenant. The power also threatened inclusion of that half in her estate at her own death. On March 21, 1969 she renounced it and assigned her life estate; the assignment addressed uncertainty whether her late renunciation would be effective or merely a release. She died September 18. It was subsequently determined that the renunciation was effective and that the nonmarital half passed free of estate and inheritance taxes at her death. (919.)

That successful corrective action did not end the asserted injury. The remaining complaint alleged inheritance tax consequences at George's death, approximately $50,000 in federal and state gift taxes resulting from the remedial steps, and $3,750 in related legal fees. The gift taxes and fees were paid after Ruby's death. Plaintiffs alleged diminution of trust and estate assets, reducing what they received. These are allegations accepted in the present posture, not independently established damages findings. (917, 919–920.)

## Procedure and parties' positions
The beneficiaries filed suit in August 1970. They stipulated to dismissal of the first five causes after the determination that the nonmarital half would escape inclusion at Ruby's death. The sixth through eighth amended causes remained, alleging diminished beneficial shares and related fees. The trial court entered judgment on the pleadings for Livingston in November 1974, relying on Ventura County Humane Society v. Holloway and the stipulation. (917 & nn. 2–4; 919.)

The appellate question was whether the complaint stated a cause of action, taking its allegations as true. Note 5 explains the court's judicial notice of the stipulation, interrogatory responses, and apparently undisputed documents quoted in the trial brief. Livingston argued that the ultimate tax-free passage fulfilled George's intent, that Ruby rather than the trust bore the asserted charges, and that he owed no legally enforceable duty to the beneficiaries. The first two arguments did not defeat the pleaded diminution of assets and shares; factual disagreement could not be resolved against plaintiffs through this judgment on the pleadings. (917–920 & n. 5.)

## Duty, privity, and reasonably competent estate planning
Professional negligence requires duty, breach, proximate causation, and actual loss. The attorney's undertaking to use ordinary care, skill, judgment, and diligence can support rights of intended beneficiaries despite lack of direct privity, under a third-party contract or negligence theory. Liability is not automatic. The court applied the policy factors concerning intended effect, foreseeability, certainty of injury, causal connection, moral blame, and prevention of future harm. The principles developed for wills equally applied to this inter vivos trust. (920–922.)

The tax consequences of a general power in a marital-deduction trust fell within reasonably competent estate practice. Although tax law can be complex, the device was familiar, and the relevant federal provisions had existed well before this 1961 drafting. Note 7 distinguishes state-law attributes of a property power from federal classification of whether the power is general. The case concerned an alleged overlooked consequence of the actual clause, not a guarantee against every difficult issue or every later dispute. (922–923 & nn. 6–8.)

The engagement directly intended to benefit these designated beneficiaries by preserving the amount of their inheritance. The allegedly defective revocation language necessitated costly correction and foreseeably reduced that amount. Accepting the allegations, the beneficiaries therefore stated a claim. The court expressly reserved that trial might show no diminished trust assets, or show that George and Ruby had been advised but deliberately preferred the provision for other important family reasons. (923–924.)

Ventura involved a materially different problem: an attorney used his client's ambiguous charitable designation, the testamentary purpose could be carried out, and the claimant did not adequately allege an intended specific share. Here both the beneficiaries and tax-saving objective were specified, and loss could be measured if proved. Nevertheless, the court required neither litigation-proof documents nor consultation with a certified tax specialist in every estate plan. It acknowledged Livingston's reputation for competence and integrity and the absence of alleged moral blame, while finding the remaining policy considerations supported a cause of action. (924–925.)

## Who can enforce and exact disposition
The supplied account quotes the prevention-of-harm rationale. Ordinarily the estate itself loses no bequest through negligent disposition, apart from paid fees; the intended beneficiaries suffer that lost benefit. Their exclusion could leave the principal injury without an enforcer after the settlor's death. Ruby's estate's absence was consequently not fatal here. This rationale supports the beneficiaries' standing and duty analysis, not automatic recovery of every tax paid by an estate. (925–926.)
