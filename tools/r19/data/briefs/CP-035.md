Parties
Grable was the former owner of Michigan land seized to satisfy its federal tax debt, the state-court plaintiff
asserting superior title, and the Supreme Court petitioner challenging federal jurisdiction. Darue bought the
land at the tax sale, defended its recorded title, removed the case to federal court, and was the respondent.
The federal government’s collection activity was central to the dispute, but the government was not the
purchaser-defendant. Casebook pp. 342-343.
Material Facts
In 1994, the Internal Revenue Service seized Grable’s land to satisfy unpaid federal taxes. Grable actually
received certified-mail notice of the seizure before the sale, and also received notice of the sale. It did not
exercise the statutory right to redeem the property within 180 days, meaning regain it under the statutory
redemption procedure. After that period, the government gave Darue a quitclaim deed conveying the
government’s interest. Casebook pp. 342-343.
Five years later, Grable brought a quiet-title action, a suit seeking a judicial determination that its title was
superior to the competing claim. Grable asserted that 26 U.S.C. § 6335(a) required personal service of
seizure notice and that certified mail did not satisfy it. Its theory was that the defective federal notice
invalidated Darue’s title, not that Grable had failed to learn of the seizure. Casebook pp. 343-345.
Procedural History
Darue removed the case from state court to federal district court, invoking federal-question jurisdiction.
Removal shifts an eligible pending state action to federal court. The district court refused Grable’s request to
remand, or return, the action to state court. It concluded that the necessary federal-law issue supported
jurisdiction even though federal law created no private quiet-title claim against Darue. Casebook p. 343.
The district court then granted summary judgment to Darue, resolving the merits without trial. It held that
the notice statute required personal service but that substantial compliance sufficed. The Sixth Circuit
affirmed. The Supreme Court granted review of jurisdiction, not the proper interpretation of the notice
statute. The full opinion’s footnote 1 expressly confirms that limitation; the substantial-compliance ruling
remains the lower court’s merits determination, not the Supreme Court’s holding. Casebook p. 343; full
judicial opinion, n.1.

Issue
May a federal court hear a state-law quiet-title claim between nondiverse parties when the claim necessarily
depends on a disputed, substantial question about federal tax-sale notice, although Congress created no
federal private cause of action against the purchaser?
Rule and Holding
Yes. A state-law claim can support federal-question jurisdiction when it necessarily raises a stated federal
issue that is actually disputed and substantial, and a federal forum can decide the case without disturbing the
congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. The existence of a federal
cause of action is not an invariably necessary condition. Conversely, the mere appearance of a federal issue
is insufficient. Casebook pp. 343-344, 345-347.
Grable’s claim satisfied that standard. Federal notice law was an essential, genuinely disputed element of its
superior-title theory. The federal government had a strong interest in reliable tax collection and the validity
of titles arising from its sales. Such contested federal-title questions would be rare enough that accepting
jurisdiction would not materially shift ordinary state litigation into federal court. Casebook pp. 344-345,
346-347.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the Sixth Circuit’s judgment upholding federal jurisdiction. It did not decide
whether the IRS’s certified-mail notice complied with the statute. Casebook pp. 343, 347; full judicial
opinion, n.1.
Reasoning
Removal depended on whether the case could originally have been filed under § 1331, the federal-question
statute. Most such cases involve claims created by federal law, but longstanding precedent also allowed a
narrower category of state-created claims turning on important federal questions. The Court used Smith v.
Kansas City Title & Trust Co. as the classic example, where a state-law claim required resolution of a
federal constitutional issue. Casebook pp. 343-344.
That category did not extend to every application of federal law. A genuinely disputed federal issue had to
implicate a serious interest in a federal forum. Even then, the court had to consider whether federal
jurisdiction would disrupt Congress’s allocation of work between federal and state courts. Substantiality and
the institutional consequences of accepting the class of cases therefore constrained jurisdiction
independently of the presence of federal language. Casebook p. 344.
The federal issue here was necessary because Grable had to plead facts establishing superior title and chose
defective federal notice as its basis. The meaning of § 6335 appeared to be the only disputed legal or factual
issue. This was a dispute about the content of the governing federal rule, not merely the remote fact that a
chain of title once passed through a federal transaction. Casebook pp. 344-345.
The issue also mattered to federal administration beyond these parties. Reliable and prompt tax collection
required clear notice rules so purchasers could know whether they would receive valid title. The government
had an interest in a federal forum for testing its administrative acts, and buyers and delinquent taxpayers
could benefit from judges experienced in federal tax matters. The Court connected jurisdiction to those
identified interests rather than assuming every tax-related fact was sufficient. Casebook p. 345.

The expected effect on the federal-state division of labor was small. Ordinary state title cases rarely involve
contested federal-law questions of this type. Recognizing jurisdiction over this narrow category therefore
would not redirect the general run of state property cases to federal court. Casebook pp. 345, 346-347.
The Court rejected Grable’s reading of Merrell Dow as an absolute federal-cause-of-action requirement.
Read as a whole, that decision retained the contextual inquiry and approved Smith despite the absence of a
federal cause there. The absence of a private federal remedy remained evidence about congressional intent,
but it was not an automatic jurisdictional bar. Casebook pp. 345-346.
The difference from Merrell Dow lay particularly in the systemic consequences. Allowing federal
jurisdiction whenever a state negligence action invoked a federal regulatory standard could draw a very large
body of traditional state tort litigation into federal court. Congress’s failure to create a federal remedy
mattered greatly in that setting. The rare federal tax-title dispute presented no comparable shift, and its
specific federal administrative interests were strong. Casebook pp. 346-347.
Separate Opinions
Justice Thomas concurred because the Court faithfully applied its existing precedents and no party asked
that they be overruled. He nevertheless stated willingness, in an appropriate future case and with better
evidence of statutory original meaning, to consider restricting § 1331 to claims created by federal law. That
possible future approach was his separate suggestion, not the rule adopted in this case. Casebook p. 347
(Thomas, J., concurring).
Notes
Narrow reading — interpretation. A quiet-title claim directly contesting the IRS’s compliance with
federal seizure-notice law could be removed because the necessary federal issue was disputed, important to
federal tax administration, and unlikely to displace a substantial volume of ordinary state cases. Actual
receipt of notice did not eliminate the dispute over what the statute required. Casebook pp. 342-345.
Broad reading — interpretation. A federal private cause of action is not the sole path to § 1331
jurisdiction. An embedded federal issue in a state claim can qualify, but necessity, actual dispute, substantial
federal interest, and the effect on the federal-state judicial balance all matter. The decision rejects both a
categorical no-federal-remedy bar and a categorical any-federal-issue permission. Casebook pp. 344-347.
