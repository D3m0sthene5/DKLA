# Hill v. Gateway 2000, Inc.

105 F.3d 1147 (7th Cir. 1997)

## Citation, court, and assigned scope
The Seventh Circuit decided the appeal on January 6, 1997; rehearing was denied February 3. Judge Easterbrook wrote for a panel with Judges Cummings and Wood, Jr. The assigned excerpt at printed pages 294–297 presents the court's contract-formation analysis. The full opinion also states the FAA basis for interlocutory review, addresses the buyers' fraud and RICO arguments, and supplies the precise disposition. Numbered paragraph references below identify the OpenJurist transcription, not official reporter paragraph numbers. The Harvard reproduction provides a second reading copy of the opinion.

## Parties and requested relief
Rich and Enza Hill bought a computer directly from Gateway. They later sued over its components and performance and alleged, among other things, mail and wire fraud as predicates for a RICO claim. They sought relief for themselves and a proposed class, including RICO's treble damages. Gateway sought enforcement of the arbitration clause included in the documents shipped with the computer. The appeal concerned the forum for adjudicating those claims, not whether the computer was defective or Gateway had committed fraud. Opinion paras. 1–2, 12.

## Material facts
The Hills ordered the computer by telephone and provided a credit-card number. The computer arrived with a written statement of terms. Those terms said that they governed unless the buyer returned the computer within thirty days. One term required arbitration. The Hills retained the computer beyond thirty days before complaining about its performance. They acknowledged noticing the statement of terms but said they had not read it closely enough to discover the arbitration clause. Id. paras. 1–3.

The transaction included more than delivery of a physical machine. Gateway also offered warranties, support, and service, including a telephone hotline and advertised lifetime support. Those undertakings required future performance. The Hills relied on the warranty in seeking a remedy and were dissatisfied with Gateway's response. The same collection of written terms contained obligations favorable to the buyers and the disputed arbitration obligation. Id. para. 6.

Gateway's shipping carton did not display the same notice of enclosed legal terms that had appeared on the retail software packaging in ProCD. The Hills nevertheless knew before ordering that important terms would accompany the product: Gateway advertised limited warranties and lifetime support without stating every detail in the advertisement. The court treated this awareness and the opportunity to inspect and return as relevant. It did not find that the Hills had actually read or specifically negotiated the arbitration provision. Id. paras. 8–11.

## Procedural history and governing law
The district court refused to enforce arbitration because it considered the record insufficient to establish a valid agreement or adequate notice. Gateway immediately appealed under 9 U.S.C. § 16(a)(1)(A), which permits an appeal from denial of the specified arbitration relief. This was not review of a final adjudication of the buyers' underlying product claims. Id. para. 2.

The Seventh Circuit applied its ProCD formation analysis. Illinois and South Dakota were the possible sources of state law, both had enacted the UCC, and neither party identified a pertinent atypical doctrine in either state. The court therefore did not conduct an extended choice-of-law inquiry or announce that every state's law necessarily produces the same result. Id. para. 4.

## Issues
Could Gateway propose a contract whose complete written terms were delivered with the computer and accepted by retention beyond a stated return period? Did the absence of oral recitation, actual reading, special prominence for arbitration, or notice on the outside of the shipping carton defeat the agreement? Did § 2-207 treat the enclosed terms as later proposals, and did the buyers' consumer status change the result? Finally, did the buyers' fraud and RICO characterizations make their dispute nonarbitrable?

## Rules and holdings
Under the formation theory adopted in Hill and ProCD, a seller may make an accept-or-return proposal, provide terms with the product, and invite acceptance by keeping the product after an opportunity to read and reject those terms. Payment and an initial request for shipment need not be the exclusive moment at which all contractual terms are fixed. The Hills' retention beyond thirty days constituted acceptance of Gateway's offer, including arbitration. Id. paras. 4, 7, 11.

The FAA placed arbitration provisions on the same footing as other contract terms. The Hills could not invalidate the clause solely because it was not specially prominent or because they failed to read it. The court treated § 2-207 as inapplicable to this one-form formation arrangement, rather than applying its merchant/nonmerchant rules to a term added after an already concluded contract. The latter proposition is a contested feature of Hill's reasoning, directly challenged in Klocek. Id. paras. 3, 7.

## Actual judgment
The district court's decision was vacated, and the case was remanded with instructions to compel the Hills to submit their dispute to arbitration. The court did not award damages, approve the quality of the computer, or resolve whether Gateway had breached its warranty. It required a different adjudicative forum for the dispute. Id. para. 12.

## Reasoning
### Contract formation could occur after payment and delivery
The court's premise was that commercial actors may design the mode of acceptance. ProCD allowed customers to inspect both software and terms before deciding whether to keep the product. Gateway used the same general mechanism. A buyer's initial payment could initiate the transaction without foreclosing a later opportunity to accept or reject the full proposal. This premise explains why the court did not analyze the enclosed terms as an attempted unilateral modification of an existing bargain. Id. para. 4.

For examination purposes, the premise must be stated rather than smuggled into the conclusion. If a contract had already been formed on earlier terms, merely calling a later insert an offer would not resolve whether it could add arbitration. Hill instead located acceptance after the buyer had an opportunity to inspect the delivered terms. The later Klocek opinion disputes both that characterization on its record and the assumption that the vendor is necessarily the offeror.

### Commercial practicality supported written terms with an opportunity to return
Requiring a telephone operator to read every legal term before taking an order would consume time without reliably communicating the content. It also would generate disputes about what was read or understood. A written statement coupled with an inspection-and-return period, in the court's view, was more usable and less costly for buyers as a group. Similar timing occurs in transportation and insurance transactions. Id. para. 5.

The court rejected a software-only limitation on ProCD. That decision concerned contract formation, not a special exemption for intellectual-property products. In any event, Gateway's computer contained an operating system and application software, so the proposed factual distinction was weak as well. Id. para. 5.

### Unread terms and arbitration-specific objections were different questions
The Hills admitted noticing the statement of terms. The court applied the general principle that a person accepting a contractual document bears the risk of failing to read it. Their argument that arbitration should have stood out more demanded special treatment for that category of term, which the court rejected under FAA § 2 and Doctor's Associates v. Casarotto. Id. para. 3.

That reasoning is not a holding that notice never matters. The court relied on the opportunity to inspect, the stated thirty-day return mechanism, and awareness that important terms would arrive with the product. The analytical sequence was formation of a contract on the written terms, then equal treatment of its arbitration provision. An arbitration-specific policy could not itself supply assent if there were no agreement.

### Continuing obligations answered the completed-performance distinction
The buyers argued that ProCD involved an executory license whereas their purchase was complete when the box arrived. The court first distinguished contract formation from performance. It then rejected the factual premise: Gateway still owed warranty and support duties after delivery. The Hills themselves invoked those duties. A set of terms containing those promises did not cease to matter merely because the machine had physically arrived. Id. para. 6.

### Section 2-207 and merchant status did not control on the court's characterization
The Hills argued that they were consumers and that additional terms therefore could not enter automatically under § 2-207(2). The court responded that ProCD had not depended on the purchaser's being a merchant. It had instead declared § 2-207 irrelevant to the one-form situation and treated the question as when the contract was formed. On that approach the customers accepted the offered terms after inspection; the terms were not additions to a prior contract. Id. para. 7.

This must remain attributed to Hill. Section 2-207's text also refers to confirmations of earlier agreements, and Klocek later rejected the categorical one-form exclusion. A student should identify the competing formation accounts before asking whether a consumer's silence accepts an additional term. Treating Hill as a general rule that consumers automatically accept all postpurchase terms would omit the very controversy the paired cases are designed to expose.

### The shipping carton did not resolve notice, and an expense problem remained open
The court distinguished packaging displayed on a retail shelf from a carton used only for transportation. Outside notice can inform a shopper before a store purchase; shipping labels generally address handlers after the remote order has been placed. The absence of a retail-style exterior warning therefore did not distinguish the legal issue by itself. Id. paras. 8–10.

The opinion did acknowledge a harder case: a buyer first learning of the legal terms after opening the package might want to return the product but be deterred by shipping expense. The court raised a possible remedy, potentially related to that expense, without deciding it. The Hills' prior awareness of important forthcoming terms made resolution unnecessary. Available ways of investigating terms included requesting them before ordering, consulting public information, or inspecting them on delivery. Id. para. 11.

### The claim labels did not defeat arbitration
The opinion disposed briefly of the assertion that the arbitration provision participated in a scheme to defraud, citing Prima Paint. It separately cited McMahon for the arbitrability of RICO claims. That short discussion should not be expanded into an unsupported factual finding that no fraud occurred. The holding concerned arbitration of the asserted dispute. Id. para. 12.

## Separate opinions
No separate opinion is reported. The panel's decision is distinct from Klocek's later disagreement; Klocek is not a dissent in Hill and did not reverse the Seventh Circuit.

## Use and limits
The central issue is identifying the offer, the acceptance, and the point at which the parties' obligations become fixed. Under Hill's theory, retaining the product after the stated review period accepts the vendor's proposal. Under the approach later taken in Klocek, an earlier sale may already exist and the insert may merely propose additional terms. The difference is doctrinal and evidentiary, not simply thirty days versus five days. Hill is a historical decision, not a current nationwide certification of every form of delayed contractual notice.
