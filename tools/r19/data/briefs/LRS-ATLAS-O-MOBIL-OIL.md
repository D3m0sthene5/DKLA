# Mobil Oil Corp. v. Federal Power Commission
483 F.2d 1238 (D.C. Cir. 1973), as amended July 26, 1973 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, parties, and result

Judge Wilkey wrote for a panel comprising Judges Leventhal and Wilkey and Senior District Judge Jameson, sitting by designation. The opinion was decided July 11, 1973 and amended July 26. No separate writing or dissent appears. Mobil sought review of the Federal Power Commission’s nationwide minimum charges for pipeline transportation of producers’ liquid hydrocarbons and liquefiables. The court vacated the challenged orders and remanded. It found no statutory jurisdiction over liquid petroleum and, independently, found that the agency had inadequately exposed and tested the factual basis for its rates. Jurisdiction over liquefiables was uncontested; their treatment nevertheless required remand for the procedural and evidentiary reasons. (1238, 1240–1241, 1246–1249, 1263–1264.)

## The transportation problem and the Commission’s change of approach

Production from natural-gas wells could include liquid petroleum, substances gaseous when produced but subsequently liquefied, and gas itself. Producers selling gas sometimes retained ownership of the liquids and liquefiables while sending them through a pipeline together with the gas. Separation at the well was not always economically feasible. Carrying the retained products occupied capacity and incurred costs; insufficient payments from their owners could shift costs to gas consumers. (1241–1243.)

Between 1954 and 1968 the Commission addressed the problem through cost allocation in individual certificate and rate proceedings. It could exclude nongas transportation costs when determining charges for gas. That approach differed from fixing mandatory transportation prices for the producers’ retained products. In February 1968 the Commission proposed a general cost-allocation policy. Its notice did not announce the nationwide minimum transportation rates eventually adopted. Fifteen producers, eleven pipelines, and two state agencies commented; only the state agencies supported the proposal. The initial comments did not provide the cost data later used to support the minimum charges. (1241–1244 & nn.3–4.)

## Conferences and an untested settlement submission

After approximately two and a half years, the Commission announced a September 29, 1970 conference. A Commission attorney conducted it. No transcript was made; paraphrased minutes supplied the account. Staff sought factual information, but participants postponed further consideration pending a Southern Louisiana settlement. At an October conference, pipeline counsel supplied nine sheets of cost-of-service figures prepared for that settlement. The figures had not been sponsored and examined as evidence, subjected to cross-examination, or confronted with opposing evidence. They were not developed for the nationwide rule now at issue. Further conferences again were deferred. (1244–1245 & nn.7–9.)

Without another notice or conference, the Commission issued Order 449 on January 31, 1972. It adopted national minimum transportation charges for both liquids and liquefiables and stated that cost allocation was inadequate without explaining why its former method had ceased to protect gas consumers. For liquefiables the charge was two cents per Mcf per hundred miles; for liquids it was twenty cents per barrel. The regulation applied its minimum-charge conditions to the covered contractual and certificate arrangements. Mobil unsuccessfully sought rehearing and petitioned for review. (1245–1246 & nn.10–13.)

Mobil challenged statutory power, use of privileged settlement material, procedural adequacy, substantial evidentiary support, and discrimination or preference. The court rejected its settlement-privilege objection: under the Commission’s previously sustained interpretation, the protection belonged to the offeror, and Mobil had not made the offer. That conclusion did not transform the unexamined figures into a lawful evidentiary basis for the rule. The discrimination issue was not separately resolved on an inadequate record. (1246 & nn.12–13.)

## Statutory power over liquids

The Natural Gas Act conferred jurisdiction over natural gas, not liquid petroleum. The Commission argued that the liquids were involved in a larger transaction within its jurisdiction and that minimum liquid transportation charges protected gas consumers from subsidizing producers. The court rejected those arguments as authority to regulate an otherwise excluded product. Decisions addressing gas production, extraction, and pipeline matters did not enlarge the statutory definition to embrace liquid petroleum. Congress, rather than an administrative assertion of necessity, had to supply the additional power. (1246–1249.)

The former cost-allocation method also weakened the claimed necessity: the Commission had shown neither why that method failed nor why fixing liquid transportation prices was indispensable. Costs attributable to nongas service could be withheld from permissible gas charges without directly regulating the nongas service. The court therefore set aside the liquid-price provisions for want of jurisdiction and turned to the procedure governing the uncontested jurisdiction over liquefiables. (1248–1249.)

## Formal APA procedures were not categorically required

The court did not hold that the agency had to conduct full trial-type rulemaking under APA §§ 556 and 557. The Natural Gas Act did not contain the required “on the record” direction, and its legislative background did not supply the necessary intent. United States v. Florida East Coast Railway controlled that formal-procedure question. Mobil thus could not demand all formal APA hearing incidents simply from the subject matter or the statutory hearing references. (1249–1251.)

The court nevertheless treated the choice as more flexible than a binary decision between full formal procedure and untested written comments. Its discussion of prior decisions described intermediate procedures capable of testing disputed factual premises: written questions, prescreening, and limited cross-examination could be adapted to the issues without requiring unrestricted oral examination. This was the court’s historical approach to the Natural Gas Act’s evidentiary obligations, not a holding that every informal rulemaking must use those techniques. (1251–1254.)

## Section 16 could not dispense with an evidentiary foundation

The Commission relied on its power under § 16 to do what was necessary or appropriate to implement the Act. The court read that provision as implementing substantive statutory powers, not conferring independent jurisdiction or permission to avoid their safeguards. Administrative flexibility recognized in the Permian Basin litigation allowed practical area-rate regulation; it did not permit fixed rates founded on an untested factual record. The earlier Mobil decision sustaining a jurisdiction-preserving moratorium likewise did not validate the actual rate determination here. (1254–1257.)

The court also applied the Natural Gas Act’s substantial-evidence review requirement, then codified at 15 U.S.C. § 717r(b). Agency findings had to withstand examination of the whole record, including contrary evidence. Although the Attorney General’s manual associated substantial-evidence review with formal proceedings, the court declined to infer that every formal hearing incident followed. It instead concluded that this statute still required meaningful development and testing of the factual premises essential to the rates. (1257–1259 & n.68.)

## Why this record failed

The Commission relied on material never introduced with notice that it would form the nationwide rule’s evidentiary foundation. Interested parties had no adequate opportunity to rebut it. The court reasoned that a collection of informal comments did not supply the requisite whole evidentiary record in these circumstances. The settlement figures covered ten of 116 pipelines without a demonstration of representativeness and used different test years. The court could not fully evaluate later objections that the figures overstated expenses because the necessary record had never been developed. A separate Southern Louisiana proceeding had a fuller record; that did not cure the evidentiary omissions in this one. (1259–1260 & nn.78–79.)

Florida East Coast Railway did not resolve this statutory substantial-evidence problem: the Interstate Commerce Commission proceeding there lacked the same congressional review requirement. The court also rejected the Tenth Circuit Phillips majority’s reasoning and favored Judge Seth’s dissent in that other case. Those references are comparative reasoning, not separate writings in Mobil itself. (1260–1262.)

## What the remand required and what remained open

An adequate procedure had to let participants test the rates’ factual predicates and place relevant material in a record subject to meaningful review. Full §§ 556–557 procedures would satisfy that obligation but were not required. Written questioning and screened or limited oral cross-examination could be appropriate alternatives. The court described possibilities rather than prescribing an invariant hearing format. A transcript would remedy the inadequate conference minutes; written questions could provide testing that ordinary comments had not supplied. (1262–1263 & nn.91–92.)

The court did not prohibit incorporating evidence from another proceeding. Note 93 required more than importing agency conclusions or untested settlement material: the full relevant evidence had to have undergone testing, with notice and an opportunity for affected parties to comment. At argument, the Commission acknowledged that nine of the ten pipelines’ cost data used here had been found too high in later individual proceedings. That inconsistency underscored the need to examine evidence before crediting it in a national rate rule. (1263 & n.93.)

The final order vacated the orders and remanded to the Commission. The agency first had to explain why mandatory liquefiables transportation charges were needed instead of its former cost-allocation method, then develop a record adequate to justify any lawful rates. Even if the court’s jurisdictional and broader procedural conclusions were rejected, the absence of fair notice and an adequate record independently required remand. The decision did not adjudicate an appropriate rate amount or establish that all disputed cost figures were false. (1263–1264 & n.95.)
