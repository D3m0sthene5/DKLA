# Guedes v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives
356 F. Supp. 3d 109 (D.D.C. Feb. 25, 2019) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## The rule, the litigants, and the requested relief

Following the October 2017 Las Vegas mass shooting, ATF reconsidered its prior treatment of bump stocks. A standard bump stock replaced a semiautomatic rifle’s stock and allowed recoil to move the weapon back and forth against a maintained trigger finger. Unlike the spring-equipped Akins Accelerator that ATF had classified as a machine gun in 2006, standard versions had previously been excluded because ATF considered their firing insufficiently automatic. The parties agreed on the basic mechanics; the contested question was their legal classification. (119–120, 122–124.)

The National Firearms Act defined “machinegun” as a weapon firing automatically more than one shot, without manual reloading, by a single function of the trigger, and covered certain conversion parts and combinations. The Firearm Owners Protection Act incorporated that definition and generally prohibited possession or transfer of newly manufactured machine guns. Under the Attorney General’s rulemaking authority, ATF’s December 26, 2018 rule defined a single trigger function as a single pull and analogous motions, and automatic operation as a self-acting or self-regulating mechanism permitting multiple rounds through such a function. It expressly classified bump-stock-type devices as machine guns. Existing owners would have to destroy or abandon them when the rule became effective on March 26, 2019. (122–124, 128.)

Three preliminary-injunction motions challenged that rule. Damien Guedes and associated organizations initially sued together on December 18, 2018; the Firearms Policy Coalition then dismissed its claims from that suit and brought a separate action emphasizing Acting Attorney General Matthew Whitaker’s authority. That action was later consolidated with Guedes. David Codrea’s separate action was transferred as related but, at the parties’ request, was not consolidated. Guedes’s amended complaint omitted the appointment challenge, although the collective litigation still presented it through the other plaintiffs. Government-shutdown stays, subsequent lifting of the stays, and hearings preceded this February 25 decision resolving all three motions. William Barr had by then replaced Whitaker and was automatically substituted as defendant under Rule 25(d). (119 n.1, 121–122.)

Plaintiffs asserted APA defects in interpretation, explanation, disclosure, and response to comments; Guedes also invoked the statutory hearing and ninety-day notice requirements. Codrea added a Takings Clause claim. The Coalition principally developed the statutory and constitutional attack on Whitaker’s appointment. Judge Dabney Friedrich, the sole deciding district judge, denied preliminary relief across all three motions. This is the district court’s preliminary-relief decision, not the separately cited appeal dismissal or appellate affirmance. (120–121, 137–138 & n.6, 155.)

## Preliminary relief and the historical statutory interpretation

The court required a clear showing of likely success, likely irreparable injury, favorable equities, and accord with the public interest. Failure to establish likely success made examination of the remaining factors unnecessary. The APA required lawful, reasoned decisionmaking, including a rational connection between record facts and the agency’s choice. This opinion applied the then-governing Chevron framework: unambiguous congressional instructions controlled, while a permissible agency interpretation of ambiguity received deference. A changed policy required acknowledgment and good reasons, rather than a heightened substantive standard simply because it differed from prior policy. The brief describes that historical analysis without certifying the framework as current law. (126–128.)

For a single trigger function, contemporary dictionaries did not resolve whether Congress meant each mechanical resetting of the trigger or the shooter’s single initiating pull. Friedrich considered both interpretations reasonable. ATF’s shooter-centered reading received support from prior decisions describing machine guns in terms of a single pull, including Staples and Akins. For automatic operation, dictionaries referred to self-acting mechanisms performing work previously done by hand, but did not specify how much manual input remained permissible. Even an automatic sewing machine required human input. The court thus found ambiguity in both relevant terms and accepted ATF’s construction as reasonable. (129–131.)

Applying those constructions, ATF could treat maintained pressure and recoil-directed operation as a single pull followed by automatic firing. Repeated contact between the trigger and finger did not require repeated conscious trigger pulls. Nor did that interpretation sweep in all semiautomatic rifles, which without the device required a separate pull for each cartridge. Whether bump stocks were automatic was the closer question: the user still maintained forward and rearward pressure, but the device controlled recoil distance and linear motion that otherwise required manual management. The court upheld the agency’s reasonableness analysis, rather than holding that its reading was the only textually possible one. (131–133.)

## Explanation, comments, hearing, and the takings claim

ATF acknowledged its departure from earlier classifications, described the Akins history, and explained why its new legal analysis better interpreted the statutory terms. Earlier standard-bump-stock determinations had lacked extensive legal analysis. The changed construction was therefore sufficiently explained. Those prior legal opinions did not become critical factual material supporting the new rule that had to be disclosed for rebuttal: the device’s basic operation was undisputed, and the old legal interpretations contradicted the new position. Likewise, the Las Vegas shooting prompted reconsideration, but crime statistics were not the rule’s evidentiary premise. (133–135.)

The court rejected objections concerning comparative firing speeds, a former official’s views, and a demonstration video. The legal classification depended on device function rather than whether some unaided shooter could fire faster. An agency had to respond to relevant, significant objections, but not discuss every submitted item individually. ATF addressed the central operational arguments. It distinguished belt loops and rubber bands because those manual methods did not supply a device capturing and directing recoil, while leaving formal classification of those items unresolved. Binary triggers required separate pull and release functions for their two rounds. Those reasoned responses satisfied the APA even though plaintiffs disagreed with them. (134–136 & nn.4–5.)

Section 926(b)’s opportunity-for-hearing requirement did not compel an oral hearing. Applying Florida East Coast Railway and the Fourth Circuit’s interpretation of this provision, the court treated written comment opportunities as sufficient absent a contrary requirement for proceedings on the record. Technical problems during the first five days of the scheduled ninety-day period did not warrant relief: commenters could reach the proper Regulations.gov page, some comments arrived during that period, mail and facsimile remained available, and online submission continued for the remaining eighty-five days. Any failure to extend the period was harmless because Guedes showed no prejudice. (136–137.)

Codrea’s takings theory did not justify an injunction regardless of its underlying merits. The Takings Clause generally secured compensation rather than prohibiting an otherwise authorized taking before payment. Plaintiffs had not shown that Tucker Act or Little Tucker Act compensation was inadequate or identified an applicable exception. The court therefore reserved whether the rule actually effected a compensable taking while denying this form of preliminary relief. (137.)

## Why the Attorney General succession statute did not displace the FVRA

Sessions resigned in November 2018, and the President selected his chief of staff, Whitaker, to exercise the Attorney General’s duties under the FVRA. The parties did not dispute Whitaker’s statutory eligibility: § 3345(a)(3) permitted selection of an agency officer or employee who had served ninety of the preceding 365 days at the specified senior pay level. Whitaker’s acting service ended when Barr became Attorney General in February 2019. The court described it as one hundred days. (125–126, 138, 146 n.11, 153.)

The Coalition argued that 28 U.S.C. § 508’s succession sequence had to be exhausted first: an available Deputy Attorney General, then the Associate Attorney General and designated further successors, displaced presidential FVRA choice. Friedrich rejected that reading. Section 3347’s exclusivity-unless structure made the FVRA nonexclusive when another succession statute applied, not unavailable. Hooks and English supported compatible statutory alternatives. Congress had removed the old Attorney General exception when enacting the FVRA, and § 508 expressly identified the Deputy Attorney General as first assistant for purposes of § 3345. An exhaustion interpretation deprived that cross-reference of sensible effect. (138–141 & nn.7–8.)

The statutes could coexist: the Deputy “may” serve under § 508, and the Associate’s “shall” provision governed when the President did not exercise his FVRA authority and the deputy was unavailable. Specific-over-general reasoning did not help because the statutes retained different eligibility and duration rules; neither became a nullity. Legislative history from the preceding bill supported an alternative mechanism, while vague descriptions of agency statutes as exceptions did not overcome the enacted structure. Importantly, note 9 rejected the government’s additional argument that a few recent FVRA designations demonstrated congressional acquiescence. The court’s acceptance of statutory compatibility did not mean acceptance of every government argument. Constitutional avoidance supplied no contrary construction because ordinary textual analysis resolved the question and the appointment did not raise the asserted serious constitutional doubt. (140–144 & n.9.)

## The Appointments Clause reasoning and its boundaries

The Coalition argued that a non-Senate-confirmed acting head could constitutionally serve only as a first assistant whose existing subordinate duties included temporary substitution. Friedrich instead relied on Eaton’s rule that performance of a superior’s duties for a limited time under special, temporary conditions did not transform the subordinate into the permanent principal officer. Eaton involved a missionary appointed shortly before the consul’s departure, not a convincing example of continuous supervision. Morrison and Edmond had preserved Eaton’s temporary-service reasoning. (144–148 & n.12.)

Early statutes permitted broad presidential selection for temporary departmental vacancies, later statutes restricted candidates and duration, and executive practice included discretionary appointment of chief clerks, other department heads, and some persons outside government. The court considered the historical justification to be temporary service rather than a preexisting first-assistant relationship. It also found the Coalition’s supervisory explanation incomplete: after a superior died or resigned, the assistant was not supervised during the very period needing justification, and anticipated later ratification could equally support another acting official. Prior Senate confirmation in an unrelated office did not itself explain constitutionality unless duties were germane. (148–153 & nn.13–16.)

The court nevertheless preserved a limit. Congress could tighten statutory requirements, and courts could police effectively permanent appointments masquerading as temporary ones. Plaintiffs did not contend that Whitaker’s one-hundred-day service was such a pretext. His temporary service following Sessions’s resignation met the Eaton test and did not make him a principal officer requiring a new Senate-confirmed appointment. (153.)

Finally, Friedrich assumed without deciding that Whitaker had previously been an employee and that an appointment was needed. Presidential direction under the FVRA was constitutionally an appointment; Congress did not have to use a particular word. Weiss’s discussion of military assignment statutes concerned whether a second appointment had been required, not whether a statute intended to confer appointment authority failed for want of terminology. Conversely, if temporary duties meant that Whitaker could not be an officer under plaintiffs’ continuing-position theory, that theory would eliminate the constitutional need for appointment rather than invalidate his service. The court rejected both alternative challenges, not simply on the assumption that Whitaker was already an inferior officer as chief of staff. (153–155 & n.17.)

## Exact judgment and assignment limits

The court denied all three preliminary-injunction motions because plaintiffs had not established entitlement to that remedy. It did not finally dismiss their complete complaints, decide the takings merits, or issue an appellate affirmance. There was no separate judicial writing. The complete opinion and all seventeen notes have been read, including the caption’s Rule 25(d) substitution note omitted from the CAP opinion-only text extraction. (119 n.1, 155.)
