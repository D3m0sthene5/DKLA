# Burnham v. Karl & Gelb, P.C.
252 Conn. 153, 745 A.2d 178 (2000) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and disposition

The Connecticut Supreme Court decided this certified appeal on February 15, 2000. Justice Sullivan's opinion was joined by Justices Norcott, Callahan, and Mihalakos; Chief Justice McDonald dissented. The court affirmed the Appellate Court's judgment upholding summary judgment for the dental-practice defendants. Certification concerned four questions about the wrongful-discharge claim, not renewed review of the separately rejected contract and negligent-misrepresentation claims. The case did not adjudicate that the reported dental practices actually violated OSHA or that retaliatory motivation had been proved. (252 Conn. at 153–158, 171.)

## Complaint, termination, and litigation

Karl & Gelb, a West Hartford dental practice employing periodontists Edward Karl and David Gelb, hired Carole Burnham as office manager on July 6, 1993. On November 5 she anonymously complained to the Connecticut State Dental Association that the practice employed unsanitary and unhealthy procedures violating the federal Occupational Safety and Health Act. The defendants terminated her on November 22. She then complained to the Hartford OSHA office that she had been fired in retaliation for her association complaint. The agency administratively closed the matter in February 1994 after she failed to respond to correspondence. (Id. at 155.)

Her amended state-court complaint alleged breach of contract, negligent misrepresentation, and wrongful termination. The trial court granted defendants summary judgment on all three counts, and the Appellate Court affirmed. The Supreme Court accepted four certified issues: whether an OSHA remedy precluded the public-policy tort; whether such a remedy existed; whether there was a factual dispute concerning exhaustion; and whether a refusal to work under dangerously unsafe conditions supported another wrongful-discharge theory. On the undisputed material facts, review of the legal conclusions was plenary. (Id. at 155–158.)

## Narrow public-policy exception and the state statute

The court began with employment at will and the narrow exception recognized in Sheets for a dismissal whose demonstrably improper reason derives from an important public-policy violation. It also adopted Atkins's remedial limitation: the exception addresses circumstances in which the employee otherwise has no remedy and an important policy would go unvindicated. Public-policy importance alone therefore did not create another tort remedy. (Id. at 159–160.)

Burnham relied upon the policy of General Statutes § 31-51m against employer retaliation for reports of legal violations to a public body. The Dental Association was not shown to fall within the statute's definition of a public body; Burnham neither disputed that conclusion nor supplied evidence creating a material factual issue. Although she was invoking the statute's policy rather than suing directly under it, the majority required evidence supporting a violation of the policy embodied in its actual terms. It would not remove the public-body limitation merely because her action had a common-law label. Footnote 4 explains the court's disagreement with the intermediate court's contrary treatment of this point. (Id. at 160–161 & n. 4.)

Alternatively, assuming conduct contrary to the policy of § 31-51m, subsection (c) supplied its own civil remedy after exhaustion of available administrative remedies, within the specified limitations period. That statutory remedy independently precluded a common-law claim based upon the same statute. This was a conditional alternative, not a determination that the private association actually qualified as a public body. (Id. at 161–162.)

## Federal remedy and adequacy

A further ground was the remedy under 29 U.S.C. § 660(c). An employee claiming OSHA-related retaliation could complain to the Secretary of Labor; if investigation established a violation, the Secretary would bring a federal action, in which appropriate relief included reinstatement and back pay. Unlike the Connecticut whistleblower statute, the federal provision did not require the original complaint to be made to a public body. Thus, the private Dental Association complaint could fall within its antiretaliation protection. Footnote 6 additionally identified a similar state administrative remedy under § 31-379, although neither party had cited it. (Id. at 162–164 & nn. 5–6.)

Burnham argued that agency-controlled enforcement was inadequate compared with an action she could prosecute herself. The majority acknowledged the remedies were not equivalent. But equivalence was not its standard: a plaintiff's preference for a better remedy did not authorize courts to supplement the statutory scheme. Presuming the legislature would have supplied additional relief had it considered that necessary, and reiterating the exception's narrow character, the court held that the available federal remedy precluded this common-law claim. It recognized contrary authority rather than presenting its conclusion as unanimous among jurisdictions. (Id. at 163–165.)

## Exhaustion and unpreserved unsafe-work theory

The exhaustion analysis was an alternative basis for affirmance. Even a factual dispute on that question would not overcome the independently dispositive existence of the statutory remedy. Footnote 7 corrected the certified question's premise: the Appellate Court had not actually reached exhaustion, so the Supreme Court reviewed the trial court's determination. (Id. at 166–167 & n. 7.)

The agency received Burnham's complaint around November 22, 1993 and had told her to report address changes. She attributed missed correspondence to moving, but did not claim to have notified the agency. Its uncontested chronology showed a February 14 warning that the case would close unless she returned a complaint statement by February 18. She returned it only on March 15 and learned of the closure on March 30, before bringing suit on April 20. The record did not contain the asserted February communications preserving pursuit of the complaint. Failure timely to respond meant she had not properly exhausted the available administrative route. (Id. at 167–169 & n. 8.)

The claimed discharge for refusing dangerous work was different from the retaliation-for-reporting claim presented below. Neither the pleading nor the summary-judgment decision addressed that theory; Burnham conceded that the legal argument had not been raised at trial. The court therefore declined to review it. Footnote 9 reserved the additional question whether the federal remedy would also preclude such a theory if it had been preserved. The opinion did not decide that every refusal-to-work claim necessarily failed on its substantive merits. (Id. at 169–171 & n. 9.)

## McDonald's dissent

McDonald disagreed that the OSHA enforcement scheme supplied an adequate substitute. He accepted that statutory remedies could displace a public-policy tort when adequate, but stressed that the employee could not control prosecution: the Secretary's discretion could reflect budgetary or political considerations and leave a genuine retaliatory discharge unredressed. The statute did not expressly make the administrative route exclusive. In his view, permitting the employee's own tort action could vindicate the same important federal policy when agency enforcement did not. He accordingly dissented from the conclusion precluding the common-law action. (Id. at 171–173.)
