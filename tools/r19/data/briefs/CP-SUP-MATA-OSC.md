# Mata v. Avianca, Inc. — May 4, 2023 Order to Show Cause
2023 WL 3696209 (S.D.N.Y. May 4, 2023), No. 22-cv-1461 (PKC), ECF 31 | Civil Procedure

## Setting and procedural posture

In opposing Avianca’s motion to dismiss, plaintiff Roberto Mata’s counsel filed an affirmation containing numerous citations to purported judicial decisions. The cited material supported, among other arguments, the position that a bankruptcy stay tolled the Montreal Convention limitations period. Avianca’s counsel alerted the court that some authorities could not be found. Judge P. Kevin Castel then ordered plaintiff’s counsel to submit an affidavit attaching copies of particular cited opinions. Counsel filed the requested material, but six of the supplied decisions appeared to be fabricated, including their quotations and internal citations. The May 4 order begins a sanctions inquiry into those submissions; it does not resolve the underlying airline claim. ECF 31 at 1.

The leading purported authority was *Varghese v. China South Airlines Ltd.*, assigned a 2019 Eleventh Circuit citation and docket number. Counsel submitted what purported to be at least an excerpt of that decision. The court did more than rely on the opponent’s inability to find it. It contacted the Eleventh Circuit Clerk, who confirmed that no case with a party named Vargese or Varghese had appeared in that circuit since its present electronic filing system began in 2010. The supplied docket number belonged to *George Cornea v. U.S. Attorney General*, and the supplied reporter location belonged to an unrelated D.C. Circuit decision, *A.D. v. Azar*. Neither Westlaw nor Lexis contained the claimed authority. ECF 31 at 1–2.

## Supporting evidence identified in the order

The purported Varghese opinion itself cited additional nonexistent authorities. Its supposed Eleventh Circuit *Zicherman* decision occupied a reporter location belonging to *Miccosukee Tribe v. United States*. Its supposed *Holliday* decision used the citation for *Gibbs v. Maxwell House*. Its supposed *Hyatt* citation pointed to two brief orders in other cases. Its supposed *Zaunbrecher* decision used a location belonging to *Witt v. Metropolitan Life Insurance*. These mismatches showed that the problem involved manufactured judicial content and internal support, rather than merely a misspelled party name or an incomplete database search. ECF 31 at 2.

Five additional supplied opinions displayed similar defects: purported decisions called *Shaboon*, *Petersen*, *Martinez*, *Estate of Durden*, and *Miller*. The court described them as apparently fake, preserving the order’s preliminary posture. Counsel’s affidavit said the supplied texts might reflect only what was available in an online database, called Shaboon unpublished, and acknowledged inability to locate an additional Zicherman decision. Those explanations did not resolve the discrepancies sufficiently to dispense with a hearing. ECF 31 at 2–3 & n.1.

The court also identified a separate affidavit anomaly. The document filed April 25 in response to orders issued April 11 and 12 purported to have been sworn before a notary on January 25. Counsel was directed to address the date discrepancy and bring the original signed affidavit bearing the notary’s signature and stamp to the hearing. The May 4 order identifies an issue for inquiry; it does not itself make the later findings about how that document was prepared. ECF 31 at 3 n.2.

## Issue and operative ruling

The immediate issue was whether the apparent submission of nonexistent authorities and nonexistent opinion texts justified requiring counsel to explain why sanctions should not be imposed. The court ordered Peter LoDuca, Mata’s counsel, to appear personally at noon on June 8, 2023, in Courtroom 11D at 500 Pearl Street, New York. He had to show cause concerning two specified acts: citing nonexistent cases in the opposition affirmation, ECF 21, and furnishing purported judicial opinions as attachments to the April 25 affidavit, ECF 29. ECF 31 at 3.

The court identified three possible sources of sanctions authority: Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11(b)(2) and (c), 28 U.S.C. § 1927, and the court’s inherent power. It did not finally decide their application, make a final bad-faith determination, or impose a monetary sanction in this order. A single District Judge issued the order; there is no appellate vote or separate opinion. ECF 31 at 3.

## Notice and opportunity to respond

LoDuca had to file a written response by May 26. If he wished to call live witnesses at the hearing, subject to cross-examination, that response had to identify them. Avianca could respond by June 2. The requirements supplied notice of the particular questioned filings, the legal authorities potentially supporting sanctions, and an opportunity for explanation and evidence before final sanctions adjudication. The court also required personal appearance and the original affidavit so that documentary and testimonial issues could be addressed directly. ECF 31 at 3.

For classroom purposes, Rule 11(b)(2) concerns the legal support for a paper presented to the court, while Rule 11(c) provides the sanctions mechanism. The order invokes those provisions but does not fully develop the eventual standards of liability or amount. Its listing of § 1927 and inherent power identifies possible alternatives for the inquiry; it does not hold that every inaccurate citation automatically establishes liability under all three sources.

## Relationship to the later sanctions decision and source scope

This is the May 4 show-cause order, distinct from the June 22 final opinion and order on sanctions. The former establishes the inquiry and hearing obligations; the latter makes factual and legal findings and orders sanctions after further submissions and a hearing. The May 4 text does not identify ChatGPT as the source of the fabricated material. Attributing that explanation to this order would import later-developed facts into its earlier procedural stage.
