# Hayes v. Plantations Steel Co.
438 A.2d 1091 (R.I. 1982) | Contracts

## Retirement preceded the company's assurance
Edward J. Hayes worked for Plantations Steel from1947 until1972, beginning as an estimator and draftsman and eventually becoming general manager. In January1972 he announced that he would retire the following July, at sixty-five, after twenty-five years with Plantations and fifty-one years of continuous work overall. He testified that he expected a pension and would not otherwise have retired, but the Supreme Court emphasized that no company statement or conduct had induced the January decision. (438 A.2d at 1093,1094–1095.)

Approximately one week before retirement, officer and shareholder Hugo R. Mainelli, Jr., told Hayes that the company would take care of him. This was their first and only conversation about pension payments; no amount or salary percentage was specified. The shareholders and directors had not formally authorized the payments. The company had no pension provision for employees outside its union arrangement, and Hayes was not a union member. Mainelli testified that his father authorized the first payment as appreciation for past service and that he personally intended payments to continue while he remained around. (Id. at 1093.)

Plantations paid $5,000 annually from January1973 through January1976, totaling $20,000. Hayes did not take other employment. Each year he visited his former colleagues, thanked Mainelli for the prior check, and asked how long payments would continue so that he could plan retirement. Poor business years and a shareholder dispute, culminating in the DiMartino family's control, contributed to stopping payments after1976. Hayes sued in December1977. (Id. at 1093,1097.)

## The trial judgment and both appeals
After a nonjury trial, the Superior Court found an implied-in-fact lifetime pension contract. It treated voluntary retirement as consideration and the four $5,000 payments as establishing an initially unspecified amount. Alternatively, it found promissory estoppel, reasoning that Hayes retired and refrained from seeking other work in reliance on the assurance. It awarded $5,000 annually for1977 through1979. The trial court rejected Hayes's ERISA attorney-fee theory because it found the payments outside that statute. Plantations appealed the liability judgment; Hayes cross-appealed regarding fees. (Id. at 1092–1094.)

Justice Shea wrote the Supreme Court's January6,1982 opinion. Nonjury factual findings ordinarily received great weight and would be disturbed only if clearly wrong or based on overlooked or misconceived material evidence. After reviewing the record, however, the court reversed. The complete report supplies no numerical vote or separate writing and contains no judicial footnotes. (Id. at 1094,1097.)

## Implied assent did not eliminate consideration
For analysis, the court assumed that Plantations had made a legally relevant promise. It did not treat Mainelli's assurance, standing alone, as a definite express pension promise. Any agreement had to be inferred from the surrounding words and conduct. But an implied-in-fact agreement required consideration just as an express contract did; the difference lay in how assent was manifested. (Id. at 1094.)

Consideration had to be bargained for and induce the return act or promise. Past conduct undertaken without reference to the later promise could not supply the required exchange. Hayes independently announced retirement well before the assurance; his earlier years of service likewise were not induced by it. The promise did not encourage him to remain at work when he otherwise could retire, nor had it induced long service from the beginning. In the court's assessment it was a gratuity acknowledging completed service, payable as long as the company chose. (Id. at 1094–1095.)

The court distinguished Bredemann, where an employer wanted an employee to retire and made a concrete offer to pay if she did so immediately. Plantations had not sought Hayes's retirement; a founder testified that he did not want Hayes to leave. The court also rejected reliance on the final week's work and Ulmann. In Ulmann, the employee's agreement not to compete supplied the principal consideration. Here Plantations neither bargained to deter retirement nor sought a restriction on competing or other employment. The mere chronological fact that some work occurred after the assurance did not establish the missing bargain. (Id. at 1095.)

## Promissory estoppel still required induced action
The court recognized promissory estoppel as a substitute for consideration under the Restatement formulation it had adopted: a promise reasonably expected to induce definite and substantial action or forbearance, which actually does so, may become enforceable when necessary to avoid injustice. The absence of a bargain therefore did not alone dispose of that alternative theory. The decisive defect was causation. (Id. at 1095–1096.)

Hayes had decided to retire on his own initiative before Mainelli spoke. His expectation of a pension did not show that this assurance caused the decision. The court contrasted the Restatement annuity example and Feinberg, where an announced pension shaped the employee's thinking and induced subsequent retirement. Similarities in long service, a desire to retire, and an absence of later work did not overcome the different sequence and inducement. (Id. at 1096.)

Nor did failure to seek another job establish the required change of position. The premise of Hayes's original retirement decision was that he would stop working. The record did not show additional definite and substantial forbearance caused by Mainelli's statement. Receiving payments for several years did not itself establish reliance, particularly when Hayes repeatedly asked whether another payment could be expected. The court concluded that these facts did not make enforcement necessary to avoid injustice. (Id. at 1096–1097.)

## Exact disposition and the assigned comparison
The Supreme Court sustained Plantations's appeal, reversed the Superior Court judgment, and remanded the papers to that court. Because liability failed, it did not decide Hayes's cross-appeal for ERISA attorney's fees; the trial court's ERISA analysis should not be mistaken for an independently affirmed appellate statutory holding. (Id. at 1093,1097.)

The assigned problem at C&M Book1 physical174/printed128 asks whether this pension promise differs materially from Feinberg. The full judgment supplies the answer: the promise did not induce either retirement or additional employment forbearance on this record. A subjective expectation of a pension and subsequent gratuitous payments were insufficient without the necessary bargain or induced change of position. This is the court's historical application to these facts, rather than a certification of present pension or employee-benefits law.
