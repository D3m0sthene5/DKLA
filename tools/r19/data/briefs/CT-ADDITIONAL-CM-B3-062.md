# Anthony P. Miller, Inc. v. Wilmington Housing Authority
179 F. Supp. 199 (D. Del. 1959) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

On November 3, 1959, District Judge Layton denied both parties' summary-judgment motions in a construction payment dispute. The housing authority's contracting officer had committed gross calculation errors legally characterized as constructive fraud, permitting the court to set aside the contractual finding. But the correct excusable-delay period still required fact and expert assessment, and a separate reinforcement-cost dispute also remained unresolved. The ruling applied to both Eastlake and Southbridge contracts; it was not a final award of the sums the contractor claimed. (179 F. Supp. at 199–200 & nn. 1–2, 203–207.)

## Delay clause and withheld payments

Miller began work around May 26, 1952 on low-rent housing construction that suffered weather, strike and other delays. The contract authorized $148 per day in liquidated damages for late completion. Section 13(a) excused unforeseeable causes beyond the contractor's control and without its fault or negligence, including specified governmental, strike and severe-weather events. Written notice ordinarily was required within ten days. The contracting officer would ascertain the facts and extent of delay; the authority, subject to federal housing approval, would extend time when justified, with the officer's fact findings final and conclusive. (Id. at 200–201.)

Miller submitted notices seeking extensions totaling 713 days across overlapping causes, while the actual overrun was 465 days. The officer allowed approximately 304 days, leaving 161 days treated as inexcusable, and the authority ratified a $23,828 withholding at the daily rate. The fourth note's original table records 226 labor-shortage days requested but only 20.93 allowed, along with strike, reorganization, weather, change-order and miscellaneous categories. It totals 303.60 days, rounded to 304. The overlapping requests therefore were not a claim that construction finished 713 days late. (Id. at 201 & nn. 3–4.)

Miller challenged the withholding as resting on an inappropriate formula and claimed another $3,626.96 for work it considered an extra rather than contractually required. The authority also acknowledged a separately withheld $100 was due. The opinion treated identical language and common questions in the two contracts through the Eastlake discussion. A prior decision at 165 F. Supp. 275 concerned another aspect; the present ruling did not purport to reproduce or redetermine it. (Id. at 200–201 & nn. 1–2, 5.)

## Contractual finality and constructive fraud

Miller first argued that the officer lacked power to construe the contract, under both legal doctrine and the clause limiting him to findings of fact. Layton expressly declined to decide those theories. He instead asked whether the officer's determination was palpably erroneous enough to constitute constructive fraud, regardless of whether characterized as factfinding or interpretation. (Id. at 201–202.)

Agreed decisions of architects, engineers and contracting officers ordinarily received conclusive effect, but fraud could defeat that protection. Actual dishonesty was unnecessary: gross or arbitrary mistake amounting to a palpable substantial wrong, or conduct legally implying bad faith, could suffice. A note rejected the authority's reliance on Wunderlich by pointing to the intervening statutory response. This was the opinion's historical treatment of contract finality, not a current jurisdictional or summary-judgment rule. (Id. at 202 & n. 6.)

## The six-step error

The officer's plumbing analysis started with 573 days, subtracted 209.17 concurrent-delay days, then used an assumed fifty-percent labor shortage to halve the remaining 363.83 days into 181.92 days attributable to the shortage. He then multiplied by 8.35 percent—the ratio of estimated plumbing cost to estimated total contract cost—reducing the allowance to 15.19 days. The seventh note records a dispute over whether the initial shortage finding was truly 573 days or only 365, with another 208 days representing time remaining until completion. The court did not settle every component merely by striking the final multiplication. (Id. at 203 & n. 7.)

Layton found that last step irrelevant to the delay question: relating relative cost to performance time did not determine excusable delay. Estimated cost figures also differed substantially from actual costs. Three experienced construction witnesses supplied affidavits against the formula, without counteraffidavits from the authority. Other inconsistencies reinforced unreliability, including omitted electrician-shortage findings and inconsistent handling of concurrent delays for plasterers and lathers. (Id. at 203 & nn. 8–9.)

He acknowledged the caution due an officer's judgment ratified by the authority, but also that the officer was its paid agent and the ratification favored it. He expressly found no indication of actual bad faith, dishonesty or deliberate wrongdoing, attributing the mistakes to inexperience. Nevertheless, precedent required the constructive-fraud label for sufficiently gross error, and the sixth-step calculation met that threshold. The brief's use of that label therefore does not imply a finding of conscious deceit. (Id. at 204.)

## Why neither side won the delay claim outright

Miller separately argued that authority-caused coordination delays nullified all liquidated damages. The court rejected that argument because the contract itself provided extensions for authority-caused delay. Such an extension substituted a later completion date and removed the owner's delay from the chargeable period, but did not excuse delay attributable to the contractor's own fault or negligence. (Id.)

Nor did denial of the authority's motion require judgment for Miller. Its willingness to accept the officer's 181.92-day intermediate figure, supported by unopposed affidavits, did not eliminate disputed underlying facts or questions of expert judgment. Additional evidence could be needed, including concerning electricians, and reasonable experts might differ over the correct method and allowance. Under the cautious summary-judgment approach stated in the opinion, the remaining calculation belonged to a jury potentially informed by expert testimony. Miller's motion was denied too. (Id. at 204–205.)

## Wire-mesh dispute

The specifications required reinforcement of slabs with six-by-six welded wire mesh of the size scheduled on the drawings. Seven slab types had specified mesh sizes; type A instead showed vermiculite and concrete in the reinforcement column. Specifications prevailed over inconsistent drawings. The authority argued that the type-A notation was an obvious mistake and that all slabs required mesh. But the language's coverage and customary practice were not established beyond dispute. (Id. at 205–206.)

Miller argued that absence of the word “all” limited reinforcement to slabs with scheduled mesh sizes. The judge found both readings possible and rejected summary judgment. He additionally suggested, on an assumed affidavit premise, that a bidder aware of an obvious mistake might have a duty to call attention to it rather than profit from silence. That was expressly a possible alternative reason at this stage, not a final finding that Miller knowingly exploited an error or a final ruling for the authority. The tenth note likewise made a conditional observation rather than awarding judgment. Both motions on both contracts were denied. (Id. at 206–207 & n. 10.)
