# In re “Agent Orange” Product Liability Litigation — 2008 contractor-defense appeal

**517 F.3d 76 (2d Cir. 2008) · February 22, 2008 · Civil Procedure**

Judge Sack wrote the unanimous opinion of the panel of Judges Miner, Sack, and Hall. The court affirmed all sixteen district-court judgments. The report metadata also identifies the case as Twinam v. Dow Chemical Co. The assigned account is CivPro physical PDF 287, printed 538–539, Boyle Note 3. This is distinct from the early federal-common-law litigation and from the companion 2008 removal and Vietnamese-victim appeals.

## The claims and their procedural route

Vietnam veterans and relatives alleged that exposure to Agent Orange caused injuries, principally cancers. Agent Orange combined the herbicides 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T in approximately equal amounts. Dioxin was a byproduct of manufacturing 2,4,5-T; the manufacturing process could affect its concentration. The alleged defect was the herbicide’s contamination with dioxin, rather than a defect in dioxin itself. Note 13 explains the distinction through the example of pure lead making a painted toy defective. Note 1 identifies a claimant exposed in the Korean demilitarized zone; because he made no distinct arguments, the panel treated his claim with the others. (82–83 & n.1, 89 & n.13.)

Earlier litigation had produced a $180 million settlement and a distribution program whose eligibility cutoff was 1994. Isaacson and Stephenson alleged injuries arising too late for that program. A 2001 appellate decision had held that those plaintiffs were inadequately represented in the settlement proceedings. This 2008 panel did not revisit whether it was bound by that decision: the contractor defense independently resolved the appeals. Earlier opt-out judgments and settlement history explain the procedural setting; they are not fresh findings that these plaintiffs failed to prove causation. (83–85 & n.6.)

On February 9, 2004, Judge Weinstein entered summary judgment against the Stephenson and Isaacson plaintiffs and denied Stephenson’s request to amend. The other fourteen groups were eventually subjected to the same defense ruling. The district court afforded an unusual opportunity for discovery and reconsideration after the initial summary judgment, including access to the extensive multidistrict-litigation record and six depositions outside it. It allowed additional submissions before reaffirming its ruling in March 2005. These were sixteen unconsolidated appeals, despite their common treatment in the opinion. (85–87.)

The appealed merits issue concerned design defect. Plaintiffs abandoned failure-to-warn and manufacturing-defect claims by not arguing them in their briefs. They also did not appeal the order refusing to strike defendants’ affidavits and exhibits. Most arguments could be adopted across the plaintiff groups, but the amendment issue concerned Stephenson alone. Judge Hall’s companion opinion separately resolved removal; Judge Miner’s separately addressed Vietnamese victims. Neither companion judgment is silently incorporated into this brief’s full-reading claim. (82, 86–87 & nn.8–10.)

## Boyle and the alleged conflict with state law

The court reviewed summary judgment de novo, crediting plaintiffs’ evidence and reasonable inferences. Boyle supplies three conditions for protection from state tort liability: the government approved reasonably precise specifications; the product conformed to them; and the contractor warned the government about dangers known to the contractor but not to the government. The defense protects discretionary federal procurement decisions from conflicting state duties. It is not simply immunity awarded whenever the government purchases a product, and it does not turn this suit into a direct claim under the Federal Tort Claims Act. (87–89.)

Plaintiffs argued that Agent Orange was a commercially available product rather than a specially designed military one, that suppliers had independently selected the manufacturing process, and that a safer process could have satisfied the contracts. They also maintained that manufacturers had concealed toxicity information. Each contention required analysis of actual governmental decisions and knowledge, rather than treating military purchase as dispositive. (89–90, 92–94, 97–98.)

## Approval of the specifications and of the relevant risk

Agent Orange was not simply a stock commercial product. Commercial 2,4,5-T concentrations were at most 55 percent, while the military product was more than 90 percent pure. The military required a particular composition and purity. It rejected a proposed ester substitution in 1963 because its testing concerned the specified material; in 1968 it insisted on 98 percent purity and testing despite industry objections. Contractors’ proposing a design or owning patents did not prevent meaningful governmental approval. The government need not originate every specification if it actually exercises the relevant discretion. (90–92.)

Missing copies of some contracts did not defeat the defense on this record. Note 12 emphasizes that plaintiffs neither distinguished particular contracts nor grounded their arguments in varying contractual specifications. The court did not announce that lost contracts are universally irrelevant; inability to establish the governing specifications could ordinarily matter. (89 n.12.)

The manufacturing-process argument was stronger. A lower-temperature Boehringer process might have produced substantially less dioxin, and evidence that Dow used it created a triable possibility of safer production consistent with the specifications. The panel therefore did not endorse the district court’s conclusion that safer compliant production was impossible. Nor had the government expressly dictated the process or required a particular toxicity level. Note 15 nonetheless classifies a systematically chosen defective process as design defect, distinguishing it from accidental departure from the intended manufacturing configuration. (92–94 & n.15.)

That factual dispute did not require reversal because the government had itself evaluated the relevant toxicity and continued procurement. An April 26, 1963 Edgewood meeting considered the available information and concluded that the wartime uses presented no unacceptable hazard. Subsequent orders reflected that discretionary assessment. Applying its earlier Lewis decision, the panel treated knowledgeable reordering as approval of reasonably precise specifications concerning the alleged defect, even without an express contractual command to employ a particular process. A contrary state duty to eliminate that accepted risk would interfere with the government’s decision. This was a finding about the government’s contemporaneous evaluation, not a judicial declaration that Agent Orange was actually safe. (94–97.)

Notes 16–18 preserve important limits. Potential problems proving causation or distinguishing batches were not the basis of decision. Other governmental studies continued and eventually contributed to ending the defoliation campaign. And the other two Boyle conditions remained essential: defendants still had to show conformity and a sufficiently informed governmental decision. (94–97 & nn.16–18.)

## Conformity and the limits of the warnings holding

On conformity, plaintiffs did not identify a departure from the specified proportions or purity. Their own expert acknowledged that trace impurities were consistent with the product’s specifications. The court therefore found the second Boyle condition satisfied; the existence of a contaminant was not itself proof of contractual nonconformity. (97.)

The warnings condition required more care. Defendants claimed that they had disclosed everything and that the government knew at least as much as they did. The panel doubted that those broad propositions had been established as a matter of law. Note 19 expressly requires examination of the present record rather than automatic reliance on a different panel’s 1987 description of governmental knowledge. The holding thus does not erase factual disagreement about who knew more. (97–98 & n.19.)

Instead, the panel asked whether defendants withheld a known danger substantial enough to affect the relevant governmental decision. The government's decision had to be fully informed about relevant known hazards; Boyle did not require communication of every conceivable risk. The evidence largely concerned chloracne, liver injury, and other effects on manufacturing workers. Some handler and skin risks were already known to the military. The record did not establish that defendants knew of the alleged latent cancers or other comparable dangers to nonworkers that they withheld from the government's operational assessment. Knowledge also varied among manufacturers, a qualification preserved in notes 20 and 21. (98–102 & nn.20–21.)

Note 22 addresses Monsanto’s 1965 suggestion that dioxin conceivably could be a potent carcinogen. Without context establishing a known risk from the trace quantities in Agent Orange to persons outside manufacture or direct handling, that expression of possibility was insufficient. The court expressly declined to decide whether defendants should have investigated further. It also did not conclusively establish that governmental and contractor knowledge was equivalent. Its narrower conclusion was that the record did not show a material undisclosed known operational danger defeating the defense. (100–102 & n.22.)

## Discovery, amendment, and disposition

Discovery rulings were reviewed for abuse of discretion. Plaintiffs had received meaningful access to a vast existing record and additional depositions. Their broad request for materials from other litigation was not supported by first examining the multidistrict record and identifying specific nonduplicative information still needed. Rule 26 permitted limits on cumulative or disproportionately burdensome discovery, and the panel found no abuse. This conclusion depended on the opportunities actually afforded, not a rule that summary judgment may always precede adequate discovery. (102–103.)

The district court did err in denying Stephenson’s amendment. Under the version of Rule 15(a) then applicable, a party could amend once as of right before service of a responsive pleading. Defendants had not answered, and their summary-judgment motion was not a responsive pleading. Nevertheless, the error was harmless: repleading could not avoid the contractor defense that independently defeated the claim. The historical rule and procedural posture explain that result; this is not a certification of today’s Rule 15 deadlines. (103–104.)

The court affirmed all sixteen judgments. It did not determine ultimate causation, adjudicate the abandoned tort theories on appeal, establish that safer production was impossible, or decide a general duty to investigate latent risks. Its decisive holding protected the government's informed procurement choice under Boyle while preserving those distinctions. (104.)
