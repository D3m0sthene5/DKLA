# Sayres v. Bauman

188 W. Va. 550, 425 S.E.2d 226 (1992) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B2-009

## Assurances surrounding the business purchase

Cablentertainment purchased Midwest CATV in August 1981 and hired the employees working at its West Virginia cable systems. They remained employed until November 1982, when a newly adopted nepotism policy prompted their discharge. Apparently the employees were related to one another; they alleged that Cablentertainment already knew of those relationships when it bought Midwest. That allegation was not a holding that the nepotism policy itself was unlawful. 188 W. Va. at 551–552 & n.1.

The employees sued for breach of alleged oral employment contracts guaranteeing continued employment for an indefinite period. Their descriptions of duration differed. Some believed they could leave at any time while the company remained irrevocably obligated to retain them. They could not identify exact contract terms or when and where the pertinent assurances were made. They had neither written contracts with either operator nor contemporaneous memoranda, and the parties agreed that no relevant employee handbooks existed. The strongest articulated assurance was that the employees would not lose their jobs because a new company was buying the business. Id. at 552, 554–555.

## Liability verdict and review

The trial court bifurcated liability and damages. At the March 1991 trial, it directed a verdict against Charles Chesser after the employees' evidence and against Mary Wamsley after the employer's evidence. It allowed the other claims to reach the jury. Two special interrogatories expressly used a clear-and-convincing standard: the jury found both an oral agreement to employ the remaining plaintiffs and its breach around November 1, 1982. The April 24 judgment upheld that verdict; the court denied a new-trial motion in June and stayed further damages proceedings pending appeal. Thus no damages trial or monetary award was being reviewed. Id. at 551–552 & n.2.

Justice Workman wrote for the Supreme Court of Appeals of West Virginia. The complete reproduced report identifies no separate writing or numerical vote. The court reviewed whether the proof could establish a definite agreement altering presumptive at-will employment and also addressed the employer's statute-of-frauds argument. Id. at 551–555.

## Definite terms and the evidentiary burden

West Virginia presumed that an employment agreement of unspecified duration was terminable at will. The employee claiming another arrangement bore the burden of rebutting that presumption. Existing handbook decisions showed that an employer could make a sufficiently definite promise limiting discharge to specified grounds. Those cases did not make the absence of a handbook automatically fatal to an oral promise; the court used their formation principles to require ascertainable, definite terms in any asserted alteration of at-will status. Lifetime employment was an extraordinary commitment requiring clear and unequivocal language. Id. at 552–554.

A permanent employment contract or comparable substantial employment right had to be proved by clear and convincing evidence. The court found the testimony legally insufficient under that standard even though the jury had answered interrogatories expressly invoking it. One witness denied receiving a promise of continued employment absent wrongdoing. Another described hopes of employment until retirement but admitted no period had been stated and ultimately characterized the arrangement as indefinite. Wamsley described faith in assurances of security but acknowledged that no duration had been given. The court concluded that none identified job security for a specific period or continued employment on specified conditions. Id. at 552, 554.

The possible promise that the buyout would not itself cost employees their jobs did not establish an undertaking to retain them for life. Their actual hiring and employment for more than a year did not supply the missing broader contractual terms. The decision therefore separates an employee's expectation or trust from proof of an ascertainable promise changing the employer's termination rights. It does not hold that oral employment promises can never be binding or that every enforceable restriction requires a fixed calendar term. Id. at 554–555.

## Statute of frauds and precise result

Cablentertainment alternatively asserted the statute of frauds for contracts not performable within one year. The employees invoked the capable-of-performance exception, reasoning that misconduct such as embezzlement could have caused discharge for cause within the first year. The court retained that historical exception but applied its prior Thompson rule: where the disputed oral agreement was not actually performed within one year, clear and convincing evidence of its existence was required before submission to the jury. Here the employees had continued working past one year, and their evidence did not establish the asserted agreement under that threshold. The court consequently did not rest its judgment on a categorical ruling that potential discharge could never bring an employment agreement outside the statute. Id. at 555.

The court **reversed the Circuit Court of Harrison County's decision**. Its concluding mandate states reversal, without ordering a new liability trial or determining damages. The case should not have gone to the jury because the requisite contractual undertaking had not been proved. Id.
