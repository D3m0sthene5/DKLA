Surowitz v. Hilton Hotels Corp.
Citation
Surowitz v. Hilton Hotels Corp., 383 U.S. 363, 86 S. Ct. 845, 15 L. Ed. 2d 807 (1966). Supreme Court of the
United States. Argued January 20, 1966; decided March 7, 1966. Justice Black wrote for the Court. Justice
Harlan concurred separately; Chief Justice Warren and Justice Fortas did not participate as stated below.
Casebook pp. 699–702; source PDF pp. 367–368. Full judicial opinion.
Parties
Dora Surowitz was a shareholder in Hilton Hotels Corporation who brought a derivative action alleging
injury to the corporation. The defendants included corporate officers and directors accused of enriching
themselves through transactions harmful to Hilton. Surowitz sought review after her action was dismissed
because she could not personally explain its allegations at her deposition. CB pp. 699–700; 383 U.S. at
364–367.
Material Facts
Surowitz was a Polish immigrant with little formal education and limited English. She had invested slightly
more than $2,000 of her savings in Hilton stock on the advice of her son-in-law, Irving Brilliant, an
investment adviser trained in law and economics. When she received a notice concerning Hilton’s proposed
repurchase of its stock, she asked him to explain it. He and lawyer Walter Rockler investigated for months
before recommending suit. CB pp. 700–701; 383 U.S. at 368–370.
The complaint alleged that insiders used stock purchases at inflated prices to transfer corporate money to
themselves. The full opinion describes both Hilton’s repurchase of its own shares and its purchase of shares
in an affiliated credit corporation. These were unproved fraud allegations. Rockler signed the lengthy
complaint under the version of Rule 11 then in force. Surowitz verified it, partly on information and belief,
after Brilliant read and explained it to her. CB pp. 699–701; full opinion, 383 U.S. at 364–365, 370.
At her deposition, Surowitz could not explain the legal relationships, transactions, defendants, or detailed
allegations. She nevertheless knew she owned the stock, that dividends had stopped, and that her trusted
adviser believed management had harmed the corporation. Affidavits from Rockler and Brilliant described
their extensive investigation and the basis for filing. CB pp. 700–702; 383 U.S. at 366–372.
Procedural History
Before requiring defendants to answer, the district court permitted them to depose Surowitz. They then
moved to dismiss on the grounds that the pleading was a sham and that she was not a proper plaintiff.
Despite the supplemental affidavits, the court treated her verification as false and a nullity and dismissed
with prejudice. The Seventh Circuit affirmed, even while acknowledging the seriousness of the allegations
and the substantial investigation. The Supreme Court granted review. CB p. 700; 383 U.S. at 366–368.
Issue
Did the verification requirement for shareholder derivative actions compel dismissal with prejudice because
the shareholder did not personally understand the complaint, when she authorized the suit in good faith on
advice grounded in an extensive investigation? CB pp. 700–702; 383 U.S. at 367–373.

Rule and Holding
On this record, the verification requirement did not justify summary dismissal. A shareholder’s limited
personal comprehension did not nullify a bona fide derivative complaint based on reasonable beliefs arising
from careful investigation by trusted advisers and counsel. The Court interpreted the rule in light of its
purpose of discouraging abusive suits while preserving legitimate protection for shareholders. CB pp.
701–702; 383 U.S. at 370–373.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed and remanded to the district court for proceedings on the merits. Defendants
would have to respond to the allegations. The Court expressly recognized that the asserted fraud had not
been proved; its decision restored the lawsuit rather than awarding recovery. CB p. 702; 383 U.S. at
373–374.
Reasoning
The majority examined the complete record rather than treating the deposition in isolation. Surowitz’s
inability to describe complex transactions was consistent with her limited language and financial knowledge.
Her reliance on Brilliant was understandable, and his and Rockler’s affidavits supplied a substantial
investigative basis for the complaint. The verification reflected her good-faith reliance, rather than evidence
that the claims were knowingly invented. CB pp. 700–702; 383 U.S. at 368–372.
The Court distinguished the present action from a suit filed without regard to truth merely to pressure
management into paying a settlement. Surowitz sought protection of her actual investment; her adviser’s
longstanding family investments also supported the conclusion that this was not an effort to harm the
corporation through a baseless claim. The rule’s anti-abuse purpose therefore did not support excluding her
simply because she lacked sophisticated personal knowledge. CB pp. 701–702; 383 U.S. at 371–372.
The majority emphasized the Federal Rules’ role in permitting genuine disputes to reach fair adjudication.
Reading verification as an absolute financial-literacy requirement would create a procedural obstacle for
precisely the small investors derivative litigation could protect. The full opinion reserved whether a different
record of false verification could ever justify dismissal; it did not eliminate verification or approve sham
pleadings generally. CB p. 702; full opinion, 383 U.S. at 371, 373–374.
Separate Opinions
Justice Harlan concurred on a narrower ground. The rule required an oath but did not specify that the
shareholder personally had to supply it. In his view, Rockler’s affidavit constituted an adequate verification
by counsel in these circumstances. Chief Justice Warren took no part in consideration or decision; Justice
Fortas took no part in the decision. No dissent is printed with the opinion. CB p. 702; 383 U.S. at 374 &
Harlan concurrence.
Notes
Narrow reading: good-faith reliance on a carefully investigated complaint supported by counsel’s and an
adviser’s affidavits prevented dismissal on this record. Broader reading: verification should serve its
anti-abuse function without demanding that a shareholder personally master complex facts and legal
theories. The reserved possibility of a different result for an actual sham remains important. CB pp.
701–702; 383 U.S. at 371–374.
