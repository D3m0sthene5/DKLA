# Feinberg v. Pfeiffer Co.
322 S.W.2d 163 (Mo. Ct. App. 1959) | Contracts

St. Louis Court of Appeals, Missouri. Doerner, Commissioner; opinion adopted per curiam. Nos. 30183 and 30204; decided March 17, 1959; rehearing or transfer denied April 13, 1959.

## Parties

Plaintiff / respondent: Anna Sacks Feinberg, a former Pfeiffer employee who retired in reliance on the corporation's promise of $200 per month for life. Defendant / appellant: Pfeiffer Company, formerly S. Pfeiffer Manufacturing Company, a pharmaceutical manufacturer whose board adopted the pension resolution and whose later management reduced the payments. Key corporate actors: Max Lippman, president when the resolution passed; Sidney Harris and Sol Flammer, directors who communicated it; Mrs. Lippman and then Harris, later presidents involved in continuing or questioning payment.

## Procedural History

Feinberg sued in the Circuit Court of the City of St. Louis on the corporation's alleged obligation to pay $200 per month for life after retirement. The parties waived a jury, and the court tried the case. It entered judgment for Feinberg for $5,100 in accrued pension installments through trial plus interest. Pfeiffer appealed, challenging evidentiary rulings, factual findings on reliance, and the legal sufficiency of the pension promise. Missouri statutes required the appellate court to review the law and evidence and enter the judgment the trial court should have entered. The court therefore considered admissible evidence and explained that Feinberg's illness at trial did not supply the foundation of liability. Commissioner Doerner recommended affirmance; the court adopted his opinion per curiam. Presiding Judge Wolfe and Judges Anderson and Ruddy concurred. Rehearing and transfer to the Missouri Supreme Court were denied. (163–166, 169.)

## Material Facts

Feinberg began working for Pfeiffer in 1910 at age seventeen. By 1947 she served as bookkeeper, office manager, and assistant treasurer. She owned 70 of the corporation's 6,503 outstanding shares: 20 were gifts, 20 she purchased, and 30 came through a stock split or dividend. She received stock dividends and annual employee bonuses from 1937 through 1949, ranging from $300 to $2,000. (164.)

On December 27, 1947, Pfeiffer's board met under President Max Lippman. The five directors present together owned 5,007 of 6,503 shares. The board recognized Feinberg's long, faithful, skilled service; raised her monthly salary from $350 to $400; and adopted a retirement privilege intended as a firm corporate obligation and future security. The resolution allowed her to retire whenever she chose and receive $200 per month for the rest of her life. It also expressed the directors' hope that she would continue working for many years. (164–165.)

At Lippman's request, Harris and Flammer visited Feinberg that day and told her of the resolution. She had received no advance notice and was surprised. She understood that she could retire immediately. Her employment remained at will: she promised no period of future work, and the corporation retained the power to discharge her. She testified that she would have continued working even if the resolution had never passed. (165.)

Feinberg stayed through June 30, 1949, then retired at age fifty-seven while healthy and able to work. She and her husband calculated that his earnings plus the pension would support them. She testified that she would have remained employed without the pension promise. She sought no other employment while the company made the promised payments. (165–166.)

Pfeiffer began paying $200 on the first day of each month. Lippman died on November 18, 1949. His widow succeeded him as president and continued signing the checks, although she viewed them as gifts. Harris became president in October 1953. A new accounting firm, Ernst & Ernst, repeatedly questioned the payments. In spring 1956, Harris consulted company attorney Ralph Kalish; Harris testified that the accountants and attorney advised the company that it had no payment duty. Pfeiffer sent Feinberg a $100 check on April 1, 1956. She declined the reduced amount and filed suit. (165.)

At trial Feinberg was sixty-five and one-half. A cancer removal and operation performed on November 25, 1957 had left her unable to work. Pfeiffer argued that the trial court improperly relied on this condition. The appellate court located the induced action in her healthy 1949 retirement and treated the later illness and its timing as secondary to the already completed reliance. (165–166, 169.)

## Question Presented

Does a corporation's clear promise of a lifetime pension become binding when the promisee receives it as recognition of past service, later retires from a profitable at-will position in actual reliance on it, and enforcement is required to prevent injustice?

## Holding

Feinberg's past service and her additional eighteen months of work supplied no bargained-for consideration for the pension. Her retirement, however, was action the company reasonably could expect its pension resolution to induce, and the promise actually caused her to surrender continued gainful employment. Restatement (First) of Contracts Section 90 made the promise binding because enforcement was necessary to avoid injustice. The court affirmed $5,100 plus interest for the accrued installments. (164, 167–169.)

## Reasoning

Past service: Pfeiffer adopted the pension after Feinberg had completed thirty-seven years of service. Those completed acts were rendered under the earlier employment relationship and could not have been sought in exchange for a promise made in 1947. Gratitude for past performance explained the promise's motive while leaving the bargain element unsatisfied. (167.)

Continued employment: The resolution gave Feinberg the right to retire immediately and expressed a hope that she would remain. She made no promise to continue, and the company attached no service condition to pension eligibility. Her work from December 1947 through June 1949 therefore occurred without the reciprocal commitment needed to convert the pension into a bargain. (167.)

Promissory estoppel: The First Restatement's Section 90 bound a promise that the promisor should reasonably expect to cause definite and substantial action or forbearance, that does cause it, when enforcement alone can avoid injustice. This doctrine addresses reliance on a future commitment. Feinberg's decision to retire supplied the induced action. (167–169.)

Evidence of actual inducement: Feinberg said she could have continued working, used the promised pension in planning household support, and would have stayed with Pfeiffer without it. Although she remained employed for another eighteen months, the court found ample evidence that the pension induced her later retirement. The trial judge credited her testimony, and the record supported that finding. (166.)

Foreseeability: The board deliberately created a retirement privilege available whenever Feinberg chose. Retirement was the precise conduct the promise invited. Pfeiffer therefore had reason to expect that its assurance of lifetime income could cause her to leave the position while she remained capable of earning a salary. (164–165, 168–169.)

Injustice: Feinberg gave up a lucrative, long-held position at fifty-seven and organized her affairs around lifetime payments. Pfeiffer paid for almost seven years before attempting to halve the pension. By then she was older than sixty-three, and comparable employment had become realistically difficult to obtain. The court treated retirement and the passage of employable years as the operative reliance injury. (169.)

Later illness: Section 90's induced act was the 1949 retirement. The later illness strengthened the practical consequences of lost employment, yet the date of diagnosis did not control enforceability. A promise that induced the surrender of work could produce injustice through age, elapsed time, and loss of opportunity even before a medical disability appeared. (169.)

Use of Ricketts: The court treated Ricketts's promissory-note setting as closely analogous. Each promisor offered future support; each promisee left profitable employment as a foreseeable response; and each promisor later invoked the absence of consideration. Feinberg supplied the Restatement label and organized the result directly under promissory estoppel. (168–169.)

Missouri doctrinal history: Missouri decisions had reached reliance-based results through three formulations: induced conduct as consideration, induced conduct as an estoppel, and an implied return promise forming a bilateral contract. The court used the practical rule of Section 90 and described promissory estoppel as the fitting account of a promise enforced through foreseeable, actual reliance. (168.)

Remedy: The judgment covered installments already due at trial, with interest. Enforcing the lifetime pension tracks the promise that induced retirement and protects Feinberg from the continuing loss created when she surrendered employment. The later Restatement (Second) § 90(1), reproduced in the casebook after this excerpt, permits a remedy limited as justice requires. That later formulation was not part of the First Restatement language quoted by this 1959 court. The actual judgment affirmed accrued installments plus interest; it did not adjudicate every possible measure of relief under the later provision. (164, 167–169; assigned printed 128–130.)
