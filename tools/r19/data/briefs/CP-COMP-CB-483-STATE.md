# Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands v. Canadian Imperial Bank of Commerce — New York answer

**21 N.Y.3d 55, 967 N.Y.S.2d 876, 990 N.E.2d 114 (2013) — April 30, 2013.** Judge Rivera wrote the Court of Appeals opinion; Chief Judge Lippman and Judges Graffeo, Read, Smith, and Pigott concurred. No separate writing is reported. Assigned source: CivPro physical page 610, printed page 1205, Note 3. This answers questions certified at 693 F.3d 274; it is distinct from the subsequent federal judgment at 717 F.3d 266.

## Facts and proceedings

In 1994 the Commonwealth obtained separate federal tax judgments against William and Patricia Millard, each for approximately $18.3 million. The Millards had relocated before collection. In 2010 the Commonwealth learned they had renounced United States citizenship and were living in the Cayman Islands. It registered the judgments in New York in March and April 2011 and sought a postjudgment turnover order under Rule 69(a)(1) and New York CPLR §5225(b). The judgments were also registered in Florida. (58, nn.1–2.)

The proposed garnishee was CIBC, a Canadian bank headquartered in Toronto with a New York branch. The Commonwealth alleged that the Millards maintained accounts at CIBC FirstCaribbean International Bank or its Cayman affiliates. It said CIBC owned and controlled 92 percent of FirstCaribbean, imposed governance structures providing operational oversight, shared important personnel, and supervised compliance. Those links allegedly gave the parent the practical ability to direct the subsidiary to release the assets. CIBC replied that the subsidiary was legally independent and that without an information-sharing agreement it could not access the accounts or account information. (58–59.)

The district court denied turnover, although it maintained a restraining order concerning the accounts. It considered the practical-control argument colorable but the statutory interpretation unsettled. The Second Circuit certified whether turnover could be ordered against an entity lacking actual possession or custody when a subsidiary might hold the assets, and, if so, which factual considerations should govern. (59.)

## Question and answer

The state court **answered the first question no**: under §5225(b), the banking entity against which the order is directed must itself have actual possession or custody. Constructive possession through practical control of a subsidiary is insufficient. Because the first answer was negative, the court did not answer the conditional second question, deeming it academic. (57–59, 65.)

The Commonwealth sought a functional control test: if a parent could practically order its subsidiary to turn over funds, the statute should reach it. The court rejected that construction, rather than deciding that the particular ownership percentage or personnel overlap was too weak to meet such a test. Its rule excluded the proposed constructive-possession standard. (60–63.)

## Statutory reasoning

The starting point was the actual statutory language. Section 5225(b) identifies a person in possession or custody of property in which the debtor has an interest, or a transferee of the debtor’s property under the specified priority conditions. It does not include “control.” The omission mattered because the legislature used control expressly elsewhere. A court could not supply an intentionally omitted term merely because doing so would extend collection remedies. (59–61.)

History reinforced that reading. The predecessor Civil Practice Act provision had referred to possession or control. The 1962 CPLR instead used possession or custody. Changing the wording indicated a substantive change; the Commonwealth’s argument that custody simply replaced control as an equivalent would undo that choice. (61–62.)

The larger statutory scheme also distinguished the concepts. Discovery provisions use possession, custody, **or control**, supporting constructive possession when a party can obtain documents from another entity. Provisions governing disposition of property use the narrower possession-or-custody formulation. The court read those provisions together: broad discovery access did not supply authority to compel disposition of another entity’s assets. It also rejected speculation that the legislature had blindly copied wording without intending the distinctions. (62–63.)

## Personal jurisdiction does not answer custody

The Commonwealth relied on *Koehler*, which permitted an in-personam order requiring delivery of out-of-state property. The court explained that *Koehler* addressed territorial location and personal jurisdiction, not the meaning of possession or custody. A garnishee subject to personal jurisdiction may be ordered to deliver property it possesses outside New York. That does not authorize an order compelling it to direct a different entity, outside New York’s personal jurisdiction, to deliver that entity’s holdings. The cited turnover precedents concerned assets already possessed by the person ordered to deliver them. (63–64.)

The holding therefore does not categorically prohibit turnover of foreign property. It separates **where property is located** from **who actually possesses or has custody of it**. Personal jurisdiction is essential to enforcing the order against the garnishee, but it does not erase the statute’s independent custody requirement.

## Limits, notes, and judgment

CIBC additionally argued that bank deposits should be addressed under CPLR §5227 as debts owed by the bank, rather than §5225(b). The court expressly declined to decide that provision’s applicability and confined its analysis to the certified §5225(b) questions. The opinion consequently does not establish that every possible remedy for the accounts was unavailable. (59 n.3.)

The formal disposition was **question one answered negatively; question two not answered as academic**. The state court did not itself affirm the federal district court’s judgment or vacate the federal injunction. Those acts occurred when the federal appeal resumed. All three judicial footnotes concern relocation/citizenship, Florida registration, and the §5227 reservation; there are no separate writings or appendices. (65.)

The assigned paragraph accurately links the state answer to the later federal denial of turnover. The complete opinion supplies the textual and structural reasoning that the short editorial account omits. This brief describes that 2013 statutory decision without certifying the current reach of New York judgment-enforcement law.
