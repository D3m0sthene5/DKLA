# Center for Biological Diversity v. United States Department of the Interior
563 F.3d 466 (D.C. Cir. 2009) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and offshore planning framework
On April 17, 2009, the D.C. Circuit vacated Interior's 2007–2012 Outer Continental Shelf leasing program and remanded for a proper comparative environmental-sensitivity analysis and renewed statutory balancing. That result followed one successful OCSLA claim, not acceptance of every environmental challenge. The court rejected other OCSLA challenges on their merits and dismissed the NEPA and Endangered Species Act claims as unripe. Chief Judge Sentelle wrote for himself, Ginsburg, and Rogers. Rogers joined and concurred separately to qualify unnecessary standing analysis and statements about Interior's discretion. (471–472, 489–490.)

The program proposed 21 potential lease sales in eight areas, including expansion in the Beaufort, Bering, and Chukchi Seas. Those waters support polar bears, whales, seals, walruses, seabirds, and subsistence and cultural activities. Three environmental organizations and the federally recognized Native Village of Point Hope challenged the Secretary's approval. The program had completed only OCSLA's first stage when the petitions were filed. Later stages separately involve lease sales, exploration plans, and development and production plans. Approval at the planning stage gives prospective lessees no right to explore or produce; environmental safeguards and possible disapproval remain at later stages. One Chukchi Sea sale occurred after filing and faced a separate Alaska action. (471–475; 481 n. 1.)

Section 18 requires the Secretary to prepare a five-year sale schedule, consider economic, social, and environmental values and specified regional characteristics, and balance potential environmental damage, discovery of oil and gas, and coastal-zone effects. NEPA provides procedural environmental analysis, which can be tiered with increasingly specific program stages. The ESA requires consultation when agency action may affect listed species or critical habitat. Petitioners alleged inadequate climate-change consideration, missing biological baseline information and research planning, premature omission of ESA consultation, and an irrational NOAA-based comparative sensitivity ranking. (471–475.)

## Standing: substantive climate effects versus procedural protection
The court rejected petitioners' substantive theory that adopting the program would increase fossil-fuel use, cause climate change, harm the Arctic species they enjoyed, and thereby injure them. It interpreted Massachusetts v. EPA narrowly because that state asserted damage to its own coastal territory. Assuming without deciding that Point Hope could qualify for a sovereign's special solicitude, the court found no allegation of distinct harm to Point Hope's own territory. Harm to federally owned offshore areas or to members did not supply that premise. It also questioned the tribe's ability to assert a quasi-sovereign claim protecting members against the federal government. (475–477.)

Applying Lujan's ordinary requirements, the court characterized the asserted substantive injury as hypothetical and generalized. The causal chain also depended on numerous absent actors—drillers and consumers—and overlooked subsequent environmental decisions that could prevent development. The petitioners had not shown sufficiently concrete injury or causation through that chain. These are the majority's conclusions about the pleaded theory and record; the judgment did not deny that environmental or aesthetic interests can support standing. (477–479.)

The procedural theory succeeded. A statutory procedure alone cannot create standing without a threatened concrete interest, but affidavits identified members' enjoyment of indigenous animals and definite near-future plans to observe them. For standing purposes, approval of an irrationally grounded program could substantially increase risks to those interests, and vacatur and remand could redress that risk. The court therefore allowed procedural standing for the climate claims and the remaining OCSLA claims. Standing did not establish their merits or make every claim ripe. (479–480, 483–484.)

## Ripeness of NEPA and ESA claims
The court dismissed the NEPA climate and baseline-data claims as premature. Under the governing multi-stage leasing precedent, the critical irreversible commitment occurs when leases issue, not when a schedule identifies potentially suitable areas. At filing no sales had occurred, no drilling rights had been conferred, and further site-specific review remained. Waiting did not expose the animals to the harm alleged at that stage, while requiring complete earlier analysis would divert agency resources and add burdens to segmented planning. Ohio Forestry's statement that a NEPA violation cannot become riper did not establish when a violation first occurs; the precedent governing leasing answered that antecedent question. (480–482.)

The ESA claim similarly sought to treat all potential downstream development as an immediate effect of program adoption. The court instead applied the checks and balances of OCSLA stage by stage. Initial scheduling did not itself authorize activity harming animals; proposed leases might never occur, and further stages required environmental consideration and consultation. Institutional interests in a more crystallized dispute outweighed the delay's asserted hardship. Note 1 explains that petitioners identified no program authorization for independently permitted seismic work and that the postfiling Chukchi sale involved additional environmental steps and separate litigation; note 2 incorporates that explanation for the ESA issue. The court did not adjudicate that separate sale's validity. (481 n. 1; 482–483 & n. 2.)

## The unsuccessful OCSLA claims
Unlike those NEPA and ESA claims, OCSLA's initial planning requirements were ripe. The court applied a hybrid review standard: substantial evidence for ascertainable facts, relevant-factor and judgment review for policy choices, and then-prevailing deference to permissible readings of ambiguous statutory provisions. (483–484.)

The climate claims nevertheless failed. The court read OCSLA as directing attention to exploration, production, and localized effects rather than requiring analysis of worldwide consumption of the extracted fuels. Congress already had chosen offshore production to meet energy needs; moving a sale schedule elsewhere would not by itself change consumption effects. The opinion also stated that Interior lacked discretion to consider global consumption effects, language that Rogers qualified separately. Interior had in fact estimated emissions from leasing, exploration, and development and considered the current and future effects of climate change on local Arctic areas. The court therefore rejected the asserted omission under OCSLA. (484–486.)

The baseline-information claim also failed. The additional-study obligation under section 20(b) arose after leasing and development, not at initial approval; petitioners themselves conceded that comprehensive baseline research need not yet be complete. In any event, the EIS contained substantial geological, biological, and environmental information. Some gaps were compatible with the pyramidic process and Interior acknowledged the need for further research. Section 18(b)(3)'s requirement to estimate staffing and appropriations did not silently require a detailed plan specifying every missing datum and when and how it would be gathered. (486–487.)

## The successful sensitivity challenge and precise remedy
Interior's relative environmental-sensitivity ranking rested solely on NOAA's index of shoreline vulnerability to oil spills. The index ranked shorelines from 1 to 10, but OCSLA required comparison of different areas of the outer Continental Shelf itself. The Secretary could choose a reasonable methodology using the best existing information; that flexibility did not permit substituting coastline data for offshore sensitivity without explanation or considering only onshore effects. The great distance between coastlines and proposed offshore areas made the unexplained substitution particularly important. (487–488.)

Because section 18(a)(3)'s balancing condenses factors considered under section 18(a)(2), the deficient sensitivity analysis also impaired the balance used to select sale timing and location. On remand Interior had to compare offshore sensitivity more fully, identify the most and least sensitive areas, reconsider whether areas should be excluded, and rebalance timing and location. The agency might ultimately find the shorelines most sensitive, but could not reach that conclusion without examining the offshore areas too. The court exercised section 23(c)(6)'s remedial authority to vacate the program and remand; it did not order a permanent exclusion of particular regions or invalidate the separately challenged sale. (488–490.)

## Rogers's concurrence and the assigned account
Rogers joined the court's opinion but emphasized that accepted procedural standing made it unnecessary to decide substantive standing or precisely delimit Massachusetts v. EPA. Whether Point Hope's affidavits established particularized cultural and way-of-life harms remained, in her view, for another day. She also limited the permissible conclusion on consumption effects to the holding that OCSLA did not require consideration of them at stage one; this appeal did not warrant broader pronouncements about the Secretary's discretion. (490.)
