# Leal v. McHugh

**731 F.3d 405 (5th Cir. 2013) · September 26, 2013 · No. 12-40069 · Civil Procedure**

Alleging both age discrimination and a favored applicant's personal connection did not defeat a plausible age-discrimination claim at the pleading stage. The court reversed dismissal of the age claims while affirming dismissal of the separate retaliation claim and denial of further amendment. Chief Judge Carl E. Stewart wrote for the panel of Stewart, King, and Owen; no separate opinion or dissent is reported. The assigned account is CivPro physical page 341, printed page 648, continuing the note on inconsistent factual allegations.

George Leal and John Lozano were longtime employees of the Corpus Christi Army Depot's Facilities, Engineering, and Management Division. Lozano, born in 1947, had worked there since 1985, accumulating extensive engineering and construction experience and favorable recognition. Leal, born in 1953, began in 1986 and alleged consistently high performance ratings. In 2009, they applied for two newly announced GS-12 positions. Their names were omitted from the September candidate list and added in October, but selecting official Michael Webb made his choices between the lists. The complaint's job-title omission is acknowledged in the court's third note, which identifies Construction Representative as the position. **Official slip pages 1–3, notes 2–3.**

Webb selected John Clay and Rudy Solis. Solis was substantially younger and allegedly had a close personal relationship with division chief Luis Salinas. Salinas had said the department needed new blood. The plaintiffs alleged that Salinas influenced Webb's selection despite denying involvement; Webb also said Salinas told him to choose Solis. The plaintiffs asserted their own superior qualifications and departures from ordinary selection procedures as evidence of discrimination. Note 4 distinguishes an additional theory concerning Clay's similar age and purported use as cover: that allegation was not in the complaint, and the panel did not rely on it to assess the pleaded claim. **3, note 4; 11.**

After administrative investigation and an EEOC hearing, the agency adopted a finding of no discrimination. The plaintiffs filed their federal action on August 2, 2011. They asserted age discrimination under the ADEA, and Leal additionally alleged retaliation for earlier EEO activity. The Secretary sought dismissal or summary judgment. Following the first amended complaint, dismissal, and a reconsideration motion that also requested further amendment, the district court substituted a December 20, 2011 opinion dismissing the claims. It reasoned that alleging Solis's personal connection made this a mixed-motive case forbidden by *Gross*, that the retaliation allegations were temporally too remote, and that amendment would be futile. **3–5.**

The panel reviewed Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal de novo under *Twombly* and *Iqbal*, accepting well-pleaded facts and favorable inferences while asking whether the claim was plausible. It first resolved which statutory provision applied. Federal employees' age claims fell under § 633a, whose language differs from the private-sector “because of” provision construed in *Gross*. The plaintiffs' broad ADEA citation did not waive application of § 633a; the Secretary and district court had themselves treated this as a federal-sector case. The opinion's administrative-route and exclusivity notes explain this distinct statutory framework. **3–4 note 5; 6–10 note 6.**

The court expressly **declined to decide the ultimate causation standard for the federal-sector provision**. It discussed the D.C. Circuit's different approach in *Ford*, but did not adopt it as the governing Fifth Circuit rule. The complaint survived even assuming *Gross*'s more demanding but-for requirement. The protected ages, qualifications, nonselection, selection of a substantially younger applicant, and the decisionmaking official's new-blood remark collectively made age discrimination plausible. Actual proof might remain improbable; plausibility did not require the court to choose the employer's competing explanation as established. Note 7 reserved whether the claims could survive the Secretary's alternative summary-judgment motion, which the district judge had not resolved. **9–12 note 7; 13 note 8.**

Rule 8(d) supplied an additional answer to the supposed contradiction. Inconsistent factual allegations may be pleaded, and an actionable discrimination claim does not disappear merely because the complaint also identifies a lawful reason for the decision. The panel said these allegations were not actually inconsistent, but even if they were, that alone would not justify dismissal. It also distinguished **but-for cause from sole cause**: other considerations may contribute to a decision while age remains the factor making the difference. The question of what the plaintiffs could ultimately prove remained for later stages. The assigned account correctly uses the case as an example of permissive alternative pleading; it does not supply a judicial finding that favoritism or age discrimination actually occurred. **12–15; assigned physical 341, printed 648.**

The Secretary also argued that a single complaint by two unsuccessful applicants could not provide relief because they could not both occupy Solis's position. The panel rejected that argument. The complaint sought reinstatement, backpay, and litigation-related relief, and successful federal-sector plaintiffs could potentially obtain fees even if reinstatement and backpay were infeasible. The opinion discussed possible front pay in lieu of infeasible reinstatement and emphasized that the court could not yet know which applicant would prevail. Note 11 separately identified requested remedies unavailable under the federal-sector ADEA; the decision did not hold that every requested form of damages was permissible. **15–16, note 11.**

Leal's retaliation claim had a different defect. He alleged prior EEO activity dating from three to nine years before the nonselection. The court found no plausibly pleaded causal connection between that protected activity and the employment decision. Its reliance on the long interval was an assessment of the allegations presented, not a rule that retaliation can never be established after a lengthy period with other supporting facts. It affirmed this dismissal. **17.**

The court also affirmed denial of additional amendment, although on grounds different from the district court's erroneous mixed-motive rationale. The plaintiffs had already completed administrative proceedings and amended once, did not identify the additional facts they would plead, and submitted no proposed second amended complaint with the request. The panel considered further amendment of the retaliation theory futile on this record, with the relevant knowledge within Leal's control. It applied abuse-of-discretion review, recognizing Rule 15's generous standard along with delay, repeated deficiencies, prejudice, and futility considerations. **17–19.**

The final judgment **affirmed dismissal of retaliation and denial of leave to amend; reversed dismissal of the age-discrimination claims; and remanded for further proceedings**. It awarded no promotion or damages and left the federal-sector ultimate causation question unresolved. **19.**
