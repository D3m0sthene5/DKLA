# In re Pharmaceutical Industry Average Wholesale Price Litigation

**588 F.3d 24 (1st Cir. 2009) — November 19, 2009 — Civil Procedure**

Chief Judge Lynch wrote for a panel with Judges Torruella and Howard. No separate opinion appears. The court affirmed approval of a $24 million settlement of claims against AstraZeneca concerning Zoladex, rejecting named plaintiff M. Joyce Howe’s objections. Its principal holding allowed the parties to agree in advance that money remaining after payment of class members’ treble damages would go to related charities. It did not decide the distinct controversy over court-created charitable distributions outside a settlement. (26–27, 34–36, 41.)

## The pricing dispute and the class

Pharmaceutical companies published an average wholesale price, or AWP, that insurers and Medicare used to calculate payments to physicians and patients’ co-payments. The alleged wrongdoing was a widening spread between that published figure and physicians’ much lower acquisition costs. Manufacturers allegedly marketed the spread to encourage prescriptions, while patients’ co-payments remained tied to the inflated benchmark. Zoladex, the drug involved in this appeal, commonly treated prostate cancer. This appeal concerned one consumer subclass within a much larger multidistrict litigation, rather than settlement of every drug manufacturer’s claims. (27–28 & nn.1–3.)

A coalition sued in Massachusetts in December 2001; related actions were consolidated there in April 2002. The district court organized the litigation into two tracks and three Track One classes. The Medicare Part B Co-Payment Class alleged violations of state consumer-protection statutes. Medicare generally covered 80 percent of the relevant cost, leaving a 20 percent co-payment. Most members’ Medigap coverage paid 80 percent of that co-payment, leaving them with only 20 percent of the co-payment as an actual loss. People whose supplemental coverage paid the entire co-payment were excluded. These distinctions mattered because an equal check to every recorded patient would not reflect actual out-of-pocket harm. (28.)

After an August 2005 order deferred certification until representatives were found, the court certified a Rule 23(b)(3) class in January 2006. It excluded residents of nine states whose consumer-protection statutes did not create the relevant action. Howe, individually and for her husband’s estate, and Leroy Townsend became AstraZeneca subclass representatives; counsel were appointed under Rule 23(g). (28–29 & nn.4–5.)

## Settlement terms and the judge’s changes

The parties reached a settlement in May 2007 on the eve of trial. Plaintiffs’ expert estimated losses of $31,128,851. AstraZeneca agreed to $24 million in compensation, including payments to representatives of deceased members, and the settlement expanded the class to include the nine previously excluded states in exchange for comprehensive peace. Claims exceeding the total would be reduced proportionally. Distribution costs and initially negotiated $8.7 million in attorneys’ fees and costs were separate obligations, not deductions from the class’s $24 million. A trial later involving other Track One classes did not make this settlement a judgment following trial of the same consumer subclass. (29 & n.6.)

Because many patients were elderly, deceased, or difficult to locate, the agreement used a relatively simple claims process rather than an upfront distribution fund. Minimal proof of Zoladex use allowed recovery initially set at twice estimated actual loss. The formula treated Medigap coverage as paying an average 80 percent of the co-payment. Money left after claims would go to mutually acceptable charities funding cancer research or patient care, subject to the court’s later approval of recipients and an initial $10 million charitable cap. (29–30.)

Howe challenged the class expansion, claims process, charitable distribution, insurance assumptions, potential subrogation, and alleged simultaneous negotiation of fees. At the May 2008 fairness hearing, the judge considered the objections in her tardy reply. The court required more money to go to members: first, treble compensation for the strongest claims during the December 1997–2003 “heartland” period. Later, in October 2008, he required treble compensation for all members if money remained after that priority and reduced fees to 30 percent of recovery. The amended agreement retained a $10 million combined limit on additional treble payments and the remaining charitable distribution. The court finally approved the settlement on December 15, 2008. (30–32 & nn.7–8.)

A February 2009 expert projection anticipated $17.2 million in claims and $6.8 million for charity. Those were estimates, not a finding that completed administration had paid those sums. The appellate opinion explains the operative priority as payment of treble damages before money went to charity. (32, 34–35.)

## Why the charitable remainder was permissible

Settlement approval was reviewed for abuse of discretion, with legal conclusions considered de novo. Arm’s-length negotiation after sufficient discovery supported a presumption of reasonableness, but the judge still had to balance the settlement’s benefits, costs, litigation risks, and attainable alternatives. (32–33.)

The court distinguished two settlement uses of cy pres. One substitutes charitable payments where individual distribution is economically infeasible. The other disposes of a remainder after compensation, often because members do not claim recovery. This case involved the latter. Although the parties planned the remainder’s use before administration ended, requiring them to wait would elevate timing over substance. Claimants could seek and receive more than their actual damages before charity received anything. The existence of members unlikely ever to claim payment supported an agreed disposition of the residue. (33–35 & n.10.)

Howe insisted that all remaining money belonged to claimants and relied on the ALI’s proposed Principles of Aggregate Litigation. The court treated that draft as guidance rather than the binding legal test. Its concern that members should normally recover fully before strangers benefited was met by treble compensation. Payment of a larger total also represented losses of people who would never claim recovery and prevented AstraZeneca from escaping that agreed obligation. The judge reasonably accommodated both compensation and the practical need to settle difficult litigation. The court left court-mandated cy pres outside settlement unresolved and did not approve every possible charitable recipient. (34–36 & n.11.)

## Conflicts and the alternative payment proposals

Howe alleged that counsel’s earlier representation of Prescription Action Litigation, an advocacy organization, diverted their loyalty toward a charitable fund. The court emphasized ongoing Rule 23(g)(4) fiduciary duties and careful judicial scrutiny, but found no evidence that the organization’s purpose was to promote such funds. Moreover, the agreed cancer-research or patient-care description did not fit that organization, so it could not receive these funds. Advocacy for this remainder after substantial class recovery did not itself show disloyalty. (36–37 & n.12.)

Howe’s undeveloped attack on reducing recovery for supplemental insurance was waived and also unpersuasive: accounting for insurance matched actual losses and preserved resources for those who bore more of the cost. Her own policy paid 50 percent, rather than the assumed 80 percent, of the co-payment. The court nevertheless accepted the expert’s average because few members were shown to differ and double or treble recovery still exceeded their actual damages; plaintiffs estimated Howe would receive about $200 more than her loss. (37 & nn.13–14.)

Her proposal to mail equal checks using the CMS database also failed. That database did not identify supplemental coverage. It would pay some fully covered nonmembers, overpay some members, underpay others, and sharply reduce individual recovery. A Track Two settlement’s different method was not controlling: those claims involved many drugs, varying doses, and different periods, whereas regular Zoladex dosing allowed more practicable loss calculations. (31 & n.9, 37–38.)

## Preservation, certification, and exact result

The objection to simultaneous fee negotiations was waived because Howe did not renew it at final approval. The class-definition objection under Rule 23(c)(1)(B) was also raised for the first time on appeal, but the court discussed it for guidance. The rule applies to amended certification orders, yet does not require complete restatement of earlier work. Incorporation was sufficient here: prior orders described the class, claims, issues, defenses, and statutory differences in detail, and the final order’s expansion reflected the negotiated demand for total peace. Footnote 19 distinguishes defining a class and claims from necessarily justifying them. (38–41 & nn.15–19.)

The record also refuted the claim that counsel had not been appointed under Rule 23(g); an additional attack on the adequacy of that appointment was waived. The panel affirmed settlement approval.
