# Kingston v. Preston
2 Doug. 689, 99 Eng. Rep. 437 (K.B. 1773), also reported at Lofft 194 | Decided Easter Term, 1773 | Contracts

## Agreement for service followed by a business transfer
Kingston’s declaration described articles made March 24, 1770. He would serve Preston as a covenant servant in the silk-mercer business for one year and a quarter at £200 annually. At the end of that period, Preston would give up the business and stock in trade at a fair valuation to Kingston and Preston’s nephew, or another nominee. The new traders would execute partnership deeds for fourteen years and could then operate in Preston’s house. Complete Douglas report reproduced in Langdell, pp. 22–23, archived PDF pp. 32–33.

Kingston promised to accept the business and stock, execute the deeds, and procure good and sufficient security approved by Preston at and before sealing and delivering the deeds. The security would cover £250 monthly payments to Preston, in lieu of half the stock’s monthly produce, until the stock’s value had been reduced to £4,000. This was more than a freestanding exchange of service for wages. Transferring a valuable going concern exposed Preston to a continuing payment risk, and the security clause addressed that risk before he relinquished the business. Langdell reproduction, p. 22 (PDF p. 32).

## Pleadings and the parties’ competing theories
Kingston brought debt for nonperformance of the covenants. He alleged that he had performed and been ready to perform his covenants and charged Preston with refusing to surrender the business at the agreed time. Preston pleaded both failure to offer sufficient security and failure actually to give it. Kingston demurred generally to both pleas. The dispute thus concerned whether the pleaded lack of security was a legal defense to the transfer claim; it was not an appeal from a trial verdict assigning damages. Id.

Buller argued for Kingston that the covenants were mutual and independent. On that theory, Preston’s duty to transfer persisted despite Kingston’s failure to provide security, and Preston would have to sue separately for that failure. Grose answered that the covenants were dependent in nature and performance had to be alleged. Security was the transaction’s central protection; forcing Preston to hand over the beneficial business and stock in exchange for Kingston’s unsecured personal credit, admitted to be worthless, would defeat it. Id. at 22–23 (PDF pp. 32–33).

## Mansfield’s three categories of covenants
Delivering the court’s judgment, Mansfield distinguished three arrangements. With mutual independent covenants, either party can sue for breach of the promise in that party’s favor, and the claimant’s separate breach supplies no excuse for the defendant. With dependent covenants, one performance must occur first, and the other party is not liable on its covenant until that prior condition is fulfilled. With concurrent mutual conditions, the party ready and offering to perform can sue when the other refuses, even though neither party necessarily must act first. Id. at 23 (PDF p. 33).

The classification follows the parties’ evident sense and meaning. The physical order of promises in the deed does not determine their legal sequence. Courts look to the order of performance required by the intended transaction. This separates two questions: whether a party has promised an act and whether that act must precede the other party’s duty. The existence of reciprocal promises alone does not answer the second question.

## Why the security had to come first
The agreement’s essence was that Preston should receive reliable security before delivering the stock and business, rather than depend solely on Kingston’s personal credit after parting with them. Accordingly, providing the security was necessarily a condition precedent to Preston’s transfer obligation. Accepting Kingston’s independent-covenant argument would deprive Preston of the protection at the moment it mattered and replace it with a later claim against an inadequately secured purchaser. The court entered judgment for Preston because Kingston’s required prior performance had not occurred. Id.

That conclusion applies the security requirement to the transaction’s intended sequence. It does not say that any failure under any reciprocal contract excuses all counterperformance, or that the existence of a damages claim automatically makes promises independent. Mansfield’s concurrent category also preserves an offered performance as sufficient when the transaction calls for simultaneous exchange; the security here was required beforehand.
