# Le Fort Enterprises, Inc. v. Lantern 18, LLC
491 Mass. 144, 199 N.E.3d 1257 (2023) | Contracts

## Court, review, and result
The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court decided this appeal January 3, 2023; the reported reproduction identifies a January 20 correction. Justice Wendlandt wrote for the seven-member court, with no separate writing. The court affirmed summary judgment for the seller of a cleaning franchise. Neither pandemic impracticability nor frustration excused the purchaser and its individual co-obligors from paying the remaining purchase price on this record, and equity did not warrant rewriting their note. The court transferred the timely appeal on its own initiative after the Superior Court had granted summary judgment and denied reconsideration. (Slip opinion pp. 1–2, 8–10, 35; 491 Mass. at 144–145, 148–149, 164.)

Review was de novo, with evidence viewed favorably to the co-obligors. Although these defenses ordinarily raise factual questions, summary judgment was appropriate where no rational view of the evidence supported them. The decision therefore turns on the parties' agreements and the evidence actually marshalled; it does not categorically eliminate pandemic defenses in other contracts. (Slip pp. 9–10, 33.)

## Purchase, extension, and allocation of financial risk
In May 2015 Lantern 18 bought a Merry Maids of Boston franchise from Le Fort. It paid a nonrefundable deposit and a closing sum; Lantern 18 and Samuel Bergman promised monthly payments of the balance and a May 2018 balloon payment. The initial note was not in the appellate record. The purchase agreement allowed a $15,000 principal reduction if sales fell below a threshold during the first six months. No other clause linked the price to business revenue, and there was no force majeure clause. A counterclaim asserting failure to credit that adjustment was denied by Le Fort and dismissed by stipulation, rather than adjudicated as a proven breach. (Slip pp. 3–4 & nn. 3–5.)

When the original balance remained apparently unpaid, Le Fort agreed to an extension. The August 2018 amended note gave Lantern 18 and both Samuel and Marcia Bergman, individually, four more years, with a May 2022 balloon payment and joint and several liability. It specified 5.5 percent interest and an eight-year amortization schedule. A November modification reduced monthly payments to $3,243 in exchange for two $12,500 payments in November and December. Failure to pay within ten days was a default permitting acceleration; late payments beyond fifteen days incurred a separate charge. Financial-information duties and other default provisions also mattered. (Slip pp. 4–6 & nn. 6–7, 24–26 & nn. 15–20.)

The note expressly made an adverse change in an obligor's financial condition or collateral an event of default if it impaired the seller's security or increased its risk and remained uncured thirty days after notice. Each co-obligor had to permit inspection and furnish financial information. Additional default interest, late charges, collection expenses, and acceleration protected the seller rather than conditioning repayment on the franchise's success. Together with personal joint liability and the limited six-month sales adjustment, these provisions assigned subsequent business risk to the buyers. (Slip pp. 23–28 & nn. 14–21.)

## Pandemic disruption and default
Massachusetts declared an emergency March 10, 2020; the WHO declared a pandemic the next day. Executive orders effectively shut the franchise down from March until June. After reopening, many customers declined cleaning crews, and an infected crew member required colleagues to quarantine. Samuel's affidavit described a precipitous revenue decline. The co-obligors missed the April 1 payment. On April 22 the seller declared default, exercised acceleration, requested financial records, and warned that suit would follow unless stated arrearages and fees were paid. The action began May 21, and by the October summary-judgment motion six installments had been missed. (Slip pp. 6–9.)

Note 9 also records that pandemic-loan funds were offered to bring monthly installments current through July. That tender did not cover the other amounts due under the note; funds apparently went into escrow, and no payment reached Le Fort after March. The court did not treat the proposal as curing all defaults or reversing acceleration. (Slip p. 10 & n. 9.)

## Impracticability required causation and an unallocated risk
The court traced impossibility to contracts presupposing the continued existence of a necessary person, thing, or circumstance, and the later doctrine of impracticability to exceptional events making promised performance vitally different. It required a supervening extreme event causing impracticability, nonoccurrence as a basic assumption with the risk not assigned to the promisor, and absence of fault. The pandemic's impact was not the obligors' fault, so the first two elements controlled. Temporary impracticability generally suspends performance while it lasts, rather than discharging the obligation, subject to materially increased burdens afterward. (Slip pp. 12–18 & nn. 10–12.)

The affidavit could support inability to fund payments from franchise revenues, but contained no evidence about the individual co-obligors' financial condition or their own ability to pay. The promise was repayment, not repayment exclusively from business proceeds. The necessary causal link between pandemic conditions and inability to perform the actual obligation was missing. Mere financial difficulty or expense ordinarily does not excuse payment; juxtaposing a pandemic and payment problems was insufficient. (Slip pp. 19–22 & n. 13.)

Even adequate causal evidence would not cure the separate risk-allocation problem. Stable franchise revenue after the first six months was not an essential contractual assumption. The extension and personal promises protected the seller against deteriorating finances. The parties need not have contemplated COVID-19 specifically to allocate financial deterioration regardless of its source. The court considered the absence of force majeure language as additional context, alongside the affirmative repayment protections. (Slip pp. 22–28 & nn. 14–21.)

## Frustration and equitable modification
Frustration concerns destruction of a principal purpose understood by both parties, rather than inability to perform. Its defining purpose must be so foundational that the transaction otherwise makes little sense. The court distinguished performance impossibility from coronation-viewing and concrete-barrier examples where performance remained physically possible but a shared contractual object disappeared. Temporary frustration likewise ordinarily suspends duties under Restatement § 269. (Slip pp. 28–32 & nn. 22–24.)

Here the shared purpose was acquisition of the franchise and spreading its purchase-price payments over time. Le Fort had fully performed by delivering the business in 2015. Nothing supported a mutually understood purpose of funding repayment solely through later revenues. Pandemic disruption therefore did not frustrate the purpose established by this record. The court expressly left pandemic defenses available where other facts and contracts support them. (Slip pp. 32–33.)

Finally, the obligors asked the court to amend the note under its general equity jurisdiction, permitting cure to prevent prejudice and a supposed windfall. The record showed neither fraud, mistake, accident, illegality, predatory pricing, duress, nor an inequitable acceleration clause. Acceleration collected the agreed remaining price of a business already received. Missed payments, failure to cure, and failure to provide records were material defaults, unlike the technical breach in a contrasting case. The court declined modification and affirmed the judgment; it did not merely remand to consider relief. (Slip pp. 33–35 & nn. 25–26.)
