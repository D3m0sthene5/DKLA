# Calder v. Jones

**465 U.S. 783 (1984) · Supreme Court of the United States · March 20, 1984**

## The reporter’s and editor’s own connection to California

Shirley Jones, a professional entertainer who lived and worked in California, sued over an article in the October 9, 1979 issue of the National Enquirer. The article alleged that her drinking interfered with her professional obligations. Jones and her husband brought claims for libel, invasion of privacy, and intentional infliction of emotional harm against the newspaper, its local distributor, reporter South, and president and editor John Calder. Her husband later voluntarily dismissed his complaint. The publisher and distributor answered without contesting California jurisdiction; this Supreme Court proceeding concerned South and Calder. 465 U.S. at 784–785 & n.1, 788 & n.9.

The Enquirer was a Florida corporation with its principal place of business there. It had a circulation exceeding five million, including roughly 600,000 copies in California—almost twice the circulation in the next largest state market. South and Calder also lived in Florida, but their roles differed. South wrote the article’s first draft, conducted most research in Florida through calls to California sources, and read a draft to Jones’s husband by telephone shortly before publication. He regularly traveled to California. Calder approved the proposed subject, edited the final article, and declined Jones’s request for a retraction. His two California visits were unrelated to producing this article. Id. at 785–786 & nn.2–4.

Whether South had made a California research trip for the article was disputed. The Court did not resolve that dispute or rely on the trip. Its holding instead rested on the character and California focus of the allegedly wrongful conduct, which could support jurisdiction even though the defendants wrote and edited in Florida. Id. at 785–787 & nn.4, 6.

## Procedure and the parties’ arguments

The two individuals received process by mail in Florida, appeared specially, and moved to quash service. The California Superior Court believed their conduct causing California injury would ordinarily support jurisdiction, but gave added weight to the potential chilling effect on reporters and editors required to defend in distant courts. It also thought Jones could obtain adequate relief from the publisher. It therefore granted the motion. The California Court of Appeal reversed, finding jurisdiction based on the defendants’ intended and actual tortious effects in California and rejecting the additional First Amendment barrier. The state supreme court declined further review. Id. at 784–787.

The individuals sought Supreme Court review by appeal. The Court held that the appellate route was unavailable: California’s statute authorized jurisdiction to constitutional limits, and the state court had decided whether the Constitution permitted jurisdiction rather than adjudicating the statute’s constitutionality. The Court treated the papers as a certiorari petition and granted it. Although the merits trial had not occurred, the jurisdictional federal issue was sufficiently final for review. Id. at 787–788 & nn.7–8.

South and Calder argued that their employer’s circulation could not be attributed to them. Ordinary employees neither control a publisher’s distant marketing nor receive a direct stake in its local sales. Merely foreseeing that an article would reach California, they argued, could not be enough. They compared themselves to a Florida welder who works on a boiler later sold by his employer into California: jurisdiction over the manufacturer should not automatically establish jurisdiction over every employee. Id. at 789.

## Holding: the alleged intentional conduct was expressly aimed at California

The unanimous Court sustained California jurisdiction over both individuals. Applying minimum contacts through the relationship among the defendant, forum, and litigation, it identified California as the focal point of both the allegedly libelous article and the resulting harm. The story concerned Jones’s California activities and a career centered there, drew upon California sources, and threatened harm to her professional reputation and emotional well-being primarily in that state. The defendants knew the brunt of the injury would occur where she lived and worked and where the newspaper enjoyed its largest circulation. Id. at 788–790.

The Court distinguished that alleged intentional targeting from untargeted negligence. South and Calder were primary participants in the challenged conduct: one wrote the article, and the other edited it. The jurisdictional connection was therefore their own role in an alleged wrongdoing expressly aimed at California, not merely the predictable movement of an employer’s product. Persons who knowingly cause the relevant injury in California through conduct directed there could reasonably anticipate answering there for their statements, even if they remained in Florida. Id. at 789–790.

This was a jurisdictional ruling on allegedly tortious conduct. It did not establish that the article was false, that the defendants had committed libel, or that Jones had proved the elements or damages of her claims. The Court’s reasoning explains why California could adjudicate those questions. Id. at 788–791.

The Court agreed with the defendants on one premise: their contacts had to be assessed individually, rather than simply inferred from the employer’s California activities. But the same principle defeated the suggestion of automatic employee immunity. Acting in an official capacity did not insulate an individual whose own conduct established the required relationship. Both individuals met that requirement here through their participation in the targeted article. The decision neither imports every corporate contact into every employee’s analysis nor immunizes an employee who acts through a corporation. Id. at 790.

## Why the First Amendment did not create an additional jurisdictional shield

The Court rejected the trial court’s speech-based adjustment to the jurisdiction analysis. Constitutional safeguards already limited the substantive law of defamation, including liability for protected expression. Reintroducing the same concerns to defeat otherwise proper personal jurisdiction would complicate the contacts analysis and double count those protections. The Court’s ruling preserved the ability to invoke substantive speech defenses while requiring the individuals to litigate in a constitutionally permissible forum. Id. at 790–791.

## Judgment and assigned context

Justice Rehnquist wrote for a unanimous Court, with no separate writings. The Court affirmed the California Court of Appeal’s judgment. The immediate consequence was that South and Calder could not quash service on the jurisdictional theory presented; the case could proceed toward merits adjudication. Jones did not receive a Supreme Court damages award or a final determination of libel liability. Id. at 791.

The complete opinion contains nine judicial footnotes, including the disputed research trip, statutory and review-route issues, and the allegation about drinking; those details matter to distinguishing the jurisdictional decision from a factual merits verdict.
