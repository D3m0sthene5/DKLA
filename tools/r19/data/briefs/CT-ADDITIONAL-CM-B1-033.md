# Keller v. Holderman
11 Mich. 248 (1863) | Contracts

## Court, facts, and procedure

Chief Justice Martin delivered the Michigan Supreme Court’s opinion on May 12, 1863; the other justices concurred, with no separate writing. The case came by error from the Berrien Circuit Court after a trial without a jury. Holderman had sued Keller on a dishonored $300 check drawn by Keller on a banker in Niles. The watch involved was an old silver watch worth approximately $15. Keller took and kept it until trial, when he offered to return it and Holderman refused. These are the roles stated in the complete original report: Keller wrote the check and received the watch; Holderman was the plaintiff seeking payment. (11 Mich. at 248–249.)

The circuit judge found that the entire transaction was playful banter: Holderman did not expect to sell the watch, and Keller did not intend to buy it at the check’s stated price. Keller had no money with the banker and had meant to place a condition on the check preventing liability, but failed to insert it. The trial judge nevertheless held him liable because he omitted that condition and retained the watch, entering judgment for the check’s amount. The absence of a condition and the retained possession were thus the trial court’s reasons for enforcement, not findings of a serious bargain. (Id. at 248.)

## Holding and reasoning

Martin held that the trial court’s own finding of mutual banter required the opposite legal conclusion: no contract had been made, and Holderman had no cause of action on the check. The Supreme Court reversed, awarding costs of both the Supreme Court and the court below. The report does not order a new trial, award the watch’s reasonable value, or adjudicate some separate restitution action. (Id. at 249.)

The decisive finding concerned both participants’ understanding of the transaction. The opinion therefore does not establish that a party may escape a reasonably understood serious promise merely by asserting a secret intention to joke. Nor does it announce that a price substantially exceeding market value invalidates a contract for inadequate consideration. The $300 figure and $15 value form part of the reported context, but Martin’s short reasoning rests on the trial judge’s express finding that neither party contemplated the stated purchase and sale. Treating the check alone as dispositive overlooked that finding about the underlying transaction. (Id. at 248–249.)
