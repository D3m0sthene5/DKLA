## Loan, foreclosure, and asserted promise

The plaintiffs obtained a $276,000 loan to buy property in Sugar Land, Texas. A promissory note evidenced the debt and a deed of trust secured it. They defaulted on their payment obligations, and the property was sold at a foreclosure sale in April 2011. In September 2011 they filed suit in state court to delay eviction following the completed foreclosure. The chronology matters: this action followed the sale; the excerpt does not describe a suit that prevented the sale itself. (373.)

Besides an injunction, they sought relief for fraud, wrongful foreclosure due to fraud, slander of title, promissory estoppel, unreasonable collection, and an accounting. Their relevant allegation was that the defendant had promised not to foreclose while reviewing a loan modification. That promise is an allegation recorded by the court, not a finding that the bank actually made it. The supplied text does not provide the promise's wording, speaker, date, or a detailed reliance chronology. (373.)

## Procedural posture and competing positions

The defendant removed the suit on diversity grounds and moved to dismiss, arguing, among other things, that the statute of frauds barred all the claims. The plaintiffs responded that the state court had issued a temporary restraining order before removal and that they asserted promissory estoppel based on the alleged promise to postpone foreclosure. The reproduced background records both points, but the omitted sections prevent an account of how the federal court disposed of the restraining-order argument or what additional dismissal grounds it considered. The motion was directed to the sufficiency of the pleaded claims; the reproduced ruling is not a trial verdict finding that the promise never occurred. (373.)

## Issue and reproduced rules

The reproduced analysis asks whether an alleged oral agreement to delay foreclosure on this loan could be enforced notwithstanding Texas's writing requirement, and whether promissory estoppel supplied a route around that requirement. Section 26.02(b) of the Texas Business and Commerce Code required a loan agreement involving more than $50,000 to be in writing and signed by the party to be bound or its authorized representative. The $276,000 loan exceeded that threshold. The definition of a loan agreement included a financial institution's promise to delay repayment or make a financial accommodation. Thus, the court did not treat the alleged postponement as a separate undertaking outside the statute merely because it concerned the timing of foreclosure. (373, including the reproduced statutory footnotes b–c.)

The court stated a further requirement for promissory estoppel in this setting: the promisor must have promised to sign a written document that would satisfy the statute of frauds. It applied that requirement both to estoppel as an affirmative claim and to estoppel used in opposition to the statute-of-frauds defense. The excerpt cites Burnette v. Wells Fargo Bank and Ford v. City State Bank of Palacios for that proposition. Those citations identify the court's authorities; this account does not separately certify their entire opinions or subsequent treatment. (373–374.)

## Holding and reasoning available in the excerpt

The court characterized the alleged foreclosure delay as a financial accommodation covered by the statute. Because the alleged undertaking lacked the required statutory form, the plaintiffs could not enforce it on that pleaded basis. The promissory-estoppel theory failed for a more specific reason: the plaintiffs had not alleged a promise to sign a statute-compliant written agreement embodying the foreclosure postponement. Alleging the underlying oral accommodation did not itself allege the additional promise needed for the estoppel exception described by the court. The judge therefore held that the defendant was entitled to dismissal of the promissory-estoppel claim. (373–374.)

This reasoning concerns enforceability and pleading. It does not establish that a bank can always disregard a loan-modification agreement, that every foreclosure-related tort necessarily fails, or that these plaintiffs failed to receive every notice required by statute. The excerpt supplies neither a signed modification nor a determination of a statutory notice dispute. Extending its reasoning to those questions would require the omitted opinion and the relevant record. (373–375.)
