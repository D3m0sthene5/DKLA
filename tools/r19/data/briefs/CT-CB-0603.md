# Colfax Envelope Corp. v. Local No. 458-3M
20 F.3d 750 (7th Cir. 1994) | Contracts | Patent ambiguity, assent, and an arbitrator's authority

## A staffing summary produced conflicting expectations

Colfax manufactured envelopes and employed seventeen union-represented printers. Its two presses printed sheets seventy-eight inches wide: one in four colors and another capable of five, though usually operated in four. Colfax did not participate in the Chicago Lithographers Association's collective negotiations with the union. Instead it received summaries of newly negotiated changes and, if satisfied, ordinarily signed the full agreement. It could bargain separately if it disliked the association's terms. (20 F.3d at 751.)

Under the 1987–1991 agreement, smaller four-color presses required three workers, presses over fifty inches required four, and five-color presses over fifty-five inches required five unless used for four colors, when four sufficed. Colfax consequently staffed both presses with four except when its second press printed five colors. In 1991 the union sent a changes summary and requested acceptance. Its staffing entries specified a four-color sixty-inch press with three workers and a five-color seventy-eight-inch press with four. Colfax president and majority shareholder Charles Patten, encouraged by union members saying he would like the changes, understood this to allow three workers on its presses when run in four colors and signed the letter. (Id. at 751–752.)

A later full agreement contained a typographical error supporting Patten's reading. A corrected copy instead extended three-worker staffing to the smaller range ending at sixty inches and retained four workers for larger four-color presses. The change therefore did not benefit Colfax. Patten refused to sign the full agreement, hoping to negotiate improved terms, while the union asserted that acceptance of the summary already bound the company. The appellate court emphasized that the parties treated the accepted summary, interpreted with the agreement it modified, as the relevant contract; the subsequently corrected full text did not conclusively settle the summary's meaning. (Id. at 752, 754.)

## The company challenged formation; the union sought arbitration

Colfax sued under Labor Management Relations Act section 301 for a declaration that no collective agreement existed because the parties had not agreed on essential staffing terms. The union counterclaimed to compel arbitration under a clause covering disputes arising from application or interpretation. The district court granted the union summary judgment, treating the summary entry as unambiguously referring to sixty-inch presses alone. The union itself read it as extending a smaller-press range upward to sixty inches; Colfax read it as reaching presses of sixty inches and above. Those interpretations were not identical, and the appellate court did not bind the arbitrator to the district court's point-specific construction. (Id. at 752, 755.)

## Posner distinguished interpretive risk from hidden misunderstanding

Chief Judge Posner explained that literal coincidence of subjective understandings is not generally essential to an enforceable contract. Parties often leave contingencies unaddressed or hold different expectations, yet their dispute calls for interpretation rather than dissolution. Cases such as *Raffles* and *Oswald* permit an escape where no sensible, nonarbitrary basis selects between equally situated understandings. If one interpretation is more reasonable, permitting an idiosyncratic but sincerely held alternative to defeat formation would undermine contractual enforcement. (Id. at 752–753.)

The clearest hidden-misunderstanding examples involved an offer distorted in transmission: purported duplicate contracts bearing different prices through an architect's fraud, or an oral price that both parties carelessly expressed and interpreted differently. These were illustrative precedents, not findings of fraud in the staffing negotiations. Posner's distinction was between language that reasonably appeared unequivocal to each party but contained a buried ambiguity and language whose competing readings should have been apparent when accepted. (Id. at 753–754.)

The summary muddied the new staffing rules, but its sixty-inch entry did not expressly address Colfax's seventy-eight-inch four-color press. A reasonable company could recognize the union's alternative as plausible. Colfax could hope to obtain its preferred interpretation, but could not accept on the assumption that either it would win the interpretation dispute or leave without liability. Assenting to a patently ambiguous term accepted the risk of an authorized tribunal's construction. Posner accordingly treated the dispute as one about meaning under an existing arbitration arrangement, rather than a hidden misunderstanding necessarily preventing assent. (Id. at 753–754.)

## Assent to arbitration remained the judicial question

The court distinguished a dispute about agreeing to arbitrate itself. Arbitration is contractual, and a court must decide whether that agreement exists. A party cannot invalidate an agreed arbitration clause merely by asserting that the larger contract is voidable, such as through fraudulent inducement; it must show a defect in assent or validity affecting the arbitration agreement itself. Colfax and the union had consistently chosen arbitration in their prior dealings, and this disagreement concerned contract meaning. It therefore fell within their chosen mechanism. (Id. at 754–755.)

Posner went further: even if the court's staffing analysis were wrong and no relevant agreement existed on that substantive term, the parties shared sufficient assent to submit disputes over the collective agreement they accepted to arbitration. The arbitrator consequently could interpret the summary and could ultimately conclude that misunderstanding required rescission. The district judge's own staffing interpretation did not bind that decision. The essential judicial holding at this stage was an enforceable agreement to arbitrate, not a final administrative determination of how many workers each press required. (Id. at 755.)

## Two concurrences clarify the dispute about arbitral rescission

Judge Cudahy concurred separately. He accepted Posner's treatment of the misleading meeting-of-minds metaphor but thought the formation question simpler: the summary listed staffing changes for specifically identified presses and contained no entry changing Colfax's four-color seventy-eight-inch staffing. He saw no textual basis for Colfax's upward-range inference and favored the district court's rationale for finding an agreement containing arbitration. Interpreting the terms still belonged to the arbitrator. (Id. at 755–756.)

Cudahy distinguished literal nonformation from using absence of agreement as a metaphor for an interpretation dispute for which rescission was an appropriate remedy. He ultimately agreed that the arbitrator could grant rescission and restitution after construing the agreement. His footnote thought widespread arbitral abandonment of labor contracts unlikely because arbitration itself is an important benefit of the relationship. He also observed that the union's briefs conceded the court had only established a binding arbitration obligation, making later insistence on a preclusive judicial merits decision difficult. (Id. at 756–757 & n.1.)

District Judge McDade, sitting by designation, also concurred in affirmance and in the conclusion that Colfax accepted the risk of a patently ambiguous staffing term. He described the approach as novel and noted that a more traditional analysis might have remanded for extrinsic evidence of formation. He nevertheless agreed that the express arbitration clause covered the dispute. (Id. at 757.)

McDade disagreed with allowing the arbitrator to revisit contract existence. In his view, the court had determined that a contract formed; formation belonged to the court, and an arbitrator authorized to interpret that contract could not reverse that finding. Thus all three judges agreed to compel arbitration, while Posner and Cudahy permitted a later rescission determination and McDade would limit the arbitrator on that issue. Calling the entire reasoning unanimous would conceal this substantive disagreement. (Id. at 757–758.)

## Judgment and complete reading

The Seventh Circuit affirmed on April 1, 1994. Colfax had to arbitrate. The panel did not enter a staffing award, grant rescission, or conclusively require the arbitrator to use the district court's meaning. Posner wrote the principal opinion, with separate concurrences from Cudahy and McDade; the result was unanimous. (Id. at 755–758.)
