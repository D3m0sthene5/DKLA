# Tusa v. Roffe and KKA, Inc.
791 So. 2d 512 (Fla. 4th DCA 2001) | Contracts

## Decision and the two contracts
On July 18, 2001, Florida's Fourth District affirmed the dismissal of pizza-restaurant tenant Gaetano Tusa's claims against competing tenant KKA, but reversed to allow his claim against their landlord, Ralph Roffe, to proceed. A statutory requirement defeated Tusa's effort to enforce KKA's lease as a beneficiary: that restrictive covenant neither expressly identified him as a beneficiary nor expressly stated that it was for his benefit. His own lease, however, protected him against competing pizza sales, and Roffe breached it by knowingly permitting such sales without ordering their cessation or threatening eviction. Polen, C.J., wrote for a unanimous panel with Stone and Gross. Rehearing was denied August 22, 2001. (512, 514–515.)

## Chronology and trial dispositions
In October 1997, Tusa entered a ten-year business lease beginning in January 1998 to operate Rocco's Pizza in Hollywood, Florida. The lease allowed other restaurants on property owned or controlled by the landlord, with an exception prohibiting anyone other than Rocco's Pizza from selling pizza on the premises. In December 1997, Roffe leased space in the same building to KKA for The Oasis. That separate lease prohibited KKA from making or selling specified foods, including pizza. The Oasis nevertheless sold pizza. After learning of those sales and Roffe's refusal to act, Tusa sued both defendants for injunctive relief, alleging breaches of the two leases' restrictive covenants. (513–514.)

KKA moved for summary judgment because Tusa was not its contracting counterparty and could not satisfy the special statutory conditions for third-party enforcement. The trial court agreed. Tusa then attempted to add a declaratory-relief count asking for a determination of the parties' rights. KKA sought to strike or dismiss that amendment because the earlier summary judgment had resolved its liability. Roffe moved to strike Count II and for summary judgment on the amended complaint, arguing that he had not breached Tusa's lease. The trial court granted all three motions, entered a final order dismissing all claims, and awarded Roffe $11,075 in attorney's fees. The consolidated appeals challenged the merits dispositions and fee order. (514.)

## Why the competing tenant could not be sued as a beneficiary promisor
Section 542.335 governed business restrictive covenants entered after July 1, 1996. Subsection (1)(f)(1) required the restrictive covenant to identify the claimant expressly as a third-party beneficiary and state expressly that the covenant was intended for that person's benefit. KKA's prohibition on pizza sales did neither for Tusa. The court therefore affirmed dismissal of the claims against KKA. A practical benefit from preventing nearby pizza sales could not substitute for the express contractual identification that this statute required. (514.)

The court recognized an awkward procedural consequence in note 1. A later injunction against Roffe might require him to terminate KKA's lease, even though KKA had successfully left this litigation. That might necessitate a second suit. The concern about judicial economy did not alter KKA's entitlement to dismissal, and the note did not itself direct termination or create a presently entered injunction against KKA. (514 n. 1.)

## Why the landlord remained answerable under Tusa's own lease
The statute also required a legitimate business interest and a restraint reasonably necessary to protect it. Its listed interests included customer relationships and goodwill, along with trade secrets, confidential information, and specialized training. Courts were instructed to construe the restraints to protect established legitimate interests reasonably and could enforce a signed covenant by effective remedies, including injunctions. (514–515 & n. 2.)

It was undisputed that the covenant in Tusa's lease aimed to prevent loss of customers to another restaurant selling pizza on the same premises. The court classified that protective interest within the statutory customer-relationship category. It read the covenant to prohibit Roffe from leasing space in the same building to another restaurant that sold pizza. An interpretation allowing him to authorize that competition would defeat the covenant's acknowledged protective purpose. Contract terms should receive a reasonable construction rather than one granting an unfair advantage or producing an absurd result. (515.)

Roffe knew KKA was selling pizza in the relevant leased premises. The appellate court held that his failure to order KKA to stop or face eviction breached the covenant in his contract with Tusa. Unlike the unsuccessful beneficiary theory against KKA, this claim enforced a promise in a lease to which Tusa and Roffe were parties. (515.)

## Remedy, territorial limit, and the assigned account
The decision affirmed in part and reversed in part, directing that the case proceed against Roffe alone. It identified a viable claim for injunctive relief but did not itself issue a detailed injunction, order eviction, or fix a damages award. The $11,075 figure records the appealed trial-level fee award, not a recovery granted to Tusa. (514–515.)

Note 3 also limited the possible remedy geographically. The Oasis apparently occupied two joined areas: its original premises, owned by KKA and outside Roffe's lease, and an adjoining 29-seat area in Roffe's shopping center subject to that lease. Tusa's counsel agreed that relief would apply only to the 29-seat portion. The decision cannot be read to prohibit pizza sales throughout the separate KKA-owned premises. (515 n. 3.)

The assigned Tenant Mix problem at printed page 1176 adapts Gianni's tobacco restriction and cites Tusa as an example where a statute defeated beneficiary enforcement. That short account accurately captures the disposition against the competing tenant. The complete opinion adds the distinct successful route against the landlord under the claimant's own lease and the limited premises that a resulting injunction could cover. It is a decision applying a specific business-covenant statute, rather than a general rule that every commercial tenant lacks beneficiary rights under another lease.
