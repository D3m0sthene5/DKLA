# In re Labatt Food Service, L.P.
279 S.W.3d 640 (Tex. 2009) | Contracts

## Holding and mandamus relief
The Texas Supreme Court held that a deceased employee's parents and children had to arbitrate their wrongful-death claims against his employer under the agreement he had signed. The decisive ground was the entirely derivative character of wrongful-death claims under Texas statute: the beneficiaries occupied the employee's legal position, including his contractual choice of forum. The court did not need to decide the employer's alternative third-party-beneficiary or parental-authority theories. Justice Johnson delivered the court's opinion on February 13, 2009; the published report contains no separate writings and records rehearing denied May 1, 2009. (640, 642, 644, 649.)

The court conditionally granted mandamus, directing the trial court to compel arbitration. The writ would issue only if the trial court did not comply. It awarded no wrongful-death damages and did not determine whether the employer caused the death. (649.)

## Occupational plan and the underlying claim
Labatt did not subscribe to workers' compensation insurance. Instead, employees could elect an occupational-injury plan by signing an Election of Comprehensive Benefits, Indemnity, and Arbitration Agreement. Paragraph three elected coverage individually and on behalf of heirs and beneficiaries and required indemnification for occupational injury or death claims other than plan claims. Paragraph four required binding arbitration under the FAA for disputes concerning the agreement, the plan, and occupational injury or death. Paragraph eight provided severability of invalid provisions. (642.)

Carlos Dancy, Jr. elected the plan and signed. He later died from an apparent asthma attack while working. His parents and children sued for wrongful death. Labatt moved to compel arbitration. The beneficiaries denied that their nonsignatory status permitted compulsion and asserted that the indemnity provision was an unlawful preinjury waiver, making the whole agreement void. The trial court denied the motion without reasons, and the intermediate court denied mandamus relief. (642.)

## Court decides whether these nonsignatories are bound
The court applied the historical Texas mandamus rule for denial of FAA arbitration: an inadequate remedy by appeal and a clear abuse of discretion warranted relief. It deferred to supported factual determinations but reviewed legal enforceability de novo. (642–643.)

Who is bound was a gateway issue for a court absent clear and unmistakable delegation. The agreement was silent on who should resolve that question, so the court decided it. The opinion acknowledged uncertainty then concerning the relative roles of federal and state substantive law in nonsignatory arbitration, applied Texas substantive law consistently with federal principles, and emphasized that the FAA made arbitration contracts as enforceable as other contracts, rather than more enforceable. That historical discussion is not a certification that the issue remains unresolved today. (643.)

Texas law and equity could bind nonsignatories where they would be bound to the underlying contract generally. Labatt advanced beneficiary status, derivative claims, and statutory authority to bind minor children. The court selected the derivative-claim ground as dispositive. It did not hold that these relatives were independently intended contract beneficiaries merely because the plan referred to heirs. (644.)

## Why the statutory claim carried the decedent's forum obligation
Texas's Wrongful Death Act permitted the beneficiaries' suit only if the injured person would have been entitled to sue had he lived. The court treated that limitation as placing beneficiaries in the decedent's exact legal shoes, subject to the same defenses. Prior decisions had allowed a decedent's settlement or release to limit or bar wrongful-death recovery even though the beneficiaries did not sign. A forum agreement that preserved adjudication but changed its venue could likewise bind them. Giving arbitration less effect than those other contracts would also conflict with the FAA's equal-treatment requirement. (644–646.)

The beneficiaries' damages compensated their own personal losses, but that did not make the underlying statutory right independent of Dancy's. The court distinguished consortium claims, which depend on an underlying injury but constitute separate claims in relevant respects: an injured spouse's settlement does not necessarily bar the other spouse's consortium action. Texas wrongful death, by contrast, was expressly conditioned on the decedent's right to maintain suit. Note 1 adds that Dancy's parents would not have had a consortium claim had he survived. (646–647 & n. 1.)

The opinion surveyed other states whose statutes treated wrongful death as independent or derivative, as well as jurisdictions focusing on contract intention. Its holding rested on Texas's statute, not a universal rule for every wrongful-death claim nationwide. It disapproved inconsistent intermediate Texas decisions to the extent they conflicted with this result. (647.)

## Contract illegality challenge reserved to the arbitrator
The beneficiaries argued that the indemnity clause violated Labor Code section 406.033(e), which prohibited an employee's preinjury waiver of claims against a nonsubscribing employer. They expressly challenged the entire contract rather than the arbitration clause alone and maintained that the allegedly unlawful indemnity provision was inseverable. The court distinguished a challenge specifically directed at an arbitration agreement, which a court may decide, from a broader attack claiming that an illegal provision invalidates the contract as a whole, which must go to the arbitrator. (647–649.)

Poly-America did not require judicial resolution here. In that case, the agreement challenged was itself an arbitration agreement, and the offending remedial restrictions were challenged as part of it. Here, the objection to the arbitration clause arose only from the asserted invalidity of the whole occupational-benefits contract. Applying Buckeye's separation principle, the court therefore left both the challenged contract validity and the related indemnity issue to the arbitrator. It did not uphold the indemnity clause as lawful or sever it itself. (648–649.)

The court also expressly declined to address Labatt's argument that the FAA preempted the Labor Code provision insofar as it would prevent enforcement of arbitration. No holding invalidating that state statute should be extracted from this case. Dancy would have been obliged to arbitrate had he survived; his beneficiaries' derivative claims therefore carried the same obligation. Refusing to compel arbitration was a clear abuse of discretion, supporting the conditional writ. (649.)

## Assigned comparison and reading scope
The assigned discussion at printed 1196–1197 includes the accurate parenthetical that the employment arbitration clause bound the employee's wrongful-death beneficiaries. Its surrounding discussion of intended beneficiaries and direct-benefits estoppel supplies general doctrinal context. The actual decision's dispositive theory was the statutory derivative nature of these claims, not a separate estoppel finding or a holding on Labatt's beneficiary-status argument.
