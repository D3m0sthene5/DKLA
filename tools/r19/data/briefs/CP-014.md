Hess v. Pawloski
Citation
Hess v. Pawloski, 274 U.S. 352, 47 S. Ct. 632, 71 L. Ed. 1091 (1927). Supreme Court of the United States,
argued April 18, 1927, decided May 16, 1927. Justice Butler delivered the Court’s opinion. No separate
opinion appears in the report. Casebook pages 91-93. Full judicial opinion.
Parties
Pawloski was the plaintiff in a Massachusetts personal-injury action and the defendant in error, the party
defending the judgment in the Supreme Court. Hess was the defendant below and the plaintiff in error
seeking reversal. Hess lived in Pennsylvania. Massachusetts’s registrar received process under the
challenged statute but was not the opposing litigant. 274 U.S. at 353-55.
Material Facts
Pawloski’s declaration, the pleading stating his claim, alleged that Hess negligently and wantonly drove a
motor vehicle on a Massachusetts public highway and struck and injured him. The Supreme Court reported
those assertions as allegations; it did not independently reconstruct the collision. Hess was not personally
served, and none of his property was attached, meaning brought under the court’s control to support a
property-based proceeding. 274 U.S. at 353.
Massachusetts treated a nonresident’s operation of a motor vehicle on its public ways as appointing the
registrar to receive process in litigation growing out of an accident or collision during that operation. Service
required delivery of a copy and a two-dollar fee to the registrar or the registrar’s office, immediate registered
mailing of notice and a copy to the defendant, and filing of the defendant’s return receipt and the plaintiff’s
affidavit of compliance. Service on Hess complied with that statute. Id. at 353-54.
The full report prints an additional sentence, omitted from the casebook’s statutory quotation, allowing the
court to grant continuances necessary for a reasonable opportunity to defend. The excerpt nevertheless
includes the Court’s reliance on that protection. A continuance postpones proceedings to provide additional
time. Id. at 354, 356; casebook pp. 91-92.
Procedural History
Hess appeared specially, meaning solely to challenge jurisdiction without consenting to it. He filed an
answer in abatement, a procedural objection seeking to defeat the action without deciding liability, and
moved to dismiss. He argued that enforcing this service would violate the Fourteenth Amendment’s Due
Process Clause. The trial court rejected his objection, and the Supreme Judicial Court upheld the statute as
an exercise of the police power, the state’s authority to regulate for public safety and welfare. 274 U.S. at
354-55.
Hess renewed the objection at trial and preserved an exception to its denial. The jury returned a verdict for
Pawloski. The Supreme Judicial Court overruled Hess’s exceptions, after which the Superior Court entered
judgment. The case reached the United States Supreme Court by writ of error, the then-available procedure
for reviewing that judgment. Id. at 355.

Issue
Did due process permit Massachusetts to treat a nonresident’s use of its highways as appointing a state
official to accept service in an accident-related personal-injury suit, where the statute required actual receipt
of mailed notice and process and allowed reasonable time to defend?
Rule and Holding
The statute did not violate the Due Process Clause. Massachusetts could require a nonresident motorist to
appoint an official to receive service for litigation arising from that use of its highways. It could also make
the act of driving the equivalent of that appointment, rather than insist on a formal designation. The Court
found no substantial constitutional difference between the two forms of appointment. 274 U.S. at 356-57.
The holding depended on the limited connection between the local driving and the suit, together with notice
and opportunity to defend. The statute required the nonresident actually to receive and receipt for the mailed
papers and contemplated needed continuances. The Court did not hold that any business activity within a
state constitutes consent to every suit there. Id. at 355-57.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the Massachusetts judgment for Pawloski. The jurisdictional challenge did not
invalidate service or the judgment following the jury’s verdict. 274 U.S. at 357.
Reasoning
The Court began with the territorial rule rather than discarding it. Process issued by one state could not
simply summon an absent nonresident in another state into an ordinary personal money action. Service had
to occur within the state upon the defendant or someone authorized to receive it. The question was therefore
whether the statute validly supplied such an authorized agent. 274 U.S. at 355.
The Court distinguished individuals from corporations for this purpose. Mere business activity by a
nonresident individual did not imply consent to jurisdiction, and the Privileges and Immunities Clause
protected citizens of other states against discriminatory exclusion from ordinary pursuits. The state’s
authority to condition or exclude certain corporate activity did not itself establish unlimited authority over
nonresident individuals. Id. at 355-56.
Highway use supplied a more specific regulatory basis. Motor vehicles presented dangers to persons and
property, allowing safety regulation of resident and nonresident drivers alike. Requiring a driver to answer in
the state for an accident arising there promoted accountability for local conduct and gave an injured claimant
a practicable forum. The appointment covered only highway-accident litigation involving that operation. Id.
at 356.
The safeguards mattered to the constitutional analysis. Receipt of mailed notice and process prevented the
appointment from operating merely as an unexplained fiction of notice. Available continuances provided
reasonable time to defend. The Court regarded the statute as tending to place residents and nonresidents on
comparable terms rather than imposing hostile discrimination. It did not demand literal equality in the
mechanics of service. Id. at 356.
Finally, the Court relied on Kane v. New Jersey as recognizing power to require a formal appointment
before allowing a nonresident to drive. Because the state could impose that preliminary condition, it could
treat driving itself as the appointment. That conclusion concerned the particular regulated activity and the

Notes
Narrow reading — interpretation. A state may serve a nonresident motorist through a designated official
in an action arising from that motorist’s local highway accident when the statute requires receipt of mailed
notice and process and affords reasonable time to defend. The local accident, limited statutory reach, and
safeguards are parts of the holding. 274 U.S. at 356-57.
Broad reading — interpretation. A state’s legitimate regulation of a particular local activity may support
an implied appointment of an agent for related litigation even without a signed designation. The opinion’s
rejection of implied consent from ordinary business activity limits that inference. It does not decide
jurisdiction over claims unrelated to the driving, the question posed in the casebook’s second note. Id. at
355-57; casebook p. 93.
