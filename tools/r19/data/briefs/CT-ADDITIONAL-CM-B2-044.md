# De Cicco v. Schweizer
221 N.Y. 431, 117 N.E. 807 (1917) | Contracts

## Decision and votes

On November 13, 1917, the New York Court of Appeals affirmed with costs a judgment enforcing a father's promised marriage annuity. Judge Cardozo wrote for Chief Judge Hiscock and Judges Cuddeback, Pound, and Andrews. Judge Crane concurred separately, agreeing with the consideration discussion but preferring marriage-settlement reasons. Judge Collin did not vote. The six participating judges agreed on affirmance. Cardozo did not reject New York's existing rule against a promise merely inducing one party to perform a duty already owed to another. He distinguished an inducement addressed to both engaged persons to refrain from mutually abandoning or delaying the marriage. (431, 435–441.)

## Agreement, marriage, and unpaid installment

On January 16, 1902, Joseph Schweizer, his wife Ernestine, and Count Oberto Gulinelli executed formal articles in Italian concerning the coming marriage of the Count and their daughter Blanche. The translated operative provision promised Blanche $2,500 annually, or its equivalent in francs, with the first payment on January 20. The father promised payment during his lifetime to her during hers. Other articles restricted alteration of the testamentary provision for Blanche and her issue and provided that her mother would continue annual payments after the father's death. The promise was formally made through the agreement with the prospective husband, for the daughter's benefit. (432–433.)

The marriage occurred January 20, and the father made the first payment to Blanche that day. He continued annual payments until 1912; the action sought that year's unpaid installment. Blanche assigned her rights to Attilio De Cicco, with her husband joining the assignment. The father defended on lack of consideration: the Count was already engaged and thus merely fulfilled an existing legal duty by marrying. The trial court directed a verdict for De Cicco. The Appellate Division modified and affirmed the judgment as modified, and the father appealed. The report does not describe the lower court's modification as a separate substantive holding to reconstruct. (431, 433.)

## Consideration through joint freedom to rescind or postpone

Cardozo reviewed the competing theories concerning a third person's promise inducing performance of an existing contract, including distinctions between new promises and actual performance. Shadwell provided a point of departure, but neither its majority nor dissent controlled this different case. New York decisions treated a promise to one contracting party simply to prevent breach of a duty to another as lacking consideration. The court had not extended that rule to a promise addressed in substance to both parties, inducing them not to exercise a shared freedom to rescind or modify their agreement. (433–435.)

The annuity undertaking was unilateral: the Count made no reciprocal promise in these articles, and the father would be bound only when marriage actually occurred. The Count's continuing willingness alone was insufficient; Blanche had to be willing and the wedding had to follow. Although the promise formally ran to the Count, it benefited her. On learning of and acting on it, she could adopt and enforce it in the same substantive position as a named promisee. Her prior knowledge was inferred from the family relationship, the parents' signatures, delivery to her intended husband, execution four days before the wedding, the wedding-day payment requirement, and payment to her that day. It was not established by an invented express statement from her. (435–436.)

The couple could, by common consent, end their engagement or postpone marrying. Their refraining from that course and undertaking marriage in reliance on the annuity supplied the relevant detriment. The court acknowledged the criticism that, where an inducement is offered only to one party, assuming the other would agree to rescission can be speculative. A promise naturally directed to both carried different implications: the effort to strengthen both in their course permitted the inference that wavering, rescission, or delay was realistically possible. They need not disclose every conscious or unspoken motive to show that this promise influenced them. Its tendency to induce perseverance, followed by the marriage, permitted an inference of reliance and detriment. (436–438.)

This reasoning concerned the bargaining freedom the couple jointly held. It did not find that either could unilaterally abandon an engagement without consequences, nor did it hold every third-party reward for existing performance enforceable. The father offered an inducement to both while they could jointly retract or delay, and then sought to deny consideration once the contemplated marriage had removed that alternative. (435–438.)

## Contractual intention, factfinding, and marriage policy

The father also characterized the annuity as a gift merely motivated by an already planned marriage, rather than an undertaking intended to influence conduct. The court accepted that marriage is consideration only if the parties deal with it on that basis. Formality alone did not replace the inquiry. Here the covenant's language, recitation of engagement and coming marriage as consideration, and ineffectiveness if marriage did not occur supported contractual intention and inducement. (438–439.)

Both parties had moved for a directed verdict, thereby consenting to the trial judge's determination of the facts. Where competing inferences were available, he chose those favoring the plaintiff. The appellate court could not replace those permissible inferences with a conclusion as a matter of law that the articles were only a gift. Public policy favoring marriage settlements reinforced the conclusion: the law sought to uphold solemn undertakings influencing the parties' deepest personal relations and sometimes sustained such settlements despite doubtful consideration inferences. That policy supported the analysis rather than erasing the court's preceding consideration reasoning. (439.)

## Crane's separate concurrence and exact outcome

Crane emphasized that third-party marriage settlements receive the same protective policy as settlements between spouses. He added that the father's lawyer prepared the articles on January 16 and delivered them to the Count on January 18, two days before the wedding. In his view the only reasonable inference was a settlement made in contemplation of marriage and effective on its occurrence. Citing Phalen's recognition that marriage settlements can be exceptions to strict consideration rules, he would enforce on that footing; if consideration was necessary, the marriage supplied it. (439–440.)

He also allowed actual consideration to be established through circumstances rather than being conclusively limited by the instrument's recitals. The trial judge was justified in finding that the settlement influenced the couple, at least in part, to marry when they did. Crane thus agreed with Cardozo's law of consideration but articulated an alternative emphasis on solemn marriage settlements and their induced action. He did not dissent or replace the majority's rationale. The judgment for the assignee was affirmed with costs, sustaining recovery of the unpaid installment rather than awarding a newly calculated lifetime annuity. (440–441.)
