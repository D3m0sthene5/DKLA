# Trinidad Bean & Elevator Co. v. Frosh
1 Neb. App. 281, 494 N.W.2d 347 (1992) | Contracts

## Facts and proceedings
Trinidad operated a grain elevator in Imperial, Nebraska. On April 26, 1988, farmer Elmo Frosh contracted to sell it 1,875 hundredweight of edible navy beans after the year’s harvest. An inexperienced employee mistakenly completed both alternative payment options: $16.25 per hundredweight deferred until January 15, 1989, and $16 per hundredweight paid in halves at harvest and December 1. The Denver office sought clarification. A replacement page identifying one option was prepared, but no corrected contract was signed. The employee and Trinidad’s local secretary, who was Frosh’s wife, differed about whether she had been asked to obtain his signature. 1 Neb. App. at 282–283.

Frosh testified that around May 1 he told the elevator manager to tear up the contract. The manager denied that conversation. Contract and market prices were equal around that date. Frosh asked again in August whether the contract had been destroyed and sent a September letter saying he considered it void. He delivered no beans after the October harvest. Drought drove prices from approximately $16 in April to $32 in late August or early September and $36 in late September, when Trinidad bought beans elsewhere. Although the factual account describes those purchases, the opinion characterizes Trinidad’s claim as one for the market differential under § 2-713, rather than cover damages under § 2-712. Id. at 283–284, 288.

The trial court instructed that a contract existed and measured market damages at harvest, while also submitting common-law mitigation and unreasonable delay after anticipatory repudiation as defenses. The jury returned a general verdict for Frosh. Trinidad unsuccessfully sought a directed verdict, judgment notwithstanding the verdict, and a new trial, then appealed. It contended that October market prices of $37 to $38 established substantial damages and that the mitigation instruction was improper. Id. at 284–286.

## Issue and statutory rule
The principal issue was when to measure a buyer’s § 2-713 market differential after anticipatory repudiation: when the buyer learns of repudiation or when delivery becomes due. The court held that the relevant date is the date of repudiation, provided cover on that date would be commercially reasonable. If immediate cover would be commercially unreasonable, the buyer may wait a commercially reasonable period. The buyer claiming that qualification bears the burden of showing why cover at repudiation was unreasonable. Id. at 286–292.

This is a valuation rule, not a command that a buyer must always make an actual substitute purchase to preserve a claim. Section 2-712(3) preserves other remedies despite failure to cover, and § 2-711 allows the alternative market measure. The court nonetheless considered the reasonableness of prompt cover when reconciling that market measure with § 2-610’s limited permission to await performance. Id. at 287–288, 292.

## Competing readings and reasoning
The court recognized plausible textual arguments for each date. Performance-date valuation could align buyer damages with § 2-708’s seller remedy and draw support from § 2-723’s express reference to learning of repudiation for an action tried before the performance date. Earlier Nebraska precedent did not resolve the distinction because the repudiation and nondelivery dates had coincided. The court therefore interpreted the provisions together instead of treating that precedent as controlling this earlier-repudiation dispute. Id. at 286–290.

The court preferred repudiation-date valuation because otherwise § 2-610’s commercially reasonable waiting limit and § 2-712’s bar on unreasonable cover delay would have little practical effect. A buyer in a rising market could postpone its substitute transaction while charging the seller the higher later price; if the market instead fell, the buyer could acquire the goods more cheaply. The court considered that speculation inconsistent with compensation, rather than enhancement of the buyer’s position. It also rejected treating the pre-Code entitlement to wait until performance as unchanged despite the Code’s commercial standard. Id. at 290–292.

## Application and review standards
The disputed May conversation prevented a directed verdict for Trinidad. If Frosh’s testimony was credited, his instruction to tear up the agreement communicated a clear determination not to perform. On judgment notwithstanding the verdict, favorable evidence and reasonable inferences had to be taken for Frosh. His account placed repudiation in early May, when no market/contract differential existed. The appellate court did not independently decide that the conversation necessarily occurred; it held that the evidence supporting the general verdict could establish it. Id. at 284–285, 292–293.

Trinidad argued that fluctuating futures prices made a four-month wait commercially reasonable. That assertion did not show lack of access to the bean futures market or another concrete reason why prompt cover was unreasonable. A well-ordered accessible market supported valuation at the earliest commercially reasonable point after repudiation. The verdict was therefore not clearly wrong under the rule adopted. Id. at 293–294.

## Erroneous mitigation instruction and disposition
The complete opinion separately held that the common-law mitigation instruction was erroneous. Under § 1-103, common-law principles supplement the Code only when its particular provisions do not displace them. The timing, cover, and market rules adopted here supplied the applicable system for preventing avoidable loss, making an additional general mitigation defense inconsistent with that system. The holding did not declare every common-law principle displaced in every Article 2 dispute. Id. at 294–295.

The error did not warrant reversal because Trinidad failed to show prejudice. The jury could reach the same result under the anticipatory-repudiation instruction by finding an early-May repudiation and unreasonable delay. The court affirmed the defense judgment and rejected Trinidad’s posttrial challenges. Connolly wrote for Chief Judge Sievers, Connolly, and Miller-Lerman; no separate writing or numbered judicial footnote is reported. Id. at 282, 293–295.
