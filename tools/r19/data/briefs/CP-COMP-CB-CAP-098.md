# Mitchell v. W. T. Grant Co.

**416 U.S. 600 (1974), decided May 13, 1974. Civil Procedure.** Justice White wrote the Court’s opinion, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justices Blackmun, Powell, and Rehnquist. Powell also concurred separately. Stewart dissented with Douglas and Marshall; Brennan joined in part and supplied a separate dissenting statement. The judgment was 5–4. The assigned accounts are CivPro physical page 166, printed 284, Note 3 before North Georgia, and physical page 601, printed 1187, before the contrasting North Georgia account. Both summarize a particular approved prejudgment remedy; the actual opinion expressly confines its ruling to the Louisiana sequestration procedure considered here.

## The transaction and proceedings

Lawrence Mitchell bought a refrigerator, range, stereo, and washing machine from W. T. Grant under an installment sales agreement. Grant sued in the First City Court of New Orleans on February 2, 1972, alleging an overdue balance of $574.17 and a Louisiana vendor’s lien on the goods. Its credit manager verified the petition and asserted a reason to believe Mitchell would encumber, alienate, or dispose of the merchandise during litigation. Without first notifying Mitchell or hearing his response, Judge O’Keefe authorized sequestration on Grant’s posting a $1,125 bond. A writ issued directing the constable to take the property. 416 U.S. at 601–602.

Mitchell moved on March 3 to dissolve the writ as to the refrigerator, stove, and washing machine; the motion did not address the stereo, for a reason the record did not explain. He argued that the goods were exempt under Louisiana law and that seizure without advance notice or a hearing violated due process. At the March 14 hearing the parties stipulated that a vendor’s lien existed, and counsel presented argument. The court denied dissolution on March 16, rejecting both exemption and due-process objections. Louisiana’s appellate courts left that ruling intact. The Supreme Court reviewed and affirmed the Louisiana Supreme Court’s judgment. Id. at 602–603 & n.1.

The precise seizure date was disputed. Mitchell’s motion alleged February 7, while a sheriff’s return supplied to the Supreme Court indicated February 18 and apparently was not in the record below. The Court assumed February 7 because the state supreme court had done so and no legal consequence depended on choosing between them. That assumption should not be presented as a resolved evidentiary finding. Id. at 603 n.2.

## What process did Louisiana provide?

The buyer had ownership and possession interests, but Grant also had a present property interest: the vendor’s lien securing the unpaid price. The majority therefore rejected an analysis treating the dispute as an unsecured creditor’s seizure of property exclusively belonging to the debtor. The buyer’s interest was encumbered, and the seller’s security could lose value through use or disappear upon a transfer of possession. Both interests counted in deciding what process was due. Id. at 604–605.

The governing statutory safeguards worked together. The applicant had to supply specific facts in a verified petition or affidavit showing the claim, amount, and grounds for sequestration. In Orleans Parish a judge had to authorize the writ. The creditor had to provide security against wrongful seizure. The debtor could immediately request dissolution, at which point the creditor had to prove the grounds for issuance; failure to do so required dissolution and could support damages and attorney’s fees. Alternatively, the debtor could recover possession by posting security. Id. at 605–607.

The Court did not approve clerk-issued writs throughout Louisiana. Its note 5 identifies the Orleans Parish rule and expressly excludes procedures elsewhere in the state from the issue decided. Note 7 also qualifies what happened after seizure: the sheriff initially safeguarded the property; the creditor could obtain possession if the debtor failed to secure release within ten days, but ordinarily could not sell it before a final judgment. The statutory appendix reproduces the relevant provisions, including the dissolution burden, release bonds, restriction on sale, and sequestration grounds. Id. at 606 nn.5–7, 620–623.

## Why the majority allowed seizure before a hearing

Mitchell maintained that his protected possession interest required a hearing before any deprivation. The majority accepted the importance of that interest but rejected an inflexible sequence of process. It considered the procedure as a whole, rather than treating temporary loss of possession as constitutionally indistinguishable from final deprivation.

First, continued use reduced the resale value of the seller’s security when installments stopped. Second, a buyer could conceal or transfer the goods, and advance warning could facilitate that action; under Louisiana law a transfer of possession could defeat the vendor’s lien. Third, the interim issues—debt, lien, and delinquency—were relatively narrow and suited to documentary proof. A judge’s review of sworn facts thus created a lower risk of erroneous seizure than an unsupervised claim resting on disputed fault. The creditor’s bond and potential liability for damages supplied additional checks. Id. at 607–610.

Finally, an immediate post-seizure hearing allowed the debtor to put the creditor to its proof. The Court observed that Mitchell had not used that opportunity immediately. It balanced the temporary household-goods deprivation against the seller’s risk, the safeguards, and the fact that the seizure did not remove Mitchell’s source of income. Its conclusion concerned interim possession while the underlying controversy remained unresolved, rather than a creditor’s entitlement to a final judgment solely on its affidavit. Id. at 610–611.

## Fuentes, Sniadach, and the disagreement about precedent

Mitchell relied heavily on Fuentes v. Shevin and Sniadach. The majority distinguished Sniadach’s wage garnishment: wages had a special subsistence importance, and the creditor lacked a pre-existing interest in the attached property. It distinguished the Florida and Pennsylvania replevin procedures in Fuentes because those systems allowed action on bare assertions without judicial supervision and did not assure the immediate hearing Louisiana supplied. Specific factual verification, judicial authorization, narrowly defined issues, and prompt creditor-burden review made the difference. Id. at 614–619.

That distinction was contested within the Court. Powell agreed with the judgment and majority but candidly regarded the decision as overruling Fuentes to the extent Fuentes announced a universal requirement of an adversary hearing before temporary dispossession. He would require adequate security, a specific probable-cause showing before a neutral officer, and a prompt adversary hearing with the burden on the creditor. He also argued that constitutional precedents remain open to reconsideration because ordinary legislation cannot correct them. Those formulations belong to his concurrence rather than a separate unanimous rule. Id. at 623–629.

Stewart, joined by Douglas and Marshall, thought the two procedures constitutionally indistinguishable. A standardized affidavit and a judge’s formal review could not test the creditor’s version against the debtor’s response. He disputed that Louisiana’s lien/default issues differed materially from the issues in Fuentes and maintained that complexity could affect the form or timing of a hearing without eliminating the need for advance opportunity to be heard. In his view the majority had adopted the earlier Fuentes dissent rather than preserved Fuentes. His concluding discussion warned against overruling a recent constitutional decision merely after a change in membership. Id. at 629–636. Brennan’s separate statement agreed that Fuentes required reversal; the reporter identifies his joinder in Stewart only as partial. Id. at 601, 636.

## Judgment and limits

The Court **affirmed the Louisiana Supreme Court** and held the sequestration procedure constitutional, both facially and as applied in the circumstances presented. The majority’s final formulation is at 619–620. Its note 13 disclaims any position on the desirability of competing credit-law reforms and limits the holding to the Louisiana procedure. Note 14 likewise resists treating the decision as automatically validating garnishment or summary self-help systems. Id. at 619–620 nn.13–14.

The assigned accounts properly focus on the linked safeguards and the creditor’s property interest. The actual opinion adds the Orleans Parish limitation, the statutory appendix, the competing conceptions of Fuentes, and the distinction between provisional possession and final entitlement. This brief describes the 1974 ruling and does not certify current state-law procedures or the present law of prejudgment seizure.
