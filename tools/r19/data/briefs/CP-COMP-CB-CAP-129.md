# Rose v. Giamatti

**721 F. Supp. 906 (S.D. Ohio 1989) — July 31, 1989.** District Judge Holschuh decided this removal-and-remand dispute. The assigned account appears in CivPro physical pages 182–183, printed pages 318–319, under the discussion of collusive joinder and the real party in interest. This decision determines the federal forum; it does not decide whether Rose gambled or whether the Commissioner afforded a fair disciplinary process.

## Facts and procedural course

Pete Rose, manager of the Cincinnati Reds, faced a baseball disciplinary investigation. Commissioner Peter Ueberroth and Commissioner-elect A. Bartlett Giamatti learned of allegations that Rose wagered on baseball games. Investigator John Dowd was retained in February 1989 and submitted a report in May. Giamatti scheduled a June 26 disciplinary hearing. Rose contended that Giamatti had prejudged the matter and could not conduct a fair hearing. Those were allegations underlying Rose’s requested injunction, rather than findings that Giamatti had violated his duties. **908–909.**

On June 19, Rose sued in Ohio state court, naming Giamatti, the Reds, and Major League Baseball. His seven state-law theories included contract, fiduciary-duty, negligence, and common-law natural-justice claims. After an evidentiary hearing, the state court issued a temporary restraining order on June 25 preventing disciplinary action and interference with Rose’s employment. The defendants unsuccessfully sought immediate state appellate review: the temporary order was not appealable. Giamatti removed on July 3, with the other defendants’ consent. The case was transferred from the district’s Cincinnati judges to its Columbus judges because of Rose’s close identification with Cincinnati, without a determination that either judge was biased. **908–910, n.1; 923–924.**

Rose moved to remand. He argued that he and two defendants shared Ohio citizenship, destroying complete diversity, and that defendants had waived removal by vigorously litigating the temporary restraint in state court. Pending resolution, the parties stipulated to restraints on defendants’ action. The court considered written submissions and oral argument before issuing this order. **910–912, 922–924.**

## Parties, governing rules, and the jurisdictional issue

For this motion, the court accepted that Rose was an Ohio citizen and Giamatti a New York citizen. The Reds were a limited partnership; the parties agreed to treat them as Ohio citizens, and the court assumed at least one partner was an Ohio citizen. It also accepted, for the remand analysis, Rose’s position that Major League Baseball could be treated as an unincorporated association whose member clubs included the Ohio Reds. The court did not finally settle whether “Major League Baseball” was an independent juridical entity or merely a collective name. Its resolution instead turned on whether the Reds and the clubs were real parties to this controversy. **911–913, nn.3–7.**

Removal under the then-applicable provisions of §1441 required an action within original federal jurisdiction, with the diversity-based forum-defendant restriction also relevant. Complete diversity ordinarily demanded different state citizenship across opposing sides. Two related doctrines permitted disregarding parties whose inclusion did not represent a substantive controversy: fraudulent joinder and nominal-party analysis. The court explained that the litigation’s principal purpose and controlling dispute, rather than the names in the pleading alone, identify the real parties. A defendant with no substantive duty at issue, or no actual interest or control over the controversy, can be nominal. **910–914.**

“Fraudulent joinder” here was a jurisdictional term of art. The court expressly found no evidence or suggestion of personal dishonesty by Rose or his counsel and recognized counsel’s integrity. The label therefore must not be converted into a finding that Rose committed fraud. **913–914.**

## Why the Reds and Major League Baseball were disregarded

The Reds had not allegedly done anything wrongful. Rose’s anticipated-breach theory did not identify a clear and unequivocal refusal by the club to perform its obligations. The Commissioner’s procedural rules were also distinct from the Major League Rules incorporated into Rose’s employment arrangement. Most significantly, the parties agreed that the Reds had no power to interfere with the Commissioner’s disciplinary authority. Their practical concern about losing their manager did not create the substantive legal duty that Rose was seeking to enforce against Giamatti. The court regarded the Reds as fraudulently joined in the technical jurisdictional sense and, at best, a nominal party. **914–917.**

The clubs’ relationship to the Commissioner did not make them legally responsible for the alleged disciplinary misconduct. The court examined the baseball governing agreement and the history of establishing an independent Commissioner. The clubs had placed disciplinary decisions outside their own control and agreed not to challenge his actions in court. This supported treating the Commissioner as independent for the particular function challenged. It was not a holding that he could never act as an agent for any other purpose. Nor did independence license him to disregard his procedural obligations: the opinion included his acknowledgment that persons appearing before him could rely on those rules. **917–920, nn.10–12.**

Potential future obligations of the clubs, if Rose were placed on an ineligible list, did not make them present adversaries in a dispute over the fairness of the Commissioner’s hearing. Cases challenging association rules, discriminatory treatment, antitrust conduct, or other collective action involved different substantive controversies. Ohio law permitting an unincorporated association to be sued under its collective name addressed capacity, not whether it had a real interest in this suit. Major League Baseball was therefore, at best, nominal as well. **920–922, nn.13–19.**

## Waiver, order, and limits

Waiver required a clear and unequivocal submission to state adjudication. Defending against preliminary restraint was different from seeking a decision on the underlying merits. Defendants’ participation in the temporary-restraint hearing, attempted appeal, and discovery concerned preliminary relief preserving the status quo. The record showed neither bad faith nor an unequivocal surrender of removal, and removal was timely. The court rejected Rose’s reliance on a district decision that had already been reversed on appeal. **922–923, n.20.**

The court **denied remand**: with the nominal defendants disregarded, diversity existed between Rose and Giamatti, and the non-Ohio Commissioner had not waived removal. It also certified the jurisdictional ruling for a possible §1292(b) interlocutory appeal, directing Rose to apply within ten days. Certification was not itself an appellate decision. If no application was filed, a preliminary-injunction hearing would occur August 14; the stipulated restraints would remain until that date. **923–924.**

The assigned quotation accurately emphasizes nominal parties, but its shorthand description of an injunction against “investigating” should be read alongside the actual dispute over the scheduled disciplinary hearing and its fairness. The opinion has twenty judicial footnotes and no separate judicial writing. This brief describes the historical ruling without certifying subsequent developments.
