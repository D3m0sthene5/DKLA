# Sally Beauty Company, Inc. v. Nexxus Products Company, Inc.
801 F.2d 1001 (7th Cir. September 26, 1986) | Contracts

## Facts and chronology
In August 1979, Nexxus appointed Best Barber & Beauty Supply as its exclusive distributor of hair-care products throughout Texas except El Paso. The agreement, embodied in Best president Mark Reichek’s July 24 letter accepted August 2, specified prices, freight, promotional support, a technician, and payment secured by letters of credit. Termination could occur only on an anniversary date with 120 days’ notice; if Nexxus terminated, it would repurchase inventory at cost and pay return freight. The distribution of products to salons and barbers was the commercial relationship’s central object. (1002–1003.)

In July 1981, Best was acquired by and merged into Sally Beauty, a distributor wholly owned by Alberto-Culver, a hair-care manufacturer competing with Nexxus. Sally succeeded to Best’s contractual rights and interests. Nexxus refused to permit its products to be distributed by a company controlled by that competitor, despite Sally’s claim that its distribution operation was autonomous and sold many manufacturers’ lines. Sally alleged that Nexxus terminated without the required notice and away from an anniversary date. (1003–1004.)

## Procedure and arguments
Sally sued in 1983 for antitrust violations and breach of contract. Nexxus counterclaimed on other statutory and unfair-competition theories. The district court granted Nexxus summary judgment on the contract claim in January 1985, reasoning that this distributorship depended on personal trust and confidence and therefore could not be assigned without consent. Following stipulated dismissal of the remaining claims and counterclaims in May 1985, an appealable final contract judgment was entered. (1003–1004 & n.2.)

Nexxus relied on Reichek’s experience and the negotiations with the Redding family to characterize the agreement as personal. Sally answered that the parties were corporations, substitution would not change performance, and the UCC governed the sale and distribution of goods. Conflicting affidavits disputed whether Nexxus actually relied on Reichek’s particular abilities. The appeal thus presented both the availability of summary judgment and the limits on delegation of the distributor’s still-executory duties. (1004–1005 & nn.3–5.)

## Majority rules and holding
Judge Cudahy, joined by Judge Eschbach, affirmed on a different ground. The district court could not resolve the disputed personal-trust facts in Nexxus’s favor on summary judgment. Nor was every distributorship categorically a personal-services contract. Nevertheless, the undisputed competitor ownership supported judgment under Texas UCC § 2-210(1), restricting delegation where the obligee has a substantial interest in the original promisor’s performance or control. (1005–1008.)

Texas’s dominant-factor approach asks whether goods or services predominate. The court predicted that Texas would follow the majority of jurisdictions treating this distributorship as a goods transaction because its sales aspect predominated. UCC coverage permits ordinary assignment and delegation, but does not make every substitution acceptable. An exclusive distributor also owes best efforts to promote sales under § 2-306(2); that undertaking was the consideration for Nexxus’s territorial exclusivity. (1006–1007.)

The majority held that the duty of performance under an exclusive distributorship cannot be delegated without consent to a competitor or its wholly owned subsidiary. Nexxus had a substantial interest in keeping its best-efforts distributor outside the competitor’s control. Sally’s present intention to distribute impartially did not eliminate the parent’s power to change its policies whenever competing needs arose. Nexxus need not bear that risk as part of a bargain it made with Best. (1007–1008.)

## Reasoning and boundaries
The rule protected the obligee’s agreed performance while preserving ordinary commercial alienability. It did not declare competing manufacturers’ distribution arrangements commercially improper: the supplier could consent when it found the arrangement advantageous. It also left partially owned subsidiaries unresolved, acknowledging that the extent of competitor control might then require factfinding. The majority did not require a trial on whether this wholly owned subsidiary would actually perform as well as Best; undisputed ownership and the nature of the undertaking sufficed. (1008 & nn.8–9.)

The opinion carefully distinguished transfer of executory obligations from assignment of accrued rights, such as an account receivable or damages for breach. The latter receives stronger protection under § 2-210(2). Thus, the case does not support a universal bar on transferring payment claims to competitors. The court also found it unnecessary to decide Nexxus’s alternative assertion that Sally breached by failing to give 120 days’ notice of the merger. (n.6; 1008 & n.10.)

## Posner’s dissent
Judge Posner would reverse and remand for a trial on whether the merger materially altered the conditions of performance. He rejected an ownership-based rule founded on judicial intuitions about business conflicts. The same operating business and personnel largely continued, and a change in corporate form ordinarily does not itself change contractual obligations. Competitors often legitimately distribute each other’s products; a distributor is not necessarily a fiduciary like a lawyer. (1008–1010.)

The dissent also examined economic incentives absent from the majority’s categorical approach. Favoring Alberto-Culver could damage Sally’s goodwill with other suppliers and therefore harm its parent. Nexxus and Alberto-Culver sold products with differing prices and channels; diverting customers might benefit other manufacturers instead. The agreement’s limited termination cycle, damages for breach of the best-efforts obligation, and Nexxus’s alternative distribution diminished the asserted danger. The probability of favoritism was not zero, but the record did not show impairment sufficient to justify cancellation as a matter of law. At most, insecurity might warrant demanding adequate assurance under § 2-609. No such demand had been made, and the merger was not shown to make performance impossible as an anticipatory repudiation. (1010–1011.)

## Judgment and source scope
The Seventh Circuit affirmed summary judgment for Nexxus, 2–1. Cudahy wrote for the majority with Eschbach; Posner dissented. The operative ground was nondelegability to the competitor’s wholly owned subsidiary under the UCC, rather than the district court’s disputed personal-services theory. (1005, 1008, 1011.)
