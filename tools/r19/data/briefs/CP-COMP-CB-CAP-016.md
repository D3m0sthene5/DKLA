# Fetter v. Beal

1 Ld. Raym. 339, 91 Eng. Rep. 1122 | Civil Procedure | King's Bench

## Identity and assigned scope

The supplied CivPro text identifies the case as King's Bench, 1697, and quotes its first report at 339–340, 91 Eng. Rep. 1123. Its developed passage occupies PDF 66, before the following Des Moines Navigation case. A historical collection, A Collection of Cases on the Measure of Damages, reproduces proceedings reported at both 1 Ld. Raym. 339 and 692, under a caption giving 1698 and 1701. ## Parties, facts, and relief

Fetter had already recovered £11 against Beal in an action for battery. Later, a part of Fetter's skull came out as a consequence of that same battery. He brought a further special action, alleging the earlier recovery and the subsequent loss. He sought additional relief for what his counsel characterized as a distinct maim, rather than merely the bruising and battery compensated before. The reports do not supply a new numerical damages demand, an exact injury date, or a modern appellate sequence. (Assigned 339–340; collection 267.)

## Procedure

Beal pleaded the previous recovery as a bar. Fetter demurred and argued that the different subsequent injury permitted the new action. The first report records judgment for the defendant, with the qualification “nisi, &c.” retained in the historical reproduction but elided by the textbook. The reproduced second proceeding records counsel's renewed attempt to obtain judgment for Fetter and the court's rejection of that attempt. These accounts should not be converted into an invented final appeal or treated as two different batteries. (Collection 267–269.)

## Issue

Could a plaintiff who had recovered damages for a battery bring another action for later physical consequences of the same battery, on the theory that the later loss constituted a different injury?

## Rule and holding

The prior recovery barred the further action described in the assigned report. The court treated the earlier jury's award as compensation for the damage caused by the wound. The later manifestation did not supply a new actionable battery. The court attributed the resulting difficulty to the plaintiff's having proceeded too hastily in the earlier action. (Assigned 339–340; collection 268.)

This is a historical application of finality to a single battery and its consequences. It does not decide modern statutory exceptions, latent-disease rules, or the availability of a new action for a genuinely new wrongful act.

## Judgment

The assigned account gives judgment for Beal. The historical reproduction retains the first report's qualified formulation and then states, in its further proceeding, that Fetter could not obtain judgment because the court inclined strongly against him. The £11 remained the earlier recovery; the court did not award that sum anew. (Assigned 340; collection 268–269.)

## Reasoning and additional reported explanation

In the first account, counsel compared the new claim to successive periods of taking and detaining goods and to later property damage after a roof was uncovered. The court rejected the proposed escape from the first recovery. Its unanimous treatment of the wound and resulting damage supported Beal's plea in bar. (Collection 267–268.)

The separately reproduced later argument used continuing nuisance and a sea-wall example to suggest that subsequent damage could support another action. Chief Justice Holt responded that the battery supplied this action's foundation; its probable consequences increased damages for that action. Those consequences could and should have been put in evidence before their physical manifestation. A nuisance could differ because each new dropping of water was a new nuisance, while maiming could already have been proved in the battery action. These are the report's comparisons, not facts involving Fetter's property. (Collection 268–269.)

Holt also recalled trying the original cause eight years before, when both parties appeared to have been drinking and the jury was uncertain which was at fault, resulting in lesser damages. That recollection appears in the second reproduced account and is not contained in the textbook's first-report excerpt. It does not establish an adjudicated finding that Fetter alone caused the original injury. (Collection 269.)

## Textbook connection and limitations

The textbook asks whether finality is fair to either party. Those are editorial questions. Its next discussion of judgments allegedly entered without subject-matter jurisdiction concerns a different problem and is not part of Fetter's holding.
