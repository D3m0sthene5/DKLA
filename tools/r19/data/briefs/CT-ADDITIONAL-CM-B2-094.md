# United States ex rel. DRC, Inc. v. Custer Battles, LLC
562 F.3d 295 (4th Cir. 2009) | Contracts

## Court, parties, and the scope of the judgment
The Fourth Circuit decided this appeal on April 10, 2009. Judge Niemeyer wrote for a unanimous panel of Judges Niemeyer, Shedd, and Duncan. Private relators brought the action on behalf of the United States under the False Claims Act. The government declined to intervene but participated as amicus, including on appeal. The decision reversed two rulings concerning a cost-plus currency-exchange contract while affirming summary judgment concerning a separate fixed-price airport-security contract. It remanded with an election of a new trial and alternative instructions for resolving remaining defenses; it did not finally order an award on the currency claims. (295, 297–298, 300–301, 309.)

## The currency contract and the invoicing evidence
The Coalition Provisional Authority governed Iraq from May 2003 until June 28, 2004. Its personnel came largely from the United States but also from numerous other coalition countries. In July 2003 Administrator Paul Bremer announced replacement of currency bearing Saddam Hussein's portrait. The exchange required a large logistical operation, including three hubs at Baghdad, Mosul, and Basra. On August 27, 2003 the Authority contracted with Custer Battles to build, equip, and service those hubs. The contractor would receive its actual costs plus twenty-five percent for overhead and profit. (298.)

The Authority advanced $3 million through a Treasury check drawn on seized Iraqi assets. It subsequently paid roughly $12 million from the Development Fund for Iraq, for approximately $15 million altogether. The Development Fund included non-American sources and $210 million in Iraqi bank funds confiscated by the United States and transferred to the Fund. The later analysis records the parties' agreement that no appropriated funds were used for this particular contract, notwithstanding the Fund's broader mixed composition. Invoices usually reached American contracting officers detailed to the Authority, passed through American-retained consultants or military personnel for approval, and then reached the Authority's finance office. (298–299, 302–303.)

After an October 18, 2003 meeting about performance problems, Michael Battles accidentally left a spreadsheet comparing actual costs with billed amounts. Its examples included two trucks costing $18,000 but billed at $80,000, generators costing $74,000 but billed at $400,000, and laundry facilities costing $4,000 but billed at $12,000. Inflating costs also increased the twenty-five-percent addition. These were concrete trial evidence underlying the fraud claims, rather than merely dissatisfaction with a contractor's profit under a permissible cost-plus arrangement. (299.)

DRC, a subcontractor, and its managing director Robert Isakson brought the fraud action. Former employee William Baldwin separately claimed whistleblower retaliation. The district court dismissed a conspiracy count on an intracorporate-conspiracy rationale, limited the currency fraud claims to the $3 million Treasury-funded advance, and severed the airport claim. At trial, the jury found the defendants liable under the then-existing sections 3729(a)(1) and (a)(2), awarded the maximum permitted $3 million in fraud damages, and awarded Baldwin $165,000 for retaliation. Before judgment on the fraud verdict, the district court granted an earlier-filed, deferred Rule 50(a) motion for judgment as a matter of law because it considered presentment to the Authority insufficient. This was a post-verdict decision on a reserved trial motion, not summary judgment on the currency claim. (300–301.)

## A qualifying claim did not require continued American ownership or privity
The district court traced the funds and concluded that only the $3 million advance involved American property; it treated funds transferred to the Development Fund as Iraqi funds outside the statute. The Fourth Circuit rejected that restriction under the statutory language then applicable. Section 3729(c) included demands made to a grantee or other recipient if the United States provided any portion of the requested money or would reimburse any portion. Accordingly, a claim paid from a grantee's mixed funds could qualify in full even though the United States did not pay the claimant directly. The statutory inclusion of grantees also defeated a requirement that the government retain title or control after transfer. (302–304.)

The contract stated that no coalition country's appropriated or other funds would be obligated under it. That did not answer the statutory question: the Act asked whether the United States had provided funds, rather than whether it was contractually obligated to the contractor. The $210 million contribution to the Development Fund supplied the requisite connection. Nor did the Act require contractual privity with the United States. Its reach to indirect recipients and subcontractors would contradict such a requirement. The court concluded that the false or fraudulent demands under the currency contract qualified as claims. (304–305.)

Qualifying claims and recoverable damages were distinct. The court expressly limited damages to loss sustained by the United States and acknowledged that the erroneous restriction could prove harmless if that loss did not exceed $3 million. It could not resolve the actual loss on appeal. It therefore allowed the relators to elect a new trial, rather than mandating one or awarding the entire approximately $15 million paid under the contract. (305.)

## Presentment to officials detailed to the Authority
The Fourth Circuit reviewed judgment as a matter of law de novo, without weighing credibility. Under the then-existing section 3729(a)(1), a false claim had to be presented to an American officer, employee, or service member acting officially. The district court had assumed that personnel detailed to an international Authority necessarily stopped acting in their American official capacities. The evidence permitted the opposite conclusion. Contracting officer Patricia Logsdon worked for and was paid by the Army Contracting Agency, held a warrant authorizing government contracting, reported to an American colonel, and administered performance and funding. Her successor described comparable authority. American military personnel and USAID-retained consultants also participated in approval and payment. They were doing the jobs for which the United States hired and paid them. (305–307.)

That evidence sufficed for a jury to find presentment; the court did not have to hold that the Authority itself was an American instrumentality. Section (a)(1) identified the person receiving the claim and did not add the requirement of payment or approval by the government appearing in section (a)(2). Courts could not import that different wording. The fact that the advance preceded an invoice likewise did not eliminate presentment: the advance required later invoices for approval, and the statute covered claims for approval as well as payment. (306–308.)

The district court had also imposed a presentment requirement on section (a)(2). Allison Engine, decided after its ruling, rejected that inference from the statutory text. Reversing the presentment rulings left unresolved the contractor's other Rule 50 arguments: material falsity, loss to the United States, and the individual defendants' sufficient knowledge. Those remained for the district court if the relators declined a new trial. (305, 308.)

## Why the airport claim failed
The airport agreement followed a June 2003 proposal and successive temporary agreements, with a final ten-month contract beginning August 31, 2003. It was a fixed-price undertaking for $16,840,832. The relators alleged fraudulent inducement because staffing dropped below approximately 138 personnel. But neither the proposal's promises nor the operative agreements fixed that staffing level. A 138.5-person figure appeared only in the contractor's cost estimate supporting its price. It did not become a contractual commitment. No personnel-cost invoices were submitted under the fixed-price arrangement, and performance initially employed more than 138 people before staffing fluctuated. The responsible minister confirmed that payment did not require a particular staffing level. The court affirmed summary judgment because the evidence could not establish the claimed fraud. (299–300, 308–309.)

## Disposition and assigned-source limits
The court reversed the currency-claim restriction and the Rule 50 judgment, affirmed airport summary judgment, and remanded. The relators first could choose a new currency trial. If they declined, the district court had to consider the remaining Rule 50 grounds; only if it rejected those grounds was it to enter judgment on the existing verdict. (309.)
