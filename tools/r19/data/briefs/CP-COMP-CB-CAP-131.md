# Ankenbrandt v. Richards

**504 U.S. 689 (1992) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 15, 1992.** Justice White wrote for Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter. Justice Blackmun concurred in the judgment. Justice Stevens also concurred in the judgment, joined by Justice Thomas. All nine agreed on reversal; six joined the majority’s statutory explanation. Assigned account: *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 186, printed pages 325–326, in the note on judicially created exceptions to diversity jurisdiction.

## Allegations and lower-court disposition

Carol Ankenbrandt, a Missouri citizen, sued in the Eastern District of Louisiana on September 26, 1989, as mother and next friend of her daughters. She alleged that their divorced father, Jon Richards, and his companion, Debra Kesler, both Louisiana citizens, had sexually and physically abused the children. She sought money damages, invoked diversity jurisdiction, and pleaded the required amount in controversy. She did not seek a divorce, alimony, or custody decree. **691, 704, 707.**

Ankenbrandt represented that a Louisiana juvenile court had terminated Richards’s parental rights and prohibited contact the preceding month. Footnote 1 expressly says the lower courts and Supreme Court did not determine that representation’s accuracy. It should therefore remain a reported assertion rather than an adjudicated fact supplied by this opinion. **691 n.1.**

The district court dismissed without prejudice, reasoning both that a domestic-relations exception deprived it of jurisdiction and that *Younger* abstention justified refusal to proceed. The Fifth Circuit affirmed in an unpublished opinion. The Supreme Court reviewed the exception’s existence, its application to an intrafamilial damages action, and the abstention ruling. **692–693 & n.2.**

## Holding and judgment

The majority recognized a domestic-relations exception to statutory diversity jurisdiction, but confined it to federal issuance of divorce, alimony, or child-custody decrees. This tort suit requested none of those decrees. Diversity jurisdiction was therefore proper. Neither *Younger* nor the discussed *Burford* principles justified abstention on these facts. The Court **reversed and remanded** for further proceedings; it did not decide whether abuse occurred or award damages. **703–707.**

## Constitutional authority versus the statutory exception

The exception’s usual starting point was *Barber v. Barber*, which allowed federal enforcement of a state alimony decree while disclaiming power to grant divorce or alimony initially. That disclaimer was technically dictum and lacked an explanation, but lower federal courts had relied on it for nearly a century and a half. The majority chose to explain and preserve the narrow tradition rather than erase it. **693–695.**

Article III did not itself exclude domestic-relations subjects. Its categories of cases and controversies contained no such limitation. Later decisions had reviewed divorce matters from territorial courts and recognized divorce jurisdiction in the District of Columbia. The Court reasoned that these examples were incompatible with a categorical Article III barrier. *De la Rama* also had explained the historical constraint through statutory diversity requirements, such as amount in controversy, rather than a constitutional prohibition. **695–697 & n.3.**

Constitutional permission does not require Congress to give lower federal courts every jurisdiction Article III allows. The majority therefore turned to the diversity statute. Before 1948, its language covered civil suits “at common law or in equity.” The *Barber* dissent linked that phrase to the perceived limits of English chancery over divorce and alimony; the majority inferred that *Barber*’s accepted disclaimer rested on that statutory understanding. It did not resolve the historical debate about chancery’s actual powers. **697–700 & n.4.**

Instead, congressional acceptance and statutory precedent carried the explanation. Congress had left the longstanding interpretation undisturbed. Replacing the old law/equity wording with “all civil actions” in the 1948 recodification did not clearly express an intention to change substantive jurisdiction. The Court presumed Congress carried the established interpretation forward. Statutory stare decisis had added force because Congress remained free to amend the result. **700–701 & n.5.**

## The exception’s limits and policy support

Preserving the exception did not justify its broad lower-court applications. *Barber* itself permitted enforcement of an already obtained alimony decree; it did not exclude every dispute related to marriage. Later child-custody decisions supplied the third narrow category. The majority framed the exception around issuing the specified status or support decrees, rather than the identity of family-member litigants. **701–704 & n.6.**

That line also reflected institutional considerations. Such decrees may require continuing supervision and social-worker involvement. State tribunals have close connections with local services and specialized experience. Those reasons do not transfer automatically to an ordinary damages suit concerning alleged torts. The Court also noted the unexplained application of a family exception to Kesler, who appeared positioned like any other tort defendant. **703–704 & n.7.**

## Why abstention was unavailable here

Abstention is exceptional even when state interests matter. *Younger* protects comity in relation to pending state proceedings. The defendants alleged none, and Ankenbrandt maintained that the state proceedings had ended before filing. Without a pending state proceeding, the lower courts’ reliance on *Younger* was erroneous. **704–705.**

The majority did not foreclose *Burford* abstention in every family-related tort case. A suit depending on unresolved domestic status before a divorce, alimony, or custody decree might implicate difficult state-law issues of broader public importance. Here, however, the domestic status had been determined under state law and did not affect whether the alleged torts occurred. *Burford* abstention was inappropriate. Footnote 8 also suggests that, when appropriate elsewhere, retaining jurisdiction pending a state determination could protect prompt disposition. These were qualifications and possibilities, not a holding requiring abstention here. **705–706 & n.8.**

## Competing concurrences

**Blackmun** agreed federal courts should generally avoid granting divorce, alimony, and custody decrees, but rejected reading an exception into a statute granting jurisdiction over “all civil actions.” He considered the majority’s historical inference too uncertain to attribute to Congress. *Barber* did not expressly interpret the diversity statute; later territorial and consular cases did not support a consistent common-law/equity explanation; and *Burrus*, relied on for custody, concerned habeas rather than diversity jurisdiction. His notes developed those distinctions and the uncertain history. **707–713 & nn.1–7.**

For Blackmun, longstanding practice and state specialization instead supported discretionary abstention, absent contrary congressional direction. He acknowledged the expansion of federal family-related legislation and warned against converting older practice into a statutory or constitutional barrier. Nevertheless, this case was only peripheral to domestic relations: it requested no status determination, threatened no pending proceeding, and would not upset an existing status decree. He therefore agreed the tort claims should proceed. **713–717 & nn.8–9.**

**Stevens, joined by Thomas,** would decide still less. Every proposed understanding of the exception placed this ordinary tort suit outside it. He therefore would reverse without resolving whether an exception exists or which principle sustains it. His concurrence does not endorse either the majority’s statutory basis or Blackmun’s abstention framework. **717–718 & n.*.**
