# Laredo Hides Company, Inc. v. H & H Meat Products Company, Inc.
513 S.W.2d 210 (Tex. Civ. App.—Corpus Christi 1974) | Contracts

## Setting and facts
Laredo Hides bought hides from American meatpackers for resale to Mexican tanneries. H & H operated a meatpacking plant in Mercedes, Texas. Their February 29, 1972 written contract covered H & H’s entire hide production from March through December, with deliveries at least twice monthly and payment cash upon delivery. Their actual practice was payment by check, including the first March delivery. The controversy concerned a forgotten check for the second delivery and the seller’s attempt to terminate the remaining output contract. 513 S.W.2d at 213–215.

On March 17 Laredo issued a $9,000 check and left it with its trucking agent for the next day’s pickup. The driver departed without it. The agent telephoned H & H’s manager, Hinojosa, who authorized mailing the check; H & H then released the hides. The majority treated the clerk’s testimony as conclusively establishing proper addressing, stamping, and mailing on March 18. On March 21 Hinojosa demanded that money actually reach him by 4:30 that afternoon or H & H would deliver no more hides. The time of this call was disputed. Laredo arranged a bank transfer, but the correspondent bank had closed before the transfer could be completed. Two telegrams reached H & H after the deadline, announcing the transfer and that payment on the mailed check had been stopped. The transfer occurred March 22; H & H’s account was credited March 23. H & H returned the mailed check and the excess over the shipment’s price. On March 30 it unequivocally refused future deliveries. Id. at 214–216.

Laredo already had a resale commitment to a Mexican tannery. It bought replacement hides periodically during the remaining contract term because hides could not practically be stored for an extended period. Prices rose sharply. H & H’s actual production supplied the quantity for the damage calculation: 17,218 hides during April through December. Laredo’s evidence identified replacement price differences and additional transportation and handling expenses. These were actual expenditures and output figures, rather than a forecast of what the plant might produce. Id. at 215–216, 221–223.

## Procedure and issues
After a bench trial, the trial court entered a take-nothing judgment. Its findings treated time as essential, the absence of payment on March 18 as a breach, and the March 21 deadline as an agreed extension that Laredo failed to satisfy. Laredo appealed both the seller’s asserted right to terminate and the denial of damages. The appellate questions were whether authorized mailing and the seller’s delivery waived strict concurrent payment; whether the new deadline became an enforceable modification or reasonable notice restoring strict performance; whether nonpayment justified terminating all remaining installments; and whether the record established cover damages sufficiently to render judgment. Id. at 213–214, 216–223.

## Governing rules and majority application
The majority rejected the premise that every delay in payment made time essential or authorized forfeiture of an entire contract. The agreement contained no express termination provision tied to punctual payment. The nature of the transaction, the parties’ conduct, and the absence of abandonment or material injury mattered. Because H & H authorized payment through the mail, proper mailing constituted payment for this purpose, and H & H bore the risk of postal delay. Its knowing release of the hides without the check also waived the requirement of simultaneous payment. Id. at 216–220.

After waiving strict performance, a party seeking to restore it must give reasonable notice and an opportunity to comply. A demand for money within a few hours, between parties about 165 miles apart, was unreasonable on this record. The majority also declined to transform the demand into a newly agreed contract merely because Laredo tried to avoid termination by obtaining a bank transfer. Its original discussion invoked the absence of new consideration; that statement must be read alongside the rehearing dispute over the Uniform Commercial Code’s rule that an agreed modification needs no consideration. The majority’s rehearing explanation emphasized that the asserted modification was neither established by an actual agreement nor supported by the pleadings and proof. Id. at 219–221, 224–226.

H & H’s March 30 refusal to deliver the remaining production was an anticipatory repudiation. Section 2.610 excused Laredo from repeatedly tendering payment and presenting trucks for futile future pickups. On rehearing, the majority addressed §§ 2.703 and 2.612: the seller’s right to insist on payment before releasing a particular delivery did not automatically establish a right to cancel all future installments. Even an objection concerning one installment required the requisite substantial impairment of the whole contract to justify that broader response. Rights to adequate assurance or appropriate security did not permit the immediate wholesale cancellation asserted here. Id. at 220–221, 224–225.

## Cover and rendered recovery
Under §§ 2.711 and 2.712, a buyer may make reasonable substitute purchases in good faith and without unreasonable delay, recovering the difference between cover and contract cost plus recoverable incidental damages, less expenses saved. Cover and § 2.713 market-price damages are alternative measures. A buyer who proves proper cover need not additionally prove the market price at the time it learned of the breach. The majority treated Laredo’s documented purchases as presumptively proper; H & H supplied no evidence rebutting their reasonableness or establishing expenses saved. Periodic replacement purchases matched the perishable nature of the goods and the output contract’s delivery pattern. Id. at 221–222.

The price differences were $134,252.82 for steer hides and $8,001.66 for bull hides, totaling $142,254.48. Added transportation and handling totaled $3,448.95. The accountant’s invoice-based evidence was admitted without objection, and the majority found the record fully developed. A separate existing purchase at a lower price did not itself establish that the documented replacement purchases were improper cover. The prayer for specific performance or at least $100,000, together with general relief and the absence of an exception to the pleading, did not cap recovery at $100,000. Id. at 222–223, 225–226.

The court reversed and rendered $152,960.04. That total included the cover and incidental sums, $7,132.51 prejudgment interest on the cover difference, and $124.10 interest on incidental damages from December 31, as requested. The rendered judgment carried six percent interest from August 6, 1973, the trial judgment’s date, until paid. This was a final rendered recovery rather than a remand for calculation. Id. at 223.

## Separate writings and rehearing
Bissett wrote the majority opinion and its rehearing response. Chief Justice Nye initially agreed that reversal was warranted but would have remanded because he considered the evidence insufficient to establish the amount of damages conclusively. That original dissent appears at page 223. On rehearing he expressly withdrew it and substituted a different dissent, arguing that the trial judgment should instead be affirmed. The substituted writing controls the account of his ultimate position. Id. at 223, 226–229.

Nye stressed deference to supported findings after a nonjury trial, including the finding that the telephone demand came early enough to permit performance. He read the buyer’s transfer efforts, telegrams, and stop-payment instruction as assent to modified payment terms. Section 2.209 dispensed with consideration, and the merchants’ written confirmation satisfied the Code’s formal requirements in his view. Stopping the original check defeated the mailed-payment arrangement; failing to transfer money by the new deadline breached the replacement agreement. Id. at 226–228.

Nye further relied on earlier dishonored checks and their effect on H & H’s ability to pay suppliers as evidence supporting substantial impairment and termination. Alternatively, he construed this indefinite-output agreement as outside § 2.612’s installment-contract definition, taking the position that its cancellation did not require impairment of the whole contract. The majority rejected those grounds. His substituted dissent would have granted rehearing and affirmed; his additional short dissent repeated that proposed result. Rehearing was denied August 29 and the second motion September 23, 1974. Id. at 228–229.

## Significance and source scope
The decision connects waiver and anticipatory repudiation to the buyer’s cover remedy. It also illustrates why a cover calculation should not be replaced by a different market-price inquiry and why delivery of one shipment does not settle the right to terminate an ongoing output arrangement. The disagreement over modification and whole-contract impairment remains part of the historical decision’s reasoning.
