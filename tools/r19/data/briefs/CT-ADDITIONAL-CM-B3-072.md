# McKennon v. Nashville Banner Publishing Company
513 U.S. 352 (1995) | Contracts

## Court, procedural posture, and disposition
Justice Kennedy delivered the Supreme Court’s unanimous January 23, 1995 opinion. The district court had granted the employer summary judgment against all relief under the Age Discrimination in Employment Act, and the Sixth Circuit had affirmed. Both treated after-acquired evidence of sufficiently serious employee misconduct as a complete bar. The Supreme Court reversed and remanded to the Sixth Circuit for further proceedings. It announced remedial principles without awarding a specified sum or making a final trial finding of age discrimination. (352–356, 363.)

## Discharge and later discovery of misconduct
Christine McKennon had worked for the Nashville Banner for about thirty years and was sixty-two when discharged. The company attributed the discharge to a workforce reduction required by costs; she alleged that age was the true reason. She sought the ADEA’s legal and equitable relief, including backpay. During discovery, she testified that in her final year as secretary to the comptroller she had copied confidential financial documents, taken them home, and shown them to her husband. She said that fear of an age-related discharge prompted her to preserve them for protection. (354–355.)

A few days after the December 18, 1991 deposition, the employer sent another termination letter. It said that removing and copying the records violated her responsibilities and that, had it known of the conduct, it would have fired her immediately. For summary-judgment purposes it conceded discrimination. The district court nevertheless held that her misconduct foreclosed backpay and every other remedy, and the Sixth Circuit agreed. The Supreme Court reviewed the legal consequence on two stated premises: age was the sole reason for the original discharge, and the misconduct was sufficiently grave that immediate discharge would have followed its disclosure. Those premises explain the legal ruling; they are not a finding that the employer had known the facts at the original firing. (355–356.)

## Later evidence did not eliminate the statutory violation
The ADEA’s purposes included both deterrence of discrimination and compensation for its victims. Its private action vindicated public policy as well as an individual injury: disclosure of even one violation might expose practices or wider noncompliance. An absolute bar based on later-discovered wrongdoing would disregard the earlier discrimination and frustrate this enforcement scheme. The Court reviewed the Act’s remedial powers—including backpay, reinstatement, injunctions, declaratory relief, fees, and liquidated damages for willful violations—but did not award all those remedies in this case. (357–359.)

The employer could not defend the original discharge as a mixed-motive decision. Mt. Healthy concerned lawful and unlawful motives both operative when the employer acted. Here the misconduct was unknown at the time. Information the employer did not possess could not have motivated its decision, and later proof that a firing would have been justified was different from proof of why the original firing occurred. The issue therefore was the effect on relief for an assumed violation, rather than whether an unknown legitimate reason retroactively removed that violation. (359–360.)

## The wrongdoing nevertheless affected relief
The Court also rejected the opposite implication that misconduct was irrelevant to every remedy. Traditional unclean-hands doctrine could not mechanically bar a private action serving important statutory public purposes. But the ADEA prohibited discrimination rather than comprehensively regulating all employment choices. An employer retained lawful prerogatives concerning serious employee misconduct, and relief had to account for those legitimate interests. Considering wrongdoing for that purpose was not punishment based on the employee’s moral worth. (360–361.)

As a general rule in this category, reinstatement and front pay were inappropriate. Reinstating someone whom the employer would immediately and lawfully terminate would be inequitable and pointless. Backpay required a more careful adjustment. The starting point was the period from unlawful discharge to discovery of the new information. The employer did not have to ignore misconduct once learned, even if discovery occurred only because the employee brought suit. At the same time, eliminating all backpay would weaken incentives to examine discriminatory motives. Extraordinary equitable circumstances affecting either party’s legitimate interests could warrant further adjustment; the starting point was not announced as an inflexible cap in every possible case. (361–362.)

## Proof required and protection against discovery abuse
An employer invoking after-acquired evidence had first to establish that the wrongdoing was so severe that the employee actually would have been terminated on that ground alone had it been known at the time of discharge. Merely identifying an error or an additional possible justification did not satisfy that formulation. The Court acknowledged the concern that employers would conduct extensive discovery into work history to resist discrimination claims. It expected statutory fee awards and appropriate use of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure to deter most abuses, without declaring that concern imaginary or prohibiting discovery altogether. (362–363.)

## Assigned account and limits of the result
The textbook’s linked note on printed page 871 uses McKennon to distinguish an ADEA claim from the surrounding employment-contract and public-policy tort discussion. It quotes the statutory deterrence rationale and the backpay starting point. The same printed page is supplied twice, as physical PDF pages 213 and 215; both original scans and complete native accounts were read. They represent one assignment and one Supreme Court judgment, not separate decisions.

The complete result was reversal and remand to the Sixth Circuit. Neither the opinion nor the assigned note establishes a specific backpay award, finds a willful violation, or dictates that every ordinary contract action follows this statutory remedial approach. The misconduct’s severity was accepted for this review; discrimination was assumed for summary judgment. Those qualifications are essential to understanding why the employer lost its complete-bar defense while retaining a basis to limit relief. (356, 361–363.)
