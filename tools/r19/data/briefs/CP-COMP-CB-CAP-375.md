# Zubulake v. UBS Warburg LLC

**217 F.R.D. 309 (S.D.N.Y. 2003)** — May 13, 2003; No. 02 Civ. 1243 (SAS). District Judge Shira A. Scheindlin. Civil Procedure: electronic discovery and allocation of production costs. Assigned: CivPro.pdf physical page 478, printed pages 931–932, Note 5. This brief concerns the May 13 discovery order commonly called Zubulake I.

## Facts and procedural posture

Laura Zubulake sued UBS entities for sex discrimination and retaliation under federal, state, and city law. Hired in August 1999 as a director and senior salesperson on the Asian Equities Sales Desk, she alleged that a male supervisor appointed in December 2000 belittled and excluded her. She filed an EEOC charge August 16, 2001, was fired October 9 with two weeks' notice, and sued February 15, 2002. UBS denied discrimination, citing the supervisor's treatment of male employees and hiring of women. These were competing merits positions, not liability findings in this discovery ruling. 312 & n.13.

Her document request sought communications concerning her. UBS produced approximately 350 pages, including 100 pages of emails. A September 12, 2002 agreement focused retrieval on five employees' communications between August 1999 and December 2001. UBS produced no additional emails and had searched neither the backup tapes nor optical archives. Zubulake herself had approximately 450 pages of emails; numerous responsive messages appeared to have been created and deleted. An August 21 message suggested firing her promptly after her EEOC charge, partly to avoid a year-end bonus. The court treated the suit as nonfrivolous without adjudicating the discrimination claim. 311–313 & nn.8–19.

UBS estimated restoration at approximately $175,000, excluding attorney review time. An earlier statement had estimated $300,000. Neither figure was a damages award or a final allocated discovery charge. Zubulake moved to compel production at UBS's expense. Her estimate of potential damages up to $13 million, and the roughly $500,000 annual salary mentioned in note 9, also described the claimed stakes rather than an adjudicated recovery. 312–313 & nn.9–10, 18.

## The storage systems mattered

UBS employees received approximately 200 emails a day. HP OpenMail messages were periodically backed up through Veritas NetBackup: daily tapes were retained for twenty working days, weekly tapes for a year, and monthly tapes for three years before recycling. Ninety-four tapes contained potentially responsive material. Restoring one server's monthly tape typically required five days, although outside assistance could accelerate the work at substantial expense. Intermittent backups could omit messages received and deleted between backups. 313–314 & nn.20–25.

Optical disks separately preserved external email of designated registered traders. Those archives could be searched through Tumbleweed by headers and message text without the restoration needed for backup tapes. Internal messages were not archived there. The technology witness thought desk members probably were designated traders but could not be certain; the human-resources employee probably was not. The court did not find that every employee's every message was in the optical archive. 314–315 & nn.26–28.

## Issue, rule, and holding

**Were responsive electronic messages discoverable, and did their electronic form justify shifting production costs to Zubulake?** They were discoverable. Electronic form alone did not justify shifting costs. The usual presumption required the responding party to pay; cost shifting could be considered for relatively inaccessible data after examining the actual system and obtaining a factual basis for the expense and likely value. 315–320.

Rule 34 covered electronic data, including recoverable deleted material. UBS could not establish completeness by pointing only to its initial search of employees' current files: that search did not reach earlier deleted messages preserved elsewhere. Nor did Zubulake's possession of some messages show she possessed the rest. A request for relevant material remained subject to the historical Rule 26 limitations on disproportionate burden and expense. 316–318 & nn.35–47.

The court distinguished five storage categories: active online data, near-line data, offline storage or archives, backup tapes, and erased or fragmented data. The first three generally were accessible; the latter two generally required restoration or processing. Accessibility depended on the particular format and system. Electronic discovery could be cheaper and easier than paper discovery, and automatically shifting costs threatened to price plaintiffs out of meritorious cases. UBS's active servers and searchable optical disks were accessible, so their production costs stayed with UBS. Its backup tapes warranted consideration of cost shifting. 318–320 & nn.49–61.

## Seven factors and their relative weight

The court revised Rowe's eight-factor approach into seven factors. 321–323:

1. How specifically the request targets relevant information.
2. Whether other sources can supply that information.
3. Production cost compared with the amount in controversy.
4. Production cost compared with each party's available resources.
5. Each party's ability and incentive to control cost.
6. The importance of the litigation's issues.
7. The relative benefit each party receives from production.

These were not equal votes on a checklist. The first two formed the central marginal-utility inquiry. The next three addressed expense and who could bear it. The sixth would arise infrequently but could predominate in litigation with major public consequences. The seventh was least important because discovery ordinarily benefited its requester, although a tangible benefit to the responding party could weigh against shifting costs. The guiding question remained whether the request imposed undue burden or expense relative to the information's importance. 322–323.

The court rejected absolute production cost and relative wealth as sufficient measures. Cost must be assessed against the stakes and the resources available to each party; complaint demands could be inflated. It combined Rowe's specificity and relevance factors and generally discarded the purpose for retaining data. Purpose might correlate with accessibility, but accessible material remained discoverable even if retained inadvertently or without a current business use. SEC preservation requirements reinforced accessibility of the relevant archive; the court did not adjudicate a private claim under that regulation. 321–322 & nn.64–70.

## Exact order and limits

**UBS had to produce all responsive emails on its optical disks and active servers at its own expense. It also had to restore and produce responsive emails from any five backup tapes selected by Zubulake, initially at UBS's expense.** UBS then had to submit an affidavit reporting search results, time, and money spent. The court would conduct the cost-shifting analysis after reviewing the sample and certification. A conference was set for June 17, 2003. No final percentage or dollar allocation for the remaining tapes was made. 324.

Sampling supplied actual evidence of responsive material and restoration expense. The court rejected requiring advance proof that discovery would yield a gold mine. Note 77 qualified sampling itself: its cost might be inappropriate if significant against the suit's value or if the suit was patently frivolous. Note 11 referred to a separate same-day opinion denying requests concerning the IT deposition's expense and disclosure to securities regulators; those rulings were not the seven-factor order's disposition. 312 n.11, 323–324 & nn.75–77.

## Textbook comparison and source verification

The textbook accurately reproduces the seven factors and the $175,000 estimate. Its short account omits the decisive accessible/inaccessible distinction, unequal weighting, and the five-tape sample before any final shift. Its discussion of the 2006 amendments and their advisory note is later editorial material, distinct from this 2003 decision. The employee and securities-retention description should be read with the court's qualifications about registered traders, external messages, and missing internal messages.
