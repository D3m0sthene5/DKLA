# Lewis v. Mobil Oil Corporation
438 F.2d 500 (8th Cir. 1971) | Contracts

## Court, chronology, and scope of the assignment

The Eighth Circuit, through Judge Gibson on a panel with Chief Judge Matthes and Judge Mehaffy, decided this diversity warranty appeal on January 7, 1971. Its February 16 memorandum denied both parties’ rehearing petitions and clarified why the damages award remained excessive. No separate opinion appears. The casebook prints the facts and warranty-of-fitness discussion, ending before the full opinion’s causation and damages analysis. Reading the complete decision materially changes the account of the outcome: Mobil’s liability survived, but the $89,250 judgment did not. The citations below identify Parts I and II, the damages discussion (archived reproduction lines 121–185), and the rehearing memorandum (lines 186–205); the assigned passage appears at casebook printed pages 616–619.

## Facts and the parties’ positions

Lewis had operated an Arkansas sawmill since 1956. To remain competitive, he bought a functioning used hydraulic system in May 1963 and stored it until installation in a new building in November 1964. A longtime Mobil customer, he asked its local dealer, Frank Rowe, to provide the proper hydraulic fluid. Lewis supplied the pump type and made clear that he did not know the system’s lubricant requirements. Rowe likewise did not know; he relayed the request within Mobil and supplied the recommended Ambrex 810, a straight mineral oil without chemical additives. Mobil did not dispute Rowe’s authority to represent it. Within days, the oil discolored, foamed, and became hot. Repeated oil changes did not help. By April 1965 the system failed and was replaced; the initial cause was undetermined. Lewis asked Rowe to verify that he was furnishing the right oil, but Mobil continued recommending Ambrex. During the next two years six Commercial-brand pumps failed. Lewis cleaned their specified metal strainer daily. In April 1967 he switched to a Tyrone pump with an additional disposable filter, but Mobil engineer Ted Klock again recommended Ambrex and that pump failed after three weeks. Full opinion, introductory facts and Part I; casebook 616–618.

On May 9, 1967, Mobil and Tyrone representatives visited the mill, flushed and cleaned the system, installed a new Tyrone pump, and recommended DTE-23 with Del Vac Special additives, including a defoamant. The system then operated satisfactorily through trial about two and a half years later. Footnote 1 qualifies the oil chronology: Lewis used Mobil’s additive-containing oil until July 25, 1967, then switched to Cities Service Pacemaker XD-15, also containing antiwear, oxidation, and foam-control additives. The successful later operation therefore cannot accurately be attributed to uninterrupted use of one Mobil product. Full opinion, facts and n.1.

Lewis claimed that Mobil furnished oil warranted suitable for his particular system, that its lack of additives caused the failures, and that the failures generated repair expenses and lost profits. Mobil denied a particular-purpose warranty, attributed the failures to filtration and air entrainment, challenged consequential damages and their proof, and asserted inadequate notice and mitigation. A jury awarded $89,250, and Mobil appealed the judgment. The appellate court evaluated causation evidence in the light favorable to that verdict; it did not independently find disputed engineering facts after a trial of its own.

## Why an implied warranty of fitness arose

Under Arkansas’s version of UCC §2-315, the circumstances must give the seller reason to know the buyer’s particular purpose and the buyer’s reliance on the seller’s skill or judgment. Both requirements had substantial evidentiary support. Mobil knew the oil was being selected for Lewis’s actual sawmill system, not merely for hydraulic machinery in the abstract, and knew that he depended on its recommendation because he lacked the necessary knowledge. Comment 1 explains that the buyer need not communicate actual knowledge of every detail if the circumstances give the seller reason to realize the purpose and reliance. The court therefore did not need to decide whether Ambrex also breached the separate warranty of merchantability. Part I; casebook 617–619.

Mobil argued that Lewis never specified additive-containing oil and supplied insufficient technical information to establish a warranty for his system. That argument inverted the transaction. Lewis asked Mobil to choose the suitable product precisely because he could not make the technical selection himself. If Mobil needed more information before recommending oil, it should have obtained it; its later inspection demonstrated that doing so was feasible. Its claim that the machinery had abnormal filtration or excessive air entrainment primarily concerned causation rather than whether a warranty arose. Moreover, the record contained evidence of satisfactory operation under the previous owner and after cleaning and use of additive oil; the jury did not have to accept the alleged abnormality. Part I; casebook 618–619. The decision concerns a recommendation made with reason to know this buyer’s system and reliance; it does not declare every lubricant sale a particular-purpose warranty.

## Causation and the competing engineering evidence

The causation evidence supported submission to and acceptance by the jury. Lewis’s qualified expert Edwards explained that oil without appropriate additives permitted cavitation: air bubbles interrupted lubrication, metal contacted metal, and resulting particles could spread through the system and cause further failures. Defoaming additives would disperse the bubbles. Other testimony and the equipment evidence supported that explanation. Mobil’s comparative tests on new oil did not conclusively answer what occurred after chemical changes in used oil. Its example of another mill using inexpensive “pipeline drip” oil likewise did not establish that additives were irrelevant, because that oil contained additives. Part II, “Breach of Warranty as the Cause of Plaintiff’s Damage.”

Mobil’s filtration argument also failed to compel a verdict in its favor. Commercial pumps used metal strainers under the manufacturer’s instructions, whereas the later Tyrone pump had the disposable filtration Mobil emphasized. Evidence of failure in a wholly new system weakened the theory that residual contamination from an earlier broken pump necessarily explained every breakdown. Air could enter hydraulic equipment through ordinary operating conditions, including packing glands and reservoir drainage during shutdowns. These points allowed a reasonable jury to accept the oil explanation without requiring the court to announce that filtration, cleaning, and additives could never interact. The liability verdict had sufficient support. Part II.

## Notice, mitigation, and recoverable losses

The usual difference between the value of warranted and delivered goods under §2-714 did not adequately describe this injury: Lewis’s problem was damage caused by unsuitable oil, rather than overpayment for oil as a commodity. The court treated extra oil expense and repair or replacement expenses as recoverable incidental consequences without a disputed question about those categories. Lost production profits required the separate consequential-damages analysis under §2-715. Damages discussion, reproduction lines 121–185.

Mobil could not obtain judgment as a matter of law merely because Lewis suspected the oil before the eventual change. Suspicion was not established knowledge of its unsuitability. He repeatedly consulted Mobil and equipment representatives, while Mobil continued recommending Ambrex. Seeking an independent expert might have been desirable, but reasonable mitigation did not invariably require it. The jury received an appropriate reasonable-diligence instruction. The court regarded §2-712’s cover provision as directed to nondelivery or a buyer able to reject known defective goods, rather than automatically governing this continuing use with uncertain cause. The broader duty to avoid preventable losses through reasonable efforts still applied. Damages discussion, mitigation discussion.

His repeated communications also furnished sufficient notice under §2-607. Notice need identify the transaction as troublesome; it need not articulate every legal objection or establish bad faith. Whether notice came within a reasonable time after discovery or when discovery should have occurred was properly submitted to the jury. Arkansas’s older “tacit agreement” approach did not defeat consequential damages under the Code. Comments 2 and 3 to §2-715 reject requiring a seller consciously to agree to insure the buyer’s losses. Mobil knew it was supplying a productive sawmill; losses of production from unsuitable operating oil were foreseeable in the required sense. Evidence of customers, available markets, and the mill’s inability to supply close-tolerance bridge timbers supported lost-profit proof for an established business, although the amount still had to rest on a reasonable approximation rather than speculation. Damages discussion, notice, foreseeability, and proof discussions.

## Excessiveness, rehearing, and exact disposition

The initial opinion understood the award to include lost profits both during the thirty months of Ambrex use and during the following period, when capital shortages restricted production despite successful operation with additive-containing oil. It rejected charging Mobil with later losses based simply on Lewis’s undercapitalization: Mobil was not his source of business financing. Because the verdict was a lump sum and the appellate court could not separate permissible from impermissible amounts, it reversed and remanded for a new trial on damages only. Liability was not reopened. Damages discussion and disposition, reproduction lines 184–185.

The rehearing memorandum is essential. Lewis argued that the claimed $80,000 in lost profits concerned only the thirty months of Ambrex use. The court accepted that interpretation for its analysis but still found the award excessive. Approximate figures showed about $9,250 in direct losses and $80,000 in profits; adding the latter to approximately $12,000 actually earned during those thirty months implied annual profits of roughly $37,000. The record did not establish profits approaching that figure. Earlier annual results fluctuated widely; after the change in oil, two years yielded about $41,000, or $20,500 annually. The court rejected extrapolating those later profits from an asserted 50–60% capacity to hypothetical full-capacity profits in a way that indirectly transferred responsibility for capital limitations to Mobil. Rehearing memorandum, reproduction lines 189–202, including the paragraphs beginning “Plaintiff’s profit records show” and “If the $80,000 loss of profits is confined”.

Actual prior and subsequent profits were useful guides rather than exclusive measures. The damages factfinder should consider lumber prices, market conditions, capacity, and operating efficiency and should favor periods relevant to the injury rather than mechanically fixing a four-year average. Both rehearing petitions were denied; the direction for a damages-only new trial remained. A correct brief therefore distinguishes the initial understanding of the lump-sum verdict from the court’s clarification on rehearing: the final excessiveness conclusion did not depend solely on finding that the jury awarded a separate post-use loss category. Rehearing memorandum, concluding paragraphs.
