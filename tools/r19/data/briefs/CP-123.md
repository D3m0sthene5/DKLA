# Aetna Casualty & Surety Co. v. Yeatts

**CP-123 · Civil Procedure · 122 F.2d 350 (4th Cir. 1941) · August 22, 1941 · Parker, with Soper and H.H. Watkins on the panel**

Evidence sufficient to permit a jury verdict does not necessarily foreclose a new trial. A motion for judgment notwithstanding the verdict tests legal sufficiency while preserving favorable evidence and credibility choices for the prevailing party. A new-trial motion allows the trial judge to assess the clear weight of evidence, falsity, and miscarriage of justice. This distinction did not require a new trial in Yeatts: the appellate court affirmed because the denial was within the trial judge’s discretion.

## Facts and procedural posture

Aetna initiated a declaratory-judgment suit concerning coverage under an indemnity policy. After an earlier appeal, Yeatts amended his answer, alleging that a consent judgment had been entered against him in the state damages action. He sought that amount, costs, interest, and attorney fees from the insurer. Aetna denied coverage on the ground that the liability arose while Yeatts performed a criminal abortion and that the policy expressly excluded that liability. The jury decided whether he had engaged in the alleged criminal conduct and returned a verdict in his favor. This was the second appeal in the coverage litigation. 122 F.2d at 352.

Evidence could have supported the insurer’s position, but Yeatts testified himself and, if believed, had committed no criminal act. Aetna did not move for a directed verdict or otherwise challenge sufficiency before the verdict. It then sought judgment notwithstanding the verdict and alternatively a new trial, asserting that the verdict contradicted the credible evidence. Denial of those two motions supplied the only appellate issues. The opinion does not recount the medical evidence in detail or adjudicate criminal guilt independently. Id.

## Judgment notwithstanding the verdict

Aetna’s request failed procedurally and substantively. Under the historical Rule 50(b), an appropriate directed-verdict motion was required as the foundation for a postverdict sufficiency challenge. No such motion was made. The court also explained that absence of a proper trial challenge prevented appellate review of evidentiary sufficiency. Id.

Even had the insurer preserved the request, Yeatts’s favorable testimony prevented the requested judgment. The court had to view the evidence favorably to the party against whom judgment was sought and resolve conflicts in his favor. A judge could not reject his testimony merely because contrary evidence appeared more credible. Thus the existence of evidence supporting Aetna did not eliminate the jury’s authority to find for Yeatts. Id.

## New-trial power and its rationale

Rule 59 supplied a different inquiry. The motion involved federal procedure and was not governed by state practice rules. A trial judge should grant a new trial if the verdict conflicts with the clear weight of evidence, rests on false evidence, or would produce a miscarriage of justice—even where substantial evidence prevents directing a verdict. The constitutional jury guarantee assigns credibility and weighing to the jury for purposes of deciding whether to take the case away from it; the court’s historical supervisory authority nevertheless permits a fresh jury trial to remedy an unjust result. Id. at 352–354.

Parker supported this distinction through historical authorities explaining that new trials protected the jury institution by allowing another jury to reconsider mistakes, prejudice, confusion, or misconduct. The long passages from *Smith v. Times Publishing Co.*, *Bright v. Eynon*, and *Mellin v. Taylor* are authorities quoted within Parker’s opinion. They are not separate opinions in Yeatts. His quoted discussion of *Garrison* crystallized the difference: the judge’s disbelief cannot justify a directed verdict when substantial favorable evidence exists, but it can inform a new-trial decision concerning clear weight or falsity. Id. at 353–354.

## Appellate review and exact result

The appellate court stressed a further distinction between the trial judge’s power and its own review authority. Granting or refusing a new trial rested in sound trial-court discretion and was reviewable only in exceptional circumstances. The opinion quoted historical explanations grounded in statutory restrictions on reviewing factual errors, the Seventh Amendment’s reexamination limitation, and discretion. This is the court’s 1941 formulation, not an assertion of every aspect of present appellate-review doctrine. Id. at 354–355.

On its examination, the Fourth Circuit thought the trial judge could properly have granted a new trial. That observation did not establish that denial was an abuse of discretion. It found neither such abuse nor special circumstances permitting interference, and affirmed the judgment. Id. at 355. The reported opinion contains no separate judicial writing or footnotes; Parker speaks for the panel without an express numerical vote announcement.

The study point is a sequence of distinct questions: whether favorable evidence legally permits the jury’s verdict, whether the trial judge should order another trial after weighing the result, and whether an appellate court may disturb that discretionary decision. Success on the insurer’s argument that other evidence was more credible would not automatically satisfy the requirements for judgment, a new trial, and appellate reversal alike.
