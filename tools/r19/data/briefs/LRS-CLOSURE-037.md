# Federal Trade Commission v. Universal-Rundle Corp.
387 U.S. 244 (1967) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and enforcement proceedings

Universal-Rundle manufactured china and cast-iron plumbing fixtures sold throughout the United States. In 1960, the FTC charged that its differing prices to competing purchasers violated Clayton Act § 2(a), as amended by the Robinson-Patman Act. The company denied the essential allegations and pleaded cost justification and good-faith meeting of competition, but made no effort to sustain those affirmative defenses during the evidentiary hearings. The Commission found that its approximately ten-percent truckload discounts in 1957 favored customers able to buy truckloads over competing customers unable to do so. The finding concerned discriminatory effects in the demonstrated competitive relationships, not a rule that every quantity discount was unlawful. (245–246.)

The Commission ordered the company to cease selling like-quality fixtures to a purchaser at a higher price than that charged to another purchaser competing with it in resale or distribution. During four years of proceedings, Universal-Rundle had neither supplied competitors' pricing information nor proposed an industry-wide inquiry. One month after the order issued, it sought a stay long enough for the FTC to investigate industry-wide truckload discounts and institute whatever proceedings the Commission considered appropriate. (246.)

Its affidavits and documents suggested competitors offered discounts averaging approximately eighteen percent. It claimed a 5.75-percent market share excluding Sears sales, compared with larger rivals, and pointed to its own losses and their profits. Its marketing vice president stated on information and belief that some competitors sold to customers who might not buy truckloads, and predicted that enforcement against Universal-Rundle alone would divert sales and worsen its financial losses. Those assertions were evidence offered to support the stay, not findings establishing identical illegal practices throughout the industry or inevitable destruction of the company. (246–247 & n. 2.)

## Agency and appellate decisions

The Commission unanimously denied the stay. A general allegation of truckload discounts did not establish unlawful industry-wide discrimination. Its order did not prohibit all truckload discounts: legality depended on an actual price difference, competition at the same functional level, the smaller customer's ability to obtain the discount, and the difference's anticompetitive effect in the demonstrated market. Even a prima facie violation could be met by statutory defenses. Earlier losses also did not prove future hardship caused by enforcement. (248 & nn. 3–4.)

The Seventh Circuit set aside the denial and required an industry investigation without reaching the merits of the petition challenging the underlying cease-and-desist order. It acknowledged Moog Industries' patent-abuse-of-discretion standard but concluded that the evidence showed a widespread industry practice, that enforcement would sacrifice a small participant, and that industry giants rather than the public would benefit. The Supreme Court therefore reviewed the appellate court's intervention in the timing of enforcement, not a completed appellate ruling on the underlying price-discrimination violation. Chief Justice Warren delivered the Court's opinion on May 29, 1967; the report contains no separate opinion or participation qualification. (244–245, 249.)

## Rule: review the agency's evaluation, not the evidence anew

Moog placed decisions about postponing enforcement within the Commission's specialized understanding. Whether apparently widespread illegal practices actually existed required administrative judgment. Accordingly, a court could interfere only upon a patent abuse of discretion. Its inquiry was whether the Commission's evaluation of the stay petition was patently arbitrary and capricious, rather than whether the court independently thought the supporting evidence tended to establish the asserted facts. The appellate court had crossed that boundary by making its own findings. (249–250.)

Universal-Rundle's evidence did not establish the supposed industry-wide practice identified as illegal in its own case. Its violation depended on simultaneous discounted sales to customers who competed with disadvantaged customers unable to buy truckloads. The stay materials did not show comparable anticompetitive effects from competitors' discounts. Nor did the vice president's unsupported speculation establish that Universal-Rundle would be sacrificed or substantially injured. The agency could reasonably find these materials inadequate; even the appellate court's improper independent assessment lacked a sufficient factual basis. (250–251.)

## Enforcement priorities and the limit on discretion

The Court added that proof of identical violations by every competitor and substantial injury would not necessarily require a stay. The Commission could assess the competitive harm of permitting continued violations and decide how to allocate funds and personnel to implement Congress's objectives. One adjudicated violator did not gain an automatic entitlement to postpone compliance until enforcement reached the entire industry. This recognizes expertise in both substantive competitive conditions and enforcement sequencing. (251.)

That discretion was not unbridled. The FTC could not arbitrarily destroy one of many violators through its choice of proceedings. Here, however, its refusal to suspend enforcement rested on a reasonable evaluation of the stay petition. The decision therefore applied a demanding but real standard of review; it did not hold remedial timing categorically immune from judicial scrutiny. (251–252.)

A final record limitation matters. After denial of the stay, the company supplied staff with additional information about the anticompetitive effects of discounts offered by two competitors. The Seventh Circuit apparently relied on that material when requiring an investigation and finding a pending Justice Department inquiry insufficient. The material was not properly before that court on the review petition and was absent from the Supreme Court record. The Court therefore did not decide whether it required an industry investigation or whether the Justice Department's investigation rendered an FTC inquiry unnecessary. (252 n. 5.)

## Judgment and assigned scope

The Court **reversed the Seventh Circuit's judgment, set aside the stay, and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion**. It did not itself resolve the appellate petition challenging the underlying cease-and-desist order or establish the legality of competitors' discounts. (252.)
