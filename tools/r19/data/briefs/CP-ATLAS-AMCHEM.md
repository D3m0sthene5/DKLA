## Concrete dispute and how this settlement arose

This was an effort to create a nationwide administrative settlement of present and future asbestos claims against twenty former manufacturers belonging to the Center for Claims Resolution (CCR). Asbestos exposure could produce disease after decades of latency. Existing litigation had accumulated large backlogs, high transaction costs, and risks that later claimants would find defendants' resources depleted. The majority acknowledged that crisis and the attraction of a national compensation scheme. It nevertheless treated the limits of a procedural class action as a distinct question from whether such a scheme would be desirable. 521 U.S. at 597–599.

The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation had transferred already-filed federal asbestos cases to the Eastern District of Pennsylvania for pretrial proceedings. Negotiations among steering committees failed to produce a comprehensive agreement. CCR then approached lawyers who represented thousands of existing, or “inventory,” plaintiffs. Those lawyers negotiated an administrative system for people whose claims had not yet been filed, although they did not then have attorney-client relationships with those prospective claimants. CCR separately settled inventory cases, including an agreement exceeding $200 million. Only afterward did the parties institute this settlement class action. No transferred MDL case was included: note 3 expressly distinguishes the new, unfiled-claims class from the pending cases within the Panel's authority. Id. at 599–602 & nn. 1–5.

On January 15, 1993, the parties simultaneously presented a complaint, an answer, a proposed settlement, and a certification motion. They never intended a trial. Nine lead plaintiffs and their families purported to represent a potentially enormous class of persons exposed to defendants' asbestos, or related to exposed persons, who had not already sued a CCR defendant. More than half the named plaintiffs alleged existing physical injuries; others alleged exposure without manifested disease. Every representative stood for the whole class. There were no subclasses. The complaint invoked diversity and asserted state-law negligence, strict-liability, warranty, emotional-distress, enhanced-risk, medical-monitoring, and conspiracy theories. Id. at 601–603.

## Terms that made the representational question concrete

The settlement created a detailed administrative compensation mechanism. Four principal categories covered mesothelioma, lung cancer, other specified cancers, and nonmalignant conditions. Ordinary mesothelioma payments ranged from $20,000 to $200,000. Exceptional and extraordinary claims could qualify for additional treatment, subject to limits. Annual caps restricted how many claims could be paid within each disease category. Payments were not inflation-adjusted. The defendants waived liability defenses, including limitations defenses. Id. at 603–605 & n. 6.

Certain claims received no separate compensation even where applicable state law recognized them: loss of consortium, exposure-only emotional distress, enhanced risk, and medical monitoring, as well as pleural plaques without impairment. Exposure-only and pleural claimants could later qualify if compensable disease developed. Most members would remain bound indefinitely, while defendants could withdraw after ten years. A small annual number could later pursue litigation, but without punitive damages or increased-risk claims. These restricted later options were distinct from the initial three-month opportunity to exclude oneself from the class. Id. at 604–606.

Objectors argued that present compensation was too low, that future claimants lacked inflation and scientific-change protection, that valuable state-law claims were eliminated, and that the same lawyers and representatives could not fairly bargain for groups with conflicting needs. A claimant who wanted immediate payment could trade away protections that another claimant, facing illness decades later, needed most. Id. at 606–611.

## Procedure and question before the Supreme Court

Judge Weiner conditionally certified a Rule 23(b)(3) opt-out class and appointed Locks, Motley, and Rice as counsel. Judge Reed conducted the fairness proceedings. The district court found the settlement fair, notice adequate, and certification appropriate, rejecting conflicts and declining subclasses. It issued an injunction against class members' separate suits. The Third Circuit vacated certification and the injunction, concluding that Rule 23's prerequisites were unmet. Id. at 605–612.

The appellate court stated that certification requirements had to be satisfied without considering settlement, although it actually examined settlement terms when analyzing representation. The Supreme Court reviewed settlement's proper role in certification. The central question was whether a settlement-only proposal can overcome failures of class cohesion and representation by presenting an attractive global compromise. The Court answered no, while correcting the proposition that settlement is wholly irrelevant. Id. at 618–622.

## Holdings and reasoning

**Settlement affects the inquiry but does not replace it.** Because no trial was proposed, the district court need not assess whether a trial would create intractable management difficulties under Rule 23(b)(3)(D). The other safeguards remain fully applicable and can demand heightened attention: a settlement court will not have the usual opportunity to adjust the class as litigation develops. Rule 23(e)'s fairness review is additional to the requirements of subdivisions (a) and (b), not a substitute. Judicial approval of a bargain cannot alone authorize binding people who lack an adequately cohesive and represented class. Id. at 619–622 & nn. 16–17.

This conclusion also follows the Rules Enabling Act. Courts must apply a rule produced through the prescribed rulemaking process, rather than invent a fairness-only certification standard that changes substantive rights. Reducing certification to an overall impression of a good bargain would remove practical protections. Counsel unable to threaten a viable litigated action would lose bargaining leverage, and a judge might confront an agreed performance without adversarial testing. The majority accordingly modified the Third Circuit's settlement-irrelevance formulation but found no need for a remand. Id. at 620–622.

**Predominance was not established.** The majority distinguished Rule 23(a)(2) commonality from the more demanding Rule 23(b)(3) predominance requirement. Shared asbestos exposure and a desire for compensation could supply a common feature without predominating over the individual controversies. Members encountered different products, exposure routes, durations, smoking histories, medical conditions, and state-law rules. Some were currently ill; others might never develop a disease, or might develop different conditions with different expenses. A common interest in accepting a fair settlement cannot itself establish predominance, because that reasoning would make the requirement empty in every settlement case. Id. at 622–625 & nn. 18–19.

The Court did not categorically prohibit mass-tort certification. A common disaster or sufficiently cohesive set of claims could satisfy predominance depending on circumstances. The problem was this extraordinarily sprawling class, not the mere presence of serious injuries or a settlement proposal. Nor was this a limited-fund class under Rule 23(b)(1)(B); note 19 expressly identifies the exclusive reliance on subdivision (b)(3). Id. at 623–625.

**The undivided class lacked adequate representation.** Presently injured members sought generous immediate payments. Exposure-only members needed durable future funding, inflation protection, evolving medical criteria, and meaningful later alternatives. Diversity of medical conditions among named plaintiffs did not solve the conflict if each purported to bargain for the entire class rather than a separate constituency. Although the district court found adequate assets to pay qualifying settlement claims, the bargain still made allocation decisions: limited categories, no inflation adjustment, capped later exits, and uncompensated consortium claims. Id. at 625–628.

Adequacy therefore required structural assurance that different groups' interests had been represented in the negotiations. A shared wish to maximize aggregate recovery was not enough; representatives needed to understand and protect the particular interests entrusted to them. Note 20 recognizes counsel competence and conflicts as relevant to adequacy but expressly declines to decide counsel-adequacy questions separately, given the dispositive predominance and representative defects. The decision is not a finding that these attorneys committed collusion or professional misconduct. Id. at 626 n. 20, 627–628.

## Questions not finally decided

Objectors asserted that the proceeding lacked an Article III controversy, that exposure-only claimants lacked standing or redressability, and that the diversity amount in controversy was unmet. The Court did not decide those objections. It treated the dispositive certification issues as logically antecedent because the jurisdictional issues depended on the certification at issue. That reasoning does not establish that uninjured people generally have standing, or authorize courts routinely to decide merits before jurisdiction. Id. at 612–613 & n. 15.

Notice also remained unresolved definitively. The Court emphasized how difficult it would be to notify people unaware of their past exposure, future spouses, or children not yet identifiable as claimants, and how little information some members would have to evaluate an opt-out choice. Those concerns explain the stakes, but predominance and adequacy already disposed of the case. The Court did not finally hold this notice plan unconstitutional. Id. at 628.

## Breyer and Stevens: concurrence in the standard, disagreement with the disposition

Breyer agreed that settlement matters to certification but would have vacated and remanded for the lower courts to apply that principle with proper factual review. He emphasized unusually costly litigation, weak recoveries, extensive negotiations, and the district court's five weeks of hearings and more than 300 findings. On his view, the importance of common features depends on the actual proceeding contemplated; waived defenses and an administrative resolution could reduce the significance of differences that would dominate a trial. Id. at 629–636.

He did not say settlement always supports certification. He expressly rejected that characterization at 635, accepting that it can reveal divergent interests as well as common issues. He acknowledged serious present-versus-future conflicts but regarded their assessment as fact-dependent and suitable for district-court judgment reviewed for abuse of discretion. Benefits for future claimants included limitations protection, waived defenses, predictable payments, potential additional recovery upon cancer, and some assurance of available resources. Those benefits might or might not outweigh the absence of inflation adjustment. Id. at 635–639.

Breyer also declined to pronounce the bargain fair. His point was that the record required more informed review than the Supreme Court could supply. He similarly emphasized the extensive actual notice effort and resisted adverse implications about its sufficiency without proper factual examination. His proposed remand, rather than automatic approval of the class or settlement, appears expressly at 641. Id. at 639–641. There are no judicial notes in his separate writing; the complete report contains all twenty-one majority notes and the counsel/amicus starred note as well.

## Exact judgment and assigned-source precision

The Court **affirmed the Third Circuit's judgment**, leaving this class decertified and the district court's orders vacated. Ginsburg wrote for Rehnquist, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and herself; Breyer and Stevens agreed with the basic standard but dissented from the disposition. The result was 6–2, with O'Connor not participating. It was not a products-liability merits judgment or a categorical rejection of settlement classes. Id. at 595–596, 629, 641.
