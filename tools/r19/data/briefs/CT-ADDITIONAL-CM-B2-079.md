# Vantage Technology, LLC v. Cross
17 S.W.3d 637 (Tenn. Ct. App. 1999) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

The Tennessee Court of Appeals, Eastern Section, decided this appeal on October 19, 1999. Judge Susano wrote; Presiding Judge Goddard concurred, and Judge Inman did not participate. There was no separate writing. After a bench trial, the chancellor had rejected Vantage's noncompetition covenant as unreasonable and unenforceable. The appellate court reversed that determination, narrowed the covenant, affirmed denial of Vantage's proposed loyalty-claim amendment and the application of Tennessee law, and remanded to determine any breach and resulting relief. Appeal costs were divided equally. The report records denial of permission to appeal on April 17, 2000; this brief addresses the appellate judgment. (637, 640, 648–651.)

## Business relationships and the employee's departure

Vantage supplied rural hospitals with cataract-surgery equipment, supplies, and technicians. Surgeons' preferences influenced which hospital they used and which mobile supplier the hospital hired. Vantage initiated hospital relationships through sales efforts and relied on technicians to maintain them. Technicians received doctor diaries containing machine settings, instrument and supply preferences, and personal information; they updated those records, reported business opportunities, and could entertain physicians and staff at company expense. A technician transported and prepared equipment and adjusted machine modes during surgery, while the surgeon performed the operation. No medical license was required, and basic machine operation could be learned quickly. The business nevertheless depended on familiarity with physicians and equipment as well as the employer's established goodwill. (640–642.)

Cross joined Vantage in October 1994 without relevant equipment training. His first month included 241.5 training hours, primarily observation of about seventy operations; he also watched videos, read manuals, attended monthly meetings, and participated in a wet lab. He subsequently served forty-nine hospitals in at least six states, used the diaries, and had limited knowledge of prices and other contract terms. By summer 1996 he was the principal Vantage technician for many operations at Fort Sanders-Loudon and Lakeway and had developed a strong relationship with Dr. Gollamudi. (642–643.)

In January 1995 Cross signed a covenant restricting direct or indirect competition during employment and for three years afterward. Its definition of competitive activity covered services in Vantage's business within fifty miles of any company office or client location. Another provision called for enforcement to the fullest extent permitted by the law and public policy of each jurisdiction where enforcement was sought, with invalid portions deleted for that jurisdiction. (642–643.)

When Vantage proposed assigning Cross to Ohio hospitals in summer 1996, he and Gollamudi discussed an independent business using a different machine preferred by the doctor. Cross met LaFollette's operating-room director on August 13 without disclosing that he remained a Vantage employee, gave two weeks' notice on August 15, and sent LaFollette a quote the next day. Gollamudi financed the new business with a $40,000 loan; Cross ordered a refurbished machine on August 29, his last employment day, and learned its operation from the manufacturer's representative. He began serving LaFollette September 10, Lakeway within the next month, Fort Sanders-Loudon in October, and Fort Sanders-Sevierville in January 1997. Vantage had served LaFollette only once in 1994 without securing an ongoing customer relationship, and first contacted Sevierville in April 1998. These differing histories matter to the covenant's permissible scope. Vantage sued May 16, 1997, for contractual injunctive relief and damages. (642–644.)

## A protectable interest requires more than ordinary competition

Tennessee disfavored restraints on trade and construed employment covenants strictly for the employee, while enforcing reasonable restrictions. Relevant considerations included consideration, threatened harm to the employer, employee hardship, and the public interest; consideration was not disputed on this appeal. The employer first had to identify special circumstances giving the employee an unfair competitive advantage beyond ordinary competition. Specialized training, confidential information, and customer relationships could establish that interest individually or together. General occupational knowledge and skill remained the employee's even when acquired through expensive employer training. Information readily available in the trade was not protected merely because an employer possessed it. Customer goodwill could be different: repeated contacts made the employee the employer's representative, providing relationships and personal information originating in the employer's business. (644–646.)

The chancellor had separately discounted the training's uniqueness, the confidentiality of lists, prices, and diaries, and the importance of relationships to hospitals concerned with quality and price. The appellate court required consideration of these factors together. Vantage's training, its initiation of physician and hospital relationships, Cross's agency role in developing them, and his access to preferences and business information collectively created a protectable interest. In particular, his relationship with Gollamudi and the information acquired through it enabled competition at Vantage's expense. Footnote 1 rejects the contention that using a different machine, or no longer needing diary information, defeated this totality analysis. The court did not hold that all general training or all publicly available customer identities independently justified a restraint. Reviewing the bench record with a presumption favoring factual findings unless the evidence preponderated otherwise, it found the evidence contrary to the chancellor's conclusion. (644–647 & n. 1.)

The threatened loss of these investments outweighed Cross's hardship. The court acknowledged that restraints might increase already expensive health-care costs but also considered the incentives to train and inform employees and the unfair exploitation of employer-created advantages. Those public considerations did not preclude enforcement. (647.)

## Narrowing territory and resolving the mileage ambiguity

A restraint could extend no further in time or territory than necessary to protect the interest. A court could modify excessive scope, but credible evidence of deliberate oppression could invalidate the whole covenant rather than reward calculated overbreadth. Vantage's rationale—that influential surgeons worked at nearby hospitals—supported a fifty-mile area around customer hospitals where Cross had served, but did not justify areas surrounding company offices or hospitals he had never served. No evidence established deliberately oppressive drafting. The court therefore removed those unsupported anchors rather than enforcing the original covenant unchanged. (647–648.)

Two additional ambiguities favored Cross. Fifty miles meant the shortest driving distance rather than a radius. A client location meant a hospital where Vantage was regularly providing services when Cross left, rather than every former client. The final restriction consequently covered three years and fifty miles by the shortest driving route from qualifying hospitals where Cross had performed services for Vantage. The court held three years reasonable on this record. It remanded to determine whether and to what extent Cross violated that narrowed covenant and, only if a violation were found, the appropriate injunction or damages. It did not itself establish liability at each named hospital or award a sum. (648.)

## The attempted amendment and governing law

After four witnesses testified on the first trial day, Vantage sought to add breach of the duty of loyalty based on learning that Cross solicited LaFollette before giving notice. It argued implied consent because Cross's lawyer also questioned witnesses about timing. The court affirmed denial of amendment: that evidence also bore on the pleaded noncompetition claim, so its introduction did not imply consent to a new theory. Witnesses had already been examined and dismissed, and adding the claim would deprive Cross of fair notice and an opportunity to present responsive evidence. Footnote 2 reproduces Rule 15.02, including amendments for issues actually tried by consent, prejudice, and possible continuances. The ruling concerns amendment and trial fairness, not a merits adjudication that employees owe no loyalty duty. (648–650; n. 2.)

Cross also urged Illinois law, arguing that the enforcement provision was only severability language and that allowing law to vary by enforcement forum undermined predictability. The court read its first sentence as a choice-of-law clause and its second as severability, giving both effect. It accepted good faith, Tennessee's connection, absence of sham, and lack of a materially greater Illinois interest. It therefore applied Tennessee law. The possibility of different forum outcomes did not defeat this clause: the court considered its jurisdiction proper and its judgment entitled to full faith and credit, preventing these parties from relitigating the same matter elsewhere. This was enforcement of the particular clause on the established connections, rather than a rule that every forum-selection-by-enforcement provision invariably controls. (650–651.)

## Assigned exercise and reading scope

C&M 351–700, PDF 258, printed 565, presents the mileage ambiguity as a drafting exercise involving a location forty-five miles away but fifty-five miles by road from the company office. The actual opinion supports the shortest-driving-distance interpretation, but also removes company offices as permissible anchors. The exercise abbreviates the clause and supplies hypothetical distances; those distances are not actual findings about Cross's operation.
