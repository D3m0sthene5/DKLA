# National Nutritional Foods Association v. Weinberger
512 F.2d 688 (2d Cir. 1975) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, issues, and result

The Second Circuit decided No. 74-1738 on February 3, 1975. Mansfield wrote the opinion with Waterman; Lumbard concurred separately. All three agreed to remand. The court held that the FDA could issue binding substantive regulations under the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act's general rulemaking grant, using APA notice and comment. But the agency had not sufficiently explained why these higher-dose vitamin preparations were drugs in the first place. The district court therefore had to examine the complete administrative record and obtain the necessary explanation before deciding whether its summary judgment for the government should stand. This was neither unconditional affirmance nor immediate invalidation of the regulations. (691, 695–704; concurrence 704–705.)

## Regulations, medical record, and proceedings

The National Nutritional Foods Association and Solgar challenged regulations classifying vitamin A preparations containing more than 10,000 international units per dosage form and vitamin D preparations containing more than 400 IU per dosage form as prescription drugs. The Commissioner proceeded under § 701(a), which authorized regulations for efficient enforcement of the Act. His December 14, 1972 proposal described toxicity from prolonged excessive intake, sometimes causing death, and widespread promotion of high doses for disease prevention and treatment. Recommended allowances were much lower for most consumers. The agency made its medical bibliography available and invited comments for sixty days. (691–692.)

More than 2,500 comments came from consumers, health professionals, manufacturers, trade associations, and others. Some supported the restrictions; others agreed vitamins could be toxic but proposed higher thresholds; still others characterized vitamins as foods whose consumption should remain an individual choice. Both plaintiffs submitted extensive opposition. The Commissioner announced the final rules on August 2, 1973, effective October 1. He reasoned that amounts within the existing nutritional ranges met normal needs and that, with one recognized exception, higher amounts were appropriate only for therapeutic use. The exception permitted vitamin D up to 1,000 IU under medical supervision for people with poor absorption. (692–693 & n. 4; 703.)

Plaintiffs sued on August 6. Judge Frankel denied preliminary relief, and the Second Circuit affirmed that earlier ruling. After the rules became effective, Frankel granted summary judgment dismissing the complaint on April 5, 1974. He upheld notice and comment and applied arbitrary-and-capricious review rather than allowing a new evidentiary trial. He also vacated the notice to depose the Commissioner. This appeal concerned that final summary judgment, not simply the earlier injunction decision. Note 1 separately identifies the court's dismissal of a direct petition for review for lack of jurisdiction. (691, 693–694 & n. 1.)

## Competing positions and statutory authority

The challengers argued that only regulations adopted under § 701(e)'s detailed hearing procedure could bind private parties. Rules under § 701(a), they said, merely announced agency interpretations that courts must independently adjudicate in enforcement proceedings. They also argued that the products were foods, that prescription status required inherent unsafety rather than potential misuse, that the record and explanation were incomplete, and that the proper review standard was substantial evidence. They sought a de novo trial and discovery as a way to establish those propositions. (694–695.)

Section 701(e) required elaborate procedures for specified subjects, including notice, objections, an evidentiary hearing on request, and appellate review. Section 701(a) contained a general grant without those express conditions. The majority acknowledged that the statute did not explicitly identify the authoritative effect of each kind of regulation and that interpretive rules could guide courts without controlling them. Yet it rejected a rigid classification that would deny binding force to every § 701(a) regulation. General delegations could support substantive rules, especially where agency scientific expertise made industry-wide regulation more useful than repeated judicial enforcement suits. (695–696 & nn. 5, 7.)

The Supreme Court's Hynson, Ciba, Bentex, and USV decisions were particularly important. They recognized FDA authority to prescribe evidentiary standards, reject deficient applications without futile hearings, and decide new-drug status administratively. The statute did not create parallel administrative and judicial systems requiring a fresh judicial decision for every manufacturer. The specific § 701(e) procedure supplemented the general grant rather than restricting § 701(a) to advisory statements. Rulemaking also allowed the agency to consider an industry's range of alternatives and gave manufacturers and the public advance guidance. Note 8 emphasized that streamlined procedure still required APA notice and comment for rules intended to have legal force; the interpretive-rule exemption did not erase that obligation. (696–698 & n. 8.)

The court also rejected the narrower argument that the 1951 Durham-Humphrey prescription-drug amendment withheld substantive rulemaking power for prescription classifications. Legislative history contained competing statements, including House opposition to changing the judicial enforcement model and Senate references to administrative interpretation and enforcement. The majority gave greater weight to the final Senate report than to individual statements at earlier stages. More decisively, the Supreme Court's recognition of administrative authority over new-drug status supported authority to determine prescription status as well. (698–699 & nn. 9–10.)

## Adequate procedure did not dispense with meaningful review

No evidentiary hearing was required for these § 701(a) rules. Notice and comment was adequate: interested parties submitted detailed materials collected in roughly forty-six folders, examined competing submissions and medical literature, and criticized the proposal. The decision turned on an appraisal of scientific information and toxicity risks rather than witness credibility or a narrow disputed historical fact. That supported using written participation instead of a trial-type hearing. The Commissioner acknowledged that consumers could take many weaker tablets, but believed that convenient single high-dose units increased the risk of excessive consumption. (699–700.)

The majority held that § 706(2)(A)'s arbitrary-and-capricious standard governed this informal rulemaking, rather than § 706(2)(E)'s substantial-evidence standard applicable to the hearing proceedings it distinguished. It nevertheless insisted on an explanation adequate for review. A concise statement of basis and purpose need not reproduce formal findings, but unexplained action could not survive simply because the court lacked a formal record. The agency's experience, if used as a factual foundation, also had to enter the reviewable record. (700–701 & n. 11.)

An inadequate explanation did not authorize the district court to build an entirely new merits record through a de novo trial. Under Camp and Overton Park, the focal point remained the administrative record already before the agency. Affidavits or testimony could supply the necessary explanation of its reasoning. If the finding ultimately lacked support in that record, the proper response was to vacate and remand, not substitute the court's independent scientific judgment. (701.)

## The unresolved drug classification

Section 201(g)'s drug definition included articles intended for diagnosing, curing, mitigating, treating, or preventing disease. The FDA could not establish that intent merely by showing that higher doses were useless as nutrition for most people. In the distinct 1974 NNFA v. FDA proceeding, this circuit had invalidated a broader rule treating vitamin and mineral doses above the recommended allowance as drugs. Pharmacopoeia listing, raised as an after-the-fact justification rather than the agency's actual reason, did not save that rule; nor did nutritional uselessness establish therapeutic intent. (701–703 & nn. 6, 12.)

That earlier result did not automatically decide this appeal. The vitamin A threshold here was above 10,000 IU rather than above 8,000; the vitamin D exception expressly accommodated a nutritional use; these rules concerned two vitamins rather than the full range of vitamins and minerals; and informal rulemaking carried a different review standard. The agency's proposal mentioned widespread therapeutic promotion, and labels or evidence of almost exclusively therapeutic use might establish objective intent. But the final explanation and the record supplied to the court were incomplete. The court could not simply assume that this potentially sufficient rationale was actually the agency's supported reason. (702–703.)

## Prescription status and exact remand

If the preparations rationally qualified as drugs, the court considered the prescription restriction defensible. Section 503(b)(1)(B) allowed consideration of toxicity, potential harmful effects, methods of use, and necessary collateral precautions. The challengers conceded toxicity at sufficiently high levels. Regular intake of concentrated tablets could create dangers even if theoretically safe use remained possible. Ordinary foods and aspirin did not establish irrational discrimination: the record did not indicate comparable routine consumption patterns, and vitamins obtained from food could increase the risk from additional tablets. Note 14 distinguished Decholin, where toxicity had not been proved and practical use was safe. (703–704 & nn. 13–14.)

The court remanded to the district court for an Overton Park-type inquiry, including explanatory affidavits or testimony as necessary and scrutiny of the entire administrative record. If classification as drugs was rational, the regulations could be upheld and summary judgment should stand. If that antecedent classification was irrational, summary judgment had to be vacated. The court did not finally find therapeutic intent, invalidate the rules immediately, or grant a de novo trial. Its closing disposition was remand for proceedings consistent with those directions. (703–704.)

## Lumbard's concurrence

Lumbard was less confident about Congress's original prescription-rulemaking intent, although statutory breadth, the absence of clear contrary history, and judicial developments persuaded him that the Supreme Court would uphold the power. His central concern was the adequacy of review as courts enlarged substantive agency authority under old general grants. Arbitrary-and-capricious review should involve close examination of the information before the agency, rather than approval of anything short of complete irrationality. In substance, he thought unsupported substantive rules abused discretion if they lacked substantial evidentiary support. That was his proposed understanding of meaningful review, not the majority's replacement of § 706(2)(A) with § 706(2)(E). He believed Frankel's actual review approached this standard and concurred in the judgment. Both of his notes, including the duty to explain rules sufficiently, support that concern. (704–705 & concurrence nn. 1–2.)
