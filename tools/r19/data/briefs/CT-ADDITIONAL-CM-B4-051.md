# Kama Rippa Music, Inc. v. Schekeryk
510 F.2d 837 (2d Cir. 1975) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
The Second Circuit decided the appeal February 10, 1975, after argument January 6. Judge J. Joseph Smith wrote for Judges Oakes and Timbers without a reported separate writing. It affirmed partial summary judgment granting songwriter and performer Melanie Schekeryk declaratory and injunctive relief on two counterclaims. Rights in her submitted compositions had reverted to her because the publisher failed to pay royalties after notice and the contractual cure period. The publisher could no longer claim an interest or collect royalties on those compositions. Other claims remained below; the appeal was authorized by the district court's express Rule 54(b) findings and judgment direction. Diversity jurisdiction and the parties' choice supplied New York contract law. (510 F.2d at 837–839, 844.)

## Agreements and the unpaid royalties
Melanie's May 1968 songwriter agreement required her to deliver compositions, which Kama Rippa would publish and share royalties from. A rider made timely royalty payment essential. Failure to account and pay persisting after thirty days' written notice would terminate the agreement and return rights to her, a provision expressly capable of specific enforcement. The rider permitted suspension upon notice for delays, impossibility, or impracticability caused by listed events such as fire, earthquake, strikes, government action, war, or material shortages, followed by a general reference to similar or dissimilar reasons. (Id. at 839 & n.1.)

Melanie also recorded for affiliated Buddah Records, later moving to Paramount after friction developed. An August 1971 letter agreement settled further obligations to Kama Rippa: she would deliver twenty compositions and record at least three within two years. For each unfinished recording, $25,000 liquidated damages would be full compensation and the sole remedy. Her rights in the twenty compositions retained reference to the earlier agreements, including claims and counterclaims. What the recording undertaking ultimately required remained disputed. (Id. at 839–840.)

Kama Rippa paid February 1973 royalties late after a demand. In August it intentionally withheld royalties, claiming that Melanie had recorded but not released the required songs. She denied that release was required and gave written notice of payment default. The publisher sued August 31 for specific performance of the alleged release obligation. In mid-September it repeated its claim in state court and obtained attachment of almost all its debt to her. It then asserted that this order made paying her legally impossible. The state court later vacated the attachment on technical grounds: the $75,000 attached fell short of approximately $80,000 acknowledged debt. The publisher then sent a check, but the contractual thirty-day cure period had long expired. The district court reserved the complaint for an evidentiary hearing while granting Melanie relief on the two counterclaims. (Id. at 840 & nn.2–3.)

## Related writings did not create dependent duties
Kama Rippa argued that the letter agreement made royalties contingent on Melanie's performance and that the district court wrongly refused extrinsic evidence. The appellate court rejected its premise. The district court had read the agreements together and reconciled them. Reading related documents together does not necessarily make every obligation mutually dependent. The letter agreement expressly made liquidated damages the sole remedy for failure to record, while preserving rights and claims arising under the songwriter agreement. A possible recording breach therefore did not excuse timely royalty payment. (Id. at 841–842 & n.4.)

The publisher identified no material ambiguity justifying the offered custom-and-usage evidence. Its evidence attempted to vary the objective agreement, particularly the forfeiture rider, rather than clarify an unclear term. The rider's precedence over printed provisions and the parties' practical conduct also supported the construction. Obtaining attachment shortly before cure expired, then paying when it was lifted, suggested that the publisher itself understood payment to be independent of the recording dispute. That conduct informed construction without necessarily binding the court. No material factual dispute remained on this independence issue. The merits of Melanie's alleged recording breach were still pending. (Id. at 841–842.)

## The catchall did not swallow the negotiated protection
Note 5 addressed the one claimed ambiguity: the phrase covering dissimilar reasons at the end of the force majeure list. To give effect to the rider's purpose, the district court disregarded the phrase, a method the appellate court found supported by New York law. Alternatively, ejusdem generis could assimilate the generic language to the preceding specific events. Either approach produced the same construction. The court did not hold that every contractual catchall is meaningless; it construed this language within a rider expressly protecting timely payment and reversion. (Id. at 841 n.5.)

## Self-induced impossibility was no defense
The attachment did not excuse default because the publisher procured it. A party asserting impossibility had to show that it took virtually every action within its power to perform, rather than deliberately incapacitating itself. Recognition of a lawful attachment procedure did not imply permission to exploit it to defeat contractual duties. Note 6 suggested that acquiring jurisdiction was the procedure's ostensibly legitimate purpose; that observation did not validate its use as an excuse for unpaid royalties here. The publisher could not convert its own litigation tactic into force majeure protection. (Id. at 842–843 & n.6.)

## Equity did not require compensation or undo reversion
Kama Rippa next argued that restitution required Melanie to compensate it for development expenditures before rights returned to her. The district court characterized the order as specific performance rather than restitution. The appellate court acknowledged that this label-based answer could be unsatisfying, but found a stronger reason to reject the claim: the publisher had already received substantial sums rewarding its efforts and expenses. Note 7 reproduced the co-publishing arrangement, including recoupment of advances, deductions for agreed exploitation and production expenses, an administrative charge, and collection costs before division of net income. The claimed uncompensated expenditures lacked record support. (Id. at 843 & n.7.)

The general equitable aversion to forfeiture also did not authorize erasing a reversion right deliberately included in the bargain. Moreover, the publisher's own attachment maneuver implicated unclean hands. The court affirmed the summary judgment and contractual reversion; it did not decide the still-pending complaint, give Kama Rippa specific performance, or award the letter agreement's $25,000-per-recording remedy. (Id. at 843–844.)
