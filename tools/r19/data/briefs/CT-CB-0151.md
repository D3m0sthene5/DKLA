# Callano v. Oakwood Park Homes Corp.

91 N.J. Super. 105, 219 A.2d 332 (App. Div. 1966)

## Court, author, and source scope
The Superior Court of New Jersey, Appellate Division, decided the appeal on April 26, 1966. Judge Collester wrote the opinion for a panel consisting of Judges Goldmann, Foley, and Collester. No separate opinion is reported. The casebook excerpt appears at printed pages 151–153. The full opinion was consulted for the parties' arrangement, distinct monetary figures, stipulated procedural posture, and scope of the reversal.

## Parties and material facts
Julia and Frank Callano operated a nursery. Oakwood Park Homes Corporation was constructing a housing development. In December 1961, Oakwood contracted to sell Bruce Pendergast a lot with a house to be built on it. Before the house was completed, the Callanos contracted with Pendergast to supply and plant shrubbery. Their customer and expected source of payment was Pendergast, not Oakwood. 91 N.J. Super. at 107.

In May 1962, the Callanos delivered and planted the shrubbery. An Oakwood representative knew that planting was occurring. Pendergast did not pay the invoice of $497.95 and died shortly afterward. On July 10, Oakwood and Pendergast's estate canceled the real-estate purchase contract. Oakwood did not know that the Callanos remained unpaid. Six days later, it sold the property, with the shrubbery, to Richard and Joan Grantges. The resale price was not disclosed. Id. at 107–08.

The parties stipulated that the shrubbery enhanced the property's value by $475. That reasonable-value figure differs from the invoice amount. The litigation concerned whether Oakwood should pay the stipulated value because it retained the improvement after cancellation and resale, even though the Callanos had originally looked to someone else for payment. Id. at 109.

## Procedural history
The case was tried on an agreed stipulation of facts. The trial court entered judgment for the Callanos for $475 on a quasi-contractual theory. Oakwood appealed. The Appellate Division reversed, holding that the circumstances did not establish unjust enrichment requiring Oakwood to compensate the nursery. This was not a jury verdict finding that Oakwood had requested the landscaping or promised payment. Id. at 107–10.

## Issue
When a nursery supplies landscaping under an express contract with a prospective homebuyer, and the developer later retains and resells the improved property after the buyer's death and cancellation of the purchase agreement, is the developer obligated in restitution to pay for the landscaping? More specifically, does the conceded receipt of an enhancement establish unjust enrichment when the nursery expected payment from the buyer and had no dealings with the developer? Id. at 108–09.

## Holding
No. Receipt of a benefit and unjust retention are distinct requirements. Although Oakwood received the $475 enhancement, the Callanos had deliberately contracted with Pendergast and expected him to pay. They had no relevant direct dealing with Oakwood, made no mistake concerning their debtor, and alleged no fraud by Oakwood. In those circumstances, quasi-contract could not be used to substitute Oakwood for the party who had undertaken to pay. Id. at 108–10.

## Actual judgment
The $475 judgment against Oakwood was reversed. The court identified a contractual remedy against Pendergast's estate; it did not enter a judgment against the estate in this action or establish that the estate had sufficient assets to satisfy a claim. The opinion does not state that the Callanos collected from anyone afterward. Id. at 109–10.

## Reasoning
### Quasi-contract imposes a duty rather than discovering an agreement
The court described obligations implied by law as remedial duties grounded in equity and justice rather than actual assent. An express or implied-in-fact contract requires an agreement; a quasi-contract may exist without one. But that distinction does not mean a court may impose liability whenever it can identify a recipient of value. The legal fiction is used cautiously and requires a reason that retention without payment would be unjust. Id. at 108.

This explains why Oakwood's failure to promise payment was relevant but not independently conclusive. Restitution does not require a promise in every case. The question was whether the surrounding circumstances supplied the equitable obligation that would justify payment despite the absence of one. Id. at 108–09.

### A benefit was conceded, but the allocation of payment responsibility mattered
The parties agreed that the property was worth $475 more because of the shrubbery. The court therefore did not reject the claim because the landscaping was valueless, because its amount could not be proved, or because Oakwood failed to receive it. The failure concerned the additional requirement of injustice. Id. at 109.

In successful quasi-contract cases, the court observed, a claimant commonly expected payment from the defendant, or would have expected it had the true facts been known when the benefit was conferred. A direct relationship or a mistake may explain why an obligation should run between those particular parties. Here the Callanos knowingly selected Pendergast as their contractual customer and looked to him for remuneration. There was no mistaken belief that Oakwood had placed the order or was the debtor. Id. at 108–09.

The sequence of events did not erase that allocation. Oakwood's knowledge that shrubbery was being planted did not amount to an order for it, and the developer lacked knowledge that the invoice was unpaid when it canceled the purchase agreement with the estate. The stipulated facts did not show a plan to obtain uncompensated landscaping or to shift Pendergast's obligation onto the nursery. Id. at 107–09.

### Restitution could not simply replace the original debtor
The Callanos retained their express claim arising from Pendergast's contract. The court refused to use quasi-contract to substitute a different debtor merely because that other person ultimately enjoyed the improvement. It considered imposing liability on Oakwood inequitable under the existing circumstances. The opinion's reference to a remedy against the estate is a reason for rejecting this proposed substitution, not a holding that every unpaid subcontractor or supplier necessarily loses every restitution claim against a property owner. Id. at 109–10.

That qualification matters because the court repeatedly framed its result in terms of these facts. Different dealings, a mistaken payment, an owner's direct undertaking, or fraud could change the analysis. The present record supplied none of those features. An assertion that the estate was insolvent would likewise add a fact the opinion does not report. The casebook asks why the estate was not sued; the brief should preserve the question instead of inventing an answer. Id.; casebook 153.

### The cited fraud precedent did not fit
The Callanos relied on De Gasperi v. Valicenti, where quasi-contractual recovery had been permitted. The court distinguished that decision because the defendants there had perpetrated a fraud. No fraud by Oakwood was alleged in Callano. The precedent consequently did not establish that an innocent recipient of a contractual third party's improvement must pay merely because the supplier remained unpaid. 91 N.J. Super. at 110.

## Separate opinions
The reported decision contains no concurrence or dissent. The panel's reversal should not be presented as a divided equitable balancing decision or supplemented with policy views attributed to an unreported judge.

## Study analysis: benefit, injustice, and the correct defendant
Callano is most useful when the analysis identifies three actors rather than treating the transaction as a two-party exchange. The nursery supplied the benefit, Pendergast requested it and owed the contractual price, and Oakwood ultimately retained the enhanced property. The facts readily answer who benefited. They do not automatically answer who must pay.

The comparison with Cotnam exposes the importance of circumstances justifying a legally imposed duty. In Cotnam, an unconscious patient could not arrange necessary emergency care. In Callano, the supplier could negotiate and did negotiate with a selected customer. The comparison with Pyeatte is similarly instructive: failure of an enforceable contract may leave room for restitution, but the identity of the benefited party and the reason retention would be unjust remain essential.

The court did not create a universal rule that an express contract anywhere in a transaction bars all restitution against everyone else. Its narrower conclusion concerns a knowing contractual allocation to Pendergast, no mistaken debtor, no relevant direct dealings with Oakwood, and no fraud. Those facts should appear before the general language about substituting debtors is applied to a new problem.
