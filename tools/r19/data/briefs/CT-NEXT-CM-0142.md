# Walgreen Co. v. Sara Creek Property Co.
966 F.2d 273 (7th Cir. 1992) | Contracts | Permanent injunctions and the cost of remedies

## A promised monopoly inside the mall

Walgreen operated a pharmacy in Milwaukee's Southgate Mall from the mall's opening in 1951. Its 1971 lease ran for thirty years and six months and promised that the landlord, Sara Creek, would not lease mall space to another pharmacy or a store containing one. In 1990, the mall's largest tenant had become insolvent and threatened to close. Sara Creek proposed buying out that tenant and replacing it with Phar-Mor, a deep discount store. Phar-Mor would occupy 100,000 square feet, including a 12,000-square-foot pharmacy equal in size to Walgreen's. The entrances would be only a few hundred feet apart. Opinion ¶¶1–2 in the preserved OpenJurist reproduction.

Walgreen sued both Sara Creek and Phar-Mor in federal court under diversity jurisdiction. Following an evidentiary hearing, the district court found a breach and permanently enjoined Sara Creek from leasing the anchor premises to Phar-Mor until Walgreen's lease expired. Sara Creek's expert considered Walgreen's loss readily estimable; Walgreen's employees disputed that assertion and emphasized difficult-to-value goodwill. On appeal, Sara Creek challenged the choice of remedy. Its antitrust objection to the exclusivity clause, raised below, was not pressed, so the Seventh Circuit did not resolve it. ¶¶1,3.

## Why damages remained the starting point

Sara Creek argued that money damages ordinarily remedy contract breaches and that this arrangement might illustrate an efficient breach: Phar-Mor's occupancy could create more value than the competition would cost Walgreen. If Walgreen received that cost in damages, the parties could achieve a more valuable use of the premises without leaving Walgreen uncompensated. Sara Creek also pointed to cases measuring lost profits or diminution in leasehold value after breaches of similar promises. ¶4.

Judge Posner accepted much of that reasoning but rejected its conclusion here. Decisions awarding damages for exclusivity breaches did not establish a categorical prohibition on injunctions. Other decisions had granted them. The plaintiff still bore the burden of showing that damages were inadequate; the choice required comparing the costs and benefits of the available remedies in the particular circumstances. If the balance were even, the injunction should be withheld. Because that assessment depended on facts and judgment, appellate review asked whether the district judge exceeded the range of permissible choices, rather than whether appellate judges would have selected the same remedy independently. ¶¶5–6,10.

The court distinguished this final remedial decision from preliminary relief. In the preliminary setting, irreparable injury concerns harm that a later final judgment cannot rectify. A permanent injunction itself ordinarily constitutes the final judgment. Posner therefore treated inadequacy of damages as the relevant burden here and criticized casually using “irreparable harm” as its synonym. The case did not decide a preliminary-injunction motion. ¶6.

## An injunction can replace uncertain valuation with bargaining

Posner identified two possible benefits of an injunction. First, it lets the parties price the protected right themselves. If Sara Creek gains more from Phar-Mor than Walgreen loses, the parties can negotiate a payment for surrendering the injunction that makes both better off. Second, bargaining may reveal their actual valuations more accurately than an adversarial battle of damages experts. Enforcing the promise need not therefore prevent a valuable new use permanently: it can establish the right around which voluntary exchange occurs. ¶7.

That reasoning came with qualifications. Some injunctions demand continuing judicial supervision or harm third parties. An injunction can also create a bilateral monopoly: only Walgreen can sell its waiver, and only Sara Creek can buy the waiver needed for this transaction. The court's hypothetical assumed a $1 million loss to Walgreen and a $2 million gain to Sara Creek. Any payment between those amounts could benefit both, but each would prefer a different end of the range. Negotiations could consume resources or collapse as parties cultivated reputations for hard bargaining. Those amounts illustrated the problem; they were not findings of either party's actual gain or loss. ¶8.

Damages avoid those bargaining and supervision costs, but require litigation to estimate value and risk getting that value wrong. Neither remedy was inherently costless or invariably superior. The proper comparison included expenditures on evidence, judicial time, valuation error, and the effects of each remedy on future conduct. ¶¶9–10.

## Why the balance favored Walgreen

Walgreen's lease still had ten years to run. A damages award would require forecasting revenues and costs throughout that period, isolating Phar-Mor's competitive effect, and discounting the resulting loss to present value. Apart from discounting, those steps were deeply uncertain. Expert testimony might supply a tolerable damages estimate if an injunction were infeasible, but an approximate award in another case did not establish that damages were adequate in this one. ¶¶10–11.

Sara Creek offered evidence that Phar-Mor had caused little harm to Walgreen in other malls. Comparative evidence was a useful approach, but the comparability of those malls to Southgate was seriously disputed. The district judge could conclude that it did not resolve the forecasting problem. Sara Creek also criticized restrictions on discovery of Walgreen's extensive business data. Confidentiality and production expense were legitimate concerns, and those discovery burdens themselves illustrated costs of pursuing the damages remedy. ¶11.

The injunction, by contrast, imposed a simple negative obligation: do not lease this mall space to Phar-Mor during Walgreen's remaining term. It demanded little judicial supervision. Phar-Mor was already a party, and its prospective benefits could be reflected in what it would pay Sara Creek, which could in turn offer Walgreen a price for waiver. The court similarly considered customers' preferences indirectly reflected in Phar-Mor's willingness to pay rent. ¶12.

Bargaining remained a potential cost, but Sara Creek did not claim that excluding Phar-Mor would force the mall to close, and Phar-Mor was not the only possible anchor tenant. The court distinguished circumstances in which an injunction threatened an existing business's closure and produced a dramatically larger bargaining range. The relatively modest expected bargaining costs here did not outweigh the expense and uncertainty of valuing ten years of competitive injury. ¶13.

## Judgment and the rule left undecided

The Seventh Circuit affirmed the permanent injunction. Posner and Judge Kanne formed the majority; Senior Judge Harlington Wood, Jr., separately concurred, expressly joining the affirmance and Posner's analysis without adding another rule. There were no numbered judicial footnotes in the preserved full opinion. ¶¶14–17.

The holding sustained this fact-specific exercise of remedial discretion. It did not make every real-estate promise specifically enforceable or declare injunctions presumptively mandatory for all shopping-center exclusivity clauses. Posner illustrated the distinction with a hypothetical promise to remove ice and snow: hiring a replacement and charging the cost could make damages straightforward, while an injunction would require supervision. The court recognized a possible argument for a presumption favoring injunctions for exclusivity clauses but expressly left its strength undecided. ¶14.
