# United States v. Nova Scotia Food Products Corp.

**Course:** Legislation, Regulation & the State  
**Citation:** 568 F.2d 240 (2d Cir. 1977)  
**Court and date:** United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, December 15, 1977  
**Opinion:** Judge Gurfein for the unanimous panel of Judges Waterman, Gurfein, and District Judge Blumenfeld, sitting by designation; no separate writing appears.  
**Assigned source:** LRS October pages 100–end scan, PDF pages 100–106, printed pages 678–684, ending before editorial Notes.  
**Brief ID:** LRS-MAX-NOVASCOTIA

## Case orientation

The FDA had statutory authority to regulate processing temperatures and salt levels to protect against botulism. Its particular regulation nevertheless failed because the agency withheld the scientific material underlying the proposal and did not answer material comments about species differences, alternative controls, and commercial feasibility. The appeal arose from an injunction enforcing the rule. Accordingly, the court reversed the injunction and directed dismissal of the complaint; it did not simply remand a direct-review petition to the FDA.

## Facts and rulemaking

Nova Scotia received frozen or iced whitefish in interstate commerce and brined, smoked, and cooked it for sale. FDA inspections established that the company was not meeting the applicable time-temperature-salinity requirements. The company did not deny noncompliance. It argued that those processing parameters would prevent production of marketable whitefish. There was no substantial claim of other materially insanitary conditions at its plant. (568 F.2d at 242–243; notes 1 and 3.)

The FDA sought to prevent outgrowth and toxin formation of *Clostridium botulinum* Type E spores, which could be present naturally in fish and spread during preparation. Botulism incidents in 1960 and 1963 prompted research. Using informal notice-and-comment procedures, the Commissioner proposed regulations in October 1969 and adopted them in November 1970. Earlier industry guidelines had been advisory, rather than enforceable substantive law.

The original proposal required each fish, brined to 3.5% water-phase salt, to reach at least 180°F for thirty minutes. The final rule added an alternative of 150°F for thirty minutes with 5% water-phase salt. The National Fisheries Institute had suggested that alternative as an interim measure while researchers developed parameters for individual species; the FDA instead adopted it permanently. Ensuring that every fish met the minimum could require heating an entire batch above those temperatures. (At 243–245.)

Commenters asked for species-specific treatment. The Interior Department's Bureau of Commercial Fisheries questioned the commercial feasibility of uniform requirements and suggested lower temperatures using suitable concentrations of nitrite and salt. A separate regulation already permitted nitrite with salt for chub. Nova Scotia and its trade association warned that the proposed temperatures would destroy some products, including the commercial whitefish product. The FDA acknowledged that adequate parameters had not been established for every species, but asserted that its requirements were the safest then known and should take effect without delay. It did not meaningfully answer the alternative-preservative proposal or the feasibility objection.

## Procedural posture and claims

After inspections and warnings, the United States sued in April 1976 to enjoin noncompliant processing. The district court upheld the regulation and granted an injunction. Nova Scotia, two corporate officers, and the intervening National Fisheries Institute appealed. They argued that the rule exceeded statutory authority, rested on undisclosed scientific evidence and an inadequate administrative record, and lacked an adequate statement of basis and purpose. Note 4 also identifies interpretive-rule and enforcement objections; those were not independent grounds of the appellate disposition. (At 243–245; notes 1 and 4.)

## Questions and holdings

The court first asked whether the FDA's authority to regulate food prepared under insanitary conditions extended to processing requirements addressing organisms present before food entered the plant. It answered yes. It then asked whether the FDA's notice-and-comment procedure adequately exposed the scientific basis and addressed the major objections. It answered no, holding the rule invalid as applied to non-vacuum-packed hot-smoked whitefish. The court reversed the injunction and ordered dismissal of the enforcement complaint. (At 245–253.)

## Statutory authority: a broad but reasoned reading

The FDA relied on 21 U.S.C. § 342(a)(4), defining food as adulterated if prepared, packed, or held under insanitary conditions that might render it injurious to health, together with § 371(a)'s general rulemaking authority. The company argued that these provisions addressed dirty plant conditions and contamination introduced during processing, rather than requiring destruction of naturally present organisms.

The court recognized the textual force of that argument but read the provisions in light of the Act's public-health purpose. Inadequate processing could permit dangerous spores to survive, making preventive processing conditions relevant to whether food might become injurious. Comparable sanitation authority supported temperature requirements to eliminate organisms in pork. A narrow reading could also undermine established regulations addressing contaminated raw materials and pasteurization. The court therefore found authority fairly inferable from the statute's purpose and language; it did not endorse an agency's authority to act merely because an official considered initiative desirable. Note 10 makes that distinction explicit. (At 245–248.)

The statute provided formal rulemaking for certain enumerated provisions, including emergency permit controls for microorganisms, but not for this adulteration provision. The court did not convert the proceeding into formal rulemaking. It instead required careful observance of the minimum protections applicable to informal rulemaking. Statutory authority and valid procedure remained separate questions.

## The record: review rather than a new scientific trial

APA § 706 required review of the whole administrative record even under the arbitrary-and-capricious standard. Informal rulemaking did not require formal adjudicatory findings, but meaningful review still required access to the material the agency considered.

Here no contemporaneous record was made or certified. During discovery in the enforcement action, the FDA reconstructed its materials from files and participants' memories. Exhibit D included public comments and scientific materials that the agency said it had relied on but had not disclosed to commenters. The court did not hold that every later compilation of historical materials was automatically forbidden. Its concern was the agency's treatment and disclosure of the issues and evidence.

The company also sought to introduce new scientific evidence attacking the studies' methods, including the use of very high concentrations of spores in test fish. The district court properly excluded it. Although independent factfinding can sometimes occur in an enforcement action concerning issues that were not before the agency, these issues had fairly been presented in the rulemaking. The reviewing court therefore assessed the agency's record and reasoning instead of conducting a new trial to select the best processing parameters. (At 248–250; notes 11–14.)

## Disclosure: a meaningful opportunity to comment

The scientific research was central to the rule but had not been exposed as the material underlying the proposed standards. Without it, interested parties could not test methodology, data collection, extrapolation, or the interpretation of the studies. On these circumstances, the FDA's nondisclosure impaired the opportunity for relevant comment and could prevent consideration of relevant factors.

The court acknowledged that agencies may draw on their expertise in informal rulemaking. It nonetheless found no justification here for concealing readily available scientific research bearing on precise parameters for which the agency lacked special expertise. It also distinguished a case in which interested parties had themselves supplied the relevant material. Trade secrets and national-security interests were identified as possible exceptions to disclosure; neither justified this record's withholding. Note 15 addressed fears of an endless comment cycle: exposing the research here would have required a single opportunity to comment, not perpetual successive replies. (At 250–252.)

## Explanation: material alternatives and feasibility

APA § 553(c)'s concise general statement had to explain the major policy issues and why the FDA responded as it did. The agency did not need to discuss every fact or opinion submitted. It did need to answer objections that struck at the rule's central justification.

Two questions remained unanswered: whether different fish species required different treatment, particularly given the lower-temperature nitrite-and-salt alternative; and whether the whitefish requirements destroyed the commercial product. The record's historical discussion associated the reported whitefish botulism cases with vacuum packing, which the industry had abandoned, and reported no botulism case from Nova Scotia's product. Those circumstances made a reasoned explanation of a uniform rule particularly important. They did not establish that all processing controls were unnecessary or that no future hazard could arise. (At 250–253.)

Commercial infeasibility was not an absolute veto over public-health regulation. The court expressly recognized that overwhelming public interests could prevail despite economic harm. The agency needed to disclose whether it considered the rule feasible or consciously chose stronger protection despite infeasibility. It supplied neither explanation. The deficiency was thus the missing analysis and response, rather than a judicial finding that economic survival always outweighed safety.

## Judgment and limits

Because this was an appeal from an enforcement judgment, the court stated that it could not remand directly to the agency for further comments in that posture. It held the regulation invalid as it affected non-vacuum-packed hot-smoked whitefish, reversed the injunction, and directed dismissal of the complaint. The FDA retained underlying statutory authority and could reconsider the standards through lawful procedures. The decision did not invalidate every sanitation regulation or exempt all smoked fish from future regulation. (At 253.)

## Exam use

Analyze authority, disclosure, response to comments, record scope, and remedy separately. A proposed rule can be within an agency's statutory mandate yet fail because meaningful comment and an intelligible explanation were missing. An inadequate agency record also does not automatically entitle litigants to retry scientific issues in court. Pay attention to procedural posture when choosing the remedy: this court dismissed an enforcement complaint rather than issuing the usual direct-review remand.
