# Frostifresh Corp. v. Reynoso
54 Misc. 2d 119, 281 N.Y.S.2d 964 (App. Term 1967); trial judgment, 52 Misc. 2d 26, 274 N.Y.S.2d 757 (Nassau Dist. Ct. 1966) | Contracts

## Distinct stages and appellate result
The Supreme Court, Appellate Term, Second Department, decided the appeal May 24, 1967. Its per curiam opinion unanimously reversed without costs and ordered a new trial limited to assessing the seller's damages and entering judgment. Justices Schwartzwald, Fanelli, and Beckinella concurred. It accepted that the evidence clearly warranted unconscionability but changed the remedy. The earlier November 15, 1966 trial judgment was Judge Francis J. Donovan's decision in Nassau County District Court. Both actual reports are short, contain no judicial footnotes or separate opinions, and are preserved as separate stages in this account. (54 Misc. 2d at 119–120; 52 Misc. 2d at 26–28.)

## Transaction and trial findings
Frostifresh sued for $1,364.10, including the asserted balance on a refrigerator-freezer installment purchase and claimed attorney fees and a late charge. The agreement set a $900 cash price plus a $245.88 credit charge, totaling $1,145.88. The buyers had paid only $32. The seller admitted its own cost for the appliance was $348; that was not the consumers' contractual purchase price. (52 Misc. 2d at 26–27.)

Negotiations occurred in Spanish with a Spanish-speaking salesman. Reynoso told him that only one week remained on his job and he could not afford the appliance. The salesman said referral bonuses or commissions of $25 on sales to friends and neighbors would make the appliance cost them nothing. The trial court described that conduct as distracting and deluding the buyers. The written installment form was entirely English and was neither translated nor explained. The court found the buyers disadvantaged in their understanding of the commercial setting and the form's terms. These are findings from a completed trial, rather than allegations presumed true on a motion to dismiss. (Id. at 27.)

## Unconscionability and opportunity for evidence
Fraud was not pleaded as a defense and the trial court therefore treated that defense as unavailable. Unconscionability remained independently available under UCC § 2-302. When the issue emerged during trial, the court adjourned proceedings to allow both parties a reasonable opportunity to present evidence about the contract's commercial setting, purpose, and effect. It did not simply announce an unexpected price reduction without allowing that statutory evidentiary opportunity. (Id. at 27.)

The court found the price and terms shocking to the conscience and the charge oppressive in conjunction with the buyers' limited understanding and the foreign-language form. Invoking the Code's protection against oppression and unfair surprise, and its comment's discussion of Campbell Soup, it refused to enforce the written price and credit provisions. It thus used § 2-302 to police the bargain even though the separate fraud defense was procedurally unavailable. (Id. at 27–28.)

## Trial remedy and appellate correction
Because the buyers retained the appliance, the trial court required reimbursement of the seller's admitted $348 cost. It allowed nothing for sales commissions, attorney fees, service charges, or other overhead. Deducting the $32 already paid produced a $316 judgment against both buyers, with interest from December 26, 1964. The report's later reference to a $245.85 service charge differs by three cents from its earlier $245.88 figure; the latter appears in its initial account and yields the stated contractual total. The brief does not silently make that inconsistency a different transaction. (Id. at 27–28.)

The appellate court left the unconscionability assessment intact but rejected reimbursement limited to acquisition cost. The seller should receive net cost plus a reasonable profit, necessarily incurred trucking and service charges, and reasonable finance charges. It did not validate the original $900 price, award the claimed charges in full, or specify the new dollar judgment. The limited new trial was to determine the permissible damages under that formula. (54 Misc. 2d at 120.)

## Assigned account and limits
The assigned remedies note at printed 689 calls $348 the purchase price. The original trial report identifies it as the seller's cost, while the buyers' cash price was $900. The assigned description that the trial court was directed to give judgment under the expanded formula also omits the appellate requirement for a new damages trial. Its contrasting scholarly comments about deterrence and the social role of consumer credit are editorial reactions, not separate judicial opinions or additional findings.

This paired-stage account concerns the effect of unconscionability on an already supplied and retained good: avoiding oppressive terms did not entitle the buyers to keep it free, and the appeal allowed reasonable seller compensation without restoring the original bargain.
