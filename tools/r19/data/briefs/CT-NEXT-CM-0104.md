# Stoll v. Chong Lor Xiong
2010 OK CIV APP 110, 241 P.3d 301 (Okla. Civ. App. 2010) | Contracts

## Land purchase and disputed litter provision

Ronald Stoll sold sixty acres in Delaware County, Oklahoma, to Chong Lor Xiong and Mee Yang, a married couple who operated six poultry houses. The January 1, 2005 agreement described a $2,000-per-acre price and another $10,000 for an access road. Its words said “One Hundred Twenty Thousand Dollars,” while its figure said $130,000; the opinion reproduces that inconsistency. A separate provision required the buyers to construct a concrete-floor litter shed at least forty-three by eighty feet, deposit poultry litter after growing cycles, and give Stoll all rights to that litter for thirty years. Stoll had to empty the shed between cycles. The dispute concerned this provision’s additional economic burden, rather than merely whether $2,000 per acre was a high land price. ¶¶6, 12.

The record showed severe language and educational limitations. Xiong had three years of schooling in Laos, spent three years in a Thai refugee camp, and later attended adult school in Minnesota. He understood some spoken English but could read only a few English words. Yang, a Hmong immigrant from Laos, had no schooling there and only six months of adult education in the United States. The relative who interpreted for her also needed an interpreter at her own deposition and did not understand the litter term. Stoll argued that the buyers had signed a preliminary version in November 2004 and had time to obtain an explanation before January. The actual opinion says Stoll did not execute that preliminary version; the assigned passage alters the subject in brackets to “they.” Neither source establishes that the buyers knowingly negotiated the thirty-year economic burden. ¶¶4–5, 11–12 & nn.1, 5, 8.

After an initial cycle, the buyers paid $900 to remove the upper layer of bedding. Yang asked Stoll to pay half that expense, and he refused. She testified that she did not understand when or how they had transferred the litter rights. In February 2009, Xiong traded litter from a complete cleanout for replacement shavings. Stoll alleged that the buyers were selling litter to others and that their shed was empty on March 24; he sued the next day. He sought specific performance, a temporary injunction against third-party sales, and immediate possession of the litter. His deposition evidence came in part from related litigation against Xiong’s sister and brother-in-law, which the opinion warns could confuse the factual accounts. ¶¶7–9 & nn.3–4.

## Motions and competing positions

The buyers asserted unconscionability, fraud, merger into a deed that omitted the litter provision, impossibility based on alleged statutory violations, trespass, and damage to the shed. They requested reformation or invalidation. Those pleaded defenses and counterclaims should not be treated as findings that every alleged wrong occurred. Both sides moved for summary judgment. Stoll relied on the signed writing and characterized the dispute as buyer’s remorse; the buyers argued that the litter requirement was uninformed, oppressive, and unsupported by any additional return to them. The trial court held the litter clause unconscionable, granted their motion, denied his, and entered judgment against his petition. ¶¶10–13.

On appeal, Stoll contended that the court should have considered only traditional grounds such as fraud, duress, undue influence, mistake, or illegality, rather than judging fairness independently. Review was de novo, with inferences from the submitted evidence drawn favorably to Stoll as the unsuccessful opponent of summary judgment. The appellate court nevertheless found no material factual dispute requiring a trial on the enforceability question. ¶¶2–3, 14–15.

## Unconscionability and the economic evidence

Oklahoma precedent assessed unconscionability at formation against the commercial setting and asked whether onerous inequality, deception, or oppression produced terms so one-sided as to oppress or unfairly surprise a party. It linked the absence of meaningful choice to terms unreasonably favorable to the other side. Unconscionability was a legal issue for the court. UCC § 2-302 directly addresses sales of goods, but the court used its principle by analogy in this land transaction, consistent with Restatement § 208. Both parties had supplied evidence of the transaction’s setting, satisfying the opportunity to present commercial evidence described in the statute reproduced in footnote 9. ¶¶16–17 & n.9.

Stoll acknowledged that he had devised the provision. His testimony supplied a yield of about twenty tons per house from each de-caking, a pre-2008 price of up to $12 per ton, and the buyers’ five annual flocks in six houses. Those figures produced approximately $7,200 a year and $216,000 over thirty years from de-caking alone; using the $25 figure Yang attributed to Stoll produced approximately $15,000 annually. Complete cleanouts would add more. These were estimates derived from testimony, not a damages award or a guarantee of actual future market revenues. The court saw the clause as making the buyers’ independently funded business generate another major stream of payment for Stoll while they paid the costs of producing the asset. ¶18.

The court analogized to deed-cancellation cases in which grossly inadequate consideration and inability to understand the transaction could support a presumption of fraud. Here the direction of the inequality was reversed: the buyers’ real burden far exceeded the stated price. Ordinary price inadequacy alone would not suffice, but the combination of this extreme hidden burden and the buyers’ inability to understand it shocked the conscience. Stoll’s claim that cleared and leveled land deserved a premium over nearby land did not justify the separate thirty-year litter burden. ¶¶19–20. The opinion contains an apparent inconsistency in its per-acre extrapolation; the explanation uses its stated annual and thirty-year estimates without repeating that calculation.

## Disposition and source scope

Judge Hetherington’s September 17, 2010 opinion, joined by Presiding Judge Buettner and Judge Hansen, affirmed the judgment for the buyers on Stoll’s petition. The holding invalidated the chicken-litter paragraph supporting his enforcement claim; it did not award the buyers $216,000, cancel their entire land purchase, or separately resolve every counterclaim. ¶¶1, 20. The assigned excerpt is C&M 701–1050, PDF pages 35–41, printed pages 692–698. The complete six-page actual report preserved by counsel was read, including paragraphs 1–20 and all nine footnotes. Those notes identify a missing page in the produced agreement, distinctions from the companion case, the joint motion hearing, and the statutory framework. No separate judicial writing appears.
