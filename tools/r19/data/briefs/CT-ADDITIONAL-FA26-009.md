# Condominium associations v. SCI du 12, place des Terreaux
Cour de cassation, Third Civil Chamber, January 25, 1995, pourvoi no. 92-19.600; Bull. civ. 1995, III, no. 29, p. 17 | Contracts

## Decision and parties

The Third Civil Chamber quashed a judgment denying two condominium associations restoration of a passage altered to create a shopping gallery. The decision addressed the passage’s legal status, the body competent to authorize the work, and whether absence of injury defeated the request to undo a contractual violation. Beauvois presided, Capoulade reported, and Sodini was the avocat général. The published text contains no numerical vote, separate writing, or judicial footnote. Its actual setting is condominium regulation, rather than the leased-premises example used in the assignment. (Official identifying material; each ground; disposition.)

## Property arrangement, work, and suit

The condominium regulations gave the owners of ground-floor lots bordering a passage an exclusive and particular right to enjoy its ground surface. The passage lay inside a group of buildings, depended on each of them, and was open to public circulation. SCI du 12, place des Terreaux owned the relevant lots and carried out a shopping-gallery project affecting the passage. The associations for two of the buildings sought restoration of the premises to their earlier state. The Court of Appeal of Lyon rejected that demand on June 4, 1992. The Supreme Court’s short report does not provide a project budget, a detailed engineering description, or a damages amount. (First ground, factual “Attendu.”)

## First ground: exclusive enjoyment and private property

Under article 2 of the July 10, 1965 law, private portions of buildings and land are those reserved for the exclusive use of a particular co-owner. The Court of Appeal treated each building’s part of the passage as private property because the relevant lot owner had exclusive enjoyment. The Court of Cassation held that this did not follow from the regulations before it. They had created an exclusive enjoyment right attached directly to the premises bordering the passage, which could neither be sold independently of those premises nor rented to a third party. Treating that arrangement as establishing a private portion violated article 2. (First ground, statutory visa and all following paragraphs.)

The distinction mattered because owning a lot with a special right to use common property did not by itself place the passage outside the rules governing common areas. The lower court could not dispose of the associations’ objection merely by equating that right with ownership of a private portion.

## Second ground, first three branches: authorization

Articles 25 and 30(4) of the 1965 law required the votes of a majority of all co-owners for permission to carry out work, at the interested owners’ expense, affecting common areas or the building’s external appearance and consistent with its destination. If the general assembly refused permission, the tribunal de grande instance could authorize qualifying improvements on conditions it fixed. The Court of Appeal had said the associations wrongly withheld permission because their conseils syndicaux had agreed to endorse both the completed works and breaches of the regulations. But it also acknowledged that the general assemblies had not given that agreement. Relying on approval from a different condominium body violated the statutory rules. (Second ground, first three branches, visa and all reasoning.)

This was not a ruling that improvements could never be approved or judicially authorized. It rejected the specific substitution of council endorsement for the legally required authorization. The published judgment does not determine that a proper general assembly subsequently voted to ratify the works.

## Second ground, fourth branch: restoration without proof of harm

The Court of Appeal also denied restoration because the works had improved the building and the associations had shown no injury. Under former Civil Code article 1143, a creditor of a contractual obligation could request destruction of what had been done contrary to the commitment. The absence of demonstrated prejudice therefore did not support rejection of this restoration claim. The court distinguished enforcing the obligation by undoing the offending work from awarding compensation measured by an established loss. (Second ground, fourth branch, visa and two reasoning paragraphs.)

The holding does not quantify injury or award damages. Nor does the short judgment undertake a cost-benefit comparison of each component of the gallery. The lower court’s claimed benefit to the building did not excuse compliance with the obligation in the historical legal framework applied.

## Exact disposition and assignment

The court quashed and annulled the Lyon judgment in all its provisions, restored the parties to the position before it, and remanded to the Court of Appeal of Dijon. It did not itself specify the physical restoration measures, fix an astreinte, or enter a final damages award. (“PAR CES MOTIFS.”)

The assignment cites this January 25, 1995 decision as Bulletin III no. 29 in footnote 48 when explaining enforcement of negative promises. Its surrounding illustration describes leased premises modified by a tenant and says improvement did not defeat restoration. The no-prejudice principle is supported by the actual judgment, but the lease, tenant, and landlord are not its parties or factual arrangement. Its paragraph also cites several other authorities; those are not silently combined into this decision. (FA26 physical pages 117–118, printed 202–203, complete paragraph across the break and footnote 48.)
