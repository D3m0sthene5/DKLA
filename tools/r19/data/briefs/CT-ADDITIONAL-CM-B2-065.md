# Hand v. Dayton-Hudson Corporation
775 F.2d 757 (6th Cir. 1985) | Contracts

## Holding and alignment
The Sixth Circuit affirmed summary judgment against a former corporate attorney who secretly rewrote an employment release to reserve the very claims his employer intended to settle. His undisputed conduct constituted fraud under Michigan law, the employer's induced failure to read did not prevent relief, and the majority upheld reformation to the employer's understanding despite Hand's deliberate lack of assent to that understanding. Contie wrote the October 28, 1985 opinion on a panel with Chief Judge Lively and Wellford. All three agreed on fraud and the result; Wellford separately questioned the reformation reasoning but concurred in affirmance. (757–762.)

## Termination and substituted writing
John Hand worked for Dayton-Hudson as an attorney from 1967 until February 1982, when he lost his job allegedly through corporate restructuring. The employer offered $38,000 for a general release of claims. Hand initially refused, maintaining that his existing employment contract already entitled him to that amount. The employer nevertheless gave him a release embodying its offer and required acceptance or rejection by March 20. On March 19 Hand said he was ready to sign and met employer representative Harms. (758.)

Hand brought a replacement release reserving age-discrimination and contract claims. He had retyped it on the same type and model of machine, retained identical paragraph structure, headings, other wording, and punctuation, and attached the benefit outline as it appeared on the original. The documents looked superficially identical. Both parties signed the substituted writing and the employer paid the settlement amount. The majority treated the historical facts as undisputed, not merely accusations awaiting a trial. (758–759, 761–762.)

## Suit and summary judgment
On December 7, 1983, Hand sued in diversity for breach of his employment contract and age discrimination. Dayton-Hudson asserted fraudulent procurement of its representative's signature and requested reformation to its original offer. After a hearing, the district court found fraud on summary judgment, reformed the document, and entered judgment because the reformed release barred Hand's claims. On appeal Hand contested both the fraud finding without trial and the availability of reformation absent mutual mistake. Review required viewing evidence favorably to him and identifying any genuinely disputed material fact. (758–759.)

## Fraud by suppression and induced failure to read
Michigan fraud generally required a material false representation, knowledge or reckless disregard of falsity, intended action, actual reliance, and injury. Suppression could also constitute fraud where a legal or equitable disclosure duty arose from the transaction's circumstances. Hand's affidavit admitted deliberately preparing the release to turn the tables on the employer. His elaborate replication of the original and silence about the changes permitted only the conclusion that he intended the employer to believe it was signing its original document. The court unanimously sustained the fraud finding. (759.)

A signer ordinarily bears responsibility for an unread agreement, but the rule does not protect a person whose trick or artifice caused the failure to read. Harms's noninspection was induced by Hand's deliberate imitation; it therefore did not bar the employer from challenging the writing. This is a circumstance-specific exception, rather than a general entitlement to ignore a contractual document. (759–760.)

The district court recognized a genuine factual dispute over whether Hand already possessed a right to $38,000. The appellate majority nevertheless deemed it immaterial once fraud allowed reformation. His subjective belief that the altered writing better protected his rights did not defeat a remedy directed at his knowing deception of the employer. The court did not adjudicate the underlying entitlement dispute on its merits. (760.)

## Unilateral mistake induced by fraud
Michigan usually required mutual mistake for reformation because changing a contract to repair only one party's mistake could impose terms never agreed on by the other. Its recognized exception permitted relief where one side's mistake coincided with fraud or inequitable conduct on the other. Knowledge that the writing failed to reflect the innocent party's intent required clear and convincing proof. Restatement (Second) section 166 supported this approach; the majority acknowledged that Michigan courts had not relied on that provision but had cited the parallel First Restatement section 505. Woolner's denial of relief rested on missing proof, rather than rejection of the standard. (760–761 & n. 1.)

Hand argued that the exception still required a prior meeting of minds and that he never intended to surrender his age and contract claims. The majority rejected that limitation. Holbeck illustrated reformation where a promisor deliberately used narrow words to exploit the other side's broader understanding of promised tax payments. The remedy prevented the knowing deceiver from invoking his own private nonagreement. Here Hand knew the employer's intent, copied its release, and meant it to think he accepted the offer; he could not recast the secretly altered writing as an honestly communicated counteroffer. Reformation to Dayton-Hudson's understanding was therefore proper. (761.)

Majority note 2 rejected a requirement of an additional finding of unjust enrichment or inequity beyond the relevant fraud-and-mistake circumstances. Even previously existing rights could be surrendered through negotiation. Thus the disputed preexisting benefits did not preserve the claims excluded by the reformed release. (761 & n. 2.)

## Wellford's reservation
Wellford fully agreed that deliberate deception allowed the employer to challenge the release and could support setting it aside and recouping the $38,000. He doubted whether Michigan law permitted imposing the employer's version through reformation, especially because the disputed preexisting entitlement had never been determined. His sole note emphasized that absence of a determinative finding. (761–762 & concurrence n. 1.)

He nonetheless declined to dissent. Hand had acted unconscionably as an attorney toward a former client entitled to rely on him, and had never offered to return the money if reformation occurred. Wellford considered affirmance just and did not want the misconduct to afford another opportunity to litigate age discrimination. His concurrence did not order repayment or establish that an actual tender would necessarily have revived the claims. Those possibilities explain the assigned question but are not the judgment. (762.)

## Exact result and assigned scope
The court affirmed the district court's judgment: the release remained reformed to cover the asserted claims, and summary judgment barred the suit. This was an actual judicial finding of fraud on undisputed evidence, followed by equitable reformation, rather than merely permission to pursue fraud at trial. No new damages or repayment award was made on appeal. (761–762.)
