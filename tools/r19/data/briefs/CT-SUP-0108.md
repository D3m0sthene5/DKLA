# Klein v. PepsiCo, Inc.
845 F.2d 76 (4th Cir. 1988) | Contracts

## Court, decision, and assigned scope
The Fourth Circuit decided the appeal on April 25, 1988. Judge Ervin wrote for a panel including Judge Sprouse and Chief District Judge Britt, sitting by designation. The published decision contains no separate opinion. It addresses both contract formation and the remedy of specific performance. The FA26 Contracts Supplement prints the complete opinion and its single footnote at PDF pages 108-112. The independent Justia reproduction was read through the final disposition and footnote. Locators below use the assigned PDF pages, with reporter markers where they are expressly visible.

## The proposed aircraft sale
Eugene Klein wanted a used Gulfstream G-II corporate jet. He engaged Universal Jet Sales, Inc. (UJS), whose president, Patrick Janas, located PepsiCo's aircraft. UJS was negotiating to purchase the jet from PepsiCo and resell it to Klein; those were related but distinct transactions. Klein's pilot and mechanic inspected the aircraft in New York, and Klein personally inspected it in Arkansas on March 29, 1986. PepsiCo vice president Rashid and Janas attended. Klein gave Janas a $200,000 deposit and authorized a $4.4 million offer. (Assigned PDF page 109; 845 F.2d at 78.)

Janas transmitted that offer on March 31 by telex to PepsiCo's broker, Welsch. The offer referred to a satisfactory factory inspection and a definitive contract. PepsiCo counteroffered at $4.7 million; negotiations reached $4.6 million, which Janas accepted by telex on April 3. UJS planned to resell the aircraft to Klein for $4.75 million. Janas's confirming message referred to a $100,000 down payment and requested the proper name of the corporate seller. The different deposit and purchase-price figures belong to different stages and participants; they should not be collapsed into one payment or one direct PepsiCo-Klein contract. (Assigned PDF page 109.)

The parties then acted toward completing the sale. Janas circulated agreements for both transactions and sent a bill of sale to PepsiCo. PepsiCo executed the bill of sale and sent it to the escrow agent. Its counsel described the proposed agreement as acceptable apart from the delivery date. The aircraft went to Savannah on April 7 for inspection, with the buyer's mechanic and PepsiCo's maintenance representative present. Most problems were addressed, but cosmetic work and cracked turbine blades remained. An examination found eight to eleven cracks in the right engine's blades, with estimated repairs costing $25,000-$28,000. The district court found that PepsiCo agreed to pay for those repairs. (Assigned PDF pages 109-111.)

## Withdrawal and the dispute
The plane returned to New York on April 9. Despite Rashid's wish to ground it, it was used that evening to retrieve PepsiCo chairman Donald Kendall from Dulles airport. On April 10, Kendall asked Rashid to remove the aircraft from the market. The withdrawal reached Janas, who told Klein on April 11, the scheduled closing date, that PepsiCo would not tender the plane. On April 14, Klein demanded delivery from UJS, and UJS demanded delivery from PepsiCo while expressing satisfaction with the inspection. PepsiCo replied that negotiations had not resulted in an agreement and that Klein had not been prepared to proceed. (Assigned PDF pages 109-110.)

The chairman's decision explains why PepsiCo wanted to keep the jet. It did not itself establish whether PepsiCo had already become contractually bound. That depended on the earlier messages, the parties' conduct, and the findings made in the ensuing litigation.

## Procedure and standards of review
After extensive factual findings, the district court concluded that UJS and PepsiCo had formed a contract for the $4.6 million sale and ordered specific performance. The appellate caption reflects appeals involving PepsiCo, UJS, and Klein. The reported appellate analysis concentrates on PepsiCo's challenge to formation and the order requiring the aircraft's transfer. (Assigned PDF pages 108-111.)

The Fourth Circuit distinguished its two review standards. It reviewed factual findings for clear error, giving particularly strong weight to consistent findings grounded in credibility assessments and corroborated by other evidence. It reviewed the grant of specific performance for abuse of discretion. Thus, affirming the district court's formation findings did not require affirming its equitable remedy. (Assigned PDF page 110.)

## Formation issue and holding
PepsiCo argued that the parties intended not to be bound until a final written agreement was signed. Because PepsiCo had not signed the circulated sales agreement, it claimed that negotiations could still be abandoned. The district court instead found a contract from the April 3 confirming telex and the parties' subsequent conduct. The Fourth Circuit upheld that finding. (Assigned PDF pages 110-111; 845 F.2d at 79.)

The evidence went well beyond an unresolved price discussion. PepsiCo received the required $100,000 down payment, raised no objection to the confirming telex, communicated that the memorializing agreement was acceptable, executed and deposited a bill of sale, sent the plane for the agreed inspection, and made admissions about accepting the purchase offer. Those acts supported the finding that a later writing was intended to memorialize a transaction already agreed upon rather than serve as an indispensable condition to its existence. (Assigned PDF pages 110-111.)

This was an application to a particular record, not a holding that an unsigned document can never prevent formation. The court acknowledged the general possibility of withdrawing from negotiations, but PepsiCo failed to demonstrate clear error in the findings that these negotiations had already produced a contract. Disagreeing with the trial judge's characterization was not enough to establish a firm and definite conviction of mistake. (Assigned PDF page 111.)

## The inspection condition
PepsiCo separately argued that the satisfactory-inspection condition remained unmet because the buyers would not take the aircraft as it was. The district court found that PepsiCo's agreement to make the repairs satisfied the condition. Its alternative explanation was that refusal to tender excused the condition. The Fourth Circuit relied on the first ground: the supported finding that PepsiCo had agreed to perform the repairs. It did not need to announce a broader ruling on every consequence of prevention or excuse. (Assigned PDF page 111.)

The opinion's footnote also addressed PepsiCo's assertions that Klein could not sue because the underlying contract failed the statute of frauds, Klein was not an intended beneficiary, or the Klein-UJS contract had been rescinded. The panel affirmed the rejection of those arguments on the district court's reasoning. It did not provide an extensive independent account of that reasoning, so the footnote should not be expanded into newly invented doctrinal tests. (Assigned PDF page 112 n.1.)

## Specific performance issue and governing rule
Virginia's version of UCC section 2-716 permitted specific performance for unique goods or other proper circumstances. But adopting that provision had not displaced the equitable principle that specific performance is inappropriate where money damages are available and adequate. The district court considered this jet unique and treated Klein's inability to cover as another proper circumstance. The Fourth Circuit concluded that the record did not support either ground. (Assigned PDF pages 111-112; 845 F.2d at 80.)

The court did not deny that particular aircraft have individual characteristics. It asked whether those characteristics and the available market made forced delivery necessary in this transaction. Klein's expert described twenty-one G-II aircraft on the market, three roughly comparable. Klein's chief pilot acknowledged that other G-IIs could be purchased. UJS acquired two such aircraft and offered them to Klein after the transaction failed; Klein bid on two other G-IIs himself. Those facts undermined the supposed inability to obtain a comparable replacement. (Assigned PDF page 112.)

Klein explained that he did not buy another G-II because prices were rising and instead decided to purchase a G-III. Increased replacement expense alone did not establish entitlement to specific performance. It could be addressed through damages. The district judge had repeatedly said that damages would make Klein whole, and Klein had described a resale-for-profit objective. On this record, the claimed loss was adequately compensable without compelling PepsiCo to surrender the particular plane. (Assigned PDF pages 111-112.)

## Judgment and practical consequence
The Fourth Circuit affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded. The determination that a contract existed remained in place. The specific-performance order was reversed, and the case was returned for a trial on damages. The panel did not excuse PepsiCo's breach, award Klein a specified amount, or determine that he would ultimately recover the anticipated resale margin. (Assigned PDF page 112.)

Klein illustrates why formation, breach, and remedy must be analyzed separately. A seller may lose the argument that no contract existed yet still defeat an order compelling delivery. For a purchaser seeking specific performance, a distinctive asset or a higher replacement price is not enough by itself: the record must support why ordinary monetary compensation is inadequate under the applicable equitable standard.
