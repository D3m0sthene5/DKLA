# Ross v. Johnson and Dowson

**Court of King’s Bench, February 4, 1772 — 5 Burr. 2825; assigned parallel citation 98 Eng. Rep. 483.** Civil Procedure. Assigned: CivPro physical page 304, Bushel Note 1, printed 572–573. Pinpoints below use the running printed pages 2825–2827 of the original Burrow volume V, third edition, 1812; bracketed marginal page references are distinguished from those running pages.

A wharfinger’s inability to return goods that were lost or stolen was not, without evidence of an injurious conversion, enough to maintain trover. The appropriate form was an action on the case. The court therefore allowed Ross’s nonsuit to stand, despite Lord Mansfield’s stated dislike of nonsuits based on objections unrelated to the merits. The decision did not award the damages that the parties’ submitted case had conditionally provided, nor did it decide that the defendants were free of all possible liability. (2825–2827.)

## Facts and procedure

Hugh Ross owned goods that a ship’s captain delivered to John Johnson and William Dowson as wharfingers, for Ross’s use and account. The goods were then stolen or lost from the defendants’ possession. Before bringing suit, Ross demanded their return and tendered the wharfage charges. The defendants did not deliver the goods. These facts were included in the case submitted for the court’s opinion; the report does not resolve whether theft or some other loss actually occurred. (2825.)

Ross chose an action of trover, the historical action based on conversion of personal property. The defendants pleaded not guilty. At the Guildhall sittings after Michaelmas Term 1771, Lord Mansfield nonsuited Ross subject to the full court’s determination of whether that form of action would lie on the submitted facts. If the court answered yes, the nonsuit was to be set aside and a verdict entered for Ross for £92 damages and forty shillings costs. Those amounts were conditional terms of the submitted case, not an award the court ultimately made. (2825–2826.)

## The parties’ positions

Mr. Mansfield, arguing for Ross, maintained that ownership and conversion sufficed in trover and that an actual affirmative conversion need not be directly proved. Demand followed by non-delivery was evidence of conversion and should be sufficient unless the defendants offered a lawful excuse. In his view, loss or theft did not excuse a wharfinger who received goods for hire: the wharfingers remained obligated to deliver the goods, and the owner could not know what had become of them. He acknowledged that a lawful reason for non-delivery could defeat the conversion inference but disputed that the defendants had established such a reason here. This advocate’s argument should be distinguished from Lord Mansfield’s judicial conclusion. (2826.)

Mr. Walker answered that withholding was not itself using another person’s property as one’s own. Pawnees and carriers with liens illustrated that demand and non-delivery did not necessarily establish conversion. Mere negligence in losing goods, without an actual wrong amounting to conversion, could not support trover. Walker expressly did not undertake to prove that Ross lacked every possible action. His narrower position was that this particular action would not lie. (2826.)

## Holding and reasoning

Lord Mansfield disapproved of nonsuits founded on objections unrelated to a cause’s merits, but he considered principles and authorities controlling here: the proper action was case rather than trover. He assimilated the wharfinger’s position to that of a common carrier because both received goods upon a contract. The required feature for trover was an injurious conversion, not simply a failure to perform the obligation to return the goods. The defendants could not deliver these lost or stolen goods; delivery was no longer within their power. Their omission was consequently different from withholding goods they had the ability to return. (2826–2827.)

Justice Aston agreed that this bare omission supplied no evidence of conversion and that the clear remedy was case. He cited Owen v. Lewyn for Hale’s statement that general trover would not lie against a carrier who lost goods committed to him. Justices Willes and Ashhurst concurred with Mansfield and Aston. The report therefore records agreement by all four named judges, including Aston’s separately reported explanation, without a dissent. (2827.)

The reasoning concerns the legal significance of these facts for conversion. It should not be expanded into a factual finding that the defendants intentionally appropriated the goods, a general immunity for negligent bailees, or a judgment awarding damages in case. Conversely, the court did not require every plaintiff to prove conversion through direct evidence: the parties debated what demand and non-delivery could establish, and the court concluded that an inability to return lost goods did not establish the necessary conversion here. (2826–2827.)

## Disposition and assigned significance

The court ordered that **the nonsuit should stand**. It did not enter the conditional £92 verdict or the forty-shilling costs provision. Its recognition of case as the appropriate remedy explains the form-of-action distinction; it is not a reported judgment in a second action. (2827.)

The textbook’s Bushel Note 1 accurately reproduces the central distinction between injurious conversion and bare omission, applying it to goods entrusted to wharfingers and then lost or stolen. Reading the whole report adds the demand, tender, conditional verdict, competing arguments, and the other judges’ concurrence. The complete report and both marginal editorial reference groups were read against all three original judicial pages; the title was separately verified. The original English Reports parallel reprint remains uncollated. This is a historical account, with no certification of present-day conversion law.
