# La Buy v. Howes Leather Co., Inc.

**Course:** Civil Procedure
**ID:** CP-130
**Review:** Complete opinion and assigned excerpt independently compared; corrected legacy pinpoint verified.

## Citation

La Buy v. Howes Leather Co., Inc., 352 U.S. 249 (1957). Supreme Court of the United States. Argued October 17–18, 1956; decided January 14, 1957. Justice Clark delivered the Court’s opinion. Justice Brennan dissented, joined by Justices Frankfurter, Burton, and Harlan. Assigned excerpt: Civil Procedure casebook pp. 1231–1235, beginning at repository PDF p. 622. Full opinion: 352 U.S. 249–269, including all six Court footnotes and all sixteen dissent footnotes. The Court affirmed by a 5–4 vote.

## Parties

Judge La Buy, a federal district judge in the Northern District of Illinois, was the Supreme Court petitioner because the Seventh Circuit had directed him by mandamus to vacate orders referring two antitrust actions to a master. Howes Leather and the other respondents were parties seeking relief from those references. The judge’s position in the caption reflects the extraordinary-writ procedure; he was not a defendant accused of participating in the underlying antitrust conduct. All parties to the underlying cases objected to the references. 352 U.S. at 250–254.

The underlying litigation involved independent retail shoe-repair businesses and wholesalers asserting antitrust claims against manufacturers, wholesalers, mail-order businesses, and chain operators. The claims alleged monopolization, price fixing, and discriminatory pricing of shoe-repair supplies. The plaintiffs sought injunctions, treble damages, and accountings. Neither antitrust liability nor the appropriate relief had been decided when the judge referred the cases. Id. at 251–252, 256.

## Material Facts

Both actions began in 1950. One, Rohlfing, involved eighty-seven plaintiffs and six remaining defendants when mandamus was sought; the other, Shaffer, involved six plaintiffs and six defendants at that stage. Extensive preliminary proceedings had occupied the district judge for years. The record included amended pleadings, dismissals of parties, discovery disputes, numerous depositions, hearings, and rulings on requests for summary judgment. The judge consequently had substantial familiarity with the factual and legal issues before trial. Id. at 251–253 & nn.2–3.

When the cases were called to reset trial in February 1955, counsel estimated that one trial would require six weeks. Judge La Buy asked whether the parties would agree to a master, received no agreement, and the next day ordered references on his own initiative. The orders assigned the master to take evidence and report findings of fact and conclusions of law. They covered the general issues, including conspiracy, liability, damages, and injunctive relief, rather than a discrete accounting after liability had been established. Id. at 253–256.

The judge cited an extremely congested calendar, the complexity of the cases, and the expected length of trial. The parties deposited approximately $8,000 as security for costs but objected and moved to vacate the orders. Their compliance with the cost requirement did not establish consent. The then-governing Rule 53(b) made references exceptional; for nonjury actions other than matters of account, it required an exceptional condition. Id. at 252 n.4, 253–254.

## Procedural History

After Judge La Buy refused to vacate the references, the parties petitioned the Seventh Circuit under the All Writs Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1651(a). The court agreed unanimously that it possessed authority to issue mandamus in an appropriate case, although it divided over whether the circumstances warranted exercise of that authority. It issued writs directing that the reference orders be vacated, treating the transfer of the basic adjudicative work as a refusal to try the causes in due course. Id. at 250–251, 254.

Judge La Buy sought certiorari. He challenged both the appellate court’s power to intervene before final judgment and its conclusion that his exercise of Rule 53 discretion warranted extraordinary relief. The Supreme Court addressed those questions separately. It affirmed the writs without adjudicating the antitrust claims or determining any damages. Id. at 250–251, 254–260.

## Issue

May a court of appeals use mandamus before final judgment to direct a district judge to vacate orders referring entire nonjury antitrust cases to a master? If such authority exists, do congestion, complexity, and anticipated trial length constitute exceptional conditions permitting these broad references under the then-applicable Rule 53(b), or do the orders amount to an exceptional abuse warranting the writ? Id. at 254–260.

## Rule and Holding

Under the All Writs Act, a court of appeals that may eventually exercise appellate jurisdiction over a case has discretionary authority to issue mandamus in aid of that jurisdiction in proper, exceptional circumstances. The existence of ordinary review after final judgment does not categorically eliminate that authority. The Court nevertheless disclaimed indiscriminate use of writs to review interlocutory rulings. Id. at 254–255.

The references here constituted a clear abuse of the district judge’s authority under the historical Rule 53(b). A master may assist a court with specified work; the rule did not permit the judge to displace the court on the basic issues of these actions merely because they were lengthy, complex, and burdensome to the docket. The exceptional scope and circumstances of the references justified mandamus. A later, limited reference for detailed damage accounting could be appropriate after the judge determined overall liability. Id. at 256–260.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court affirmed the Seventh Circuit’s judgment issuing the writs. Judge La Buy was required to vacate the broad reference orders, leaving the underlying antitrust actions for proper district-court adjudication. The judgment did not forbid all assistance by a master, require judgment for either antitrust side, or award damages. It preserved the possibility of a properly limited accounting reference under appropriate circumstances. Id. at 259–260.

## Reasoning

The Court separated the existence of mandamus power from the discretion to use it. Because the Seventh Circuit could later hear appeals in these cases, it possessed authority under the All Writs Act to issue a writ reaching them in a proper situation. The 1948 recodification had not eliminated that established auxiliary authority. Determining that power existed did not resolve whether every erroneous Rule 53 order should be reviewed immediately. The majority expressly confined its approval to the exceptional circumstances presented. Id. at 254–255.

Those circumstances included the breadth of the delegation. The judge had assigned the master to try the central disputes before determining conspiracy or liability. Damages and the possible injunction were also included. The majority regarded that arrangement as nearly an abdication of the judicial function because the parties would lose the trial before the court to which they were entitled under the rule. The master was being used as a replacement adjudicator on the general issues rather than an aid with a particular judicial task. Id. at 255–256.

The judge’s extensive preparation reinforced that conclusion. Years of preliminary rulings had acquainted him with the pleadings, claims, defenses, discovery, and much of the anticipated proof. His antitrust experience and knowledge of these actions made him particularly well placed to resolve them efficiently. The purported complexity therefore did not demonstrate that a newly appointed substitute would produce a better or faster adjudication. Id. at 252, 255–256.

The majority relied on earlier cases restraining improper references and on the courts’ responsibility to enforce the procedural rules. It distinguished decisions refusing extraordinary review of ordinary discretionary orders. Here, in its assessment, the use of Rule 53 crossed from an arguable case-management choice into a clear abuse of the delegated authority. The governing rule would lose its practical force if a judge could transfer core adjudication whenever a demanding case burdened the calendar. Id. at 256–257.

The local history also mattered. The Seventh Circuit had warned for years that references should be rare and require unusual circumstances. The record showed that Judge La Buy had made eleven references during the preceding six years. The Court did not purport to find a fully documented circuitwide policy, but it considered the repeated admonitions and reference practice relevant to the need for supervisory intervention. It acknowledged that naming judges in extraordinary proceedings is undesirable and that mandamus should remain an extreme remedy. Id. at 257–258.

Calendar congestion did not supply the required exceptional condition. Congestion was sufficiently common that accepting it alone would make reference routine, contrary to the rule. The Court identified other administrative methods that might improve case assignment and pretrial handling while recognizing that methods must fit local circumstances. It did not deny the practical problem; it rejected the claimed consequence that the problem authorized wholesale substitution of masters for judges. Id. at 258–259.

Complexity and length likewise failed. Antitrust cases often involve difficult factual and legal questions and take substantial time. In the majority’s view, those characteristics supported adjudication by an experienced regular judge rather than by a temporary appointee ordinarily lacking comparable judicial experience. The parties’ entitlement to judicial determination of the basic issues did not diminish because their disputes were difficult. Id. at 259.

An accounting presented a different division of labor. Once the judge resolved overall liability, detailed calculations of individual losses might appropriately be assigned to a master when receiving and tabulating the proof did not warrant use of the judge’s time. This qualification made the holding task-specific: the Court rejected delegation of the entire litigation, while allowing specialized assistance with appropriate subordinate matters. Id. at 259.

The majority concluded that appellate supervisory control through mandamus was necessary in these circumstances to maintain proper administration of the federal courts. Its holding approved the writs on this record and did not establish an unrestricted interlocutory appeal whenever a party alleged an abuse of discretion. That limiting language is essential when comparing the case with the final-judgment rule. Id. at 255, 259–260.

## Separate Opinions

Justice Brennan, joined by Justices Frankfurter, Burton, and Harlan, argued that review had to await final judgment. He did not frame the disagreement as whether the references were permanently immune from review. Instead, he distinguished an erroneous exercise of jurisdiction from an excess or refusal to exercise jurisdiction. Rule 53 gave Judge La Buy discretion to determine whether exceptional conditions existed; even a mistaken determination ordinarily remained an error within that jurisdiction. Id. at 260–262.

For Brennan, the All Writs Act furnished an auxiliary power to protect appellate jurisdiction granted elsewhere, not an independent power to review unappealable interlocutory orders. Nothing about these references prevented the Seventh Circuit from reviewing the resulting final judgments. Added expense and delay were consequences of the statutory finality policy, not sufficient reasons to circumvent it. He contrasted an order transferring a case outside a circuit, which might frustrate that circuit’s prospective jurisdiction, with an order leaving ordinary later review available. Id. at 262–265.

Brennan distinguished precedents involving the Supreme Court’s historically different mandamus authority and a district’s wholesale disregard of governing rules. He feared the majority’s approach effectively introduced discretionary interlocutory appeals without congressional authorization and would itself worsen congestion by generating appellate petitions and delaying trials. His conclusion was that any error in the references was correctable through ordinary final-judgment review, so the writs should not have issued. Id. at 265–269.
