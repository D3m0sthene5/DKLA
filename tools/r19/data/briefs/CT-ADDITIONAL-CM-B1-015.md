# Harris v. Time, Inc. — published opinion, unpublished section unavailable
191 Cal. App. 3d 449, 237 Cal. Rptr. 584 (1987) | Contracts

## Source status and court

The complete published reporter reproduction has been read, including its judicial text and available notes, but the complete original opinion has not. The report expressly excludes Part I from publication and explains that the opinion originally appeared in full in the advance pamphlet. Part II contains the contract analysis; Part III contains the affirmance. The missing Part I must not be described as missing consideration analysis. This account therefore gives the verified published reasoning while retaining an unresolved full-original-opinion gap. King, J., wrote for California's First District Court of Appeal, Division Five, on April 27, 1987; Low, P.J., and Haning, J., concurred. (449; 452–460.)

## Facts and procedural history

A mailing addressed to three-year-old Joshua Gnaizda displayed a promise of a free calculator watch through a window in its envelope, apparently conditioned only on opening the envelope before February 15, 1985. His mother opened it. The window concealed additional instructions requiring mailing a certificate, and the certificate required a Fortune subscription. The complaint alleged that Joshua's father demanded the watch without a subscription and Time refused. Mark Harris and Richard Baker, recipients of the same mailing, joined the action; the complaint did not allege comparable demands by them. These facts were allegations considered in reviewing pleading sufficiency, not findings after a trial. (452–454.)

The complaint asserted contract, statutory advertising, promissory-estoppel and fraud claims. It sought a declaration concerning recipients' entitlement to watches or subscription rescission, an injunction, compensatory damages measured by the watch's value, and $15 million in punitive damages for a consumer fund. Note 2 specifies that plaintiffs did not claim Time failed to supply watches to people who subscribed. The trial court sustained a demurrer to the contract, estoppel and fraud claims, then granted summary judgment on the advertising claims and entered dismissal. The appellate court treated the premature notice of appeal as filed immediately after judgment. Plaintiffs abandoned estoppel and fraud on appeal. The contract and advertising challenges remained; the latter's separate Part I analysis is unavailable in this report. (453–454 & nn. 1–2.)

## Offer and consideration

Time argued that the unopened mailer was an advertisement rather than an offer, that opening it furnished no adequate consideration, and that plaintiffs failed to notify it of their performance. The trial court had not specified the grounds for sustaining the contract demurrer. The appellate court addressed each contractual ground separately. An advertisement ordinarily invites bargaining, but one requesting a specified performance without further communication or negotiation can offer a unilateral contract. The visible promise fell within that exception: it promised the watch for the requested act of opening the envelope. (455–456.)

The court also rejected Time's consideration objection. A bargained-for act ordinarily suffices without equivalence of values, an additional benefit to the promisor, or additional detriment to the promisee. Opening this envelope exposed its recipient to Time's sales pitch, which the court considered valuable to Time even if insignificant to the recipient. Assuming the complaint's allegations, the promise and requested act supplied offer and consideration. Consequently, affirmation of dismissal must not be turned into a holding that opening an envelope can never be consideration. (456.)

## Notice and anticipatory breach

Because Time could not otherwise learn that an envelope had been opened, the court required reasonable notice of performance. Joshua's pleaded demand supplied notice; Harris and Baker had not alleged notice before suit. Note 4 distinguishes the UCC's formulation of an offer lapsing before acceptance from the Restatement's discharge of the offeror's contractual duty. Note 5 records the coplaintiffs' argument that their complaint itself gave notice. The court held their contract allegations insufficient on this ground, unlike Joshua's. (456–457 & nn. 4–5; 458.)

Plaintiffs argued that the concealed subscription requirement anticipatorily breached the unilateral undertaking and excused notice. The court applied California precedents declining anticipatory-breach relief for unilateral contracts, discussed criticism of that approach, and found no compelling public-policy reason to depart from binding state supreme court precedent here. Its qualified discussion did not adopt a new exception. This is the 1987 court's account of then-applicable law, not a certification of current California doctrine. (457–458.)

## Disposition and de minimis reasoning

The court nevertheless affirmed dismissal of the entire action under Civil Code section 3533's principle that the law disregards trifles. It emphasized the absence of alleged loss beyond the time and effort of opening the envelope, contrasted that limited injury with the requested $15 million remedy, and considered the litigation an excessive use of judicial resources. Its discussion of a technically supported unilateral-contract claim is thus distinct from its final reason for affirmance. The court affirmed on a correct alternative theory even though the trial court's stated reasoning could differ. Each party was to bear its own appellate costs. This was a unanimous published disposition; no separate writing appears in the available report. (458–460.)
