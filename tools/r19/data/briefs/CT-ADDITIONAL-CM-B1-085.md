# Union Carbide Corp. v. Oscar Mayer Foods Corp.
947 F.2d 1333 (7th Cir. 1991) | Contracts

## Facts and document sequence
Union Carbide sold plastic sausage casings to Oscar Mayer. Its prices included two one-percent taxes applicable to sales originating in Chicago. In 1980 a competing supplier began receiving orders outside Chicago and reduced its price by one percent, believing one tax no longer applied. When Oscar Mayer raised that discount, Union Carbide likewise directed orders to an address outside Chicago, stopped paying both taxes, and removed both charges from its invoices. Its two-percent reduction thus exceeded the competitor’s discount. Eight years later Illinois assessed Union Carbide $88,000 in back taxes on Oscar Mayer sales and $55,000 interest. Union Carbide paid and sought reimbursement from Oscar Mayer under invoice and price-book language requiring the buyer, in addition to the purchase price, to pay governmental taxes the seller might be required to pay concerning the goods’ production, sale, or transportation. (947 F.2d at 1334.)

The actual transactions used four documents. Oscar Mayer sent standing purchase orders retained for later reference. As inventory was needed it telephoned a quantity request, and Union Carbide shipped the next day. After delivery Oscar Mayer sent a shipment-specific release order on its usual purchase-order form; around the same time Union Carbide sent an invoice. The tax clause appeared on invoice backs and in periodic price books. For purposes of appeal Union Carbide did not dispute that Oscar Mayer’s purchase orders were offers and its invoices acceptances, or that the price book, even if an offer, had never been accepted. The record did not establish the origins of their dealings. (Id. at 1337.)

Union Carbide brought a diversity contract action. The district court granted Oscar Mayer summary judgment. Judge Posner wrote the November 15, 1991 Seventh Circuit opinion on a panel with Judges Flaum and Manion. There are no judicial footnotes or separate opinions in the complete report, and no express numerical vote. (Id. at 1333–1334, 1338.)

## What the tax language meant
The court first rejected Union Carbide’s interpretation. The text could linguistically accommodate its claim, but also naturally allowed the seller to add applicable taxes to an agreed purchase price. The latter reading differed substantially from imposing open-ended liability for retroactive taxes, interest, or possible penalties years after delivery because the seller miscalculated its obligations. The discussion of fraud penalties was hypothetical; no such penalty was stated as assessed in this case. (Id. at 1334.)

Economic context favored the ordinary invoice-charge reading. Union Carbide, the taxpayer dealing with Illinois, was better positioned than its customer to prevent a tax mistake; the record supplied no suggestion that either side was a superior insurer of an unavoidable error. Oscar Mayer sought a competitive price reduction, not a discount that might later be reclaimed with interest. The court considered whether the extra one-percent reduction could compensate for assuming that risk, but Union Carbide had not argued that theory. Another supplier’s differently worded tax provision did not establish that this clause meant indemnification. (Id. at 1334–1335.)

Although genuine doubt about contractual meaning can require a jury, the court found Union Carbide’s proposed meaning insufficiently supported to require trial. Summary judgment was proper on interpretation alone. That holding is distinct from the alternative §2-207 analysis: calling every invoice tax clause an indemnity provision would bypass the first reason the claim failed. (Id. at 1335.)

## Additional terms, material alteration, and actual consent
Alternatively, even if construed as the proposed indemnity, the clause materially altered the bargain without Oscar Mayer’s agreement. Under UCC §2-207, an acceptance can form a sales contract despite additional terms. Between merchants such terms may become part of the agreement, but a material alteration does not enter automatically. Exclusion of the term need not prevent formation of the underlying contract. Both parties here were merchants. (Id. at 1335–1336.)

The opinion described materiality as an alteration to which consent cannot reasonably be presumed, equivalent to unreasonable surprise. It criticized treating hardship as an independent criterion merely because Official Comment 4 described material changes resulting in surprise or hardship. Hardship ordinarily followed the alteration; a party could not abandon an agreed contract just because performance became burdensome, absent an applicable excuse doctrine. This is the opinion’s articulated analysis of the comment, not a certification that every court adopts that formulation. (Id. at 1336.)

Materiality and actual acceptance remained separate questions. A buyer could accept even a material change, expressly or through silence against a course of dealings reasonably supporting an inference of consent. For a material term, however, the proposer needed additional evidence beyond the term itself to justify that inference. Repeated forms could be relevant; their existence did not make every disputed interpretation binding automatically. The opinion also recognized the offeror’s ability under §2-207(2)(a) to limit acceptance expressly to the offer’s terms. No finding that Oscar Mayer used that limitation was necessary to the result here. (Id. at 1336–1337.)

## Applying the distinction to these dealings
Oscar Mayer’s purchase-order form included space for sales tax, and it had paid invoice taxes without protest. It acknowledged a contractual obligation to pay those charges, rooted partly in prior dealings. If rates increased and the increase appeared on an invoice, Oscar Mayer would have to pay but could then switch suppliers. It had no requirements contract and could discontinue buying from Union Carbide. (Id. at 1337.)

That limited obligation differed from an incalculable obligation to reimburse later tax assessments on completed purchases. Construing the clause as retroactive indemnity would materially alter the orders. Neither express nor implied consent supported that shift. In particular, the clause was at best ambiguous about indemnity; silence in response to successive invoices containing it could not realistically demonstrate assent to Union Carbide’s interpretation. The court found no breach and affirmed the judgment for Oscar Mayer. Union Carbide’s tax payment and reimbursement demand were not a recovery awarded against the buyer. (Id. at 1337–1338.)
