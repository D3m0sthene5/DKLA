# Leonard v. Pepsico, Inc.
88 F. Supp. 2d 116 (S.D.N.Y. 1999), affirmed, 210 F.3d 88 (2d Cir. 2000) | Contracts

## Two judicial stages and disposition

District Judge Kimba M. Wood granted PepsiCo summary judgment on August 5, 1999, rejecting John Leonard’s demand for a Harrier jet and his fraud theory, directing closure of the cases, and treating pending motions as moot. The Second Circuit’s unanimous per curiam opinion of April 17, 2000, by Judges Feinberg, Jacobs, and Cynthia Holcomb Hall sitting by designation, affirmed substantially for Wood’s reasons: the commercial did not constitute an offer, no objective reasonable viewer would understand it as a genuine jet offer, and the supposed agreement did not satisfy New York’s statute of frauds. These are two expressly assigned stages, preserved together here. Neither court ordered delivery of a plane or awarded Leonard damages. (88 F. Supp. 2d at 117–118, 132; 210 F.3d at 88–89.)

## Promotion and attempted redemption

Pepsi’s test-market promotion ran from October 1995 to March 1996 in the Pacific Northwest, including Seattle, where Leonard lived and saw the commercial. Consumers collected points from marked packages and could redeem them for branded merchandise. The ad depicted a teenager wearing a shirt, leather jacket, and sunglasses, each accompanied by its point price. It concluded with the teenager landing a Harrier beside his school, wind disrupting the classroom and stripping a teacher’s clothes, and a screen stating a seven-million-point price. The teenager commented that this beat taking the bus. The ad referred viewers to the catalog and carried a reservation directing them to package details. (88 F. Supp. 2d at 118–119 & nn.1–2.)

The catalog’s order form listed fifty-three merchandise items, with no Harrier. It required an original order form and at least fifteen original points, but allowed additional points to be bought for ten cents each. Leonard initially contemplated collecting points through consumption, then raised approximately $700,000 through acquaintances. Around March 27, 1996, he submitted fifteen points, a check for $700,008.50, and an order form on which he wrote in a Harrier. The fulfillment company returned his check in May, explaining that the jet was humorous advertising and not catalog merchandise. Demand letters followed; Pepsi’s advertising agency likewise denied a genuine offer. (Id. at 119–120.)

## Procedural route and choice of law

Pepsi first filed a New York declaratory action in July 1996. Leonard filed in Florida in August, although he had not seen the commercial there and the dispute lacked a meaningful connection to that forum. Removal and transfer brought his action to New York. The declaratory case was initially dismissed for lack of personal jurisdiction. Leonard then attempted voluntary dismissal of his own action, conditioned on fees; a later order required $88,162. Rather than pay, he resumed litigation. In February 1999, the parties dismissed the intervening appeals by stipulation, Leonard consented to jurisdiction, and Pepsi agreed not to enforce that fee award. It was not a penalty imposed by the final merits judgment discussed here. (Id. at 120–121 & nn.3–4.)

Florida’s conflicts rules followed the transferred action. They looked to the place of the final act needed to complete a contract. If the ad were an offer, acceptance would occur in Washington; if it merely invited orders, acceptance would have to occur in New York. The court first used shared common-law formation principles, then applied New York law to the statute of frauds once it determined that Leonard’s order was an offer awaiting Pepsi’s acceptance. (Id. at 122, 131 & n.15.)

## Advertisement rather than an operative offer

Advertisements ordinarily invite offers instead of immediately conferring a power of acceptance. A sufficiently definite advertisement can depart from that rule, as in Lefkowitz’s specified goods, time, limited quantity, and first-come terms. Pepsi’s commercial instead reserved transactional details to a separate catalog and failed to prescribe a definite means of accepting a jet offer. Leonard’s submission could not convert that invitation into Pepsi’s binding offer; no acceptance or cashing of his check occurred. The court additionally considered the absence of limiting terms significant because an unlimited commercial could expose a seller to acceptances exceeding inventory. (Id. at 122–124 & nn.5–7.)

Leonard invoked reward cases such as Carlill. Those decisions involved commitments inducing completion of an identified act, rather than an ordinary invitation to order commercial merchandise subject to catalog conditions. Pepsi had not promised a plane to anyone performing a specified act at a specified place or otherwise dispensing with further assent. The catalog’s omission of the jet mattered, notwithstanding Leonard’s contrary personal understanding and his reliance on a press release’s reference to an ultimate award. The court considered the press release in its full promotional context. (Id. at 125–127 & nn.8–10.)

## Objective humor and the discovery request

Formation depended on a reasonable interpretation of the ad, not Pepsi’s undisclosed intention or Leonard’s sincere personal belief. A secretly intended joke may still bind when it reasonably communicates a serious offer; an evident joke does not. Here the extraordinary school commute, youthful pilot, physical disruption, military purpose of the aircraft, and enormous difference between the roughly $23 million plane value and the suggested point-purchase cost made the scene an adolescent fantasy. Identical on-screen treatment of ordinary merchandise and the plane did not eliminate its tongue-in-cheek character. This was a contextual assessment, not a rule that any unusually favorable price invalidates a bargain. (Id. at 127–130 & nn.11–13.)

The court rejected Leonard’s demand for a jury composed of members of the “Pepsi Generation.” Contract formation could be resolved on summary judgment when reasonable people could not disagree about the communications. Additional discovery into Pepsi’s internal intentions, audience responses, or later changes to the ad would not change the objective meaning of the commercial Leonard actually saw. Later increases to the point figure and an express joke disclaimer did not prove that the original ad was serious. The court also noted that this proposed discovery would not cure its other independent grounds. (Id. at 128, 130–131 & n.14.)

## Writing requirement, fraud, and appellate confirmation

New York’s then-applicable UCC § 2-201 required a sufficient writing signed by the party charged for a goods contract of $500 or more. The television commercial was not such a writing, Leonard’s form lacked Pepsi’s signature, and hypothetical agreements with advertisers would establish neither a commitment to Leonard nor his status as a party or beneficiary. Combining writings under Crabtree could not bypass the threshold requirement of a signed document establishing the contractual relationship and related writings referring to that same transaction. (Id. at 131–132.)

The fraud claim alleged that Pepsi never intended to fulfill the supposed jet offer. It identified no collateral misrepresentation of present fact inducing a separate agreement. Under the authorities applied, merely alleging entry into a contract without an intention to perform did not establish this fraud cause of action. Summary judgment therefore reached that claim as well. The short appellate opinion adopted the district court’s three grounds concerning contract enforcement substantially, without adding a separate factual trial or separate writing. (Id. at 132; 210 F.3d at 89.)
