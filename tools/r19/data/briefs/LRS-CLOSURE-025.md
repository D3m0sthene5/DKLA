# Fox Television Stations, Inc. v. FCC
613 F.3d 317 (2d Cir. 2010) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, stage, and judgment

On July 13, 2010, Judge Pooler wrote for a unanimous Second Circuit panel with Judges Leval and Hall. Following the Supreme Court's 2009 reversal of its administrative-law ruling, the panel considered the broadcasters' constitutional challenge to FCC's indecency policy. It held the policy impermissibly vague under the First Amendment, granted the petition for review, and vacated the challenged order and underlying policy. Although the caption lists three dockets, only No. 06-5358 remained; the others had previously been dismissed as moot. Leval's dissent belonged to the 2007 APA judgment, not this opinion. The 2010 judgment was subsequently vacated by the Supreme Court in 2012, which supplied a narrower fair-notice ruling. (319–320 n. 1; 325, 335.)

## From limited enforcement to fleeting expletives

Section 1464 prohibited obscene, indecent, or profane broadcast language; Congress also authorized FCC civil forfeitures. Pacifica sustained action against George Carlin's deliberately repeated expletives in a specific afternoon broadcast but emphasized its narrow setting. For years FCC initially focused on those seven words and brought no enforcement actions between 1978 and 1987. It later adopted a broader contextual approach, while retaining a restrained policy toward single, nonliteral expletives. (320–322.)

FCC's 2001 guidance asked whether material described sexual or excretory organs or activities and was patently offensive under contemporary broadcast standards. The latter inquiry considered explicitness, repetition or dwelling on the material, and pandering, titillation, or shock value. Isolated expletives remained ordinarily nonactionable. After Bono's fleeting expletive during the 2003 Golden Globes, FCC's 2004 order changed course: the F-word inherently had sexual connotations even when used nonliterally, and fleeting use no longer insulated a broadcast. It similarly reworked profanity doctrine, although it abandoned the profanity finding for this appeal and the court did not decide it. (322–324; 327 n. 7.)

Potential sanctions also increased. FCC treated separate licensees' broadcasts as separate violations, and Congress raised maximum fines tenfold, from $32,500 to $325,000. The opinion describes the resulting possibility of very large aggregate penalties; its discussion does not mean such a fine was assessed against Fox in the order under review. The notes distinguish historical enforcement totals and the separate Super Bowl litigation. (322–323; 322 nn. 2–3.)

## Broadcasts, reconsideration, and the issues left by the Supreme Court

FCC's 2006 omnibus order applied the new approach to Fox's 2002 and 2003 Billboard Music Awards, episodes of NYPD Blue, and The Early Show. Cher used an unscripted F-word during an acceptance speech; Nicole Richie used expletives while presenting an award. FCC treated variants of the F-word and S-word as presumptively indecent and found the broadcasts explicit, shocking, and gratuitous despite their brief nature. (323–324.)

After petitions for review and a voluntary remand, FCC's November 6, 2006 order reaffirmed the two Billboard findings. It reversed the Early Show finding because the comment appeared in a bona fide news interview, while denying an absolute news exemption. It dismissed the NYPD Blue complaint on a procedural ground: the complainant lived in the Eastern time zone, where the program aired after the safe-harbor cutoff. That complaint was not the separate nudity forfeiture later reviewed by the Supreme Court in 2012. (324 & n. 5.)

The Second Circuit's 2007 majority found inadequate explanation for changing the longstanding fleeting-expletive policy and did not reach constitutionality. The Supreme Court reversed in 2009, holding the change sufficiently justified under the APA, but remanded to address the constitutional questions. The 2010 panel therefore could not simply repeat its rejected APA conclusion. It examined notice, chilling, and discriminatory-enforcement risks under the First Amendment. (324–325.)

## The court followed Pacifica without treating it as a general constitutional authorization

The networks urged changed media conditions and parental-blocking technology as grounds for abandoning broadcasting's distinctive treatment. The panel discussed cable and internet competition and V-chip technology favorably to that argument, but expressly remained bound by Supreme Court precedent. It neither overruled Pacifica nor held all broadcast restrictions subject to strict scrutiny. (325–327.)

The parties also disagreed whether Pacifica gave FCC broad authority or limited regulation to material comparable to Carlin's repeated verbal shock treatment. The court did not decide that outer boundary because it found the current policy vague regardless. Fair notice and protection against arbitrary enforcement mattered especially where ambiguous restrictions chilled protected speech, although perfect precision was unnecessary. (327–328.)

Neither side could automatically resolve this case through Reno. FCC argued Reno's distinction between the internet and broadcasting foreclosed vagueness review; the court answered that lower scrutiny for broadcasting did not eliminate broadcasters' right to clarity. The networks argued Reno's rejection of similar indecency language necessarily invalidated FCC's test; the court answered that FCC's additional factors and decisions required a fresh evaluation. Pacifica itself had not resolved vagueness and relied on restrained enforcement now materially changed. The notes addressed the related circuit precedents and rejected reliance on Humanitarian Law Project's different procedural setting. (328–330, nn. 8–9.)

## The announced factors and exceptions did not give a discernible standard

FCC's decisions labeled some sexual or excretory expressions indecent and others permissible, but often simply repeated the factors without explaining their application. Declaring one word sufficiently vulgar and another insufficiently graphic supplied little predictive guidance. The agency's need for flexibility to catch new expressions did not justify a standard that broadcasters themselves could not anticipate. The court did not mandate restoration of a seven-word list; it used that prior approach to contrast notice with current uncertainty. (330–331.)

The presumptive ban on F-word and S-word variants had equally uncertain exceptions for bona fide news and artistic or educational necessity. Saving Private Ryan's repeated expletives were permissible for realism, while real musicians' language in The Blues was not considered necessary to its educational purpose. The same Early Show remark was first indecent because it occurred during morning news and then excused because it was a bona fide news interview. FCC counsel's qualified prediction about a hypothetical teenage educational program did not supply dependable notice where major sanctions and protected expression were at stake. (331–333.)

Broad discretion also created a risk of subjective content discrimination. The court did not find or suspect that FCC was actually using the policy to suppress particular viewpoints. The constitutional problem was that the standard did not constrain that possibility. Context remained relevant, but invoking context alone could not replace discernible standards. Assurances of deference to news editorial judgment did not cure that uncertainty. (332–333.)

## Chilling evidence, exact result, and scope

The record illustrated suppression beyond the award-show remarks. Stations declined or restricted broadcasts of a September 11 documentary and a novel reading. Live broadcasting posed a particular problem: Fox precleared scripts and employed an audio delay, yet Richie's rapid unscripted remarks outpaced the bleeping process. No system could guarantee total prevention, and avoiding live coverage altogether or excluding controversial speakers impaired protected speech. The notes distinguish estimated delay costs, a personnel consequence, and ongoing investigations as additional record context. (334–335, nn. 10–12.)

News concerns included a cancelled local debate, curtailed memorial coverage, and a station's reduced live news practice. Programs discussing sexuality were revised or withheld even without expletives. These examples showed the policy's chilling reach into public affairs and art, not merely speakers' preference to use offensive words. (334–335.)

The panel granted the petition and invalidated the order and policy, while expressly leaving open FCC's ability to create a constitutional policy.
