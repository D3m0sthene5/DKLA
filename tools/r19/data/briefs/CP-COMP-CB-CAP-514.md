# Torres v. Oakland Scavenger Co.

**487 U.S. 312 (1988) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 24, 1988. Civil Procedure.** Justice Marshall wrote for seven Justices. Justice Scalia concurred in the judgment; Justice Brennan dissented. The judgment was 8–1 to affirm. The case applies the historical version of Appellate Rule 3(c), requiring specification of the appealing parties.

## Facts and procedural history

Jose Torres was one of sixteen plaintiffs who intervened in an employment-discrimination action against Oakland Scavenger. They had received notice of the action under a settlement between the employer and the original plaintiffs. The intervenors purported to sue both for themselves and for similarly situated persons, but no class had been certified when the district court dismissed their complaint for failure to state a claim on August 31, 1981. The Supreme Court’s decision concerned appellate procedure, not whether the employer had discriminated. 487 U.S. at 313.

A notice of appeal was filed on September 29. It named the other fifteen intervenors but omitted Torres because of an undisputed clerical error by his attorney’s secretary. The Ninth Circuit reversed the dismissal and remanded, but its order, like the notice, omitted Torres’s name. On remand, Oakland Scavenger obtained partial summary judgment against Torres on the theory that the original dismissal had become final as to him because he had not appealed. The circuit affirmed that judgment, treating omission from the notice as a jurisdictional defect. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve disagreement among circuits about the party-specification requirement. Id. at 313–314 & n. 1.

Torres maintained that the notice’s use of “et al.” adequately included him. He also sought relief through liberal construction, the courts’ power to suspend appellate rules for good cause, and harmless-error reasoning. The employer’s successful position was that the other intervenors’ notice had not invoked appellate jurisdiction for Torres, regardless of the clerical reason for his omission. Id. at 314–318 & n. 3.

## Issue and holding

Could the court of appeals exercise jurisdiction over Torres when he was not named or otherwise adequately designated in the timely notice? No. The Court regarded Rule 3(c)’s requirement to specify the appealing parties as jurisdictional. An omitted party’s failure to appeal could not be treated as a mere informality of form or title. In this record, “et al.” did not identify Torres sufficiently, and he had neither filed a functional equivalent of a notice nor sought timely amendment. Id. at 314–318.

## Majority’s reasoning

Rule 3(c) required the notice to specify the party or parties taking the appeal. Its 1979 amendment protected notices against dismissal for informality of form or title, but the majority distinguished imperfect form from the complete absence of an appeal by a particular party. The latter was not excusable informality. Id. at 314.

Rule 2 permitted suspension of appellate-rule requirements for good cause, subject to Rule 26(b)’s exceptions. Rule 26(b) barred enlargement of the time to file a notice of appeal. The majority reasoned that allowing an unnamed party to become an appellant after that period would effectively extend the time to appeal. It therefore understood the rules to withhold both powers. The Advisory Committee’s discussion of Rules 3 and 4 as combining to require a timely notice supported that interpretation, although the Court acknowledged that the committee’s view was not independently determinative. Id. at 314–316.

The Court preserved a role for substantial compliance. The committee’s examples, including a prisoner’s letter accepted as a notice, demonstrated that literal form need not invariably control. Foman likewise supported liberal construction of papers that performed the required function. The majority read Foman as a determination that its particular filings complied with the rule, rather than permission to waive a jurisdictional condition. Thus a court could recognize functional compliance while lacking power to excuse actual noncompliance, even for good cause. Id. at 315–317 & n. 2.

On the majority’s application of that distinction, Torres had never been designated in the notice, even inartfully. The phrase “et al.” did not tell the employer or court which unnamed persons intended to appeal. That uncertainty also affected who would be bound by an adverse appellate judgment and responsible for costs or sanctions. A designation had to give fair notice of the specific person or entity taking the appeal. The Court acknowledged the result’s harshness but considered it required by its reading of the governing rules. Id. at 317–318.

Footnote 3 rejects harmless-error relief because failure to clear a jurisdictional threshold could not be harmless or judicially waived. It also preserves an important limit on the disposition. Torres argued that absent class members might obtain relief while he could not. The district court had expressly left open whether Torres could participate as a member of a subsequently certified class. The Supreme Court did not decide that separate eligibility question. This judgment therefore should not be described as a categorical prohibition on every possible recovery through the class. Id. at 317 n. 3.

## Scalia’s concurrence and Brennan’s dissent

Justice Scalia agreed with affirmance but considered the majority’s language about liberal construction inconsistent with its result. Under genuinely liberal functional-equivalence reasoning, he thought a caption naming the first party and adding “et al.” could suggest that all parties were appealing, with a later omission creating only ambiguity. He instead read the rule’s express specification requirement strictly according to its apparent intent. The limited protection for informality suggested that a generally strict application was contemplated. His approach neither favored liberal nor stingy construction as a general interpretive preference. Id. at 318–319.

Justice Brennan would have reversed and remanded for a factual inquiry into whether the notice actually communicated Torres’s intent to appeal. He distinguished Rule 4’s expressly protected timing requirement from Rule 3(c)’s content requirement. In his view, Rules 2 and 26(b) did not expressly exclude party specification from the courts’ equitable power. The majority’s reasoning assumed that omission necessarily meant failure to appeal, which was the disputed question. An effective timely notice could preserve the time limit even if it identified a party imperfectly. Id. at 319–322.

Brennan also read Foman differently: its deficient notices were sufficient when considered together because the intent to seek review was manifest. Torres argued that common issues and inclusion of fifteen of sixteen intervenors similarly communicated his intent. Brennan would have examined actual notice, prejudice, and commitment to appellate obligations rather than treating clerical omission as invariably fatal. His starred footnote acknowledged the predictability of a bright-line rule, but considered that advantage marginal given the rarity of cases finding sufficient notice despite an omitted name. Id. at 322–325 & n. *.

## Judgment and assigned source

The Supreme Court **affirmed the Ninth Circuit’s judgment**, leaving the partial summary judgment against Torres in place on the procedural ground described above. Id. at 318. It did not decide the discrimination merits or the separately reserved possibility of participation as a class member.
