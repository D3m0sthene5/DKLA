# Ohio v. Environmental Protection Agency
603 U.S. 279 (2024) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, procedural stage, and exact order

On June 27, 2024, the Supreme Court granted four applications to stay enforcement of EPA’s Good Neighbor Plan against the applicants while their challenges proceeded. Justice Gorsuch wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas, Alito, and Kavanaugh. Justice Barrett dissented, joined by Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson. This was a 5–4 ruling on interim relief. The Court predicted likely success on an arbitrary-or-capricious challenge; it did not enter a final judgment invalidating the plan or decide the merits of EPA’s separate state-plan disapprovals. 603 U.S. at 283, 290–294, 300–304.

The stay covered the applicants in Nos. 23A349, 23A350, 23A351, and 23A384, including state and industry applicants. It would remain effective through disposition of their D.C. Circuit petitions and any timely certiorari petition. If certiorari were denied, the order would terminate automatically; if granted, it would terminate when the Supreme Court’s judgment was sent down. The order therefore included review beyond the D.C. Circuit rather than simply expiring at that court’s decision. Id. at 300.

## Statutory framework and regulatory history

The Clean Air Act divides responsibility between EPA and the States. EPA establishes national ambient air-quality standards, and States ordinarily submit State Implementation Plans specifying implementation, maintenance, and enforcement measures. The Good Neighbor Provision requires those plans to prohibit emissions contributing significantly to another State’s nonattainment or interfering with its maintenance of the applicable standard. EPA must approve a state plan meeting the Act’s requirements. A deficient or missing plan can require a Federal Implementation Plan unless the State first cures the deficiency. States’ primary planning role thus coexists with EPA’s duty to address interstate pollution and statutory attainment deadlines. Id. at 283–284, 301–302.

In 2015, EPA tightened the ozone standard from 75 to 70 parts per billion. Many States submitted plans asserting that no additional measures were required because they lacked relevant downwind links or had no further cost-effective controls. In 2022 EPA proposed disapproving nineteen plans, then four more, and proposed a multistate federal replacement. The final 2023 plan covered twenty-three States, with additions and exclusions that differed from the initial disapproval list. The opinion’s notes identify those changes; the number twenty-three does not mean every originally proposed disapproval and every ultimately covered State were identical. Id. at 284–289 & nn.1–5.

The federal plan targeted nitrogen oxides, a family of ozone precursors, through two parallel programs for power plants and other industries. EPA examined control measures, their costs per ton of emissions reduced, anticipated emissions reductions, and projected downwind ozone improvements. It selected a cost threshold near the point of diminishing returns, described as the knee in the curve. For power plants, resulting state emissions budgets supported an allowance-trading system; other covered industries faced specified emissions limits. The majority and dissent disagreed about how the selection of cost thresholds depended on the identity and number of covered States. Id. at 286–288, 311–316 & nn.6–9 of the dissent.

Comments objected to the relationship between proposed state-plan disapprovals and the federal plan. Some asserted that different action on those disapprovals would require new assessment and modeling. Before and after the final rule, courts stayed numerous state-plan disapprovals. EPA adopted a severability provision keeping the federal rule in operation for remaining jurisdictions. By the Supreme Court’s consideration, stays prevented application of the plan in twelve of the original twenty-three States, representing over seventy percent of the emissions EPA had planned to address. Those were preliminary stays, not final invalidations of the state-plan disapprovals; their status and the plan’s coverage could change. Id. at 287–290 & nn.4–8, 302.

## Parties’ positions and stay standard

Ohio and other state and industry applicants challenged the federal plan in the D.C. Circuit and sought interim protection against its enforcement. After that court denied a stay, they applied to the Supreme Court. They argued that a plan formulated for twenty-three States lacked an adequate explanation for unchanged application after many States dropped out. EPA defended its severability rationale, contested preservation of the particular objection, and argued that applicants should first seek administrative reconsideration. The federal respondents and supporting downwind States and public-interest parties emphasized air-quality benefits. Applicants emphasized state regulatory interests, competitive disadvantages, and substantial unrecoverable compliance costs. Id. at 290–292, 294–298.

Under Nken, the Court considered likely success on the merits, irreparable injury, injury to other interested parties, and the public interest. The majority acknowledged weighty harms on both sides. It treated likely success as decisive in this setting rather than finding that environmental protection or compliance costs categorically resolved the balance. Barrett also stressed the demands of emergency relief and the importance of predicting whether the Court would grant certiorari in the ordinary course. Id. at 290–292 & n.9, 304.

## Majority’s prediction of likely success

Agency action must be reasonable and reasonably explained. Courts may not substitute their policy judgment, but they must require a satisfactory explanation connecting the facts found to the choice made and addressing important aspects of the problem. The majority applied those familiar State Farm principles to EPA’s explanation, rather than holding that every decline in state participation necessarily changed the scientifically correct emissions limit. Id. at 292–294.

For the majority, EPA had linked its chosen control stringency to cost-effective downwind improvements calculated with the contemplated multistate participation. Each State differed in industries, existing controls, and resulting emissions and ozone effects. Removing States could therefore change the point of diminishing returns. Comments put EPA on notice of that possible problem, yet the final rule did not explain why its chosen measures remained appropriately cost-effective for a reduced set of States. At argument, the government would not confidently say the same analysis would produce the same results for a substantially different set. The majority left open the possibility of a valid explanation; it found that explanation absent from the final rule. That omission made applicants likely to succeed. Id. at 286–288, 293–294.

A severability clause did not supply the missing substantive explanation. It showed awareness of possible reduced coverage and a desire to preserve public-health benefits and reliance interests. But announcing continued implementation did not explain why the same measures remained justified under the cost-effectiveness method EPA selected. EPA did not have to analyze every possible permutation of States. It did have to respond reasonably to the identified concern. Id. at 294–295 & n.11.

The Court also declined to use EPA’s April 2024 reconsideration explanation to validate the original rule. The majority relied on the Act’s restrictions concerning information in the promulgation docket and the record for judicial review. It evaluated the grounds invoked when the rule was issued; the government had not urged reliance on the later analysis for validating the final rule. This restriction was important because the dissent regarded that explanation as confirming how the existing methodology worked. Id. at 295 n.11, 303–304, 320 n.13.

## Preservation, reconsideration, and forfeiture

The Act permits judicial review of objections raised with reasonable specificity during the comment period. The majority rejected a requirement that a commenter repeat in precisely the same words the ultimate litigation argument. In its reading, comments calling for new assessment and modeling if different state-plan decisions altered coverage conveyed the relevant substantive concern. EPA’s claimed consideration of reduced coverage and addition of severability reinforced that it understood the issue. Id. at 296–297 & n.12.

Applicants did not have to seek reconsideration merely to ask again for an explanation of a concern already presented. The majority distinguished an objection that the final rule was not a logical outgrowth of the proposal: such an objection can arise only after the final rule and may require the statutory reconsideration route. Here, the underlying problem could and did arise during comments, and the alleged defect was EPA’s inadequate response to it. Id. at 297–298.

The majority treated additional defenses developed by the dissent—state-independent methodology and harmlessness—as forfeited arguments not adequately made by the government. It also noted uncertainty about the methodology and the Act’s distinction between procedural determinations and arbitrary-or-capricious agency action. It declined to decide the reach of harmless-error rules through an unbriefed defense. Its holding therefore should not be enlarged into a categorical rejection of harmless-error review in all explanation challenges. Other merits arguments offered by applicants were unnecessary to decide. Id. at 294 n.10, 298–300 & nn.13–14.

## Barrett’s dissent

Barrett identified three substantial barriers to the applicants’ theory and described her merits conclusions as tentative in the emergency posture. First, she doubted preservation. Comments attacking particular technologies or industries did not necessarily challenge dependence on the number of States. The most relevant modeling comment, in its full context, objected to proposing a federal plan before finalizing the state-plan disapprovals. It did not specifically state that later judicial stays would alter cost thresholds. EPA had responded to the sequencing objection by invoking its deadline obligations. Barrett also regarded the claimed inadequacy of the final rule’s explanation as a procedural objection that could not arise until that rule existed and thus might require reconsideration first. Id. at 305–310 & nn.1–5.

Second, the record arguably showed a methodology independent of the number of participating States. EPA used nationwide industry data to determine achievable control performance and costs. It then calculated each State’s budget from its sources under those independently selected limits. State-specific modeling assessed impacts and potential overcontrol, rather than determining the underlying limits. If the full plan did not overcontrol, removing States could not create overcontrol by increasing total reductions. EPA’s preference for nationwide data, contemplated state exits, and public-health and deadline explanations made its path reasonably discernible. Barrett did not claim to resolve every technical uncertainty definitively; she argued that the applicants had failed to establish likely success despite this contrary evidence. Id. at 311–318 & nn.6–10.

Third, the Act permits invalidation for procedural error only where the error is serious, centrally relevant, and substantially likely to have significantly changed the rule. An inadequate explanation, as distinct from substantively unreasonable control limits, plausibly fell within that rule. Applicants had not shown the necessary likelihood of a changed outcome. The later reconsideration denial reinforced the point, though Barrett also relied on the original record and questioned whether the majority’s record restriction controlled harmlessness analysis. Id. at 318–320 & nn.11–13.

Barrett rejected forfeiture as a sufficient answer. In her account, applicants’ theory evolved in reply briefs and argument, while EPA had little time to answer numerous applications. The government had argued that the plan operated coherently regardless of the number of jurisdictions. The applicants bore the burden of showing they could overcome statutory barriers in eventual merits litigation; considering a likely future harmless-error defense was appropriate to that prediction. Given public-health consequences and the limited emergency record, she would deny the stay. Id. at 320–323.
