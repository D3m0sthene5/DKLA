# Siegel v. Spear & Co.
234 N.Y. 479, 138 N.E. 414 (1923) | Contracts

## Court and result
Judge Crane wrote the New York Court of Appeals' January 16, 1923 opinion. Chief Judge Hiscock and Judges Hogan, Cardozo, Pound, McLaughlin, and Andrews concurred, making the decision unanimous without separate writings. The court affirmed with costs the judgment compensating William Siegel for furniture lost by fire after the defendant failed to procure promised insurance. The promise was part of an undertaking that the defendant began performing when it received the furniture in a gratuitous bailment. The court left open both a separate consideration theory based on Siegel's refraining from obtaining insurance himself and reconsideration of the older Thorne v. Deas rule. (479, 482–484; the following locators use the archived judicial reproduction, independently compared with the original report.)

## Purchase, storage, and insurance undertaking
Siegel bought household furniture from Spear in August 1917 and January 1918 for $909.25. Two chattel mortgages secured monthly installments and prohibited removal from his apartment without the mortgagee's written consent. By May 1918 he had paid $295. Planning to leave the city for the summer and give up his apartment, he arranged with Spear's creditman, McGrath, to transport the furniture by his own truck to Spear's storehouse. Storage would be free. (Lines 173–174.)

According to Siegel, McGrath suggested transferring an insurance policy to the warehouse. Siegel said he had never insured the furniture and would arrange coverage through his life-insurance agent before delivery. McGrath responded that he would procure the insurance more cheaply because he handled considerable insurance business, and that Siegel could pay the insurance bill with the next installment. The furniture went to the warehouse around May 15 and was destroyed by fire around June 15. No insurance had been obtained. This was a promise to procure coverage, not a finding that insurance already existed or that the promise included free premiums. (Lines 175–177.)

## Judgment and appellate objections
Siegel sued in New York City's City Court for the loss caused by failure to insure. He prevailed below, and the Appellate Division certified a question of law for review by the Court of Appeals. Spear challenged consideration and McGrath's authority. The appellate court accepted that the promise had been made because evidence sustained the jury's finding. It did not resolve a fresh credibility dispute in Spear's favor. (Lines 172, 179–180.)

Spear's existing mortgage did not change its status as a bailee without pay. Under the historical bailment rule described by the court, an ordinary gratuitous bailee was not responsible for fire damage absent gross neglect, and no such element existed here. Liability therefore did not arise simply from storage, mortgage ownership, or the occurrence of a fire. It arose from the additional insurance undertaking linked to receipt of the property. (Lines 180–182.)

## Delivery linked the promise to an executed undertaking
McGrath promised insurance while the furniture was still in Siegel's possession. Delivery followed the promise and began execution of the entrusted undertaking. The court treated the insurance promise as a term of the overall bailment transaction. Once the defendant entered on that undertaking, it was obliged to perform that term as well as to care for the goods. The gratuitous character of the storage did not reduce the accepted undertaking to nothing. (Lines 182–183.)

The court distinguished Thorne v. Deas, where one joint vessel owner promised another that he would obtain insurance, failed to do so, and was held not liable after the vessel was lost. There the other owner transferred neither possession nor an interest in the vessel to the promisor. The court emphasized this missing transfer rather than overruling Thorne. Brawn v. Lyford received the same distinction. (Lines 183–186.)

Authorities concerning voluntarily accepted trusts and bailments supported the distinction between an unexecuted gratuitous promise and an undertaking actually entered upon. Rutgers v. Lucet described the confidence and entrusted performance as sufficient consideration, avoiding deception of an owner who might otherwise refuse the bailment. Hammond v. Hussey similarly required performance of an undertaking once begun. The court also discussed a gratuitous carrier who violated the agreed terms by entrusting money to someone else who lost it. These examples supported enforcement of the insurance term associated with this accepted bailment; they did not establish a general holding that every gratuitous promise becomes enforceable upon any reliance. (Lines 187–191.)

## Reserved questions and exact disposition
The executed-bailment reasoning supplied consideration, so the court did not decide whether Siegel's decision against obtaining coverage through his own agent independently surrendered a right sufficient to support the promise. It also expressly left open whether it would follow Thorne when the issue properly arose. These reservations matter because the textbook places Siegel within the history of reliance-based enforcement, while this actual opinion explains its result through the accepted bailment and associated undertaking. (Lines 192–193.)

The challenge to McGrath's agency authority was not raised by a sufficient exception. The court therefore did not establish an unrestricted substantive rule that all credit employees possess authority to insure customers' property. It affirmed the judgment with costs. The opinion does not state a separate dollar amount for the final recovery beyond describing recovery of the loss. (Lines 179, 195–201.)
