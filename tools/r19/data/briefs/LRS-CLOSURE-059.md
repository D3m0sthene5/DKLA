# Association of American Physicians & Surgeons v. Food and Drug Administration
13 F.4th 531 (6th Cir. 2021), decided September 9, 2021 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and litigation

The FDA had approved hydroxychloroquine decades earlier for conditions including malaria and lupus. It had not approved the drug to prevent or treat COVID-19. The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act regulated drug distribution without generally prohibiting physicians from prescribing an approved drug for an off-label use. During the pandemic, the federal government held hydroxychloroquine in the Strategic National Stockpile. A March 2020 emergency use authorization permitted distribution from that stockpile for certain hospitalized COVID-19 patients when a clinical trial was unavailable or participation infeasible. The authorization concerned the federal supply; it did not regulate every available dose or prohibit off-label prescribing by doctors. (Official opinion 2–3, 16.)

The Association sued the FDA and other federal health officials, alleging that hydroxychloroquine was useful for prophylaxis and early COVID-19 treatment and seeking broader access to the stockpile. It asserted First Amendment, equal-protection, and APA claims. Its allegations included injury from a potential conference cancellation, interference with physician members’ desired prescribing practices, and harm to patients unable to obtain the drug. The asserted medical efficacy was the Association’s position, not a judicial factual finding. The district court dismissed for lack of standing. During litigation, the FDA rescinded the authorization after determining that the available data did not establish effectiveness. (3–4.)

On appeal, the Association abandoned its own conference-injury theory and relied on associational standing for physicians and third-party standing for patients. The FDA also argued that rescission made the case moot. The circuit elected to decide standing first: Sinochem permitted choosing between nonmerits grounds, and the standing defect made resolving mootness unnecessary. Review of the standing dismissal was de novo. (4, 6.)

## Associational standing: criticism and binding doctrine

Hunt generally permits an association to sue if its members could sue individually, its asserted interests are germane to its purpose, and the claim and relief do not require individual participation. In Part III.A.1, Judges Murphy and Thapar questioned how that doctrine fit more recent Article III decisions. Judge Siler expressly declined to join this discussion as unnecessary. The criticism therefore had two judges’ support, whereas all three joined the application that resolved the case. Crucially, the opinion acknowledged that Hunt remained binding and applied it; the circuit did not abolish associational standing. (7–14, 22.)

The discussion raised three concerns. First, modern standing decisions emphasize a plaintiff’s personal injury and historical practice. The majority questioned whether members’ injuries alone supplied an adequate historical foundation for an uninjured association’s affirmative suit. It distinguished assignments, next-friend litigation, and other representative arrangements from associational suits, and it distinguished third-party standing—asserting someone else’s rights while possessing one’s own Article III injury—from associational standing without organizational injury. In particular, NAACP v. Alabama involved an organization defending against a demand for its own membership list, rather than an uninjured association initiating an affirmative suit solely on members’ injuries. (7–10.)

Second, the majority questioned how relief granted to the association would redress nonparty members’ injuries and how a judgment should bind them. It connected those questions to the plaintiff-specific nature of remedies, Rule 23 safeguards, nonparty preclusion, and debates over injunctions benefiting persons outside a suit. These were identified tensions, not newly adopted requirements that every association must proceed as a certified class. Third, Lexmark’s criticism of discretionary prudential standing rules raised questions about Hunt’s germaneness and individual-participation requirements. If a member had a concrete claim, the majority asked what permitted withholding jurisdiction for prudential reasons; conversely, if germaneness was a constitutional necessity, that needed an account consistent with personal injury. Those observations invited reconsideration by the Supreme Court rather than supplying the judgment here. (10–14.)

## Application to the physician members

The dispositive Hunt requirement was an identifiable member with individual standing. An organization could not rely on its size or on a probability that some unidentified member would be injured. It had to identify a member with a concrete actual or imminent injury traceable to the defendant and redressable by the requested relief. The complaint referred to a Michigan physician, “Dr. John Doe,” and another physician in an unidentified state who wanted to prescribe the drug more broadly. It advanced direct and indirect injury theories. (14.)

Because the FDA made a facial Rule 12(b)(1) challenge, the court accepted factual allegations but not legal conclusions. It held that Twombly and Iqbal plausibility applied to standing allegations, reasoning that standing must be supported like other matters on which the plaintiff bears the burden and that a complaint must clearly allege supporting facts. General conclusory fears were insufficient. (15–16.)

The direct theory misread the authorization as barring the doctors’ desired prescriptions. It actually governed distribution from the federal stockpile and left physicians free to prescribe approved hydroxychloroquine off-label through nongovernmental channels. The court recognized that a sufficiently imminent threat of punishment for prescribing could produce injury, but no such federal restriction appeared here. The Association then argued that federal control made the drug practically unavailable. The court reserved whether patient unavailability, without threatened punishment, would itself injure physicians. In any event, the complaint did not allege a government monopoly: it described a plentiful, easily manufactured supply and the feasibility of producing a million new doses daily. Those allegations defeated the new theory on the pleading record being reviewed. (16–17.)

The indirect theory was that state medical boards might discipline doctors for prescriptions outside the authorization’s limits. The complaint did not identify a Michigan law or professional standard the physician would violate, a Michigan enforcement action, or a relevant warning from that board. Allegations that unidentified boards might retaliate against unidentified doctors under unspecified standards did not plausibly establish a credible threat of discipline. (17–18.)

Causation independently failed. The defendants were federal officials, while a state board would make the disciplinary decision. The Association did not plausibly allege that any particular board was bound by the authorization, had cited it in its guidance, or would choose a different regulatory course without it. The proposed chain depended on speculation about independent actors. A Federation of State Medical Boards press release did not cure this defect: it cautioned about inappropriate prescribing for physicians themselves or their families, rather than forbidding prescribing for COVID-19 patients, and the Federation was an advocacy organization rather than a sovereign regulator. Nor did the Fifth Circuit’s earlier Texas Medical Board case control; there, the Association sued the very board allegedly disciplining its members, whose individual standing was undisputed. (18–20.)

## Patients, judgment, and concurrence

Third-party standing ordinarily permits a plaintiff with its own Article III standing to assert another person’s rights when the relationship is sufficiently close and the other person faces obstacles to protecting those rights. The Association had abandoned any claim that it itself suffered an Article III injury. Some authorities suggested an association might combine associational standing with its members’ third-party standing to vindicate patients’ rights. The court expressly left the permissibility of that combination undecided: no physician member had a sufficiently alleged personal injury, so even the intermediate member could not invoke third-party standing for patients. (20–21.)

The court affirmed the standing dismissal. Judge Murphy wrote; Judge Thapar joined throughout, and Judge Siler joined everything except Part III.A.1, separately explaining that the critique was unnecessary. The judgment was unanimous. The opinion contains no numbered judicial footnotes. It did not decide hydroxychloroquine’s efficacy, the merits of the constitutional or APA claims, or whether the rescinded authorization rendered the dispute moot. (1, 4, 21–22.)
