## Facts and procedural history

The Affordable Care Act originally required most individuals to maintain minimum essential health insurance coverage and imposed a monetary consequence for noncompliance. In 2017, Congress reduced that shared-responsibility payment to zero, effective beginning in tax year 2019, while leaving §5000A(a)’s coverage language in place. The IRS consequently stopped requiring individuals to report whether they maintained that coverage. Texas and seventeen other states sued federal officials, contending that the remaining coverage provision could no longer be justified under Congress’s taxing power, exceeded its other enumerated powers, and was inseverable from the remainder of the ACA. Neill Hurley and John Nantz joined as individual plaintiffs. (Court 1–3.)

The federal defendants initially supported the challengers’ position on constitutionality. California, fifteen other states, and the District of Columbia intervened to defend the law; the House of Representatives intervened at the appellate stage. The district court found the individual plaintiffs had standing and declared the coverage requirement unconstitutional and inseverable from the entire Act. It stayed its judgment pending appeal. The Fifth Circuit agreed on standing and the requirement’s unconstitutionality, but held that the district court had not adequately justified invalidating the entire Act. It remanded for further severability analysis. The Supreme Court granted review on the defenders’ petition and the challengers’ conditional cross-petition. (Court 3–4; Alito 6–7.)

The Supreme Court stopped at standing. It did **not** decide either whether the zero-penalty coverage provision was constitutional or whether it was severable from the rest of the ACA. Justice Breyer’s opinion was joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas, Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh, and Barrett. Thomas also concurred separately; Alito dissented with Gorsuch. The result was **7–2**. (Court 1–2, 16; separate-opinion headings.)

## Issue, holding, and governing rule

A plaintiff must establish personal injury fairly traceable to the defendant’s allegedly unlawful conduct and likely to be redressed by the requested relief. Neither the individuals nor the states established the necessary link to federal enforcement of the particular provision they attacked, §5000A(a). The Court assumed the individuals’ insurance expenditures could be pocketbook injuries, but held them insufficient to establish standing without that connection. The presence of statutory language, by itself, did not supply an enforceable government action or a justiciable constitutional dispute. (Court 4–6.)

This is not a rule that financial harm is inherently insufficient or that state plaintiffs cannot challenge the ACA. It is a decision about these plaintiffs’ injuries, the defendant officials’ powers, the challenged provision after the 2017 amendment, and the theories presented in this litigation.

## Why the individuals lacked standing

Hurley and Nantz said they bought insurance because §5000A(a) told them to do so. But with the payment set at zero, the IRS could not impose a penalty for noncompliance. The individuals identified no actual or threatened enforcement by the Commissioner, the Secretary of Health and Human Services, or any other federal official. Cases involving the mandate before Congress eliminated the monetary consequence did not answer that problem. Even if the statutory language influenced the individuals’ purchases, their expenditures were not traceable to the challenged conduct of the defendants. (Court 5–7.)

Redressability clarified the same difficulty. The individuals sought a declaration concerning the coverage provision and broader relief against other provisions asserted to be inseverable from it. As to the coverage provision itself, there was no enforcement to enjoin. The Declaratory Judgment Act did not create jurisdiction that Article III otherwise withheld. A ruling simply declaring unenforceable statutory language unconstitutional would amount to an advisory opinion without an appropriate remedy against these officials. The Court therefore refused to equate obtaining a favorable legal pronouncement with redressing injury in a genuine case or controversy. (Court 7–9.)

The Court also declined to consider an alternative theory advanced by the federal respondents for the first time in their merits brief. It similarly declined the dissent’s theory for the states, explaining that those theories had not been directly argued below or presented at the certiorari stage. That refusal matters: the Court did not hold that every conceivable inseverability-based standing theory fails. (Court 10.)

## Why the states’ expenditure theories failed

The states first asserted indirect costs: the coverage requirement allegedly encouraged residents to enroll in Medicaid, CHIP, and other state-supported programs, increasing state expenditures. The Court found both a legal and an evidentiary problem. The states had not linked the harm to any possible federal enforcement of §5000A(a). In addition, they had not adequately shown that the now-unenforceable provision caused residents to enroll in valuable benefit programs they would otherwise decline. (Court 10–12.)

A causal chain involving independent third parties is not categorically disallowed, but it requires evidence that they will respond predictably. The state declarations largely described costs or changes while a penalty still existed. Four referred to attribution to the mandate but discussed the earlier period; another employer-cost declaration was vague about timing. A Congressional Budget Office prediction that a small number might buy insurance from a desire to obey the law did not identify enrollment in the particular state programs or adequately establish the required connection. The Court required stronger evidence for this attenuated theory. The assigned paragraph accurately emphasizes this distinction between the challenged requirement and the independent attractiveness of the programs’ benefits. (Court 11–14.)

The states separately identified direct administrative and employment-related expenses, including reporting information to beneficiaries and the IRS, providing employee insurance, covering certain employees working thirty to thirty-nine hours, and continuing coverage for some people aging out of foster care. These obligations came from other ACA provisions operating independently of §5000A(a). Some incorporated §5000A(f)’s definition of coverage, but that was different from depending on the challenged coverage command in subsection (a). Showing that subsection (a) was unconstitutional would not itself show that those other provisions were unconstitutional. The Court therefore found those costs unconnected to enforcement of the particular provision challenged under the plaintiffs’ presented theories. (Court 14–16.)

## Thomas’s concurrence

Thomas joined the Court’s standing decision while criticizing the Court’s earlier ACA decisions. He distinguished his criticism of the law’s judicial history from the limits of the current lawsuit: these plaintiffs had not established injury traceable to unlawful action under the arguments they developed. (Thomas 1–4.)

He acknowledged that a properly framed theory of standing through inseverability might be supported in some circumstances by history and case law. But he would not decide it here. He emphasized the plaintiffs’ failure to develop that theory below and in their opening brief, the limited precedential significance of earlier decisions that had not addressed standing expressly, and the unresolved characterization of inseverability as merits analysis rather than an available judicial remedy. His two notes further distinguish the actual cost theories presented from the alternative theory and explain why ordinary statutory interpretation would be necessary to establish an arguable link. His concurrence is therefore not a categorical rejection of every inseverability-based claim. (Thomas 4–6 & nn.1–2.)

## Alito’s dissent, joined by Gorsuch

Alito would have found state standing. The states faced actual costs under enforceable reporting and employer-coverage provisions, and a judgment preventing those provisions’ enforcement would redress those costs. In his view, the required connection was to the government conduct challenged as unlawful, not proof that the challenged constitutional provision itself directly generated the costs. Requiring more risked confusing standing with success on the merits. (Alito 8–15 & nn.5–7.)

He argued that the states’ theory was that the mandate was unconstitutional and the burdensome provisions inseverable from it, making enforcement of those provisions unlawful. He developed comparisons with removal-power, treaty, and earlier statutory cases in which an injury-producing provision allegedly depended on a different invalid provision. He also disputed forfeiture, pointing to the complaint, lower-court arguments, and Supreme Court briefs. Thomas and Alito expressly disagreed about what had been adequately presented; the brief should preserve that disagreement rather than silently resolve it as a factual consensus. (Alito 15–22 & nn.8–9.)

Having found standing, Alito reached merits the Court did not decide. He concluded the zero-dollar mandate no longer qualified as a tax, could not be sustained under the Commerce or Necessary and Proper Clauses, and remained a command rather than a mere recommendation. He then concluded that the provisions burdening the plaintiff states were inseverable under either the approach he had used in the earlier ACA dissent or an ordinary statutory-interpretation approach. He would have granted relief preventing enforcement of those linked provisions against the plaintiff states, rather than treating a judicial judgment as erasing statutory text in the abstract. These are the two dissenters’ conclusions. (Alito 23–32 & n.10.)

## Exact disposition and course significance

The Court **reversed the Fifth Circuit’s judgment in respect to standing, vacated the judgment, and remanded the cases with instructions to dismiss**. The dismissal resolved the litigation without a Supreme Court merits determination concerning the coverage provision’s constitutionality or the ACA’s severability. (Court 16.)

For standing analysis, identify the asserted injury, the official conduct being challenged, and the remedy’s relationship to that injury. Actual expenditures do not alone prove traceability to the challenged provision. This case also illustrates a disagreement over whether an inseverability theory supplies that link, and the difference between declining an undeveloped theory and deciding it cannot ever support standing.
