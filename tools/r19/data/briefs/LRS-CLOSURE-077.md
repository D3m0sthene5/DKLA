# National Labor Relations Board v. Universal Camera Corp. (on remand)
190 F.2d 429 (2d Cir. 1951) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and procedural setting
On July 13, 1951, the Second Circuit reversed the NLRB's order and directed dismissal of its complaint. Learned Hand wrote for the court; Chief Judge Swan participated without a separate writing, and Jerome Frank concurred in the result while objecting to Hand's interpretation of the Supreme Court's instructions. This is the postremand circuit judgment, distinct from both the circuit's earlier enforcement decision, 179 F.2d 749, and the Supreme Court's intervening decision, 340 U.S. 474. The court reconsidered the same record rather than receiving new evidence. (429–431 & majority nn. 1–2.)

The Board had treated employee Chairman's discharge on January 24, 1944, as retaliation for his testimony two months earlier. Its examiner found otherwise. Chairman and Weintraub had quarreled at the end of December; Politzer then told Weintraub that Chairman would resign. The examiner did not believe Chairman had actually announced that intention, but found that Politzer either thought he had or said so to smooth over the quarrel. When Chairman testified that Politzer asked on January 11 whether he would resign, the court found no contradiction: Politzer could naturally have sought confirmation of an earlier impression. The examiner's account explained why Weintraub waited before complaining to Kende about Chairman's undermining his influence in the factory. (430–431.)

## The competing accounts and the remand standard
The Board's retaliation theory rested on a joint plan between Kende and Weintraub, and alternatively on Kende's independent use of Weintraub's complaint to retaliate against Chairman. The Board argued that its position in evaluating witnesses in a labor dispute was superior to the reviewing court's. Hand rejected that claim for the factual issues in this record, which he regarded as outside the Board's specialized expertise. He read the amended statute and the Supreme Court's opinion as requiring a less deferential attitude toward findings on matters of ordinary knowledge, even though the court had already examined the whole record in its original decision. He acknowledged that locating the boundary of agency expertise could be difficult. (430 & majority n. 3.)

The other required change concerned the examiner. The earlier circuit opinion had treated the examiner's findings as effectively nonexistent for judicial review because the court thought their intermediate weight impossible to measure. Hand conceded that this went too far. Findings based on the witness's bearing and delivery matter particularly when the printed testimony leaves a rational choice between accounts. He formulated the operative instruction as requiring a very substantial preponderance in recorded testimony before overriding an examiner's findings about veracity. This was Hand's application of the remand, not a quotation of an identical categorical rule from the Supreme Court. (430.)

## Why enforcement failed
Accepting the examiner's explanation of Weintraub's delay made the asserted joint retaliatory plan incredible to the court. Hand emphasized that the record supported Politzer's attempt to postpone or soften the quarrel, and said the Board did not contest the contrary reasons given in the court's first opinion. The alternative theory—that Kende independently seized the complaint to retaliate—also failed once the examiner's assessment received its proper weight. Motive is difficult to disentangle; the hearing examiner's observation of witnesses was therefore especially important. An inherently unreasonable account could be rejected notwithstanding convincing delivery, but the court did not find such an account here. (430–431.)

The examiner specifically was not persuaded that Kende acted from animus against Chairman's testimony rather than from an evaluation of Weintraub's request on its merits. Hand held that this finding should have tipped the scale. He also questioned whether the Board had intended to reject it independently of the alleged joint plan: the Board's own note 7 linked its confidence in discrimination to the conclusion that the December episode was merely a pretext. That note is quoted and discussed in the circuit opinion; it is not a separate footnote written by this court. Upon the whole record, with the examiner's findings now credited as the court understood the Supreme Court to require, the earlier enforcement disposition was wrong. The exact judgment was reversal of the Board's order with the complaint to be dismissed. (431.)

## Frank's concurrence and the limits of the majority's language
Frank agreed with the outcome but regarded Hand's account of the Supreme Court's criticism as too sweeping. The Supreme Court had expressly recognized that the circuit had considered the whole record and had not acted merely as the Board's echo. For Frank, the original error was disregarding the examiner's contrary findings. The revised statute neither put appellate review of the Board on the same footing as review of a trial court nor authorized trial de novo of agency cases outside the agency's expertise. A reviewing court still could not replace the Board's rational choice between conflicting views simply because it would have chosen differently. (431–432.)

Frank likewise thought Hand's treatment of credibility could constrain the Board more than the Supreme Court intended. He understood Hand as permitting departure from an examiner's credibility findings only through specialized knowledge or when the examiner was absurdly naive; the Supreme Court, however, had denied that the Board could reverse examiner findings only under a clearly erroneous standard. Frank distinguished testimonial or primary inferences, grounded in believing a witness's testimony after observing that witness, from derivative or secondary inferences drawn from the facts thus established. The Board could draw its own rational derivative inferences, and courts should respect those inferences. He contrasted this with review of a judge in a nonjury case, where other rational derivative inferences might be substituted. His four notes identify supporting judicial discussions of that distinction. (432 & concurrence nn. 1–4.)

The assigned account is editorial note 1 on textbook PDF 138, printed page 918. It follows the conclusion and voting statement of the separate Supreme Court decision.
