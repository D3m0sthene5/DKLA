reported. Assigned casebook pp. 334–335, first October scan PDF pp. 57–58. Full judicial opinion: Full

Parties
Bi-Metallic Investment Company owned real estate in Denver. It sued to prevent the State Board of
Equalization and the Colorado Tax Commission from implementing an order increasing the valuation of
Denver's taxable property. The suit also sought to prevent Pitcher, Denver's assessor, from obeying the order.
Although named as a defendant, the assessor presented a brief on the property owner's side in the Supreme
Court. 239 U.S. at 443–444.
The challenged decision concerned the level of taxable valuation throughout Denver. Bi-Metallic's ownership
supplied its stake in the increased assessment base, but the order did not select its parcel for an individualized
adjustment. The distinction between a citywide valuation principle and a determination of a particular owner's
special liability controls the Court's analysis. Id. at 444–446.
Material Facts
The state tax boards ordered a forty-percent increase in the valuation of all taxable property in Denver. The
owner asserted that it had received no opportunity to be heard before the order and argued that collection of
taxes based on the increase would deprive it of property without due process. The opinion does not report an
adjudication of the correct value of Bi-Metallic's parcel or find that its property was individually undervalued.
Id. at 443–444.
For purposes of deciding the federal question, the Court assumed the strongest version of the hearing objection:
neither the owner, the assessor, nor a representative of the city and county received a hearing opportunity apart
from whatever opportunity followed from the legally fixed meeting time of the boards. This was an assumption
used to decide whether a hearing was constitutionally necessary, rather than a detailed factual determination
that no one knew of the meeting. Id. at 444.
The Court recognized that a uniform increase could produce injustice if a parcel had already been valued at full
worth while other property was undervalued. It nevertheless proceeded on the assumption that owners could
use the ordinary protest-and-appeal process to challenge a parcel's departure from the prevailing valuation rate.
For the issue presented, the owners therefore stood alike with respect to the general adjustment. That premise
separates a challenge to a particular assessment from a demand for an individual voice in deciding the
countywide valuation principle. Id. at 444–445.

Procedural History
The company sought an injunction against implementation of the valuation order. The Colorado Supreme
Court sustained the order and directed dismissal of the suit. The company obtained Supreme Court review by
writ of error, asserting a violation of the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. The federal hearing
question was the only issue the Court decided. Id. at 443–444.
The parties also suggested that the state court's interpretation of local law was unexpected, or that the suit could
have been rejected because an adequate legal remedy made equitable relief unnecessary. The Supreme Court
treated both matters as questions of state law. Because the state court had entertained the merits, the Court did
not manufacture an alternative state-law ground of dismissal and instead decided the preserved constitutional
objection. Id. at 444.
Issue
Does the Fourteenth Amendment require an individual property owner, or a representative of the locality, to
receive an opportunity to be heard before state tax authorities make a general determination increasing the
valuation of all taxable property in a city by the same percentage? Does Londoner's hearing requirement for
parcel-specific special assessments extend to that decision? Id. at 444–446.
Rule and Holding
Due process did not require an individual hearing before the general forty-percent increase. A determination
applying to the community on a common basis may be made without giving every affected person a direct
opportunity to argue against its adoption. The Court distinguished a general principle governing all
assessments from a determination affecting a relatively small number of owners on individual grounds. Id. at
445–446.
The constitutional protection identified for broad policy decisions was the affected public's immediate or
indirect control over those who make the rule. The Court did not hold that all disputes labeled taxation or all
decisions made by administrative boards are exempt from hearing requirements. Londoner's
individualized-assessment rule remained expressly recognized. Id. at 445–446.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the state court's judgment. The dismissal of the company's injunction suit
therefore stood, and the challenged valuation order was not invalidated for lack of a hearing. The Court did not
substitute a different valuation percentage, remand for an individual hearing on the general increase, or
determine the correct assessment of the company's particular parcel. Id. at 446.
Reasoning
The Court framed the matter as a common question concerning the principle on which local assessments had
been made. The state could decide that Denver had generally undervalued taxable property compared with the
rest of Colorado. Once parcel-specific departures were left to the ordinary protest process, the asserted right
was a right of every owner to participate individually in deciding that common issue. The nature of the
determination, rather than simply the amount of the owner's financial loss, made individual hearings
unnecessary. Id. at 444–445.
Holmes grounded the conclusion partly in the practical requirements of representative government. General
rules often affect property substantially, including through taxation, without permitting every affected owner to
appear before their adoption. Requiring universal individualized argument would transform ordinary governing

Political accountability supplied the opinion's affirmative explanation of how rights are protected in this
setting. Individuals exercise immediate or remote power over the officials making generally applicable rules.
The point does not depend on each affected person personally electing each tax official, or on a factual finding
that Denver's residents prevailed in the relevant political debate. The opinion treats the broader system of
accountability as the appropriate constitutional mechanism for this type of decision. Id.
The Court tested the claimed hearing right by comparing the order with a general change in the rate of taxation
or an equivalent determination made by the state constitution itself. An owner would have no constitutional
right to speak individually before either measure took effect. Because the proper state machinery was assumed
to have been used, the assignment of the decision to the tax boards did not create a different hearing
requirement simply because administrators implemented the common policy. Id. at 445–446.
Londoner remained compatible with that conclusion. There, the local board determined who should bear the
cost of a particular paving project and how much particular parcels had benefited. A relatively small group was
exceptionally affected on individual grounds. An owner's argument and proof could address that individual
allocation. Bi-Metallic concerned the general basis of all assessments in the locality. Holmes expressly
preserved the former decision while identifying why its reasoning did not control the latter. Id. at 446.
The opinion's acknowledgment of possible unfairness did not lead it to guarantee a hearing on the broad
increase. It separated a general determination from a property's deviation from the ordinary assessment rate, for
which the owner was assumed to have the usual protest and appeal opportunity. That assumption is material:
the Court did not approve a procedure that permanently deprived an owner of every avenue to contest an
individualized valuation mistake. Id. at 444–445.
Separate Opinions
No concurrence or dissent is reported in the judicial text consulted. Holmes's authorship of this opinion does
not supply reasons for his unexplained dissent in Londoner. Bi-Metallic's own express treatment of Londoner is
the proper source for the distinction drawn here. Id. at 446.
Notes
The casebook places the opinions together to introduce the development of adjudication and rulemaking. Those
modern statutory classifications should be applied carefully: this 1915 case interpreted constitutional due
process and did not construe the later federal Administrative Procedure Act. The holding therefore does not
itself answer whether a statute requires notice, comments, a record hearing, or other procedures for a particular
agency rule. Casebook pp. 334–336; 239 U.S. at 444–446.
An examination comparison should identify both the number of people affected and whether the government is
resolving individual grounds of liability. The opinion supplies no numerical cutoff after which hearings cease
to be required. Its actual comparison combines a generally shared valuation principle with the absence of
exceptional, parcel-specific treatment in the challenged order. Id. at 445–446.
The order changed assessed valuations by forty percent. It was not described as a direct forty-percent increase
in the nominal tax rate, although the Court used a hypothetical change in tax rates to explain its reasoning. The
decision upheld dismissal of a procedural challenge to the general order; it did not prove that every resulting
tax bill was economically correct. Id. at 443–446.
