January 19, 1976; decided March 23, 1976. Justice Rehnquist delivered the opinion, joined by every
participating Justice. Justice Blackmun took no part. Assigned excerpt: Civil Procedure casebook pp.

Parties
Wetzel and the other respondents were the employee-plaintiffs challenging Liberty Mutual’s treatment of
women under Title VII. Liberty Mutual was their employer, the defendant, the party that appealed the liability
ruling, and the Supreme Court petitioner. The case did not involve an employee suing an independent insurer
merely because it supplied coverage. The challenged insurance benefits and maternity-leave regulations were
Liberty Mutual’s own employment practices. The employees sought relief for themselves and the class
identified in their complaint. 424 U.S. at 739–741.
Material Facts
The disputed policies denied disability-income benefits for pregnancy-related disabilities and required women
to return to work within three months of childbirth or face termination. The plaintiffs alleged sex
discrimination and sought changes to employment practices, an injunction against unlawful conduct, monetary
relief including backpay and other requested damages, costs, and attorney’s fees. The complaint therefore
sought concrete remedies beyond a judicial pronouncement that the policies were unlawful. The Court
reproduced these demands to determine what remained unresolved after the district court’s order; it did not
decide which requested remedies were substantively available. Id. at 740–742.
After extensive discovery, the plaintiffs sought partial summary judgment on liability alone. The district court
found no material factual dispute and ruled that the pregnancy-related policies violated Title VII. It also
addressed hiring and promotion practices, but that portion generated a separate appeal that was not before the
Supreme Court. The appeal in this case concerned the pregnancy and benefit policies and an order that awarded
no injunction, damages, or fees. Id. at 740–742 & n.1.
The district judge initially stated that an injunction would issue and invited a proposed form of order. Liberty
Mutual filed a notice of appeal and sought a stay. The judge then withheld issuance of an injunction and
attempted to make the liability determination immediately appealable through Rule 54(b). The precise contents
of that order, rather than its designation as a final judgment, controlled the appellate-jurisdiction problem. Id. at
741–743.
Procedural History
On January 9, 1974, the Western District of Pennsylvania granted the employees’ motion for partial summary
judgment. Liberty Mutual moved for reconsideration. On February 20, the court denied that motion and
directed entry of final judgment on the policy violations, expressly finding no just reason for delay under Rule
54(b). The court simultaneously stated that it was withholding the requested injunctive order. No other
requested relief was finally adjudicated. Id. at 740–742.
Liberty Mutual appealed. The Third Circuit regarded the order as final under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 and affirmed
the substantive ruling. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and heard argument on the merits. Neither party

Issue
Can a liability-only partial summary judgment become a final, appealable decision under § 1291 merely
because the district court invokes Rule 54(b), when the complaint asserts one legal right but requests several
forms of relief and none has been awarded? If ordinary finality and Rule 54(b) do not authorize review, can
either the injunction provision of § 1292(a)(1) or the certified-interlocutory-appeal procedure of § 1292(b)
sustain this employer’s appeal? Id. at 742–745.
Rule and Holding
A determination of liability that leaves damages and other requested relief unresolved is interlocutory. Rule
54(b) does not permit a court to convert such a determination into a final decision merely by reciting that there
is no just reason for delay. The rule requires a final disposition of at least a qualifying claim or party in an
action to which it applies. As presented here, the assertion of one legal right under one theory and set of facts
remained a single claim despite the requests for multiple remedies. No qualifying final disposition had
occurred. Id. at 742–744 & nn.2–4.
Neither proposed interlocutory route supplied jurisdiction. No injunction had issued against Liberty Mutual; an
assumed refusal to grant the employees’ requested injunction did not give the employer an appeal under §
1292(a)(1). Section 1292(b) also was not satisfied: the record did not show a timely application for permission
to appeal, and the court of appeals had not exercised the discretionary permission required by that statute. The
Supreme Court held that the Third Circuit could not adjudicate the merits of this appeal. Id. at 744–746.
Judgment
The Supreme Court vacated the Third Circuit’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss Liberty
Mutual’s appeal. This was a dismissal of the premature appeal, not a dismissal of the employees’ underlying
action or a merits ruling that the employer’s policies were lawful. The district court’s interlocutory liability
determination and the unresolved requests for relief remained matters for further district-court proceedings. Id.
at 740, 746.
Reasoning
Appellate jurisdiction required an independent examination even though the parties had briefed and argued the
merits without objection. Agreement or silence could not supply statutory authority to review a nonappealable
order. The Supreme Court’s first task was therefore to identify what the district court had actually decided and
whether a statute permitted immediate review. The prior grant of certiorari did not settle that antecedent
question about the Third Circuit’s authority. Id. at 739–740.
The employees had obtained a favorable liability ruling, but the district court had not completed the action. It
had neither enjoined the practices nor awarded damages or fees. The unresolved remedies were express parts of
the complaint, not hypothetical possibilities for some later lawsuit. Accordingly, the litigation still required
judicial action before the employees obtained the relief they sought. Calling the liability ruling a judgment did
not eliminate those remaining matters. Id. at 740–742.
Counsel suggested that the order could at least be regarded as a declaratory judgment. The Court answered
conditionally: a complaint seeking only a declaration would present a different case, but these plaintiffs sought
additional relief that remained undecided. Even accepting the proposed characterization could not make the

actual action complete. The holding therefore turns on the claims and requested relief before the court rather
than a categorical rule that declaratory judgments are never final. Id. at 742.
Rule 54(b) did not cure the problem. The rule enables entry of a final judgment as to fewer than all qualifying
claims or parties; it does not authorize immediate appeals from every final resolution of a legal issue. The
district judge had made the required recital, but the prerequisite disposition was missing. Separating liability
from remedy within this claim did not produce two distinct claims suitable for certification. Id. at 742–744.
The Court carefully limited its explanation of a claim for relief. It did not attempt a comprehensive definition
for every application of the Federal Rules. It was enough that a complaint asserting one legal right does not
become a multiple-claim action merely because it requests several remedies for its violation. The opinion also
acknowledged the rule’s provision for judgments finally disposing of fewer than all parties, while explaining
that this feature was not implicated here. Those qualifications prevent reading the decision as an exhaustive
account of Rule 54(b). Id. at 743 & nn.3–4.
The liability ruling, considered without its attempted certification, was ordinary partial summary judgment.
Such a ruling narrows further proceedings but does not terminate them when damages or other relief remain
open. Its usefulness to the district court does not create appellate finality. This distinction explains how a court
can conclusively decide liability for purposes of conducting the case while the decision remains interlocutory
for purposes of § 1291. Id. at 744.
Section 1292(a)(1) supplied no alternative for this appellant. Had an injunction actually issued against Liberty
Mutual, the statute would have allowed an interlocutory appeal from that order despite other unresolved
matters. Here, however, the district court withheld an injunction. Even assuming that withholding could count
as a refusal appealable by the employees, it did not constitute a denial of an injunction sought by Liberty
Mutual. The employer could not obtain review of liability by invoking an injunction ruling adverse only to its
opponents. Id. at 744–745.
Section 1292(b) required both district-court certification of an appropriate controlling legal question and
permission from the court of appeals. The Court considered whether the Rule 54(b) findings might amount to
substantial compliance with the first step, but it did not finally endorse that proposition. The record lacked the
required application within the statutory ten-day period. Moreover, the Third Circuit had assumed it was
obliged to hear an appeal as of right under § 1291, which did not establish that it would have granted
discretionary permission under § 1292(b). One procedural route could not simply be relabeled as the other after
the fact. Id. at 745.
Allowing the attempted procedure would make almost any liability ruling immediately appealable upon a
district judge’s designation. Congress had already balanced finality against the need for limited interlocutory
review through §§ 1291 and 1292. The Court enforced those specified routes rather than permitting the parties
and trial judge to create a general additional avenue. The result preserved further merits litigation while
removing the appellate judgment entered without jurisdiction. Id. at 745–746.
Separate Opinions
No separate opinion is reported. Justice Rehnquist wrote for all participating Justices, and Justice Blackmun
took no part in the consideration or decision. The unanimity concerned appellate jurisdiction and the proper
procedural disposition; the Court did not decide the Title VII merits on which it had initially heard argument.
Id. at 739–740, 746.

Notes
The full opinion supplies the exact certification language and the footnotes limiting the discussion of claims
and parties. Those details sharpen the assigned excerpt’s central distinction between a legal issue, a claim for
relief, and the remedies sought on that claim. In applying the case, examine the operative order and what
remains to be adjudicated before considering the judge’s finality label. Casebook pp. 1214–1217; 424 U.S. at
741–745 & nn.2–4.
The case also requires attention to who appealed. Liberty Mutual, not the employees, challenged the liability
ruling. An argument that the court refused the employees’ injunction does not explain why the employer could
appeal. Finally, the judgment leaves the underlying liability determination unreviewed on its merits; vacating
the appellate affirmance is not equivalent to reversing the district court’s substantive conclusion. Id. at
744–746.
