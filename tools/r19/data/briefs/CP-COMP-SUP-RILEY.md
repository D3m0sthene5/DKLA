# Riley v. Bondi

**Citation and court:** 606 U.S. 259 (2025), Supreme Court of the United States, No. 23–1270, decided June 26, 2025. Justice Alito wrote the Court's opinion. Roberts, Thomas, Kavanaugh, and Barrett joined in full. Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, and Jackson joined only Part II–B, making the nonjurisdictional-deadline holding unanimous while the finality holding divided the Court five to four. Thomas concurred separately. Sotomayor dissented in part, joined by Kagan and Jackson throughout and Gorsuch except Part IV. All complete writings and the Court's and dissent's substantive asterisk notes were read.

**Material facts and procedure.** Pierre Riley, a Jamaican citizen, entered the United States on a visitor visa in 1995 and overstayed. He was later convicted of drug-trafficking conspiracy and a firearm offense and sentenced to twenty-five years, obtaining release in January 2021. DHS then used the streamlined aggravated-felony removal process and issued a FARO on January 26, directing removal to Jamaica. Riley did not dispute removability from the United States; he sought Convention Against Torture protection against return to Jamaica, alleging likely murder by a politically connected drug kingpin. An immigration judge ultimately found Riley credible and granted CAT deferral. DHS appealed, and the Board of Immigration Appeals vacated that relief on May 31, 2022. Riley petitioned for judicial review three days later, well within thirty days of the CAT decision but long after the FARO. Id. at 263–266, 278, 282–285.

The Fourth Circuit dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. It held that the FARO, rather than the later CAT ruling, was the final removal order triggering the thirty-day clock, and that the clock was jurisdictional. The Supreme Court granted review of both circuit conflicts. Because the government supported Riley on both questions, the Court appointed an amicus to defend the judgment below. Id. at 265–266. The Court did not decide whether Riley actually met the substantive requirements for CAT relief.

**Questions and holdings.** First, does the BIA's withholding-only decision supply the final removal order or postpone finality of the FARO for § 1252(b)(1)'s filing deadline? The five-Justice majority answered no: the FARO was final upon issuance, and filing within thirty days of the later CAT denial did not satisfy that deadline. Id. at 266–272. Second, is the deadline jurisdictional? All nine answered no: it is a claim-processing rule. Because the government declined to press untimeliness, that rule did not require dismissal here. Id. at 272–277. The two answers concern different questions; unanimity on the second does not extend to the first.

**Majority's finality reasoning.** The statutory definition of an order of deportation, incorporated into an order of removal, covers an order determining deportability or directing deportation. The FARO did both. Finality under § 1101(a)(47)(B) ordinarily turns on BIA affirmance or expiration of the time for BIA review. Because Riley could not obtain administrative review of the FARO, the majority treated that period as expiring immediately upon issuance. Id. at 267.

Nasrallah distinguishes CAT orders from removal orders: a CAT order concerns the permissible destination and does not invalidate or merge into the removal determination. Guzman Chavez similarly treats removal as administratively final despite withholding-only proceedings. The majority found those decisions instructive despite their different ultimate issues. Id. at 268–270.

The government argued that consolidated judicial review requires finality to await the CAT result. The majority separated when a petition must be filed from what issues can eventually be adjudicated within it. It agreed that CAT review must await a decision and occur in a single appellate proceeding, but rejected the asserted implication for the filing clock. It recognized practical problems and suggested notice, abeyance, expeditious proceedings, and declining to enforce the filing rule as responses. Those suggestions did not change the finality holding. Id. at 270–272. The Court's asterisk note acknowledges that withholding proceedings often last months or years, contrary to the streamlined process's expected operation. Id. at 272 n.*.

**Unanimous deadline reasoning.** Jurisdictional rules require courts to inquire independently and generally cannot be waived or forfeited. Congress must clearly indicate that status; mandatory timing language alone does not suffice. Section 1252(b)(1) directs petitioners to file within thirty days, without defining court power or invoking jurisdiction, and its statutory placement does not supply the necessary signal. Id. at 273–275.

Stone's description of an earlier deadline as jurisdictional was loose usage predating the Court's disciplined distinction. It was not the definitive interpretation supported by a century of reasoning about actual jurisdictional consequences that controlled in John R. Sand & Gravel. Later decisions repeatedly treat mandatory filing deadlines as nonjurisdictional absent the required clear statement. Id. at 275–277. This holding does not itself decide that equitable tolling is available under § 1252(b)(1); the government's decision not to press the deadline resolves its enforcement effect in Riley's case.

**Thomas's concurrence.** Thomas joined the Court fully but identified a possible separate jurisdictional problem. FARRA permits judicial review of CAT claims as part of review of a final removal order, and Riley's petition identified only the BIA's CAT denial. Thomas urged the Fourth Circuit to determine whether it could review that order without a petition seeking review of the removal order. The Court's resolution of the timing ground did not settle every potential jurisdictional defect. Id. at 277–281. This is Thomas's suggested inquiry, not a majority holding that CAT review is jurisdictionally barred in Riley's case.

**Sotomayor's partial dissent.** The dissent agreed that the deadline was nonjurisdictional but would hold that the FARO did not become final for appellate purposes until the CAT proceedings concluded. Congress both guaranteed review of CAT claims and required consolidation with removal review. The dissent argued that forcing a petition before the CAT decision existed defeated those instructions. Id. at 281–289 & 282 n.*.

The statutory finality definition, in its view, does not directly govern a FARO unreviewable by the BIA: a nonexistent review period cannot expire instantly. Ordinary finality principles, the whole statutory scheme, and the policy against piecemeal appeals instead support awaiting the last reviewable agency decision. Administrative finality for detention is distinct from appellate finality, and Nasrallah and Guzman Chavez did not resolve this filing question. Id. at 289–302. The dissent distinguished the CAT order's identity from its effect on when the associated removal order becomes appealably final.

In Part IV, which Gorsuch did not join, Sotomayor warned of protective petitions, notice and abeyance problems, and possible consequences for other removal proceedings. She urged consideration of equitable tolling and asserted due-process notice requirements. Those propositions belong to the partial dissent; they are not holdings adopted by the Court. Id. at 302–304.

**Exact disposition.** The Supreme Court **vacated the Fourth Circuit's judgment and remanded for further proceedings**. Id. at 277. The government declined to enforce the nonjurisdictional deadline even though the majority found the petition late. The Court did not grant CAT relief or conclusively resolve the additional jurisdictional question Thomas identified.

**Course significance.** Identify the triggering final order separately from the legal consequences of missing its deadline. An untimely petition can escape dismissal on a waived or unpressed claim-processing objection without becoming timely, and that does not eliminate other jurisdictional prerequisites. The assigned editorial footnote is essential to avoid describing this entire decision as unanimous.
