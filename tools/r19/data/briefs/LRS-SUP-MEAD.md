# United States v. Mead Corp.
533 U.S. 218 (2001), decided June 18, 2001 | Legislation and the Regulatory State | Agency interpretation and the historical boundaries of Chevron

## The imported planners and Customs' changed classification

Mead Corporation imported day planners: three-ring binders containing pages for daily schedules, notes, telephone numbers, addresses, calendars, and similar information. The Harmonized Tariff Schedule placed various paper articles under a common heading but divided them into two relevant subcategories. Bound diaries, notebooks, address books, and specified similar articles carried a four-percent duty at the relevant time; the residual category was duty free. From 1989 through 1993, Customs repeatedly placed the planners in the duty-free category. In January 1993, it changed its position and issued a Headquarters ruling classifying them as bound diaries. 533 U.S. at 224–225.

The first letter gave little explanation. After Mead protested, Customs Headquarters issued a carefully reasoned but unpublished letter reaching the same conclusion. Customs used the broader of two Oxford English Dictionary definitions of diary, encompassing a book with printed dates for future memoranda rather than only a narrative of past events. It considered that definition consistent with the planners' commercial identity. It also read bound as fastening more generally, including metal or plastic fittings, rather than traditional bookbinding. Id. at 225.

The dispute thus concerned both ordinary statutory classification and a prior institutional question: how much authority should a court give Customs' classification of an ambiguous tariff category? That question was consequential even if judges and Customs could reasonably disagree about the best meaning of diary and bound.

## The administrative system and the path to the Supreme Court

Federal law authorized Customs to classify merchandise under Treasury rules and regulations and authorized the Secretary to establish procedures for pre-entry binding rulings. Customs regulations allowed ruling letters responding to particular transactions. The letters represented the agency's official position and bound Customs personnel, but their practical and legal effects were limited. They applied to goods identical to the submitted sample or description, could be modified, and ordinarily did not create reliance rights for other importers. Other persons were warned not to assume that a ruling would govern their transactions. Id. at 221–223.

Forty-six port-of-entry offices, as well as Customs Headquarters, could issue letters. Customs produced approximately ten thousand to fifteen thousand yearly. Many contained almost no explanation beyond a description and classification. They ordinarily were not issued through notice and comment and needed only to be available for public inspection. Statutory amendments effective after the controversy added notice and comment for specified modifications or revocations, but not for every initial classification. Id. at 223–224, 233–234.

After another unsuccessful administrative protest, Mead sued in the Court of International Trade. That court granted the government's summary-judgment motion and adopted Customs' reasoning without addressing deference. While Mead's appeal was pending, the Supreme Court decided United States v. Haggar Apparel Co., recognizing Chevron treatment for Customs regulations. The government argued that classification letters deserved the same treatment. Id. at 225–226.

The Federal Circuit rejected that analogy. It regarded the letters as different from regulations: no initial notice and comment, no general force of law, and no intention to define all importers' obligations beyond the specific transaction. It gave the challenged letter no deference and rejected Customs' readings of diary and bound. The Supreme Court granted review to address the limits of Chevron deference, rather than simply substitute its preferred tariff classification. Id. at 226–227.

## The historical issue and the Court's two-part holding

Justice Souter's Court held that an agency interpretation qualified for Chevron treatment when Congress had delegated general authority to make rules carrying the force of law and the agency had issued the interpretation in the exercise of that authority. Formal adjudication and notice-and-comment rulemaking could demonstrate that delegation, but the Court left room for other indications of comparable congressional intent. The particular Customs letter failed that threshold. Id. at 226–231.

That did not mean it deserved no consideration. Under Skidmore v. Swift & Co., the letter remained eligible for respect according to its persuasive force. The lower courts had to assess that possibility in the first instance. The Court therefore vacated the Federal Circuit's judgment and remanded without finally deciding whether the planners were taxable bound diaries. Id. at 234–239.

## Why delegation and the form of agency action mattered

The majority distinguished two bases for judicial respect. An agency's informed experience, consistency, careful reasoning, and expertise could make its answer persuasive without making it controlling. Chevron, in the doctrine governing the case in 2001, identified an additional reason to accept a reasonable agency answer: congressional delegation of authority to resolve statutory gaps with legal force. Such delegation could be express or implied from the statutory setting and the authority Congress conferred. Id. at 227–229.

An authorization to issue regulations or decide matters through sufficiently formal procedures was a strong signal of lawmaking authority because such procedures promoted deliberation and fairness. Most prior Chevron cases involved notice-and-comment rules or formal adjudication. But the Court expressly refused to make those formats an absolute condition. NationsBank had received Chevron treatment without them, and the absence of notice and comment did not alone decide Mead. Id. at 229–231 and nn. 11–13.

The analysis was therefore not simply whether Customs had any legislative rulemaking authority. It did: Haggar concerned such regulations. The question was whether the classification letter invoked authority of the relevant kind. An agency may issue legally binding regulations through one mechanism and nonlegislative interpretations through another. Id. at 231–232 and n. 14.

## Why this ruling letter fell outside Chevron

Neither the statutory language nor Customs' practice indicated that ordinary classification letters should establish law binding beyond their individual transactions. The statute's reference to binding pre-entry rulings did not show that the rulings were legislative rules for third parties; indeed, the challenged ruling was not itself in that pre-entry category. Potential precedential usefulness also did not establish the necessary force of law, because interpretive rules could be useful precedents without qualifying for Chevron. Id. at 232 and n. 15.

The majority also considered the judicial-review scheme. The Court of International Trade could consider new grounds and develop its own record, rather than simply review Customs' record. Though Customs' decision enjoyed a statutory presumption of correctness, the majority regarded the scheme as inconsistent with the asserted general force of law. This was one part of its statutory analysis, not an assertion that every de novo factual proceeding precluded all agency authority. Id. at 232–233 and n. 16.

Agency practice reinforced the conclusion. The letters' limited reliance effects, routine informality, large volume, and distribution among many offices did not resemble the exercise of general lawmaking authority. Headquarters had supplied substantial reasoning here, but Congress had not created a separate category of Headquarters classification letters carrying greater authority. The later modification-and-revocation procedures likewise did not establish a generally legislative character or a patchwork in which otherwise similar letters had different legal status. Id. at 233–234.

## What remained under Skidmore

The Federal Circuit's categorical refusal to give the letter any weight went too far. Customs administered an intricate national classification system and could contribute specialized knowledge to whether a ring-bound planner fit the statutory categories. The letter's reasoning, thoroughness, expertise, consistency with other positions, and any other persuasive features could matter. Its change from earlier treatment was relevant to that assessment but did not mechanically settle it. Id. at 234–235.

The majority rejected Scalia's proposed either-or choice between Chevron and no deference. Statutes authorize agencies to act in many ways, and Congress does not necessarily attach identical consequences to all of them. Judicial responses should reflect that variety. The Court acknowledged that its boundary was not a hard-edged rule and that harder cases would require reasoned comparison; it defended this approach as more faithful to different statutory arrangements. Id. at 235–238 and nn. 18–19.

The majority also disputed Scalia's reliance on the government's defense of the letter as proof of authoritativeness. The challenged letter had not been issued by the Commissioner with Treasury approval, as the regulation in Haggar had been. Treating high-level approval in litigation as the event triggering Chevron would make deference attach to the litigation brief rather than to the classification letter as such. Id. at 238 n. 19.

## Scalia's dissent

Justice Scalia would have reversed the Federal Circuit and sustained Customs' interpretation. He understood Chevron as establishing a background presumption: when Congress assigned an agency administration of an ambiguous statute, it ordinarily assigned that agency the discretion to resolve the ambiguity reasonably. The decisive question was whether the interpretation represented the agency's authoritative position, rather than whether it emerged through a specially approved procedural format. Id. at 239–245, 256–259.

He attacked the majority's linkage between formality and interpretive authority. Formal adjudication principally developed a factual record; trial courts using similar procedures did not receive deference on legal questions. Agencies could legitimately choose case-by-case administration rather than rulemaking, and some decisions Congress assigned personally to Cabinet officers had no required formal process. For him, those choices did not plausibly show Congress intended courts to make the final statutory interpretation. Id. at 243–245.

Scalia predicted extended litigation over an indefinite delegation standard, artificial pressure toward rulemaking, and incentives to issue vague regulations that an agency could later interpret informally. He also feared that judicial interpretations under Skidmore would freeze statutory meaning, depriving agencies of the continuing flexibility Chevron allowed. An attempt to escape that result by permitting a later agency rule to displace a judicial interpretation would, in his account, surrender judicial authority. These were his predictions and institutional objections, not additional majority holdings. Id. at 245–250.

He treated Skidmore's persuasion factors as an uncertain restatement of the obvious rather than a useful intermediate doctrine. He disputed the majority's account of precedent, identifying several informal agency interpretations previously given Chevron treatment and regarding Christensen's contrary discussion as unnecessary to its result. Id. at 250–256.

Applying his preferred rule, Scalia found Customs' position authoritative because senior government officials, including Treasury's general counsel and the Solicitor General, represented it as the agency's official view. He did not demand deference to every low-level employee's opinion. He found the classification reasonable and also argued that Customs' traditional position deserved deference even under the majority's NationsBank analogy. Id. at 257–261 and n. 6. His reading of the Court of International Trade statutes emphasized factual review and the presumption of correctness, rather than the majority's inference against delegated interpretive authority.

## Judgment, votes, and later doctrinal change

The Court vacated the Federal Circuit's judgment and remanded for the Federal Circuit or Court of International Trade to undertake the Skidmore assessment. It neither awarded Mead a final duty-free classification nor automatically sustained the government's duty assessment. Souter wrote for eight Justices: Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer joined. Scalia alone dissented.

This is a historical brief of the 2001 decision. In Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, decided June 28, 2024, the Court overruled Chevron and directed courts to exercise independent judgment on statutory meaning under the APA. It continued to recognize that informed agency views could assist that judgment through their persuasive force. Mead's threshold for obtaining mandatory Chevron treatment therefore describes the former doctrinal system, rather than a presently operative route to that treatment.
