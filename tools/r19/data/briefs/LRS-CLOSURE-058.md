# Flyers Rights Education Fund, Inc. v. Department of Transportation
957 F.3d 1359 (D.C. Cir. 2020), decided May 5, 2020 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and regulatory setting

FlyersRights was a nonprofit that educated airline passengers about their rights and advocated on their behalf. Its petition asked the Department of Transportation to require airlines to print summaries of passengers’ Montreal Convention rights on every international airline ticket, including information about obtaining compensation for flight delays. The Convention, adopted in 1999 and effective in the United States in 2003, imposed liability for damage caused by delay in Article 19. Article 3(4) required written notice that, when applicable, the Convention governed and might limit an airline’s liability for death, injury, baggage damage, and delay. The request therefore sought a more informative notice regime than a statement that the Convention applied and limited liability. (Official opinion 2–3.)

DOT denied the petition in a February 1, 2019 letter. It found insufficient evidence that airlines were violating their existing notice obligations or that consumer confusion justified the requested rulemaking. It also pointed to another pending proceeding that addressed related issues. The resulting April 16, 2019 rule required written summaries on international airline tickets but did not include everything FlyersRights had requested. FlyersRights and its president, Paul Hudson, sought judicial review, arguing that DOT had disregarded evidence of deception and confusion and had given an inadequate explanation. (3.)

## Standing dispute and governing test

FlyersRights asserted injury to its constituents rather than injury to the organization itself. Under Hunt, an association ordinarily must establish that at least one member could sue individually, that the protected interests are germane to the organization’s purpose, and that neither the claim nor requested relief requires individual members’ participation. The complication was that FlyersRights acknowledged it was not a traditional membership organization. DOT disputed whether the people it served could count as members. (3–4.)

For a nontraditional organization, the court examined whether its constituents were the functional equivalent of members. Hunt’s indicia included participation in selecting leaders, guiding the organization’s work, and financing its activities. Prior circuit decisions had rejected membership theories based merely on reading a magazine, watching news, or having worked with an investor group. Those contacts did not establish the necessary representative relationship. The court doubted that Hunt’s list was exhaustive and examined the actual connection between FlyersRights and the passengers it purported to represent. (4–6.)

One joined by signing up to receive information. But the court did not hold that an email signup, standing alone, established associational standing. FlyersRights operated a passenger hotline, run by its board and staffed by member volunteers; frequently polled constituents about the policies and actions to pursue; and used member petitions and direct input to guide its work. Members supplied most of its funding, with many contributing to the advocacy effort. Although they did not elect the leadership, their interaction with the organization sufficiently influenced its activities. An organization sufficiently identified with and subject to the influence of its constituents could qualify without exhibiting every one of Hunt’s indicia. (5–6.)

The litigation also fit the organization’s passenger-rights mission, and a request for rulemaking did not require individual participation. Nonetheless, satisfying those requirements did not eliminate the need for an actually injured member. FlyersRights offered two declarations. Robert Lax said he had spent time communicating with an airline when his parents’ flight was canceled; DOT contested whether that was a personal injury. Leopold de Beer described his own significant flight delay, eligibility for compensation, and an inadequate notice regime that left him uninformed and impeded his ability to obtain compensation. DOT did not challenge de Beer’s standing or sworn account. The court accepted his standing and therefore FlyersRights’ standing; it did not need to resolve Lax’s theory or independently establish Hudson’s standing. (6.)

## Merits and reasoning

The court then addressed the denial of rulemaking. Under APA § 555(e), an agency must give notice and a brief statement of its grounds for denying such a petition. That explanation need not be exhaustive. Review was limited and deferential because deciding whether to initiate rulemaking resembles an exercise of prosecutorial discretion and entails allocating personnel and resources within a regulatory agenda. The court did not treat denial as wholly unreviewable; it checked that DOT had responded to the petition’s two principal concerns and that the record supported its reasons. (6–7.)

First, DOT reviewed major airlines’ contracts of carriage and concluded that they gave adequate notice of the availability of compensation for delay. The Convention’s actual requirement was notice that it governed and might limit liability where applicable. The contracts in the record contained language to that effect. That supported DOT’s conclusion that the existing treaty obligation was being satisfied, even though FlyersRights preferred more detailed explanations about asserting compensation rights. (7–8.)

Second, FlyersRights had produced some evidence of confusion from passenger interactions and media reports. The court characterized it as meaningful but not overwhelming. Whether that evidence warranted an additional rulemaking fell within DOT’s discretion to marshal its limited resources. Its ongoing parallel proceeding, which addressed many of the same concerns, strengthened the explanation. The court thus rejected the argument that the presence of some confusion compelled the requested regulatory response. (8.)

## Judgment, composition, and limits

Judge Randolph wrote for a unanimous panel with Judges Rogers and Millett. The court denied the petition for judicial review after recognizing associational standing and rejecting the APA challenge on the merits. There were no separate writings or numbered judicial footnotes. The result was not a standing dismissal, an order compelling rulemaking, or a holding that DOT could never adopt more informative notices. (1–2, 8.)
