# Daley v. Aetna Life & Casualty Company
249 Conn. 766, 734 A.2d 112 (1999) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

The Connecticut Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Katz officially released August 3, 1999, unanimously affirmed judgment for Aetna and supervisor Michele Flynn. Chief Justice Callahan and Justices Norcott, Palmer, and Peters concurred. The litigation involved several distinct theories. The trial court directed verdicts on common-law wrongful discharge, interference with employment, and statutory negligent employment practices; the jury found for defendants on the remaining claims. The appeal, transferred from the Appellate Court, challenged those directed verdicts, the treatment of speech, evidence and jury instructions, and the defamation verdict form. The Supreme Court affirmed all counts. Virginia Daley's husband's consortium claim failed derivatively. (249 Conn. at 768–769 & n. 1, 774–776, 809.)

## Employment chronology and claimed retaliation

Aetna hired Daley in October 1985 as an interior designer for a home-office renovation. In 1989 she moved to corporate leasing and coordinated design development for field offices nationwide. Evaluations for 1989 and 1990 criticized communication, missed deadlines, inaction on assignments, and unwillingness to accept responsibility. Flynn became her supervisor in late 1990 as Aetna prepared a reorganization that would reduce design staffing. After learning of Daley's pregnancy, Flynn later approved a $2,100 raise despite continuing performance concerns. These background facts were presented as facts the jury reasonably could find, not independent appellate fact-finding. (Id. at 769–770.)

After giving birth in July 1991, Daley took six weeks of paid maternity leave and two weeks of paid vacation. She asked to work at home one day a week upon returning. Following consultation, Flynn denied the request because of increased workload, reduced staffing, training demands, teamwork, and customer needs during reorganization. Daley considered that initial denial reasonable. Returning in September, she remained employed while eight designers lost their jobs and was promoted to design project manager. Further requests to human resources and Flynn for alternative scheduling or weekly vacation days were denied. (Id. at 770–772.)

By early 1992, Flynn was meeting with her weekly to address deficiencies. A written warning in March began progressive discipline; probation followed in October after renewed deficiencies. On November 9, 1992, Aetna's internal newspaper announced a family-policy award to its chairperson, Ronald Compton. A week later Daley sent Compton a memorandum criticizing the discrepancy between Aetna's promotion of flexible arrangements and her experience. She also described five unidentified coworkers' unsuccessful requests, including two said to have left, and urged greater authority to implement family flexibility. (Id. at 771–773.)

In December, Flynn removed her from probation after improvement, warning that renewed shortcomings within the next year could lead to discharge. By February 1993, she again missed deadlines and failed to coordinate projects; she had represented that three projects were on schedule despite not ordering necessary materials or updating colleagues. Aetna terminated her on February 10, citing longstanding deficiencies. That date is stated in the detailed chronology and again in the interference analysis; the opinion's earlier introductory reference to November 1993 is inconsistent with both. (Id. at 769, 773, 807–808.)

## Public-policy wrongful discharge

The assigned account focuses on the directed verdict rejecting a public-policy exception to at-will employment. Under Sheets, the exception required an improper discharge reason derived from an important public-policy violation. It remained narrow: courts should not casually displace managerial discretion or create unrecognized obligations. Relevant sources included explicit statutes, constitutional provisions, and judicially conceived policies; the court did not categorically restrict every qualifying policy to a statute. On review of a directed verdict, it considered evidence and proper inferences favorably to Daley, but affirmed if no reasonable jury could legally reach another result. (Id. at 797–798, 802–804.)

Daley urged that the right to have and raise children and policies promoting women's employment required employers to accommodate flexible scheduling and prohibited retaliation against employees seeking it. She relied on federal due process, state and federal family-leave legislation, pregnancy protections, and a child-welfare statute. The court acknowledged those provisions' important policies and employers' duty to obey them. But leave, restoration, pregnancy-discrimination protections, and child-abuse reporting requirements did not themselves require a weekly work-at-home arrangement. The claimed new mandate exceeded what those provisions expressed. (Id. at 799–804 & nn. 9–13.)

Her concessions mattered: she did not claim that her supervisor treated her this way simply because she had a child; she acknowledged that denying the requested arrangement did not necessarily violate policy and that Aetna generally met Connecticut leave requirements. She offered no basis for extending the child-reporting law to this workplace policy. Accordingly, the court rejected this particular proposed public-policy expansion. The holding did not excuse pregnancy discrimination or noncompliance with existing leave laws, nor resolve all possible future claims concerning family accommodations. (Id. at 803–804.)

## Statutory speech claim and jury's role

A separate claim invoked § 31-51q, protecting employees from discipline for exercising specified constitutional rights. The court assumed, without deciding, that the statute covered speech at a private workplace. It also limited its constitutional analysis to federal protection because Daley did not assert a broader Connecticut constitutional standard. The legal classification of topics as matters of public concern was for the court; disputed content, form, context, and motivation could require factual resolution. Here, whether the memo was a concerned citizen's broader criticism or a personal scheduling grievance was disputed, so the jury could decide the relevant factual question. The court refused to deem the memo protected as a matter of law merely because flexible scheduling interested working parents. (Id. at 776–785 & nn. 5–7.)

The instructions properly distinguished purely personal complaints from statements addressing a broader public issue. They did not require that protected speech contain no element of personal concern; the court recognized that speech could involve both. The earlier pleading-stage decision that the allegations could be read as a generalized complaint did not establish protected status after evidence was introduced. The speech verdict was therefore affirmed without deciding the reserved private-workplace issue. (Id. at 785–787 & n. 7.)

## Redaction and misrepresentation instructions

Two memo sentences describing coworkers' denied requests and calculating a 17-percent success rate were redacted. Daley offered them to show her motive and the memo's context rather than their truth, but the court sustained the evidentiary ruling. It found that their asserted relevance depended on unsubstantiated facts; alternatively, even if relevant to state of mind, they threatened distracting collateral disputes about why coworkers left during mass layoffs. The trial court recognized other portions supporting a broader concern, and footnote 8 also notes the absence of a showing of harm from redaction. (Id. at 788–791 & n. 8.)

On negligent misrepresentation, the court required proof of false information, not necessarily a contractual promise. The challenged instructions referred to Aetna's lack of commitment or support for flexible programs. Read in the actual evidentiary context, they were adequate: Daley knew the handbook made arrangements subject to supervisory discretion and business needs, and admitted that Aetna had not described them as entitlements. She supplied no false assurance that persistent pursuit of an arrangement would never result in discipline. Her proposed comparison that Aetna was merely less committed than advertised did not require reversal on this record. (Id. at 791–794.)

## Defamation, interference, and physical-safety statute

The court also sustained the defamation verdict form. The charge read each of nine allegedly defamatory statements about performance, while an interrogatory asked collectively whether Flynn's remarks were factual statements rather than opinions. The trial court had discretion over form and content. An adverse verdict alone did not establish jury confusion, and Daley had not objected to the charge's adequacy. The appellate holding concerned the asserted flaw in the interrogatory, rather than a new item-by-item ruling that every workplace accusation is immune as opinion. (Id. at 794–797.)

The interference claim was against Flynn. Such a claim required improper motive or means and intentional interference without justification, not merely ill will. The court assumed, without deciding, that an agent could be liable for interfering with her principal's employment relationship. It found insufficient evidence of retaliatory animus or unjustified action: the performance problems and discipline predated the memo, and the relied-upon denials and instructions could be justified as managerial decisions. Her own testimony recognized performance failures and the reasonableness of the initial scheduling denial. The directed verdict was affirmed on that evidentiary ground. (Id. at 804–808.)

Finally, § 31-49 concerned employees' physical welfare and safety. Her claim that management and discipline were handled negligently supplied no evidence of physically hazardous working conditions. The statute did not provide the general employment-management remedy she asserted, and the directed verdict on that count stood. (Id. at 808–809 & n. 14.)
