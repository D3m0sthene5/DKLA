# Securities and Exchange Commission v. Texas Gulf Sulphur Co.
446 F.2d 1301 (2d Cir. June 10, 1971) | Contracts

## Decision and assigned connection

Judge Waterman wrote for a panel comprising Chief Judge Friendly and Judges Waterman and Hays. No separate opinion appears. The court affirmed the judgments against the appellants except the cancellation of Kline’s stock option, which it reversed and remanded for a hearing on the appropriate remedy. This is the 1971 appeal following the 1968 en banc liability decision, rather than that earlier decision itself. The assigned C&M note uses the Kidd Creek discovery to contrast valuable research information with prohibited insider stock trading; its citation is to this later appeal. The 1971 court’s actual work concerned the misleading press release, injunctions, restitution, stock-option relief, and procedural objections to the earlier en banc process. (Reproduced opinion, ¶¶ 1, 25–26, 40; assigned printed 509.)

## Facts and prior proceedings

Texas Gulf Sulphur discovered rich ore deposits near Timmins, Ontario. The assigned account describes detection of an electromagnetic anomaly through aerial research, acquisition of nearby mineral rights and options, and insider purchases before public announcement. The full 1971 opinion refers readers to the detailed facts in the prior en banc decision, 401 F.2d 833. This brief treats that discovery background as the assignment’s context and does not attribute an adjudicated landowner-disclosure claim to the SEC appeal. The unnamed landowner lawsuit mentioned at the end of the note is a distinct account with no judicial citation supplied there. (¶ 1; assigned printed 509.)

The first trial had found several defendants not liable under section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5. The en banc court reversed some of those determinations and remanded for remedies and an unresolved corporate-liability issue. The parties had stipulated that remedies would await resolution of liability. On remand, Judge Bonsal enjoined Clayton and Crawford, declined injunctions against the corporation and several other defendants, ordered restitution of specified trading profits, and cancelled Kline’s option. Darke’s repayment included profits made by his tippees. Another defendant, O’Neill, was subject to a default repayment order but did not appeal; the note therefore does not expand the present appellant group to include him. (¶ 1 & nn. 1–3.)

## The misleading corporate release

The corporation’s April 12, 1964 press release sought to dispel rumours about the exploratory drilling. The previous appeal had left for remand whether it misled a reasonable investor and whether that resulted from lack of due diligence. Former shareholders testified that they sold because of the release. TGS objected that their testimony was nonexpert opinion on the ultimate issue; it also offered witnesses who regarded the release as optimistic rather than misleading. The district court found the release misleading but denied an injunction because no reasonable likelihood of future violation had been shown. (¶ 3.)

The panel upheld admission of the shareholders’ testimony. Their individual reasons for selling were relevant evidence, rather than a determination of the ultimate reasonable-investor issue. Perception often includes subjective conclusions, and properly founded testimony of this kind was within the trial judge’s discretion, especially in a nonjury proceeding. TGS’s objections to whether particular investors were reasonable affected weight and had been explored on cross-examination. The district court’s careful evaluation of conflicting testimony was not clearly erroneous. (¶¶ 4–7.)

TGS also challenged the lack-of-due-diligence finding as merely adopting the earlier appellate view. It had introduced no additional evidence on remand concerning that issue, and agreement with the earlier view did not show a lack of independent judicial judgment. The panel rejected the corporation’s First Amendment objection to liability for a negligent release, reasoning in this historical opinion that the factual corporate report belonged with registration statements and prospectuses rather than protected exchange of ideas. Footnote 4 explains why that constitutional question was not foreclosed by the earlier appeal. This states the 1971 court’s analysis without presenting its commercial-speech formulation or negligence discussion as an exhaustive statement of current securities law. (¶¶ 8–10 & n. 4.)

## Injunctions and retained liability findings

Clayton and Crawford argued that singling them out for injunctions was discriminatory and unsupported by likely repetition. Both had traded with inside information within twenty-four hours before public disclosure, and both had been found liable even under the principles applied at the first trial. Their conduct supplied a distinction from defendants whose liability depended on the subsequent legal development. The injunctions were tailored to future transactions in TGS securities using undisclosed material information acquired through an insider relationship. The panel found no abuse of discretion. Clayton’s attempt to relitigate when his purchase would be executed was precluded by the earlier decision. (¶¶ 12–14 & n. 5.)

Conversely, absence of an injunction did not require dismissal of the complaint or deletion of adjudicated violations. The panel upheld that distinction for TGS and for Fogarty, Mollison, and Stephens. A court could find past violations while concluding that prospective injunctive relief was unnecessary. The decision therefore cannot be summarized as an injunction against every defendant or exoneration of those not enjoined. (¶¶ 10, 38.)

## Restitution and the penalty objection

Holyk, Huntington, Clayton, and Darke had to surrender profits accrued from their respective purchases through April 17, 1964, when the discovery was fully public. The money would be held in an interest-bearing escrow account for five years, subject to disposition by the court; undistributed amounts would then belong to TGS. Private judgments arising from the same events were to be paid from the fund, protecting against double liability. Footnote 6 links the settlement design to the earlier Coates arrangement. (¶ 16 & n. 6.)

The appellants contended that section 21(e) authorized the SEC to request only injunctions and necessary ancillary relief, and that surrendering profits without direct compensation to injured sellers was an unauthorized penalty. The court distinguished the district courts’ general equitable powers under section 27 from the SEC’s ability to invoke those powers. Analogies to receivership and governmental restitution, together with the securities legislation’s purposes, supported SEC requests for appropriate remedial relief beyond prohibitory injunctions. The announced limitation was that the relief be remedial, rather than a penalty assessment. The court considered both the legislative-history objection and section 21(e)’s text, reproduced in notes 7–8. (¶¶ 17–19 & nn. 7–8.)

Surrender of the pre-disclosure gain deprived the insiders of the benefit of their wrong while leaving later appreciation intact. The parties stipulated to an April 17 average price of $40.375; by May 15 the shares were $58.25. Restitution effectively moved the insiders’ acquisition date to public disclosure. Darke’s liability for tippee profits imposed greater hardship, but otherwise an insider could evade the prohibition through relatives, acquaintances, or reciprocal tipping arrangements. Finally, the corporation could suffer reputational and shareholder-relations harm from insiders’ abuse. Sellers might have a higher equity to the gains, but that did not prevent conditional compensation to TGS. The restitution order was upheld as an equitable remedy. (¶¶ 20–23.)

## Kline’s distinct remand and Crawford’s procedure argument

The earlier en banc court had directed cancellation of Kline’s option even though remedies had not been briefed or argued under the parties’ stipulation. Judge Bonsal followed that unambiguous direction without a hearing. The panel acknowledged the earlier error: judicial power to cancel an option did not eliminate Kline’s opportunity to contest whether cancellation was an appropriate remedy. It reversed that order and remanded for a hearing. Liability remained settled, but the borderline nature of his knowledge could bear on remedy. This was not a holding that the option was necessarily valid or that no relief could be imposed. (¶¶ 25–26 & n. 2.)

Crawford separately claimed a statutory and constitutional right to oral argument before at least a quorum of the en banc court. The panel treated section 46(c) as a grant of institutional power, with hearing procedures largely entrusted to the court’s discretion, and rejected a due-process right to renewed oral argument where the parties were treated equally. Footnote 10 expressly reserved approval or disapproval of dispensing with argument at an initial appeal; this case concerned the earlier en banc proceeding. The final disposition affirmed all challenged rulings except Kline’s option cancellation. (¶¶ 28–40 & nn. 9–10.)
