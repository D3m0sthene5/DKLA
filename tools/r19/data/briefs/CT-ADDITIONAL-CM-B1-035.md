# Harvey v. Facey
[1893] A.C. 552; [1893] UKPC 1 | Contracts

## Tribunal and exact disposition

Lord Morris delivered the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council’s advice on July 29, 1893, on appeal from Jamaica. The Lord Chancellor, Watson, Hobhouse, Macnaghten, Morris, and Shand were present; no separate writing or dissent appears in the complete judgment. The committee advised reversal of the Supreme Court’s appellate judgment and restoration of Justice Curran’s dismissal with costs. The buyers were to pay the respondents’ costs of the appeal to the Supreme Court and of the Privy Council appeal. The decision rejected a concluded contract rather than finding a valid contract for which specific performance happened to be unavailable. (Complete BAILII judicial reproduction, physical pp.1–2, lines 8–44, 62–84.)

## Negotiations and the three telegrams

The appellants were solicitors practicing together in Kingston. In early October 1891, L. M. Facey had negotiated with Kingston’s Mayor and Council concerning Bumper Hall Pen, offering a £900 sale. The council discussed the proposal on October 6 and deferred acceptance. On October 7, while Facey traveled by train from Kingston to Porus, the appellants sent him a telegram asking whether he would sell them the property and requesting his lowest cash price, with the answer paid. Facey answered that the lowest price was £900. Their final telegram said they agreed to buy for that sum and requested the title deed to obtain early possession. All three messages were received. These exchanges occurred in 1891; 1893 is the judgment year. (Reproduction p.2, lines 45–62.)

The buyers sought specific performance against Facey and alleged that he could bind his wife Adelaide in selling the property. They also sought an injunction preventing the municipality from taking a conveyance. Curran dismissed because the asserted agreement disclosed no concluded contract. The appellate court instead found an agreement with Facey but held that his authority to sell without his wife’s concurrence or authorization had not been established. It refused specific performance, awarded forty shillings damages against him, and awarded costs in both courts. The buyers obtained leave and additional special leave to appeal; the resulting order expressly permitted the respondents to contest formation without separate leave. The Privy Council therefore could address the underlying absence of a contract, despite the buyers’ challenge to the refusal of specific relief. (Reproduction pp.1–2, lines 22–44.)

## Price information did not answer the willingness-to-sell question

The initial inquiry contained two distinct questions: willingness to sell to these buyers and lowest price. The placement of the request to telegraph directed it to the price question. Facey answered that question alone. His answer did not also communicate an unconditional willingness to sell to the inquirers, and the committee refused to imply such a commitment merely because the buyers wanted that inference. A statement of the lowest price at which a vendor would sell did not itself establish a contract to sell at that price to the person asking. (Reproduction p.2, lines 66–81.)

The buyers’ third telegram was accordingly an offer requiring Facey’s acceptance, rather than an acceptance completing a sale already offered by him. The committee held that the necessary acceptance was absent. The precise lesson concerns the distinction between a communicated price and a commitment empowering the recipient to conclude a bargain; it does not declare that price quotations can never constitute offers in other contexts. Because this formation conclusion disposed of the controversy, the committee did not need to address several further defenses. Its judgment therefore did not resolve Facey’s alleged authority to bind his wife or order the municipality to relinquish a completed purchase. (Reproduction p.2, lines 62–84.)
