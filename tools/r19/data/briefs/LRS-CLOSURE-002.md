# Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan; Amazon Petroleum Corp. v. Ryan
293 U.S. 388 (1935) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Cases, opinion, and judgment

Chief Justice Hughes delivered the Court’s January 7, 1935 opinion in two cases reviewing Fifth Circuit judgments. Justice Cardozo alone dissented; no nonparticipation or other separate writing appears, producing an 8–1 result. The Court held the National Industrial Recovery Act’s § 9(c) delegation unconstitutional and invalidated the July 11 and July 14, 1933 executive orders and Interior Department regulations implementing it. It reversed and remanded to the district court to modify its decrees and permanently enjoin enforcement of those orders and regulations. It did not invalidate every part of the Recovery Act or decide the Petroleum Code’s production-quota provision. (388, 405–415, 430–433.)

## Statute and presidential implementation

Section 9(c) authorized the President to prohibit interstate and foreign transportation of petroleum and petroleum products produced or withdrawn from storage in excess of amounts permitted by state law or valid state regulations. Violating a presidential order could bring a fine up to $1,000, imprisonment up to six months, or both. The statute identified the regulated commodity and its relation to state allowances, but it did not itself impose the transportation prohibition. (406, 414–415.)

The President imposed the prohibition on July 11, 1933. Three days later, invoking § 10(a), he authorized the Interior Secretary to exercise enforcement powers, appoint agents and boards, and promulgate necessary regulations. Interior’s Regulations IV and V required sworn monthly information from producers, purchasers, shippers, refiners, and processors concerning production, receipts, storage, deliveries, and compliance with state allowances. Regulation VII required accessible transaction records. The orders were reproduced in judicial notes 1 and 2. (405–408 & nn.1–2.)

A separate Petroleum Code approved in August contemplated estimates of required crude-oil production, allocation among states, and subdivisions into property quotas. Its provision treating excess quota production as an unfair trade practice was eliminated on September 13, 1933, before these October suits. It was restored in September 1934. Those changes mattered because the litigants and lower courts initially proceeded unaware that the challenged Code prohibition had been withdrawn. (408–410 & n.3, 412–413.)

## Plaintiffs and the path through the lower courts

Panama Refining owned a Texas refining plant; its coplaintiff held Texas oil and gas leases. They challenged the transportation delegation under nondelegation and commerce principles and attacked enforcement practices, including entry onto properties, gauging tanks, and digging pipelines, under the Fourth and Fifth Amendments. Amazon Petroleum and other Texas producers also challenged state production limits and the federal Code’s quota restrictions. (410–411.)

A three-judge district court considered the state-order challenge in Amazon. By agreement, the claims against federal officials were severed for decision by the district judge. The three-judge court upheld the state orders and dismissed that branch. The district judge permanently enjoined federal enforcement in both cases and specifically enjoined the Code quota provision in Amazon, unaware that it no longer existed. The Fifth Circuit reversed the federal decrees and ordered dismissal. Supreme Court review concerned those federal judgments; the Court’s disposition did not reverse the distinct state-law branch. (411–412.)

## Live controversy and equitable relief

The Court declined to decide the removed Code provision. A threatened prosecution under its later reinstatement could not import a new claim into a suit begun and heard when that prohibition did not exist. If officials enforced the restored provision, affected parties could challenge it on the facts then presented. The Court expressly reserved the Code’s interpretation and validity. (412–413 & nn.4–5.)

The reporting and recordkeeping regulations were different. Subsequent amendments continued and expanded substantially the same obligations and presented the same constitutional issue, so the challenge remained live. Repeated statutory criminal penalties also justified equitable intervention if the statute and implementing orders were invalid. This was a civil suit to restrain enforcement, not a criminal conviction being reviewed. (413–414.)

## The majority’s missing-policy and missing-standard analysis

The majority assumed, without deciding, that Congress itself could prohibit transportation of petroleum exceeding state allowances. The question was who made that policy choice. Section 9(c) neither directed prohibition nor specified when, under what circumstances, or on what findings the President should act. State production limits defined the oil eligible for prohibition but supplied no federal criterion for deciding whether to prohibit its transportation. Disobedience to the resulting presidential policy was a crime. (414–415.)

The Court looked beyond § 9(c). Other § 9 provisions addressed pipeline rates and monopolistic practices under their own restrictive clauses; they did not constrain the transportation power. Section 1’s declaration of policy listed wide-ranging objectives: free commerce, fair competition, industrial cooperation, fuller production, temporary restrictions, purchasing power, employment, labor standards, industrial rehabilitation, and conservation. For the majority, this list supplied no governing policy for excess-oil transportation or conditions requiring presidential action. (416–419 & n.6.)

Nor did the other provisions governing codes, imports, licenses, trade organizations, and labor relations repair that absence. Some expressly required findings or incorporated standards, but action under § 9(c) did not depend on a code; the President’s oil prohibition actually preceded code approval. Construing a general menu of objectives as a sufficient criterion would leave the President choosing legislative policy rather than executing it. (419–420.)

## Permissible administration distinguished from transferred legislation

Hughes acknowledged the practical necessity of entrusting detail and factual determinations to administrators. Congress could set policies and primary standards, authorize subordinate rules within limits, and make legislation operative upon specified contingencies. It could not abdicate the essential legislative choice. Good motives or a presumed desire to serve the public did not supply constitutional authority. The majority also reasoned that accepting this unrestricted delegation would permit Congress to transfer similar policy choices over other commodities and subjects to other officials or boards. (420–421.)

The opinion extensively examined precedents. Early embargo statutes involved stated circumstances, declared congressional policies, and presidential foreign-relations functions. The Brig Aurora and Field v. Clark involved ascertainment of contingencies implementing legislative will. Standards for tea imports, bridge obstructions, railway rates, enemy property, radio allocation, and forest management supplied legislative policies administered through practical subordinate decisions. Hampton sustained tariff adjustments under an intelligible principle. The Court distinguished those authorizations from § 9(c)’s lack of a transportation policy, rule, or defined conditions. (421–430 & nn.7–13.)

## Findings as an additional defect

The July 11 order also stated no findings or grounds for the prohibition. Even if statutory context could yield prerequisites, the President had to demonstrate compliance with them rather than select among objectives and act without any finding. In the setting of delegated legislative orders carrying criminal punishment, the majority invoked due process: the order must appear within delegated authority, and factual determinations conditioning that authority must be shown. It applied the administrative-finding reasoning of Wichita Railroad and Mahler rather than exempting the President from restrictions accompanying delegated legislative power. (431–433 & nn.14–15.)

The opinion distinguished actions properly belonging to the executive sphere and ordinary presumptions attaching to them. Its conclusion therefore should not be reduced to a rule that every presidential action always requires formal findings. The delegation and this penal legislative order were the issue. The resulting mandate permanently restrained enforcement of the identified orders and regulations, with district decrees modified to reflect the Court’s reservations concerning the separate Code claim. (432–433.)

## Cardozo: a narrow power with implied standards

Cardozo agreed that the removed Petroleum Code provision was not before the Court. He also accepted the need for a reasonably clear governing standard. His disagreement concerned construction of the statute as a whole. The President could act only on one commodity, unlawfully produced or withdrawn under state quotas, through the specified transportation prohibition. That was not a roving power to discover any evil and invent any remedy. Section 1 implicitly required action tending to advance Congress’s declared recovery policies. (433–435 & dissent n.1.)

The record connected excess oil with unfair competition, wasted resources, destabilized prices, and unemployment. To Cardozo, those policies could operate together toward industrial recovery rather than invite arbitrary personal preference. Changing conditions made advance congressional specification impractical; the President had to ascertain when the defined prohibition would advance the declared objectives. Reading all provisions together and preferring a reasonable constitutional construction justified implying the policy connection. (436–439.)

He regarded the discretion as confined by subject and occasion, and compared it with familiar delegations concerning embargoes, tariffs, forests, and the Interstate Commerce Commission. Temporary restriction could serve longer-term productive capacity and recovery. Congress could responsibly leave the timing decision to an official able to assess later conditions. Practical delegation did not abandon constitutional structure. (440–443 & dissent n.2.)

## Cardozo: no constitutional demand for recited findings here

Cardozo treated the findings issue as subsidiary: a new order could correct a deficient recital without requiring invalidation of the statute. He thought this order valid already. Neither statute nor Constitution required formal reasons; presidential action under a valid grant carried a presumption of adequate inquiry and grounds. Martin v. Mott illustrated that principle. Arbitrary oppression or the absence of any possible rational connection to statutory purposes could still be challenged, but the oil order’s relation to the declared policies was apparent. (444–447 & dissent n.3.)

He distinguished judicially supervised administrative adjudication and statutes expressly requiring findings. Legislative rule formulation did not acquire an unwritten constitutional findings requirement simply because delegated power was involved. Other objections concerning § 9(c) and Interior’s regulations had been addressed below; he would affirm each appellate decree. These arguments were the dissent’s position, not the Court’s holding. (447–448.)

## Assigned scope and interpretive caution

The assigned September PDF 52 / printed 94 contains a scholarly account from Cary Coglianese’s Dimensions of Delegation, within an excerpt concluding on PDF 53. It presents Panama Refining as a difficulty for a theory emphasizing the dimensions of delegated power and criticizes its reconciliation with other cases. That evaluative account is not judicial language or Cardozo’s opinion. The actual majority supplies an extended precedent analysis and a findings objection; the brief preserves them while distinguishing the scholar’s assessment of their adequacy. The complete majority at 405–433, all fifteen notes, and Cardozo’s complete dissent at 433–448 with all three notes have been read. The official source also contains preceding-case material, a syllabus, and counsel’s arguments, which are not judicial holdings. This brief describes the historical judgment and does not certify current nondelegation doctrine.
