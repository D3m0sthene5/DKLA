# Dupree v. Younger
598 U.S. 729 (2023) | Civil Procedure

## The rejected defense and the missing posttrial motion
Kevin Younger was assaulted by three corrections officers while detained before trial in a Maryland state prison. He believed that Neil Dupree, a former prison lieutenant, had ordered the attack. Younger sued Dupree and other officials for damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, alleging excessive force in violation of his Fourteenth Amendment due process rights. The issue before the Supreme Court concerned preservation of an appellate argument, rather than the substantive standard for excessive force. Slip opinion at 2–3.

Dupree sought summary judgment on the ground that Younger had not exhausted administrative remedies under the Prison Litigation Reform Act, 42 U.S.C. § 1997e(a). Although the parties disputed whether Younger had followed Maryland's Administrative Remedy Procedure, the District Court considered those disputes unnecessary to resolve. It found no dispute that the prison system had internally investigated the assault and held that this investigation satisfied the exhaustion requirement. The court thus denied summary judgment on a rationale about the legal effect of an undisputed investigation. Whether that rationale made the appellate issue purely legal would become a separate question. Id. at 3.

The case proceeded to a jury trial. Dupree presented no evidence concerning exhaustion. He moved for judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50(a), but did not include the exhaustion defense, and the District Court denied that motion. The jury found Dupree and four codefendants liable and awarded Younger $700,000. Dupree filed no renewed motion under Rule 50(b). Id.

His appeal challenged only the earlier rejection of the exhaustion defense. Fourth Circuit precedent required a posttrial motion to preserve a claim or defense rejected at summary judgment even when the issue was purely legal. Bound by that rule, the appellate panel dismissed the appeal without reaching the exhaustion argument. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve a circuit split over that preservation requirement. Id. at 3–4 & n.2.

## The procedural framework: what each motion asks the court to decide
Rule 56 permits judgment before trial when no genuine dispute of material fact exists and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. It therefore contains both a factual inquiry and a legal inquiry. A denial can rest on genuinely disputed facts, on the governing law, or on a combination of the two. The title of the motion does not identify which rationale produced the ruling. Id. at 1–2, 7.

Rule 50 operates during and after a jury trial. Rule 50(a) permits judgment as a matter of law before submission to the jury, based on the trial record; Rule 50(b) permits a renewed motion after the verdict. Although the judgment standards resemble the summary-judgment standard, their evidentiary records differ. A discovery record sufficient to require a trial does not necessarily describe all the evidence ultimately presented to the jury. Id. at 2. Footnote 1, relocated to the assigned supplement's appended notes, distinguishes bench trials governed by Rule 52 when jury rights are waived or the relief does not carry a jury entitlement.

Under 28 U.S.C. § 1291, appellate jurisdiction generally awaits a final district-court decision. Denials of summary judgment ordinarily are interlocutory, so the general rule postpones their review until an appeal from final judgment. This is a rule about the timing of review, not a blanket prohibition on reviewing earlier rulings. Footnote 3 recognizes exceptions under the collateral-order doctrine, including some denials of qualified immunity. Id. at 4 & n.3.

## Why an evidentiary ruling must be renewed
In Ortiz v. Jordan, 562 U.S. 180 (2011), the Court held that a denial of summary judgment on sufficiency-of-the-evidence grounds is not reviewable after trial as an independent challenge to the obsolete pretrial record. The full trial record supersedes the summary-judgment record. An appellate sufficiency challenge must therefore concern the evidence actually presented at trial, rather than whether the earlier discovery materials warranted a trial. Dupree, slip opinion at 4–5.

A posttrial Rule 50 motion gives the judge who heard the witnesses the first opportunity to decide whether the trial evidence sufficiently supports the verdict. That district-court ruling is the decision the appellate court then reviews. Without the required posttrial motion, the appellate court cannot review trial evidentiary sufficiency by treating the earlier summary-judgment denial as an adequate substitute. The Court preserved this aspect of Ortiz; it did not abolish Rule 50 preservation for factual sufficiency challenges. Id. at 5.

## Why a purely legal ruling survives trial
The Court defined a purely legal issue as one that can be resolved without reference to disputed facts. Trial can replace the factual record, but it does not similarly replace the answer to a pure question of law. A district court's legal conclusion at summary judgment therefore merges into final judgment under the usual rule and becomes reviewable on appeal from that judgment. Id. at 6.

That distinction also answers the preservation question. The legal issue was already raised and resolved below, supplying a ruling for the appellate court to review. If disputed trial facts cannot affect its answer, making the party repeat the same argument after trial provides no additional benefit. The Supreme Court accordingly held that a posttrial Rule 50 motion is not required to preserve appellate review of a purely legal issue resolved at summary judgment. Id.

The rule addresses a resolved issue, not every legal contention a party might later invent. Nor does calling a defense legal establish that it is independent of disputed facts. The basis of the district court's ruling and the nature of the appellate question remain important.

## Younger's objections and the Court's answers
Younger first argued that a summary-judgment denial was not a final decision under § 1291, making it unreviewable regardless of its basis. The Court agreed that immediate appeal generally was unavailable, but distinguished postponing review from insulating a ruling permanently. Ortiz itself had reserved the treatment of purely legal rulings. Id. at 6–7.

Younger next urged a uniform preservation requirement for all summary-judgment denials. Rule 56, however, expressly separates factual disputes from entitlement under the law. Tailoring preservation to the reason for denial was consistent with that structure. A repeated legal motion ordinarily would require the litigant to reformat the same argument and the court to reject it again, without a new trial record relevant to its resolution. Id. at 7–8.

Younger also relied on Rule 50's reference to a legally sufficient evidentiary basis, arguing that the Rule furnished a vehicle for raising legal issues after trial. The Court did not decide that such a motion was unavailable. Its answer was narrower: even if the litigant could include the issue in a Rule 50 motion, the Rule did not require repetition of a pure question already resolved at summary judgment. Id. at 8.

Finally, Younger emphasized difficult boundaries between factual and legal questions. The Court acknowledged that concern and recognized that prudent counsel might renew an argument as a precaution. But appellate courts routinely make this distinction, and the circuits applying the different preservation rules had managed the task. A universal repetition requirement would sacrifice review of an already-decided legal issue when a litigant understandably saw no purpose in rearguing it. Id. at 8–9.

## Vote, judgment, and what remained undecided
Justice Barrett wrote for a unanimous Court; there were no separate opinions. The Court vacated the Fourth Circuit's judgment and remanded. It expressly declined to decide whether Dupree's particular exhaustion issue was purely legal. The Fourth Circuit could determine that question, and consider other properly preserved arguments, in the first instance. Id. at 9.

The judgment therefore restored the opportunity for appellate consideration under the correct preservation rule; it did not hold that the prison investigation satisfied exhaustion, that Younger failed to exhaust, or that Dupree was entitled to judgment. It also did not itself overturn the jury's liability determination or award. The central distinction is between an evidentiary challenge that must follow the trial record through Rule 50 and a resolved pure legal question that remains reviewable after final judgment without that repeated motion.
