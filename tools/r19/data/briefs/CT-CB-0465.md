# Kenai Chrysler Center, Inc. v. Denison
167 P.3d 1240 (Alaska 2007) | Contracts | Guardianship, constructive notice, restitution, and consumer remedies

## A car purchase despite the guardians' objections

David Denison had been under his parents' legal guardianship since turning eighteen in 1999. In October 2002 he lived in his own apartment, worked, and maintained a bank account, while his parents monitored his home and finances through frequent visits and nearly daily conversations. The guardianship encouraged independence while protecting him. Those facts later mattered because the dealer tried to characterize independence and imperfect control as abandonment of the guardianship. (167 P.3d at 1245, 1249.)

David first asked his father to cosign for a used car; his father refused. The next day, seeking a new Dodge Neon that could be financed without a cosigner, he asked his mother for a down payment. She refused and told him not to purchase it. Nevertheless, he used his debit card. The dealer applied a factory rebate and credit for his 1994 Pontiac Grand Am, permitting a purchase with $500 cash. The total price, including a $945 extended service plan and other charges, was $17,802; the remaining $12,851.77 was financed at 11.99 percent for five years. The dealer did not actually know of the guardianship when it made the sale. (Id. at 1244–1245.)

Within one or two days, Dorothy Denison brought David to the dealership, showed the guardianship papers, asserted his lack of contractual authority, and requested return of the car. The manager refused and, according to her testimony, said the company sold cars to many people who were not very smart. He handed David the keys over her objection. The general manager also refused after seeing the papers. David then damaged the Neon in a one-car accident. His parents recovered and returned it, but the dealer again invited him to collect it while refusing to return his Pontiac. They eventually persuaded him to leave the Neon at the dealership. (Id. at 1245.)

Advocates and a court-appointed guardianship investigator told the dealer that the contract was void. The company waited a month after the sale before consulting counsel. Meanwhile it assigned the financing to GMAC without reporting the incapacity, demanded storage fees, and sold the Pontiac while the family disputed the transaction. GMAC eventually repossessed and sold the Neon. After learning of the guardianship from the family's lawyer, it treated the loan as uncollectible. Kenai Chrysler paid GMAC's deficiency and later sought that money from the Denisons. These post-notice acts, rather than merely the original mistaken transaction, supported the consumer-protection verdict. (Id. at 1245–1246, 1256–1257.)

## Probate proceedings, summary judgment, and trial

On December 4, 2002, the dealer petitioned in probate court to modify David's guardianship so he could buy the car. The court denied the petition as frivolous and without good cause and awarded fees. The next day the guardians filed this action seeking a declaration that the sales contract was void, an injunction against enforcement, damages under Alaska's Unfair Trade Practices Act, and punitive damages. They named Kenai Chrysler and DaimlerChrysler Insurance Agency; the opinion refers to both collectively as Kenai Chrysler. The defendants counterclaimed for restitution. (Id. at 1246 and majority n.1.)

Initial cross-motions on UTPA liability were denied. The guardians then sought summary judgment declaring the contract void. The dealer obtained a discovery continuance to investigate alleged abandonment of the guardianship, but did not pursue suitable discovery. When the motion was renewed after discovery with Dorothy's affidavit describing her ongoing duties, the dealer did not oppose it. The superior court declared the contract void and entered summary judgment against the dealer's defenses and counterclaims. The UTPA claim proceeded to a jury. (Id. at 1246–1248.)

The jury awarded $500 for the retained down payment, $4,650 for permanent loss of the Pontiac, and $5,000 for loss of its use, totaling $10,150. Although it found reckless indifference, the foreman indicated no desire to award punitive damages; the guardians waived that claim. The court trebled the actual damages under the UTPA to $30,450 and awarded eighty percent of the requested fees after excluding work in the separate probate proceeding. The dealer appealed the liability, procedure, and damages; the guardians cross-appealed the fees and unresolved sanctions motions. (Id. at 1247.)

## A valid guardianship prevented contractual liability

Chief Justice Bryner first observed that the dealer had abandoned its objection to the renewed declaratory-judgment motion by failing to oppose it. The court nevertheless addressed the merits. Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 13 precludes a ward under a valid adjudication-based guardianship from incurring the asserted contractual duties. The order also supplies constructive public notice of incapacity. Its effect does not depend on whether every potential seller personally learned about the order before dealing with the ward. (Id. at 1248 and majority nn.4–9.)

The court distinguished Pappert v. Sargent, involving an incompetent person outside legal guardianship and no circumstances suggesting incapacity. Pappert allowed restitution only without actual or constructive knowledge of incapacity. Here the guardianship gave constructive notice, defeating the dealer's request for restitution. Territorial precedent likewise treated an adjudication of incompetence as notice to the world. Thus the lack of actual knowledge at the original sale did not save the contract or establish a restitution entitlement. (Id. at 1248.)

A lucid interval did not restore David's power to contract under the continuing order. Termination or abandonment could do so, but the Restatement contemplates such circumstances as death or removal of a guardian or the ward's sustained resumption of complete control without interference. The dealer's assertions of missed reports, a bank account, retail work, and inability to prevent every purchase or drive did not raise a genuine issue of that kind. Encouraging a job and independent life complied with the guardianship's terms. The parents were not required to monitor every movement or give personal advance warnings to all merchants. Their continued efforts to control finances contradicted abandonment. The summary judgment declaring the contract void therefore stood. (Id. at 1249.)

## Affirmative defenses and trial procedure

The dealer could not invoke mitigation concerning the down payment or permanent loss of the Pontiac, because it controlled whether to refund the money and return the car. Loss-of-use damages were different: the guardians had to act reasonably in replacing the Pontiac. The superior court's broad pretrial exclusion of the mitigation defense was erroneous to the extent it conclusively resolved the reasonableness of their delay. But the court later permitted the dealer's contrary evidence and argument and instructed the jury to award use value only for the time reasonably necessary for replacement. Those trial proceedings removed the prejudice, making the error harmless. The appellate court thus affirmed despite identifying an error, rather than holding every summary-judgment ruling correct. (Id. at 1250–1252.)

GMAC was not a necessary party. No valid financing obligation had existed for the dealer to assign, and the UTPA action concerned the dealer's treatment of David and his guardians. Complete relief could be obtained against the defendants already present. The discovery ruling also survived: letters demanding responses and threatening a motion, without engaging the objections or offering genuine dialogue, did not satisfy the required good-faith effort to confer. (Id. at 1252–1253.)

The court upheld use of a rental quotation to refresh a witness's memory, even though it had earlier excluded the quotation as an exhibit for nondisclosure. Evidence Rule 612 permits an otherwise inadmissible writing for refreshing recollection, with inspection and cross-examination safeguards. The dealer identified no prejudice and had received the document the previous day. A different challenge concerned the trial judge's prepared responses limiting testimony about legal advice. The dealer had neither objected nor supplied an adequate offer of proof, and no plain error appeared. The advice arrived a month after the sale, reducing its relevance to acts already taken. (Id. at 1253–1255.)

## The UTPA verdict rested on more than a mistaken legal position

The UTPA prohibits unfair or deceptive commercial practices and representations that agreements create rights or obligations they do not legally confer. Intent to deceive is unnecessary under the standards applied; unfairness may also arise from oppressive or unscrupulous conduct, established public policy, and substantial consumer injury. But the court agreed that merely asserting a good-faith, mistaken belief in a contract's validity would require something more before it supported this claim. (Id. at 1255–1256.)

That additional conduct was present. After receiving the guardianship documents and repeated explanations, the dealer resisted return, repeatedly gave keys to David against the guardians' directions, disposed of his trade-in, advanced paperwork, threatened storage charges, and continued seeking the deficiency even after GMAC considered it uncollectible. A jury could find that this sequence blatantly disregarded the family's rights and went beyond a simple legal disagreement. The record therefore supported the unfair-practices verdict. The opinion did not make every effort to enforce an ultimately invalid agreement automatically actionable. (Id. at 1256–1257.)

## Damages, trebling, and Matthews's partial dissent

The $4,650 permanent-loss award had adequate support: David bought the Pontiac for $5,500 a few months earlier and owed approximately $850, while the dealer's own manager estimated retail value at $4,000 to $5,000. The jury was not confined to the $1,300 wholesale trade-in valuation. The $5,000 use award also had evidence behind it. About twenty months had elapsed without replacement, allegedly because David lacked money. Rental testimony supported rates of $499 or more monthly; the award averaged $250 monthly across the entire period. The jury could disbelieve the manager's retrospective statement that he would have supplied a free car. (Id. at 1257–1258.)

The majority rejected a rigid cap equaling the property's value, while preserving proportionality: a use award cannot be wholly out of proportion to value. Here $5,000 only slightly exceeded the Pontiac's value. A separate legal time cap might warrant consideration in a properly presented case, but the dealer had not argued that twenty months was categorically unreasonable or sought a shorter legal maximum. The court declined to adopt one on that record. Footnote 18 additionally notes the potential for double recovery when loss-of-use damages accompany prejudgment interest on permanent-loss damages; that distinct objection was not raised here. (Id. at 1250, 1258–1259; majority n.18.)

Justice Matthews dissented only on the use award and agreed with the remainder. He thought unincurred retail rental charges became unrealistic over a lengthy period: reasonable people do not rent indefinitely at weekly or monthly prices. He would limit hypothetical rental value to the time a person of ordinary means needed to buy a replacement, while permitting proven additional expenses, such as transport costs, for an impecunious claimant afterward. With no evidence of actually incurred use expenses here, he would remit the award to one month's lowest rental figure, $499. The majority's affirmance of that award was therefore four to one; the other holdings were not contested by his separate opinion. (Id. at 1262–1263 and dissent nn.1–4.)

Withdrawal of common-law punitive damages did not waive statutory treble damages. Section 45.50.531(a) made trebling available upon the qualifying UTPA violation and loss, independently of other relief. Trebling also encouraged private enforcement and compensated the effort of suit, so its purposes extended beyond punishment. The separate provision diverting half of punitive damages to the state referred to punitive awards obtained as other remedies, not the statutory trebled recovery. The punitive instruction caused no reversible error after waiver, and the claimed conversion instruction had not actually been given. (Id. at 1259–1260.)

## Cross-appeal, disposition, and source scope

The UTPA required full reasonable fees, but left the trial judge discretion to assess reasonableness under all the circumstances. The twenty-percent reduction did not question the truth of the billing or hourly rates; it reflected the court's assessment that the litigation consumed excessive time. The separate probate statute governed fees there, and the probate court had already awarded $1,600, so another award for the same work risked duplication. Both fee rulings were affirmed. (Id. at 1260–1261.)

The court did not decide the merits of the guardians' two sanctions requests. The trial judge had reserved them and apparently overlooked them before judgment, and the guardians did not insist on rulings before entry of final judgment. They forfeited appellate review under the applicable preservation rule. The Alaska Supreme Court affirmed the judgment on both appeal and cross-appeal, including the $30,450 trebled damages. Bryner wrote; Eastaugh, Fabe, and Carpeneti joined, while Matthews dissented only as described. (Id. at 1244, 1262–1263.)
