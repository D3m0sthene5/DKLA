Cooper v. Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond
Citation
467 U.S. 867, 104 S. Ct. 2794, 81 L. Ed. 2d 718 (U.S. 1984). Argued March 19, 1984; decided June 25,
1984. Justice Stevens wrote for the Court. Justice Marshall concurred in the judgment; Justice Powell did
not participate in the decision.
Casebook pp. 886–890; source PDF pp. 456–458. Full judicial opinion.
Parties
The respondent was the Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond, whose Charlotte branch employed the workers
at issue. The original action was brought by the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. Sylvia
Cooper, Constance Russell, Helen Moore, and Elmore Hannah intervened as individual and class plaintiffs.
The later individual claimants, collectively called the Baxter petitioners, were members of the certified class
who had received notice and had not opted out. 467 U.S. at 869–871 & nn.2, 5; CB 886–887.
Material Facts
The EEOC alleged racially discriminatory employment practices, including failures to promote Black
employees, at the Charlotte branch during the relevant period. The intervening employees asserted both Title
VII and § 1981 claims. A class was conditionally certified under Rule 23(b)(2) and (3), covering Black
employees employed since January 3, 1974 who had suffered discrimination in specified employment
matters. Notice by publication and mail explained that remaining class members would be bound. 467 U.S.
at 869–871; CB 886–887.
Both the named intervenors and the Baxter petitioners testified at the class trial. The district court initially
found a discriminatory pattern in grades 4 and 5, but no sufficient classwide pattern above those grades. It
separately found discrimination against two named intervenors, Cooper and Russell, while rejecting the
other two named plaintiffs' individual claims. It deferred adjudication of the individual entitlement of other
testifying class members. Those trial findings were later altered on appeal; they are not findings of
discrimination made by the Supreme Court. 467 U.S. at 871–873; CB 887–889.
Procedural History
The Baxter petitioners sought intervention after the class trial. The district court denied intervention,
explaining that one employee's rights would be addressed during class relief proceedings and that the others
held jobs above the grades for which class relief had been ordered. It expressly contemplated separate §
1981 suits. The Baxter petitioners then brought a separate action alleging discriminatory denial of
promotions. The court refused the Bank's preclusion-based dismissal motion and certified an interlocutory
appeal under § 1292(b). The distinction concerning the employee eligible for relief proceedings is supplied
by the full opinion. 467 U.S. at 872–873; CB 887–888.
The Fourth Circuit consolidated that appeal with the class-action appeal. It rejected the remaining classwide
discrimination finding and the two successful named plaintiffs' individual claims, leaving all claims actually
decided in the first action resolved for the Bank. It also held that the class judgment barred the Baxter
petitioners' separate claims and ordered dismissal. The Supreme Court's limited grant reviewed the
preclusion ruling, not the merits rulings against the original class and named plaintiffs. 467 U.S. at 873–874
& nn.6–8; CB 888.

Issue
Does a binding class judgment rejecting a general pattern or practice of racial discrimination also extinguish
absent class members' individual discrimination claims when those claims were not adjudicated in the class
action?
Rule and Holding
A judgment in a properly entertained class action binds class members under ordinary claim- and
issue-preclusion principles. It bars renewed litigation of the same claim and conclusively establishes issues
actually litigated, determined, and essential to the judgment. Its scope nevertheless depends on what was
adjudicated. Rejection of a classwide pattern or practice does not determine whether every individual
employment decision was nondiscriminatory. 467 U.S. at 874–878; CB 888–889.
The Baxter petitioners could pursue their individual claims. They remained bound by the rejection of the
classwide pattern-or-practice claim and could not relitigate that common issue or bring another class action
asserting it for the same period. Their distinct, unadjudicated claims concerning particular promotion
decisions survived. 467 U.S. at 880–881; CB 889–890.
Judgment
The Court reversed the Fourth Circuit's judgment and remanded. It removed the preclusion ground for
dismissing the Baxter action; it neither found that the Baxter petitioners had proved discrimination nor
reopened the final merits determinations concerning the original named plaintiffs. 467 U.S. at 873–874 &
nn.6–8, 880–882; CB 888–890.
Reasoning
An individual discrimination claim asks why a particular employment decision occurred. A
pattern-or-practice claim requires proof of a regular discriminatory practice, rather than isolated or sporadic
acts. A plaintiff may therefore establish an individual wrong without proving a companywide practice, and
failure to prove such a practice does not establish that every decision was lawful. The Court used Falcon's
distinction between individual and class proof in both directions. 467 U.S. at 875–878; CB 888–889.
The difference is reflected in burdens of proof. A proven discriminatory pattern can support a presumption
favoring individual class members, but an individual plaintiff may instead establish a prima facie case
concerning a specific decision. The employer must then articulate a legitimate nondiscriminatory reason,
with the ultimate burden of proving intentional discrimination remaining on the plaintiff. The expanded
explanation of this framework comes from the full opinion; the casebook retains its application to the
surviving claims. 467 U.S. at 874–876, 880; CB 889.
The Bank had obtained finality on the classwide claim, and that result could help it contest pretext in the
individual suits. It had not obtained a determination of the Baxter petitioners' separate merits. Treating
testimony about particular incidents as if it necessarily adjudicated every witness's personal claim would
expand the judgment beyond the issues the district court actually decided. 467 U.S. at 880–881 & n.12; CB
889–890.
The Court rejected the Bank's appeal to class-action efficiency. Requiring all individual claims to be decided
as part of an unsuccessful common-issue proceeding could force intervention by every class member and
overwhelm the device's common-question function. The district court may decide whether to expand the
issues before it. Rule 23 did not require adjudication of every matter mentioned in witness testimony, and

Separate Opinions
Justice Stevens wrote the Court's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justices Brennan, White,
Blackmun, Rehnquist, and O'Connor. Justice Marshall concurred in the judgment without a separate
explanation in the reported decision. Justice Powell took no part in the decision. No dissenting opinion was
filed. 467 U.S. at 868, 882; CB 890.
Notes
Analyze membership and judgment scope separately. A person can be bound by a class judgment and still
possess an individual claim that the judgment did not resolve. Here the classwide issue could not be
relitigated, but the motivation for each Baxter promotion decision remained open. The decision does not
authorize class members to evade adverse findings actually essential to the earlier judgment. 467 U.S. at
874, 880–881.
The narrow holding concerns the particular pattern-or-practice adjudication and expressly unadjudicated
individual claims. The broader procedural lesson is to identify precisely the claims, issues, and parties
represented before applying preclusion. A notice warning that class members will be bound does not enlarge
the substantive scope of the adjudication itself. 467 U.S. at 870–874, 880–881.
