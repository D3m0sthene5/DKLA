# Sun Printing & Publishing Ass'n v. Remington Paper & Power Co.
235 N.Y. 338, 139 N.E. 470 (1923) | Contracts

## A sixteen-month supply arrangement with two unresolved terms
Sun Printing agreed to buy, and Remington agreed to sell, 1,000 tons of newsprint each month from September 1919 through December 1920, for a total of 16,000 tons. The writing specified the paper's sizes and quality and required payment on the twentieth of each month for the preceding month's shipments. September's price was $3.73¾ per hundred pounds; the price for October through December 1919 was $4. The initial four months were performed and paid for. 235 N.Y. at 342–343.

For the remaining twelve months, the parties agreed to fix both the price and the length of time for which that price would apply, fifteen days before each previously established period expired. The new price could never exceed the contract price charged to large consumers by the Canadian Export Paper Company, with an adjustment giving the seller the benefit of freight differentials. The benchmark limited price; it did not expressly specify the duration of each future pricing period. Id. at 342–343.

Before the time for the next agreement arrived, Remington announced that the arrangement was incomplete and disclaimed an obligation to deliver additional paper. Sun Printing demanded 1,000 tons during each month of 1920 at the Canadian company's then-current contract price, adjusted for freight. It renewed the demand month by month until the year ended, and sued for the damage caused by nondelivery. Market prices happened to rise during 1920, giving the seller an apparent economic reason to resist the ceiling, but the majority treated enforceability as a question of the parties' undertaking rather than the seller's motive. Id. at 343–345.

## Pleadings and appellate posture
This was a ruling on the complaint and the buyer's motion for judgment on the pleadings, not a trial finding that a particular reasonable price or duration existed. Special Term denied the buyer's motion. The Appellate Division reversed and granted it. The Court of Appeals heard the seller's permitted appeal with the certified question whether the complaint stated facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. Id. at 339–340, 347.

The seller argued that neither price nor the term for which a price would govern had been adequately settled, that the court could not make those agreements for the parties, and that the buyer had received no series of exclusive options. The buyer argued that the Canadian benchmark supplied an ascertainable maximum price and that its election to pay that maximum made delivery obligatory. The division between the majority and dissent was therefore not about whether the writing expressed a commercial desire to exchange paper. It concerned whether its unresolved pricing periods could be supplied by construction or required further assent.

## Cardozo's majority: determining price does not determine its duration
Judge Cardozo distinguished two subjects reserved for future agreement: the price and the interval during which it would remain fixed. If price alone had remained open, the ceiling might have supported treating the buyer as having an option to demand delivery at the maximum. But determining a price at one point does not decide how long that price will govern. The parties contemplated applying the Canadian benchmark at the start of a negotiated period and retaining that resulting price during the period. Sun Printing's counsel conceded that they had not agreed that the actual price would rise or fall with every intervening change in the Canadian benchmark. Id. at 343–344.

This distinction changes the allocation of market risk. If the Canadian price rose after a period began, the seller would remain bound to the previously fixed lower price. If it fell, the buyer would remain bound to the previously fixed higher price. The length of the pricing period therefore determined how much stability each party obtained and how much exposure each bore. It was not merely an administrative detail the court could ignore after identifying the benchmark.

Sun Printing argued that agreeing on duration mattered only if the parties negotiated a price below the ceiling. Cardozo found no support for that condition in the writing. The buyer did not admit an obligation to accept the Canadian benchmark for the remaining year; it asserted an option. Without agreed pricing periods, its interpretation would give it successive monthly choices about accepting a benchmark that might change. Nothing showed that Remington had agreed to put itself at the buyer's disposal on those terms. Even imposing a single election for the entire remaining quantity would not cure the gap if the ceiling fell from shipment to shipment. Id. at 344–345.

The majority thus treated the parties as having addressed failure to agree on price by naming a maximum, but not failure to agree on how long a fixed price would last. The court could readily imagine language that would have filled the gap. Its task, however, was to construe the writing the parties signed. Adding monthly terms, a reasonable term drawn from trade practice, or an automatically fluctuating price would revise the bargain rather than interpret it. On the complaint before the court, the remaining arrangement was an agreement to agree. Id. at 345–346.

## What the majority left open
The complaint described the Canadian contract price as a definite, readily ascertainable standard. Members of the court suggested that this price might itself have been fixed for the entire year, making the duration question immaterial. Cardozo responded that the buyer had not pleaded such a circumstance or pressed it in briefing or argument. Its renewed monthly tenders instead suggested that it assumed the benchmark varied. If that understanding of the course of dealing was wrong, the buyer could correct it through an amended pleading. The decision therefore concerns the complaint actually presented, not a categorical conclusion that no additional allegations could ever support recovery. Id. at 346–347.

The full opinion also expressly reserved a different theory that the assigned excerpt abbreviates. The court did not decide whether an arbitrary refusal to agree about duration might breach a duty if the buyer had offered to let the seller divide the terms however it chose. The complaint disclosed no such notice, no action based on that refusal to negotiate, and no readiness to proceed on a basis other than the buyer's own monthly interpretation. A demand coupled with a condition the seller had no duty to accept could not create a cause of action when the seller rejected it. Thus the majority did not adjudicate every possible obligation associated with negotiations; it rejected the particular delivery obligation claimed. Id. at 347.

## Crane's dissent: start with the evident binding transaction
Judge Crane, joined by Judge Hogan, would affirm the buyer's favorable ruling. He emphasized that sophisticated businesses had signed the seller's detailed prepared form for a fixed quantity over sixteen months, had used binding language of sale and purchase, and had performed the first four months. Construing that instrument as useless for the remaining year disregarded their evident intention to make a binding commercial agreement. The court should try to give that intention effect where an intelligible standard was available. Id. at 347–349.

For Crane, the Canadian contract price supplied precisely such a standard: a buyer offering the highest price the seller was permitted to demand had supplied the missing economic term. The seller, having refused even to attempt a new agreement, should not benefit from its own refusal. Crane offered several ways to resolve duration. The December benchmark could apply for the remaining year if the evidence showed it remained constant. Alternatively, monthly shipment quantities could support monthly pricing periods. The word “contract” in the benchmark could incorporate the period covered by the Canadian company's own contracts. If those routes failed, the court could select a reasonable period in light of the trade and surrounding circumstances. Id. at 349–351.

These were alternative grounds to sustain the complaint and permit evidence, not findings that the Canadian price actually remained fixed for a year. Crane's dissent placed greater weight on preserving the documented transaction through reasonable construction; Cardozo required assent to the particular allocation of price risk that a selected duration would produce. The disagreement concerns the extent of legitimate gap filling after commercial intent has been shown.

## Vote and judgment
Cardozo's opinion was joined by Chief Judge Hiscock and Judges Pound, McLaughlin, and Andrews. Crane dissented with Hogan, producing a five-to-two decision. The Court of Appeals reversed the Appellate Division, affirmed Special Term's denial of the buyer's motion, awarded the stated appellate costs, and answered the certified sufficiency question in the negative. 235 N.Y. at 347, 351. It did not award the seller damages or adjudicate a substitute supply contract.

For Contracts, the important question is which unresolved term carries substantive risk. A named price ceiling may appear to complete a bargain until the duration of that price is examined. The decision predates the Uniform Commercial Code, and this brief reports its 1923 reasoning without treating it as a determination of how later sales legislation would govern a comparable transaction.
