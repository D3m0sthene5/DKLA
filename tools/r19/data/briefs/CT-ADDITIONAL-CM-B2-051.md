# Barclays Bank plc v. O’Brien
[1993] UKHL 6; [1994] 1 A.C. 180; [1993] 4 All E.R. 417 (H.L., October 21, 1993) | Contracts

## Decision and question presented

The House of Lords unanimously dismissed Barclays’ appeal and affirmed the Court of Appeal’s order, with the bank paying the respondents’ appeal costs. Lord Browne-Wilkinson supplied the substantive reasoning; Lords Templeman, Lowry, Slynn of Hadley, and Woolf agreed. The question was when a bank taking security over a couple’s home must bear the consequences of a husband’s wrongdoing in procuring his wife’s agreement. The wife’s undue-influence claim had failed below and was not pursued in the House. The operative wrong was the husband’s proved misrepresentation about the extent and duration of the security. The bank’s constructive notice of the wife’s resulting equity prevented it from enforcing the charge against her. (Reproduced parliamentary order, lines 41–101; Browne-Wilkinson, “The facts,” lines 124–127; “The decision of this case,” lines 249–260.)

## Facts and formation of the security

Nicholas and Bridget Mary O’Brien jointly owned their matrimonial home in Slough, subject to an existing building-society mortgage of about £25,000. Nicholas was a chartered accountant interested in Heathrow Fabrications Ltd.; Bridget had no direct financial interest in that company. Its Barclays account was repeatedly overdrawn beyond an authorized £40,000 facility during early 1987, with cheques dishonoured. A temporary increase to £60,000 did not solve the difficulty: by June 15, its overdraft was £98,000. On June 22, Nicholas and the Woolwich branch manager agreed to increase the facility to £135,000, reducing to £120,000 after three weeks. Nicholas would guarantee the company’s liability, and both spouses would charge their home to secure his liability to the bank. The guarantee was unlimited. (“The facts,” lines 105–110; “The decision of this case,” line 250.)

The Woolwich manager sent the documents to the Burnham branch for signature. He instructed that branch to explain the current facilities of £107,000, their projected increase to £135,000, and the nature of the documents, and to advise the spouses to consult their solicitors if uncertain. Those instructions were not followed. Nicholas signed on July 1. Bridget attended with him the next day, signed the legal charge and a side letter, and had her signature witnessed by a clerk. The side letter acknowledged receipt of the guarantee, the security arrangement, and a recommendation to obtain independent advice; it referred to documents dated July 3. In reality, Bridget received no explanation or recommendation, did not read what she signed, and was not given a copy of the guarantee. These failures mattered because the safeguards the bank had itself prescribed would have corrected the misinformation on which she acted. (“The facts,” lines 109–124.)

The company’s indebtedness exceeded £154,000 by October. Barclays demanded payment under Nicholas’ guarantee in November and brought possession proceedings when the demand was not met. The trial judge found that Nicholas had falsely told Bridget that the charge secured only £60,000 and would be released shortly when the home was remortgaged. Nevertheless, he ordered possession against her because he regarded the bank as not responsible for Nicholas’ misrepresentation. (“The facts,” lines 124–127.)

## Procedure and competing explanations

The Court of Appeal reversed. Its order treated the charge as unenforceable against Bridget except to the extent of the £60,000 she thought she had agreed to secure. Scott L.J.’s leading reasoning distinguished an agency theory, which linked the bank to a debtor acting as its agent, from a special-equity theory protecting certain sureties whose relationships naturally involved influence and reliance. On the latter theory, a creditor knowing the relationship could not enforce security obtained through wrongdoing or inadequate understanding unless it took reasonable steps to secure informed consent. The House affirmed the Court of Appeal’s order but rejected special equity as the proper doctrinal foundation. (“The decision of the Court of Appeal,” lines 128–133; “Conclusions—(a) Wives,” lines 208–213.)

The bank’s counsel also objected that requiring protective steps imposed too great an administrative burden. Lord Browne-Wilkinson answered that banking recommendations and the 1992 Code of Banking Practice already called for warnings about guarantees and independent advice. A private interview was the substantial additional safeguard: written warnings could go unread or be intercepted by a husband. (“Conclusions—(a) Wives,” lines 233–238.)

## Rule: wrongdoing, inquiry, and constructive notice

The judgment separated two inquiries. First, a surety must have a legal or equitable ground for setting aside the transaction against the principal debtor, such as undue influence or misrepresentation. Mere lack of understanding does not itself give a wife a general right to disown security. Second, that equity binds the creditor if the debtor acted as its agent or the creditor had actual or constructive notice. Agency will rarely fit the ordinary situation: a debtor seeking finance usually obtains the surety’s assistance for his own purposes. The doctrine of notice supplies the principled connection between the debtor’s wrong and the creditor’s security. (“Undue influence, misrepresentation and third parties,” lines 164–168; “Conclusions—(a) Wives,” lines 211–216, 230–232.)

For the ordinary wife-surety transaction, two features put the creditor on inquiry: the transaction is not on its face to her financial advantage, and transactions of that kind carry a substantial risk that the husband has procured her participation through a legal or equitable wrong. Inquiry does not require proof that the bank knew the husband actually lied or exerted pressure. Knowledge of facts indicating a possible adverse equity is enough to require reasonable protective steps; failure to take them fixes the bank with constructive notice. (“Conclusions—(a) Wives,” lines 217–229.)

In the ordinary case, the creditor could take reasonable steps by requiring a private meeting without the husband, explaining the extent of the wife’s potential liability, warning of the risks, and urging independent legal advice. Exceptional knowledge making undue influence probable could require insistence on separate advice. The judgment distinguished ordinary precautions from such exceptional cases, and treated the adequacy of steps for past transactions as dependent on their circumstances. These are the safeguards articulated in this 1993 judgment, rather than a certification of all later developments in English suretyship law. (“Conclusions—(a) Wives,” lines 222–226.)

## Why the House rejected a special married-women equity

Lord Browne-Wilkinson sought to protect vulnerable sureties without making jointly owned homes unusable as security for business finance. He accepted that emotional ties and financial trust could create real risks, but rejected an automatic presumption of undue influence simply because the parties were married. The judgment described actual undue influence and two routes to presumed influence: legally recognized confidential relationships and relationships of trust and confidence proved on the facts. Husband and wife did not automatically fall within the former category. This background explained the risk that puts a bank on inquiry; it did not convert Bridget’s rejected undue-influence allegation into an adjudicated finding. (“Policy considerations,” lines 134–142; “Undue influence” and “An invalidating tendency?,” lines 144–162.)

The extended analysis of Turnbull v. Duval exposed uncertain foundations for the agency and special-equity theories. Its record did not establish the husband’s alleged wrongdoing; the creditor’s own agent had fiduciary responsibilities to the wife. Later attempts to generalize the case produced artificial agency assumptions and an obligation to prove understanding that exceeded the protection available in ordinary transactions between spouses. The House therefore preferred ordinary equitable notice principles. No special equity confined to married women or suretyship was necessary. (“Turnbull v Duval” and the following subsections, lines 169–203; “Conclusions—(a) Wives,” lines 208–215.)

The principles also extended to other cohabitees where the creditor knew of the relationship, and to other relationships in which it knew the surety trusted the principal debtor concerning financial affairs. The judgment’s example was elderly parents misled by a son. Emotional involvement and known financial reliance, rather than the marriage ceremony alone, explained the extension. (“Conclusions—(b) Other persons” and “Summary,” lines 239–247.)

## Application and exact result

Barclays knew the O’Briens were married and took Bridget’s security for a company in which Nicholas, but not Bridget, had a financial interest. It was on inquiry. Had Burnham explained the actual facility and projected increase, that information would have contradicted Nicholas’ representation of a £60,000 liability lasting only three weeks. Instead, its clerk witnessed signature without a warning or advice recommendation. The bank consequently had constructive notice of the misrepresentation and Bridget’s right to set aside the charge. The House dismissed the bank’s appeal with costs; the formal order affirmed the Court of Appeal’s order. It did not find undue influence proved, award damages against the husband, or announce that every misunderstood guarantee is unenforceable. (“The decision of this case,” lines 249–260; parliamentary order, lines 67–78.)
