# Jack Faucett Associates, Inc. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co.

**744 F.2d 118 (D.C. Cir. 1984), decided September 11, 1984.** Judge Mikva wrote for Judges Tamm, Mikva, and Starr. No separate writing appears in the recovered report. The assignment is CivPro PDF pages 675–676, printed pages 1341–1342, Note 3. It highlights inconsistent determinations and an evidentiary error held harmless in an earlier action. The actual opinion supplies additional grounds concerning a stipulated product market and the scope of the earlier jury’s findings.

## Facts and litigation leading to the appeal

Seven businesses sued AT&T and its operating companies on behalf of customers who paid Bell companies for interface devices between November 20, 1970 and July 1, 1978. AT&T required these devices to connect customer-owned telephone equipment to its network. The plaintiffs alleged that the tariff requirement and related conduct maintained a monopoly in terminal equipment, violating Sherman Act §§ 1 and 2. Whether the devices reasonably protected the network or instead served anticompetitive purposes was central to liability. The circuit did not decide that merits question. (120–121.)

The regulatory history complicated the dispute. Decisions rejecting an absolute prohibition on foreign attachments led AT&T to file interface tariffs. The FCC let them take effect without specifically approving them and subsequently studied alternative certification arrangements. State regulators also considered the requirement. The FCC ultimately adopted certification standards allowing qualifying customer equipment to connect without the interface device. These events mattered because regulatory decisions could bear on the reasonableness of AT&T’s contemporaneous position. Their relevance did not necessarily confer antitrust immunity. (120–121, 127–128.)

The plaintiffs sought to import findings from *Litton Systems v. AT&T*. After lengthy litigation and a trial lasting more than five months, that jury found antitrust violations and awarded $92,258,243 before trebling. The Second Circuit affirmed. It held the exclusion of a New York regulatory decision erroneous but harmless in that trial’s extensive record. It also rejected AT&T’s *Noerr-Pennington* defense: two judges found the doctrine inapplicable, while all three agreed alternatively that the conduct fell within the sham exception. (123.)

Meanwhile, a government antitrust action had produced Judge Harold Greene’s ruling largely denying AT&T’s motion to dismiss at the close of the government’s evidence. His treatment of *Noerr-Pennington* protected much of AT&T’s petitioning activity. The government action later settled, and the court did not rule on a subsequently invited list of specific contentions to strike. The circuit nevertheless treated the developed petitioning ruling as a substantial inconsistent judicial determination. (121–123, 130–131.)

The district court in the current consolidated cases applied offensive issue preclusion broadly. Its nine findings covered standing, the product market, monopoly power and intent, anticompetitive conduct, causation, the asserted lack of need for the interface device, and rejection of the petitioning defense. AT&T appealed that order. (123–124.)

## Rule and standard of review

Issue preclusion requires an issue actually litigated, actually and necessarily determined by a competent court, and application that does not work unfairness. In the offensive setting, a new plaintiff seeks to bind a defendant to a loss against someone else. *Parklane* permits that use within the trial court’s discretion, but fairness to the defendant is especially important. Relevant considerations include whether the plaintiff could readily have joined the earlier case, the defendant’s incentive to defend it, new procedural opportunities, and inconsistent determinations; that list is not exhaustive. Appellate review is for abuse of discretion, which remains meaningful when the underlying premises are erroneous or inadequately examined. (124–126.)

## The excluded regulatory evidence

AT&T argued that the *Litton* trial excluded evidence favorable to the reasonableness of its conduct. The circuit treated the exclusionary ruling as extending to the relevant state regulatory decisions, not simply one isolated document. Those decisions could support a reasonable belief that the interface tariff protected the network and served the public interest. The plaintiffs’ reliance on federal regulatory jurisdiction and *Cantor* did not make the evidence irrelevant: later federal jurisdiction did not erase its contemporaneous significance, and lack of automatic antitrust immunity did not eliminate its evidentiary value. (127–128.)

The earlier harmless-error ruling did not require offensive preclusion. An appellate court deciding whether an existing verdict must be set aside examines a particular trial record. A court deciding whether that result should conclusively govern a new action asks a different question: could important evidence excluded from the first proceeding affect the new determination? The standards need not coincide. The circuit regarded the excluded evidence as a serious obstacle, especially when combined with the other fairness problems. It did not announce that every harmless error automatically defeats estoppel. (128–129.)

## The inconsistent government determination

The district court rejected the government ruling because it lacked finality and supposedly was not inconsistent with *Litton*. The circuit rejected both explanations. Finality sufficient to use a judgment affirmatively as preclusive is not invariably required of an inconsistent determination offered to resist preclusion. The question is whether the inconsistency undermines confidence in treating the earlier result as conclusive against a new adversary. (129–130.)

That does not make every judicial remark decisive. The court directed attention to the ruling’s procedural posture, firmness, and care devoted to the issue. Judge Greene’s detailed treatment of petitioning protection was sufficiently definite; the panel described its practical effect as equivalent to partial summary judgment against the government. Its reasoning conflicted with *Litton*’s rejection of protection for substantially the same conduct. The circuit expressly declined to decide which *Noerr-Pennington* analysis was correct. The inconsistency itself made the broad estoppel unfair. (130–132.)

## Stipulation and issue identity

Two additional defects required separate attention. First, *Litton*’s product-market definition came from a stipulation, not litigation. A stipulation establishing a fact’s truth is different from one merely shaping evidence the factfinder will consider. The former does not satisfy actual litigation for binding a party against a new opponent. Accordingly, the plaintiffs could not use the stipulated market definition conclusively here. (132.)

Second, the district court imported *Litton*’s finding of intentional delay in providing and installing devices without adequately determining whether the underlying evidence concerned these plaintiffs. If the earlier finding concerned only Litton, further indicia of similarity would be necessary. The parties disputed the breadth of that evidence. The appellate court did not resolve the dispute; it required the district court to investigate and explain the predicate for preclusion rather than shelter an unexamined assumption behind discretionary review. (132–133.)

## Judgment and limits

The court **reversed and remanded**. It carefully allocated the result: findings three through nine tracing to liability could not stand because of the excluded evidence and inconsistent petitioning determinations; finding six concerning delay additionally required the missing predicate inquiry. Finding two failed because the market was stipulated. Finding one, stating that these plaintiffs had standing, was not specifically challenged and could stand. The court expressed no view on the underlying antitrust merits or whether any particular merits theory ultimately applied. (133.)

The assignment’s two points are therefore aspects of a broader requirement to use issue preclusion with care. A result can remain valid between its original parties yet lack sufficient fairness or issue identity to settle a new plaintiff’s litigation.
