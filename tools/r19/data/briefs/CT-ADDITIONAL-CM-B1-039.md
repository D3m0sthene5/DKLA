# Mitzel v. Hauck
78 S.D. 543, 105 N.W.2d 378 (S.D. 1960) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural posture

The South Dakota Supreme Court affirmed a directed verdict for Hauck in Mitzel's personal-injury action. The friends' arrangement for a duck-hunting trip did not create a commercial bargain that removed Mitzel from the automobile guest statute, nor could he avoid the statutory classification by disaffirming a supposed contract made while a minor. His evidence also failed to establish the willful and wanton misconduct required for a guest's recovery. Biegelmeier wrote the opinion, and all judges concurred; there are no separate writings or judicial footnotes in the complete report. (78 S.D. at 543, 545–550.)

The trial court directed the verdict at the close of the plaintiff's evidence. The appellate court therefore tested whether that evidence justified submission under the governing statutory standard. It did not adjudicate a claim for damages for breach of a promise to drive, and the result should not be described as a jury's finding that the driver exercised ordinary care. (Id. at 545, 549–550.)

## Facts and the claimed exchange

Harry Mitzel and Ronald Hauck were both seventeen when the accident occurred, and each appeared through a guardian ad litem. Five young men discussed a Sunday duck-hunting trip. Mitzel testified that he offered to use his car, but Hauck said they would use his. Mitzel then agreed to forgo work at his brother's place that afternoon and accompany Hauck to look for ducks, giving his time in exchange for Hauck's taking the car. Hauck owned and drove the vehicle in which Mitzel was injured. (Id. at 545–546.)

The applicable guest statute denied an action to a person transported as a guest without compensation unless willful and wanton misconduct caused and contributed to the injury; it also addressed a guest who brought injury upon himself through willfulness or want of ordinary care. Mitzel sought to avoid the statute by characterizing the trip arrangement as contractual. He further testified that before trial he disaffirmed the agreement, and argued that the law governing a minor's disaffirmance prevented imposing a host-guest relationship to which he could not legally consent. His theories concerned both an asserted compensated transportation bargain and a supposed contract creating guest status. (Id. at 546–549.)

## Why the social arrangement was not the asserted contract

Not every agreement contemplates legally enforceable rights and duties. The court assessed the surrounding circumstances and manifestations of assent, expressly rejecting any requirement of a subjective meeting of minds and acknowledging contracts implied in fact or law. It discussed family relationships, mutually understood jokes, and similar settings to explain why an apparent arrangement can lack the manifestation of a bargain. These illustrations did not establish that every promise between friends is unenforceable or that secret intent defeats an otherwise objective bargain. (Id. at 547.)

The court addressed Williston's suggestion that merely social engagements could create contracts when the ordinary requisites exist. It doubted the cited support for that observation and emphasized the accompanying explanation that such parties typically do not manifest an exchange of a promise for agreed consideration. The evidence described an enjoyable pastime arising from friendship and social relations, rather than a commercial transaction. Looking for ducks and accompanying a friend were benefits compatible with hospitality, companionship, and good fellowship. They did not remove Mitzel from guest status. His decision not to work that afternoon did not turn these circumstances into the contractual exchange he asserted. (Id. at 547–548.)

Because the trip involved no such contract, the statute permitting minors to disaffirm contracts did not apply to it. The court additionally observed that even under one version of Mitzel's own theory, disaffirming the alleged bargain that elevated him from guest to compensated passenger would return him to guest status. That was an alternative explanation of the theory's difficulty, not a finding that the court had first recognized a valid compensated-passenger contract. (Id. at 546–548.)

## Minority did not exclude him from the guest statute

Mitzel also relied on cases involving very young children who could not choose to accept a ride. The opinion contrasted those decisions with another jurisdiction's classification of a voluntarily intoxicated passenger as a guest. It did not adopt either divergent theory for those different circumstances. Mitzel was nearly eighteen, had graduated from high school, and owned and drove a car himself. No mental incompetency was alleged. His contractual disability as a minor did not require excluding him from the statute's guest category. The court held that the statutory guest status included him. (Id. at 548–549.)

This reasoning distinguishes capacity to make a binding contract from the statutory classification governing an automobile injury. The case does not eliminate minors' disaffirmance rights for genuine contracts; it rejects application of that doctrine to a noncontractual hunting arrangement and this passenger's guest status. (Id. at 546–549.)

## Willful and wanton misconduct and disposition

Mitzel's evidence showed a speed of approximately seventy miles per hour on a normal gravel road, rising to about seventy-four as the car approached a gradual grade. He told Hauck to take it easy. The grade obscured an unmarked curve, and when it came into view Mitzel shouted a warning. Hauck applied the brakes 156 feet before the turn, but according to Mitzel they did not hold much. The car went through a barbed-wire fence and stopped 104 feet into a field. There was testimony that Hauck had earlier said he would get his brakes fixed and had not done so. There was also testimony that pumping the brakes was unnecessary and that he did it because he preferred the pedal near the top. A photograph confirmed that the curve could not be seen before the crest. (Id. at 549–550.)

The court found this insufficient to show the required reckless state of mind. Hauck had no prior knowledge or warning of the curve; the road appeared wide, maintained, and to continue ahead. There was no evidence of prior careless driving or other curves suggesting danger, and accelerating on an incline was not so unusual as itself to show recklessness. Mitzel's opinion about poor braking did not supply the missing state of mind. The authorities he invoked involved additional circumstances such as racing or ninety-to-ninety-five-mile speeds in rain and fog. The court acknowledged the plaintiff's objection to the statutory protection but treated the legislative standard as controlling. It affirmed the directed verdict and judgment. It did not hold that seventy-four-mile-per-hour driving was necessarily nonnegligent. (Id. at 549–550.)
