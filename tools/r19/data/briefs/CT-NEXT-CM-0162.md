# Lake River Corp. v. Carborundum Co.
769 F.2d 1284 (7th Cir. 1985) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Circuit Judge Posner wrote for Judges Eschbach and Gibson, the latter sitting by designation from the Eighth Circuit. In this diversity action applying Illinois commercial law, both parties appealed a bench-trial judgment awarding Lake River contract damages and Carborundum conversion damages. The Seventh Circuit upheld the rejection of Lake River's claimed lien, found the contract's damages formula an unenforceable penalty, and required redetermination of both parties' damages. It affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded, permitting additional evidence, directing each party to bear its appellate costs, and making Circuit Rule 18 inapplicable on remand. There is no separate opinion; the report's asterisk identifies Gibson's designation. 769 F.2d at 1286–1288, 1293.

## Facts and proceedings
Carborundum made Ferro Carbo, an abrasive powder used in steel production. Under a 1979 agreement, Lake River would receive bulk powder in Illinois, bag it, store it, and distribute it to Carborundum's customers. Ownership remained with Carborundum until delivery. Carborundum required a new bagging system costing $89,000. To amortize that investment and earn its anticipated twenty-percent profit, Lake River obtained a promise that Carborundum would ship at least 22,500 tons during the three-year term. Any deficiency would be invoiced at the prevailing bagging rates at the term's end. Full performance would produce roughly $533,000 in revenue. Id. at 1286.

The steel-market decline reduced demand, and by expiration in late 1982 only 12,000 tons had been shipped. Carborundum paid for all services actually performed. Lake River demanded another approximately $241,000 under the deficiency formula. Carborundum refused, calling it a penalty. Lake River retained 500 tons of Carborundum's bagged product, then worth approximately $269,000, as leverage. It offered to sell the powder and escrow proceeds pending resolution, but Carborundum declined and supplied its customers from the East at an additional $31,000 expense. Id. at 1286–1287.

Lake River sued for the stipulated amount. Carborundum counterclaimed that retention was conversion, seeking the powder's value and extra delivery expenses. The trial court awarded each side its requested categories, together with approximately $17,000 interest on Lake River's award, leaving Carborundum roughly $42,000 ahead. The opinion expressly rounds these figures to the nearest thousand. Id. at 1286–1287.

## Lien issue and conversion
Invalidity of the damage formula did not by itself decide whether a lien existed, because Carborundum's breach could still support ordinary contract damages. The relevant question was whether a damages claim justified retaining goods belonging to the breaching party. An artisan's possessory lien ordinarily protects compensation for services already performed on the goods. Carborundum had fully paid for those services. Lake River instead withheld the product to compel payment for services it never performed on powder never shipped. That distinction defeated the lien and made the retention conversion. Id. at 1287–1288.

The specialized equipment did not supply a different basis. Lake River had received almost $300,000 in service revenue and used the equipment on another contract. It had not proved an urgent uncompensated outlay comparable to an artisan who completed work and received nothing. Accounting amortization did not establish actual unrecovered cash expenditure. Recognizing a lien on these facts would obstruct marketability without the urgent creditor need supporting that remedy. The court reserved whether another cited jurisdiction's broader lien rule would be Illinois law; it did not have to resolve that question here. Id. at 1288.

## Liquidated-damages rule and policy discussion
Illinois required a reasonable formation-time estimate of likely damages and a need for estimation arising from difficulty of measuring the loss. Easily determined damages, or an estimate greatly exceeding a reasonable upper estimate of probable harm, indicated a penalty. Classification was a legal question, considered independently with respectful attention to the district judge's view; Illinois resolved doubtful cases against enforceability. Id. at 1289–1290.

Posner discussed reasons competent commercial parties might want penalty clauses: a stronger promise could encourage transactions, while penalties could also discourage economically beneficial breaches. He questioned paternalistic refusal to enforce sophisticated businesses' agreements, and explained why efficient-breach arguments were not conclusive once formation incentives were considered. Those observations did not change the controlling Illinois rule. A federal court applying state law could not substitute its preferred policy for innovations properly left to Illinois courts. Id. at 1288–1290.

## Why the formula was penal
The shortfall invoice awarded unearned gross revenue without deducting avoided bagging costs. Although the stipulated amount varied with quantity, its relation to actual expected loss was systematically excessive. If Carborundum breached immediately after the $89,000 equipment purchase, Lake River could recover the full $533,000 while avoiding the remaining performance costs, yielding a $444,000 gain instead of its expected $107,000 profit. Id. at 1290.

At the approximate fifty-five-percent performance stage actually involved, the court's illustrative assumptions produced $293,000 revenue, $185,000 variable costs, and $89,000 equipment cost, or $19,000 profit before the clause. Adding the $241,000 deficiency payment yielded roughly $260,000, far above the expected $107,000. At ninety-percent performance, the same method produced $88,000 before an additional $53,000 clause payment, or $141,000. These rounded hypothetical computations tested the formula's design; they were not new findings fixing the damages to be entered on remand. They conservatively assumed the equipment had no remaining value, and the court acknowledged omission of the time value of the initial expenditure. Id. at 1290–1291.

Lake River invoked mitigation to make the formula seem less excessive. The court doubted that ordinary judicial mitigation should rewrite a clause intended to substitute the parties' damage assessment, and in any event mitigation would not cure the failure to deduct saved costs. Even selling the equipment would reduce both the demanded amount and unrecovered investment, leaving the same excess gain in the early-breach example. Id. at 1291–1292.

The court distinguished natural-gas take-or-pay arrangements rather than broadly invalidating them. Where nearly all supplier costs are incurred before delivery, promised revenue may reasonably approximate loss. Here, interruption saved substantial variable expenses. Invalidating this formula left Lake River entitled to the unpaid price less costs avoided on the remaining forty-five percent of powder, to be fixed on remand. Id. at 1292–1293.

## Counterclaim damages and final disposition
Carborundum's conversion award required adjustment too. Having been awarded the withheld product's full market value, it could not simply add all costs of selling substitute product while also receiving that product's full market price. If the substitutes would otherwise have sold in the East at the same price but lower cost, shifting them west could cause a distinct lost margin. Carborundum had not proved that alternative sale, so the counterclaim damages had to be refigured with additional evidence permitted. Id. at 1293.

Carborundum was not required to accept escrow of the proceeds to mitigate a converter's liability. Lake River was not entitled to retain the proceeds of conversion, even temporarily. The appellate judgment therefore preserved the conversion determination and the ordinary contract remedy, while requiring recalculation of both damage awards. Id.
