# McBoyle v. United States
283 U.S. 25 (1931) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, decision, and assigned scope
The United States Supreme Court decided McBoyle on March 9, 1931. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote the Court's opinion. The assigned Class 5 reading places the case at PDF page 1, introducing interpretation of a general term that follows a specific list. This brief uses that excerpt and the complete opinion at 283 U.S. 25-27. No separate opinion appears in the published decision.

## Parties, facts, and procedural history
The United States prosecuted McBoyle for transporting a stolen airplane from Ottawa, Illinois, to Guymon, Oklahoma, knowing that the airplane had been stolen. His knowledge and the interstate movement explain why the prosecution could succeed if an airplane counted as a covered motor vehicle. The Supreme Court's opinion does not give a separate narrative of the theft or identify an additional factual dispute requiring resolution. Its question is the scope of the federal criminal statute. (283 U.S. at 25-26.)

McBoyle was convicted and sentenced to three years in prison and a $2,000 fine. The Tenth Circuit affirmed, 43 F.2d 273. The Supreme Court granted certiorari specifically to determine whether the National Motor Vehicle Theft Act applied to aircraft. The Court was reviewing an actual conviction, rather than a hypothetical request for guidance about transporting airplanes. (Id. at 25.)

## Statutory issue and competing positions
The 1919 Act defined a motor vehicle to include an automobile, automobile truck, automobile wagon, motorcycle, or any other self-propelled vehicle not designed for running on rails. Another section criminalized interstate or foreign transportation of a motor vehicle with knowledge that it had been stolen. The government's necessary interpretation was that the general residual category reached a self-propelled airplane: it transports people or things, and it is not designed to run on rails. McBoyle's position was that the statute's language and context confined the covered vehicles to land transportation. (Id. at 26.)

The issue was consequently narrower than whether stealing or transporting stolen airplanes should be punishable. It was whether Congress had made this particular conduct punishable under this particular statute. The existence of a comparable social harm did not answer that question.

## Holding and judgment
The Act did not cover aircraft. The Court reversed the Tenth Circuit's judgment affirming McBoyle's conviction. The decision's final sentence is a reversal; it does not describe an additional trial, an award of damages, or a finding that McBoyle did not know the airplane was stolen. (Id. at 27.)

## Reasoning: ordinary meaning within the statutory list
Holmes first acknowledged the linguistic possibility on which the prosecution depended. In an etymological or expanded usage, vehicle can describe transportation on land, water, or air. The Court therefore did not claim that describing an airplane as a vehicle was nonsensical. It distinguished a word's possible abstract range from the meaning the statute conveyed to its ordinary readers. (Id. at 26.)

In everyday speech, the Court said, vehicle evoked something moving on land. The surrounding statutory enumeration reinforced that understanding. Automobile trucks, wagons, and motorcycles were all land vehicles, and the phrase about not running on rails continued the same subject. Read as a whole, the definition conveyed a category of land vehicles, including land vehicles beyond those specifically named. Its catchall expanded membership within that category without necessarily abandoning the category itself. (Id. at 26-27.)

That reasoning illustrates the relationship between ordinary meaning and ejusdem generis, the practice of reading a general residual term in light of the kind of things specifically listed before it. Holmes's opinion does not depend on reciting the Latin label. The operative work is choosing the relevant common characteristic of the named examples. The Court chose land transportation; it did not choose the broader characteristic of being capable of movement. A reader preparing for class should be able to explain why choosing the genus decides whether the catchall includes airplanes.

The Court also considered related legal usage. The general federal statutory definition of vehicle described means of transportation on land. A tariff provision expressly differentiated aircraft, and the statute's language followed several state statutes and District of Columbia traffic regulations that could not reasonably have been understood to regulate transportation through the air. These comparisons supported the ordinary land-based reading. They were corroborating context, rather than a declaration that a word must always have the same meaning in every statute. (Id. at 26-27.)

Airplanes were already familiar when Congress enacted the statute in 1919. Nevertheless, the carefully detailed enumeration, reports, and debates contained no reference to aircraft. Holmes used that silence with the affirmative textual pattern. Silence by itself did not prove that Congress intended an exemption; it mattered because the prosecution sought to extend a specifically framed land-vehicle statute into a different domain. (Id. at 26-27.)

## Reasoning: fair warning and the limits of policy analogy
The final paragraph adds a criminal-law justification. The law should give the public a reasonably clear warning, in language ordinary people can understand, of the line between permitted and punishable conduct. Holmes candidly observed that criminals ordinarily do not read statutes before murdering or stealing. Actual reliance on a statutory definition was thus unnecessary to the Court's reasoning. Fair warning constrained how courts interpreted and enlarged criminal prohibitions. (Id. at 27.)

The government could argue that transporting stolen aircraft created the same sort of problem as transporting stolen automobiles. The Court refused to turn that shared policy into statutory coverage. Nor could it assume that Congress would have written broader language had legislators focused on airplanes. The warning supplied by the enacted words controlled over that counterfactual prediction. (Id.)

The distinction is useful when studying lenity. McBoyle supports restraint in reading criminal statutes and emphasizes notice, but its actual analysis resolves the statutory category through ordinary usage and context. It should not be reduced to a generic assertion that every disputed criminal statute is automatically construed for the defendant. Nor did the Court invalidate the Act for constitutional vagueness; it interpreted the Act and reversed a conviction outside its coverage.

## What the full opinion supplies beyond the excerpt
The complete judicial text restores the Tenth Circuit's affirmance, the precise judgment of reversal, and the statutory comparisons compressed in the classroom excerpt. The result is easy to misstate if the brief ends after the fair-warning discussion: McBoyle won reversal of his conviction under this Act, not a ruling that airplane theft was lawful. The case's durable interpretive lesson is that a residual phrase takes meaning from both its ordinary usage and the specific statutory company it keeps.
