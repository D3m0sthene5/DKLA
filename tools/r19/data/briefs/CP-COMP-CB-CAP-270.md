# Skinner v. Switzer

**562 U.S. 521 (2011). Supreme Court of the United States, March 7, 2011. Civil Procedure.** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, physical PDF page 339, printed pages 645–646, Skinner's account and Rule 8 quotation following Johnson.

## Conviction, untested evidence, and the requested relief

A Texas jury convicted Henry Skinner in 1995 of murdering his live-in girlfriend, Twila Busby, and her two sons, and sentenced him to death. Skinner admitted being at the house but asserted that alcohol and codeine had incapacitated him. He identified Busby's uncle, Robert Donnell, as a possible perpetrator. Some tested evidence implicated Skinner; other evidence did not. Several items remained untested, including knives, an axe handle, vaginal swabs, fingernail clippings, and additional hair samples. The Court recounted his account and the prior proceedings without finding his innocence. (525–527.)

The factual notes distinguish different kinds of support for that account. A trial witness described Donnell's sexual advances, and a neighbor later described his cleaning his truck. An expert excluded members of the household as sources of one hair. Later state DNA testing had occurred without Skinner's participation; Skinner called it inconclusive, while the state court described some findings as inculpatory. The Court did not resolve that disagreement. His trial lawyer had not requested further testing, fearing that it might implicate him. (526–529 and notes 1–4.)

Texas enacted Article 64 in 2001 to provide limited postconviction DNA testing. It required either unavailable or technologically inadequate earlier testing, or a showing that evidence remained untested through no fault of the prisoner and that testing served the interests of justice. Additional requirements included a showing that exculpatory results would have prevented conviction and that the request did not unreasonably delay punishment. (527–528.)

Skinner sought testing twice. The Texas Court of Criminal Appeals affirmed the first denial because he had not shown the required prospect of a different conviction outcome. It affirmed the second denial on the no-fault requirement, accepting counsel's earlier choice as reasonable trial strategy. The prosecutor reported that the requested materials remained available, with an intact chain of custody, while qualifying that she had not obtained an expert opinion about their condition. (528–529 and note 5.)

Skinner then sued district attorney Lynn Switzer, whose office held the evidence, for injunctive relief under § 1983. He alleged that Texas denied procedural due process by refusing the requested testing. The district court dismissed under circuit precedent requiring a habeas petition for such requests; the Fifth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted review and had stayed his scheduled execution. (529 and note 6.)

## The judgment and the limited question decided

The Supreme Court held that **the federal court had subject-matter jurisdiction and Skinner could pursue this claim under § 1983**. It **reversed the Fifth Circuit and remanded**. It did not order testing, set aside the conviction, or establish a constitutional entitlement to the evidence. Switzer's unresolved merits objections remained for the lower courts. (531, 537.)

Justice Ginsburg wrote for **six justices**: Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Scalia, Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan joined her. Justice Thomas dissented, joined by Justices Kennedy and Alito. The division was **6–3**. (523, 537.)

## Pleading the claim and distinguishing an appeal from a constitutional challenge

The Court first clarified what Skinner was claiming. His complaint alleged that denial of access deprived him of liberty interests in using state procedures to seek reversal, a pardon, or a reduced sentence. Counsel explained that he attacked the DNA statute **as authoritatively construed**, rather than asking the federal district court to reverse the particular state-court decisions or adjudicate misconduct by the prosecutor. Rule 8(a)(2) required a plausible short and plain statement, rather than a precise exposition of legal argument. The complaint's imperfect drafting therefore did not prevent the Court from identifying its procedural due process claim. (529–531.)

That distinction also defeated the Rooker-Feldman jurisdiction objection. Lower federal courts cannot hear an appeal seeking rejection of a completed state-court judgment. But the existence of an earlier state decision on the same or related question does not automatically prevent an independent federal claim challenging the constitutionality of the governing statute or rule. The Court treated Skinner's claim as the latter. It did not turn Rooker-Feldman into a general bar whenever federal relief related to prior state litigation. (531–533.)

The notes preserved a separate question: preclusion. Even if Skinner could have raised his federal theory in the state testing proceeding, Rooker-Feldman was not simply preclusion under another name. Ordinary preclusion issues, unaddressed below, remained for remand. The Court also observed that Skinner had used the state procedure, distinguishing his position from a claimant trying to bypass available state processes altogether. (530–533 and notes 8–11.)

## Why DNA access did not necessarily invalidate custody

Heck and Wilkinson v. Dotson supplied the majority's dividing line. A prisoner cannot use § 1983 for relief whose success would necessarily imply that an outstanding conviction or sentence is invalid. But an action that does not necessarily have that consequence can proceed under § 1983, subject to the other requirements of that statute. (533–534.)

An order allowing DNA testing gives access to information. The result could exculpate the prisoner, further incriminate him, or prove inconclusive. Winning access would therefore not necessarily invalidate Skinner's conviction or shorten his confinement. His ultimate hope to use favorable results in a later attack did not change what success in this particular lawsuit would achieve. The majority declined to replace that established line with a broader inquiry into whether the request related generally to guilt or punishment. (534–535 and notes 12–13.)

The majority rejected predictions of uncontrolled postconviction discovery. No evidence supplied by Switzer showed a litigation flood in circuits already allowing such actions. More importantly, Osborne had rejected substantive due process as a basis for a DNA-testing entitlement and left only a narrow procedural due process route. The Prison Litigation Reform Act also constrained prisoner suits through fees, costs, sanctions, and limits on repeated frivolous filings. Allowing the statutory vehicle did not imply an easy merits victory. (525, 535–536.)

The Court distinguished postconviction Brady claims. A successful claim of suppressed favorable, material evidence includes prejudice and necessarily undermines the conviction; mere access to untested DNA does not establish whether it helps the defendant at all. Brady claims therefore remained within habeas's traditional core under the cited precedents. That comparison did not decide a Brady claim brought by Skinner. (536–537.)

## Thomas's dissent

Thomas accepted the majority's characterization of the claim as procedural for purposes of his analysis, while his first note questioned whether the complaint naturally presented substantive due process and whether the requested testing was an appropriate procedural remedy. His objection was that challenges to **state collateral-review procedures** should belong in habeas, rather than § 1983. (537–538 and note 1.)

In his view, trial, appellate, and collateral procedures all participated in determining and reviewing the validity of custody. Treating constitutional challenges to collateral procedures differently would let prisoners evade habeas exhaustion and deference requirements by attacking the process rather than the result. Article 64 testing and the later state habeas application were separate proceedings, but both formed part of collateral review. His second note distinguished that functional description from whether a testing request counted as an application for collateral review under a particular federal tolling provision. (538–542 and note 2.)

Thomas distinguished Dotson's parole procedure from review of a conviction's validity. He rejected treating the majority's necessary-effect formulations as exhaustive limits on § 1983, emphasizing that the Court had previously returned to underlying principles when drawing additional boundaries. His last three notes warned of similar workarounds in direct appeals and rejected a purely mechanical speedier-release test outside the parole setting. He would have affirmed. (542–544 and notes 3–5.)
