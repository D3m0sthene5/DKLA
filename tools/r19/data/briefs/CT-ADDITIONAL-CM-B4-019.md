# Gupta v. Asha Enterprises, L.L.C.
422 N.J. Super. 136, 27 A.3d 953 (App. Div. 2011) | Contracts

## Court and disposition
The New Jersey Superior Court, Appellate Division, decided this appeal on July 18, 2011, after argument on March 22. Judge Payne wrote for a panel comprising Judges Wefing, Payne, and Hayden; no separate writing appears. The court affirmed dismissal of the product-liability, implied-warranty, consumer-fraud, and negligence theories, but reversed summary judgment on the express-warranty claim and remanded. It permitted further litigation of emotional or spiritual injury and possible consequential purification costs; it did not award damages. The assigned statement that such damages “were awarded” overstates this appellate disposition. (136, 141–142, 145–155.)

## Transaction and alleged injuries
Sixteen Hindu vegetarians alleged that an Indian restaurant supplied meat-filled samosas after repeatedly assuring them that their order was vegetarian. They explained that their religious beliefs treated even accidental meat consumption as spiritually harmful and required a purification ceremony in India. They sought compensation for emotional distress and the economic cost of travel and participation. Those beliefs and alleged consequences were plaintiffs’ description of their injury, not judicial findings establishing religious doctrine or final damages. (141–142.)

Viewed favorably to plaintiffs, the summary-judgment evidence showed that Gupta and Agarwal ordered vegetarian samosas on August 10, 2009, explaining that the group was strictly vegetarian. An employee assured them that the restaurant did not make meat samosas. When they returned thirty minutes later, the tray was allegedly marked as vegetarian, and the assurance was repeated. The photograph showed “samosa” but did not clearly reveal “VEG,” which might have been obscured by wrinkles; the court did not silently treat the photograph as conclusive proof of the full marking. (142–143 & n.2.)

After beginning to eat, some plaintiffs questioned the filling. An employee again assured them by telephone that the restaurant did not make meat samosas. They continued eating for a time, then returned the remaining food and were told it contained meat. The menu separately identified vegetarian and nonvegetarian food and listed vegetable samosas, with no meat-samosa listing. A municipal health official’s email confirmed separate vegetarian and nonvegetarian cooking facilities and utensils. (143.)

The restaurant’s partner supplied a different explanation: two nearly simultaneous orders, one vegetarian and one containing meat, were accidentally exchanged. The second customer discovered the mix-up and returned his tray. The restaurant then prepared and supplied a replacement vegetarian order without payment. Plaintiffs did not dispute receiving and accepting that conforming replacement for free. The court assessed the legal sufficiency of the competing submissions without deciding the ultimate truth of every assurance or whether the mistake was intentional. (143–144.)

## Procedure and governing distinctions
The restaurant initially moved to dismiss for failure to state a claim. The motion judge considered certifications and other evidence, converted the application to summary judgment, concluded that further discovery was unnecessary, and dismissed all claims. Plaintiffs argued both that discovery had not begun and that the judge misconstrued each theory. The appellate court applied the same summary-judgment standard, distinguishing a defective product from supplying the wrong product, a statutory economic loss from spiritual injury, and a voluntary express promise from duties imposed by tort law. Lack of discovery justified further proceedings on the warranty theory but did not automatically defeat every other dismissal. (142, 144–146, 152–154.)

## Product liability and implied warranty
The Products Liability Act unified recovery for harm caused by a defective product, except harm caused by breach of express warranty. It subsumed defect-based negligence, consumer-fraud, and implied-warranty theories. Although cooked restaurant food came within the statute, plaintiffs did not allege that these samosas were inherently unsafe, inedible, or unfit for human consumption. Their complaint concerned employees supplying the wrong order, not a defect in the food itself. The court therefore affirmed the product-liability and implied-warranty dismissals and the other counts insofar as they attempted to recover on a product-defect theory. It separately examined nondefect consumer-fraud and negligence claims. (144–146.)

## Consumer fraud: misrepresentation without the required economic loss
An affirmative misrepresentation could violate the Consumer Fraud Act without proof that its maker knew it was false or intended deception. Material oral statements could qualify, and the statute expressly addressed misrepresenting food identity. The repeated vegetarian assurances and alleged tray label constituted prima facie evidence of an unlawful representation; the appellate court disagreed with the motion judge’s conclusion that no supporting facts could emerge. (146–148.)

A private claim nevertheless required an ascertainable loss of money or property caused by the unlawful practice. Plaintiffs offered none. The free conforming replacement undercut any claimed unpaid value of the order. Their proposed cost of a purification trip was a cost of curing alleged spiritual injury, rather than repairing lost or diminished property. The court refused to enlarge the statute’s economic-loss requirement to encompass that injury, relying on decisions excluding noneconomic and emotional losses. A projected property repair could qualify in a different case because it followed an underlying property loss; these plaintiffs had not shown such a loss. Thus, prima facie misrepresentation did not by itself rescue the consumer-fraud count. (148–149.)

## Negligence and negligent emotional distress
The court treated the spiritual-injury negligence and negligent-infliction claims as essentially equivalent. Foreseeability alone did not establish a tort duty; fairness, public policy, the parties’ relationship, the risk, and opportunities for care also mattered. The opinion described New Jersey’s recognized independent emotional-distress patterns: personal exposure to a zone of physical danger, and contemporaneous observation of death or serious injury to an intimate family member. Plaintiffs fit neither pattern. (149–152.)

Moreover, even assuming cognizable negligence claims, plaintiffs supplied no evidence satisfying the applicable standard of substantial bodily injury or sickness, or severe mental or emotional harm. Such evidence lay within their own control and did not require discovery from the restaurant. The appellate court therefore affirmed dismissal, though on grounds somewhat different from the motion judge’s refusal to create a new religious-dietary duty. It did not declare that the allegations lacked sincerity or that religious injury was inherently fictitious. (150–153.)

## Express warranty and consequential damages
The express-warranty claim was different. Under UCC §2-313, factual promises or descriptions forming the basis of the bargain could warrant conformity without formal warranty language or a specific intent to warrant. Plaintiffs had presented prima facie evidence that the restaurant’s employees warranted vegetarian samosas. The alleged failure concerned precisely that promised characteristic; it was not defeated merely because the food remained edible to people without plaintiffs’ dietary requirements. (153.)

UCC §2-715 permitted consequential loss from requirements or needs the seller had reason to know when contracting and that could not reasonably be avoided by cover, as well as injury to person or property proximately resulting from breach of warranty. The court found relevant its funeral-services precedent recognizing emotional distress from breach of a promise to provide religiously significant services. Difficulty calculating that injury did not warrant dismissal of the claim. This supported allowing the alleged emotional or spiritual loss to proceed under the voluntary express warranty despite failure of the separate tort and consumer-fraud theories. (153–154.)

Purification expenses presented an additional question. Plaintiffs had to establish that such consequential costs were reasonably foreseeable when the order was placed and a reasonably certain consequence of breach, although the exact amount need not be certain. Since discovery had not begun, the record did not permit deciding what such damages were within the parties’ contemplation. Summary judgment on the express warranty was therefore reversed and the claim remanded for development, not converted into a final travel-expense award. (154.)

## Matters expressly left open
The court declined to decide whether liability could extend beyond the person who placed the order, treating that issue as dependent on future discovery. It also provisionally observed that neutral legal principles appeared sufficient without deciding religious doctrine or ministerial functions, but left further consideration of religious entanglement open if later warranted. The latter reservation appears in note 3, which continues onto page 155 below the next case’s caption; it remains part of Gupta and was read in full. Neither issue was finally resolved against the restaurant. (154–155 & n.3.)
