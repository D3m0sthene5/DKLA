# Nixon v. Sirica

**487 F.2d 700 (D.C. Cir. 1973) (en banc).** Decided October 12, 1973; the reported caption records amendments on October 12 and October 25. **Civil Procedure — mandamus, interlocutory review, presidential communications, and in camera inspection.** Assigned account: CivPro, physical PDF page 624, printed page 1237, Note 2's Nixon paragraph. This is the 1973 grand-jury proceeding, a separate judgment from United States v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683 (1974).

## Facts and procedural course

A federal grand jury investigating Watergate sought nine recordings of particular presidential meetings and telephone conversations occurring between June 20, 1972, and April 15, 1973. Its July 23, 1973 subpoena also sought two memoranda that the President supplied. President Richard Nixon refused the recordings in a July 25 letter to Chief Judge John Sirica, asserting the public interest and the constitutional position of the Presidency. Special Prosecutor Archibald Cox sought enforcement, and the grand jury expressed its wish to obtain the subpoenaed evidence. Nixon specially appeared to contest jurisdiction and formally claimed executive privilege. 487 F.2d at 704–706.

The demand was specific rather than an effort to search presidential files generally. Conflicting accounts already given by White House participants suggested that the recordings could resolve questions of obstruction, conspiracy, and perjury. Nixon had permitted present and former officials to testify publicly about their conversations. The prosecution's August 13 memorandum explained the relevance of each requested communication. The court characterized this combination of particular need, available recordings, and conflicting public testimony as an unusually strong evidentiary showing. Id. at 705–706, 716–719.

Sirica's August 29 order required submission to him for private inspection before determining what, if anything, the grand jury could receive. It did not immediately deliver the recordings to the grand jury. Nixon sought mandamus to vacate that order. The United States sought its own writ directing immediate production to the grand jury or other relief, and also filed an appeal. These were three proceedings before the en banc court. Id. at 704–708.

Before deciding the constitutional dispute, the court proposed a negotiated procedure in which presidential and prosecutorial counsel would inspect and analyze the materials together. Appendix I reproduces the September 13 proposal and the September 20 responses from Charles Alan Wright and Cox: lengthy, sincere negotiations had not produced agreement, and their substance would remain confidential. The appendices therefore document an attempted accommodation, not a settlement that made judicial review unnecessary. Id. at 723–726.

## Issues and holdings

The first issue was whether extraordinary review was available before the President disobeyed the production order and incurred contempt. All seven judges agreed with Part II's exercise of review on the President's petition. The merits divided five to two: the per curiam majority consisted of Chief Judge Bazelon and Judges Wright, McGowan, Leventhal, and Robinson; Judges MacKinnon and Wilkey each wrote separately, concurring on jurisdiction and otherwise dissenting. Id. at 703, 706–708, 729, 762–763.

The majority held that the President was neither absolutely immune from this process nor the exclusive judge of executive privilege. Presidential communications were presumptively privileged, but that presumption yielded to the particular and exceptionally compelling grand-jury need shown here. The district court could conduct a carefully controlled in camera examination and decide particular disclosure questions. This was not a holding that all presidential communications were public, that every grand-jury demand overcame privilege, or that these tapes had already been conclusively adjudged disclosable in their entirety. Id. at 708–722.

## Why mandamus review was available

Ordinarily, a recipient cannot obtain immediate appellate review of a subpoena enforcement order simply by appealing it. Disobedience followed by contempt provides a route to review. Sirica's requirement of initial judicial inspection did not broadly distinguish this case from the ordinary subpoena situation. But the President presented a basic, undecided jurisdictional question concerning a district court's power to order the President to submit materials. Requiring presidential disobedience and contempt before considering that question could be clearly inadequate; prompt resolution also carried unusual public importance. The All Writs Act therefore permitted review at the President's instance. Id. at 706–708.

Footnote 21 preserves another possible basis for review: in this particular presidential situation, the court did not discard direct appeal as an alternative, stressing the unseemliness of contempt and the need to safeguard constitutional interests. That reservation does not announce a general right to appeal ordinary discovery orders. The distinction is between the extraordinary circumstances that justified considering this petition and the separate question whether the President should win on its merits. Id. at 707 n.21.

The prosecutor stood differently. His initial appeal did not challenge a final decision withholding material from the grand jury, and the preliminary inspection order was not yet an order suppressing or excluding evidence under the Criminal Appeals Act. The majority also doubted the propriety of prosecution mandamus to obtain immediate production. It expressly left jurisdiction over the prosecutor's mandamus petition undecided because the President's petition already brought the relevant inspection issues before the court. Id. at 707–708 & nn.23–25.

## Judicial authority and qualified privilege

Nixon's constitutional argument had two levels: courts could not compel an incumbent President at all, and his formal assertion of privilege conclusively barred examination of the conversations. The majority rejected both. The Burr proceedings showed that presidential evidence was not categorically beyond judicial process. Youngstown's restraint on executive action did not depend on naming a subordinate instead of the President. The fact that Nixon personally held the recordings did not change the legal authority involved. Whether physical enforcement might become necessary was distinct from whether a legal duty existed. Id. at 708–712.

The court did not decide whether an incumbent President could be criminally prosecuted before impeachment. An order for evidence relevant to other persons' alleged crimes was not itself a prosecution of the President, and the impeachment argument did not resolve this subpoena. The majority likewise treated legitimate privilege as a real limit: if constitutional or evidentiary law gave the President absolute discretion over these records, the order would fail because of that privilege, not because presidential status eliminated every judicial inquiry. Id. at 711–713.

Confidential executive deliberation serves important public interests. Advisers may speak differently if all advice and tentative positions can later be demanded wholesale. The majority therefore recognized a presumption protecting conversations concerning official presidential duties and gave that interest great weight. It nevertheless concluded that courts must determine the legal validity and scope of a privilege claim. Reynolds, the court's Seaborg decision, and other privilege cases did not make the executive the final judge of whether pertinent evidence was protected. Id. at 713–717.

The need here overcame the presumption. The grand jury was investigating crimes within its authority, the recordings concerned specifically identified events, and the prosecutor explained why they could be decisive rather than merely cumulative. Inconsistent recollections and public testimony increased the value of an accurate contemporaneous record. Nixon's decision to permit officials to testify also affected the weight of preserving confidentiality for those particular discussions. The majority's reasoning rests on that factual setting; it does not require finding the President himself guilty or announce that permission to testify automatically waives every privilege over recordings. Id. at 716–719; 722 n.101.

## Safeguards and exact disposition

The majority required particular claims rather than an all-encompassing refusal. Nixon could identify and index manageable portions of the material and correlate them with specific grounds for withholding. If a portion concerned national defense or foreign relations, he could decline to transmit that portion initially and ask Sirica to reconsider whether private judicial inspection was necessary. The prosecutor could inspect the claim and supporting showing and be heard in chambers; a sustained claim would be preserved under seal. Id. at 720–721.

Other items covered by the order were to be submitted to the district court, with specification of what could and could not be disclosed. On either counsel's request, Sirica would hold a hearing in chambers, then inspect the disputed material himself. The judge could allow the prosecutor limited access to aid a determination of relevance, but if he did so, he was to stay that action upon request long enough to permit an application to the court of appeals for a stay. Thus prosecutorial access was possible under controlled conditions, not automatically authorized by the original subpoena. Id. at 721.

After inspection, the judge could sustain a particular privilege in full, disclose all or a segment to the grand jury, or, if separation was impossible, formulate a complete statement of the portions bearing on possible criminality. A reasonable stay would protect the President's opportunity to appeal. Footnote 100 distinguishes these later, particularized disclosure rulings from the initial inspection order: the court regarded them as appealable by the President under §1291 and by the prosecutor under §3731, with sealed records and confidentiality maintained. Id. at 721 & n.100.

The United States' petition and appeal were **dismissed**. The President's petition was **denied except insofar as the court directed modification of Sirica's order and further proceedings consistent with its opinion**. The mandate was stayed five days to permit an application for Supreme Court review. The decision therefore sustained judicial inspection subject to express modifications; it did not order unconditional immediate disclosure to the grand jury. Id. at 722.

## The appendices and separate writings

Appendix II supplies Cox's concrete explanation for all nine communications. These included meetings immediately after the break-in, Mitchell's resignation discussions, the September 15 discussion after indictments, March 13 and March 21 conversations concerning the developing cover-up and demands for money, the March 22 meeting concerning the Senate investigation, and the April 15 discussion with Dean. Several accounts were sharply disputed. Cox explained how a recording might corroborate one version, disprove it, or identify perjury or a continuing conspiracy. Those are the prosecutor's evidentiary contentions, not judicial findings that every asserted conversation occurred as Dean described or that the President committed a crime. Id. at 726–729.

**MacKinnon, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** MacKinnon accepted Part II jurisdiction but would protect confidential communications between the President and close advisers concerning Article II duties as an absolute constitutional privilege. His claim was category-specific rather than a privilege for every private paper the President possessed. Historical presidential refusals, including an extensive table of Truman-era requests and responses, and analogous legislative and judicial confidentiality supported independence from another branch's assessment of particular need. Judicial responsibility remained to decide whether the privilege was properly invoked, but where the protected character was sufficiently apparent, he read Reynolds as rejecting inspection merely to determine the material's content. Id. at 729–755.

MacKinnon distinguished the Burr defendant's compulsory-process claim from a grand jury's request and argued that suspected misconduct by advisers did not destroy a privilege held for the Presidency's institutional benefit. He developed an impeachment-before-prosecution position that the majority declined to decide. Nor did permission for officials' testimony necessarily waive the privilege over more complete recordings: latitude to inform the public without surrendering every confidential detail was itself important. He favored voluntary disclosure and political responsibility rather than judicially compelled balancing. His final amended note discusses judicial confidentiality during the Court-packing controversy. Id. at 755–762, including n.130.

**Wilkey, concurring on jurisdiction and dissenting on the merits.** Wilkey agreed with MacKinnon's result but organized the dispute around who decides. He distinguished ordinary common-law or statutory confidentiality, which courts can balance, from a constitutional privilege protecting one branch from another. On his account, the President was the final decision-maker for the latter, just as the other branches controlled their own constitutionally protected deliberations. The extensive historical analysis includes the 1789 debates, presidential responses to legislative demands, and the Burr proceedings; he emphasized that Jefferson's actions avoided a final coercive confrontation rather than demonstrating judicial supremacy over executive privilege. Id. at 762–788.

Wilkey rejected treating the grand jury as an independent fourth branch that could override this allocation: despite its investigatory independence, it worked within the judicial branch and under significant judicial supervision. The discussion on pages 789–792 includes a developed account and quotation of that relationship. He further argued that practical inspection procedures derived from FOIA cases did not establish authority to balance a constitutional presidential privilege. Private inspection could expand to judicial staff, appellate judges, and ultimately the Supreme Court, threatening the confidentiality it purported to preserve. His alternative relied on political accountability and the working tension among coequal branches. Id. at 789–799.

## Assigned significance and source limits

The textbook uses Sirica to show that mandamus can be an appropriate mode of review even when the applicant does not obtain the requested merits relief. Its quotation from page 707 accurately identifies the unusual public importance of prompt resolution. Its abbreviated description of denial omits the majority's express modifications and safeguards, which the complete opinion supplies. This historical brief rests on the complete reported judicial text, both appendices, all numbered notes, both separate writings, and the entire supplied assigned account. It makes no current-law or later-treatment certification.
