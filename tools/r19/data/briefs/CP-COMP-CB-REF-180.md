# Larson v. AT&T Mobility LLC

**687 F.3d 109 (3d Cir. 2012) — United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit, June 29, 2012.** Civil Procedure. Assigned: CivPro PDF page 436, printed pages 844–845. Judge Jordan wrote for a panel with Chief Judge McKee and Judge Fuentes; no separate writing is reported. The court **vacated the district court’s January 15, 2010 certification-and-settlement order and remanded for further proceedings**. Pinpoints below use the numbered pages of the complete 62-page judicial PDF, not an inferred Federal Reporter page map. The assigned quotation itself identifies reporter pages 128–129.

## The dispute and settlement

Until late 2008, Sprint used approximately $200 flat early-termination fees in its cellular-service contracts. Unlike prorated fees, these amounts did not decline as the contract approached its end. Consumers sued alleging illegal penalties under federal communications and state consumer-protection law. The original complaint included AT&T and Sprint customers, but the operative complaint’s five representatives were people charged Sprint fees. AT&T was not part of this settlement or the appeal, despite remaining in the case caption. Slip pages 10–12 & nn.1–3.

Before resolution of Sprint’s dismissal motion, the parties negotiated for five months under a retired district judge’s supervision. They agreed in December 2008 to a $17.5 million settlement: $14 million cash and $3.5 million in activation-fee waivers, additional minutes, and credit forgiveness. It resolved this and ten other suits while excepting specified claims in California’s Ayyad litigation. Sprint agreed not to enter new fixed-term contracts containing flat fees for two years beginning January 1, 2009; that restriction did not itself prevent collection under existing contracts. The settlement released a broad range of fee-related claims. Slip pages 12–19.

Relief varied among four categories. People who had paid a fee could receive specified cash amounts or alternative service benefits; people charged but not paying a fee could receive credits if Sprint still held the debt, or service benefits. Category IV, for claims arising after notice but before January 1, 2011, offered noncash calling, activation, minute, or text benefits. Its claim deadline differed from the earlier categories’ deadlines. These distinctions later mattered to whether former customers had sufficient incentive to protect people still exposed to fees. Slip pages 14–19 & nn.6–7.

## Notice proceedings and objections

The District of New Jersey preliminarily approved the settlement and conditionally certified a Rule 23(b)(3) class in December 2008. After the initial fairness hearing, it found the proposed notice inadequate. A complete list spanning 1999–2008 might require six to twelve months and at least $1 million, but the inability to identify everyone did not excuse failure to identify reasonably accessible subsets. The court required a new plan identifying such subsets and providing them individual notice. Slip pages 19–24 & nn.8–13.

Sprint and class counsel then proposed an amended plan with compliant inserts to current customers, notice to 194,461 members of another California class, and notice to approximately 90,000 otherwise identifiable subscribers. They declined to search Sprint’s billing records. A declaration by Scott Rice estimated that obtaining recent records would cost approximately $100,000 altogether; its component estimates covered April–June 2009 for $20,000 and April 2007–March 2009 for $80,000. Sprint and counsel called that time and expense unreasonable, although they offered to undertake further efforts if ordered and deadlines were extended. The district court approved the amended plan after twelve days, accepting the declaration as sufficient justification. Slip pages 24–26 & nn.12–14, 35 & n.26.

The Galleguillos objectors challenged both notice and the representatives’ adequacy. Their initial estimate of 9.2 million discoverable customers included corporate and government accounts outside the class. They corrected the estimate to 4.2 million using an expert’s analysis of other Sprint data. Sprint nevertheless acknowledged that its records could identify millions of class members. The court rejected the objections and approved certification and settlement on January 15, 2010. It relied on the cost declaration, characterized the notice as robust, and concluded that the representatives’ interests were not antagonistic to the class. Slip pages 26–33 & nn.15–24.

Other objector Jessica Hall alleged a reverse auction and challenged charging renewed notice expenses to the common fund. The appellate court found no abuse in rejecting the collusion theory, which the mediator’s account contradicted. But it expressly declined to decide whether the settlement was fair, reasonable, and adequate because notice and representation still required consideration. Appeals concerning the allocation of counsel fees were also not reached given the disposition. Slip pages 11 n.1, 28–29 n.18.

## Why the notice ruling was an abuse of discretion

Rule 23(c)(2)(B) requires individual notice to all members identifiable through reasonable effort. Certification and settlement approval receive abuse-of-discretion review, but discretion is abused by legal error, clearly erroneous findings, or improper application of law to facts. The court assumed Rice’s declaration admissible without resolving the evidentiary objections or their asserted waiver; even accepting the declaration, the refusal to require any billing-record search could not stand. Slip pages 33–36 & nn.24–27.

The opinion carefully distinguished **finding recipients** from **paying to deliver notice**. Eisen treated individual notice to reasonably identifiable members as mandatory, rather than something dispensable because postage was expensive or the representative plaintiff lacked money. Oppenheimer addressed allocating identification costs and did not eliminate the need to obtain available identifying information. Nissan and Third Circuit precedent likewise required substantial efforts where records offered the best means to notify absent members. Expense, anticipated results, and the amount involved inform reasonable effort; size alone does not excuse effort, because a large class may require a large search. Slip pages 36–43 & nn.28–29.

Sprint’s reliance on Domestic Air was misplaced. That decision involved a proposed list whose relationship to actual class membership was highly uncertain. Here Sprint acknowledged that its records could identify millions of actual class members. An accurate partial list requires notice even when a complete list is impracticable. More importantly, the district court had originally recognized precisely that distinction, then changed course with little explanation beyond the same declaration’s description of time and expense. Slip pages 43–47.

The costs reinforced the inadequacy of that explanation. If the $100,000 search identified two million members, identification would cost approximately five cents each, or approximately forty-three cents including the mailing estimate. Using the objectors’ 4.2 million estimate reduced identification cost per person further. These were hypothetical calculations, not findings that exactly two or 4.2 million people had actually been identified. Sprint also conceded the obligation to mail once members were identifiable. Relative to earlier cases, the projected search was not exceptionally burdensome, and the recipients were especially important: people who paid the fees stood to receive much of the settlement yet were generally no longer current customers reachable by bill inserts. Slip pages 47–50 & nn.30–32.

The court vacated and required reassessment on a fuller record with a fuller explanation. It expressed strong skepticism about refusing every search but **left the final reasonable-effort determination to the district court**. Statistical sampling could produce a sounder estimate of likely identifications. The lengthy footnote discussing electronic-discovery proportionality offers a useful analogy, not a replacement for Rule 23’s notice requirement: identifying-information benefits were already known to be important, unlike discovery whose value might remain uncertain. Slip pages 50–53 & n.33.

## Representation concerns and matters left open

Adequacy under Rule 23(a)(4) involves both freedom from conflicting interests and ability and incentive to represent the class vigorously. The district court emphasized the absence of antagonism but did not adequately examine the representatives’ incentives. All were former customers when the settlement was made; the court questioned their incentive to prevent continued fee enforcement against current subscribers. Qualified counsel did not answer that separate issue, and counsel’s own adequacy was governed by Rule 23(g). Slip pages 54–58 & nn.34–38.

A concrete potential shortfall illustrated the concern. Customers terminating after the March 15, 2010 deadline for Categories I and II but before the end of 2010 could incur the same fees yet receive only Category IV’s noncash benefits. Counsel acknowledged different treatment in this gap. The appellate court did not finally decide inadequacy: the objection had not been developed below as clearly as on appeal, and its waiver was debatable. It nevertheless urged renewed analysis and stressed the district court’s independent obligation to protect absent members. Slip pages 59–61 & nn.39–44. This is a remand concern, not a holding that every former-customer representative necessarily fails adequacy.

## Judgment and assigned significance

The exact disposition vacates the January 15 approval-and-certification order and remands for further proceedings. It does not finally adjudicate Sprint’s underlying fee liability, direct a particular damages payment, decide the full settlement’s fairness, or resolve the counsel-fee allocation appeals. Slip page 62; pages 11 n.1 and 28–29 n.18.

The assignment accurately quotes the court’s cost reasoning but summarizes it as refusal to order a search. The complete opinion adds the important qualification that the district court retained the ultimate reasonable-effort decision on remand. The surrounding Oppenheimer and Hunt discussions concern cost allocation; Larson’s principal holding concerns adequate notice and unexplained refusal to search. The complete 62-page original judicial reproduction, all forty-four notes and continuations, and the entire assigned page in native text and original scan were read. No separate opinion or appendix appears in the source. This is a historical account without a present-law certification.
