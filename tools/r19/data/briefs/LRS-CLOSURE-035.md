# Medical Committee for Human Rights v. SEC; SEC v. Medical Committee for Human Rights
432 F.2d 659 (D.C. Cir. 1970), vacated as moot, 404 U.S. 403 (1972) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and administrative course

The Medical Committee for Human Rights received Dow Chemical stock by gift and sought to put the company's production of napalm before its fellow shareholders. In March 1968, its chairman proposed a charter amendment prohibiting sales without assurance that napalm would not be used against people. Besides its humanitarian objection, the Committee asserted adverse effects on recruitment and worldwide business. Dow answered that the proposal was too late for its 1968 proxy materials but promised later consideration. After receiving no further word, the Committee renewed its request in January 1969. Dow announced that it would omit the proposal. The Committee then revised it into a request that directors consider a charter amendment forbidding manufacture of napalm, subject to federal defense-production requirements. This asked directors to consider a change rather than purporting itself to enact a charter amendment. (432 F.2d at 661–663.)

Dow invoked the then-existing Rule 14a-8 exceptions for proposals primarily promoting general political or social causes and for matters concerning ordinary business operations. The SEC's Division of Corporation Finance advised that it would recommend no action if Dow omitted the proposal, relying on Dow's letter and counsel's opinion. The Committee retained counsel, requested Commission review, and submitted legal arguments. The full Commission approved the staff recommendation. Its minutes recorded a March 24, 1969 decision; the written notification was mailed April 2. The Committee petitioned for judicial review on May 29. The SEC argued against jurisdiction without defending the substantive basis of exclusion. (663–664, 676.)

## The 1970 appellate decision: reviewable Commission action

Judge Tamm wrote for the unanimous McGowan–Tamm–Robinson panel on July 8, 1970; rehearing was denied August 26. The court first rejected untimeliness under Exchange Act § 25(a)'s sixty-day period. The SEC relied on the date of adoption and a telephone notification, but its own rule also contemplated availability of the order for inspection. Written information concerning the decision was unavailable for a substantial interval. Starting the period with an unelaborated telephone call would undermine the balance between administrative finality and adequate notice and preparation. The court did not need to treat the SEC's initial disclaimer of a timeliness objection as a waiver. (659, 661, 664–665 & nn. 2–4.)

The harder issue was whether the approved no-action position constituted an order in a proceeding reviewable under § 25(a). The court examined practical finality, effect on a protected party, and procedural formality rather than treating an agency's negative response as inherently unreviewable. The administrative process had ended for this proposal. Shareholders were the statutory beneficiaries of corporate-suffrage protections, and the Committee was aggrieved: it had expended time exhausting administrative channels, lost potential SEC assistance, and would face an adverse agency interpretation in private litigation. The availability of a separate action against Dow did not eliminate that injury. (665–669.)

Although no trial-type hearing occurred, this was more than ordinary staff advice to one interested person. Rule 14a-8 prescribed an adversarial process between shareholder and management. Management had to provide the proposal, reasons for exclusion, and legal support to both the SEC and the shareholder; the rule's history placed the burden of establishing proper exclusion on management. The Commission accepted the Committee's legal submissions and made its own final determination. Those procedures supplied sufficient formality. Section 14(a)'s broad regulatory scheme did not become immune from review because its principal enforcement mechanism was an injunction rather than a conventional administrative command. (669–672.)

Direct appellate review also fit the relief requested. The Committee sought correction of the Commission's legal approach and administrative reconsideration, not resolution of disputed facts requiring a district-court trial or an injunction uniquely available there. Requiring a private suit would create a circuitous route and impair access for small investors unable to finance competing solicitations or litigation. The court therefore held this Commission determination reviewable. (672–673 & n. 12.)

## Review of legal premises without commanding enforcement

The court rejected an all-or-nothing conception of administrative discretion. The important question was how far the particular action was committed to discretion, assessed in its statutory setting. Prosecutorial discretion remained significant, but could not automatically shield an erroneous legal premise. The opinion discussed published criticisms of opaque proxy administration as criticisms, rather than proving that every alleged institutional failing occurred in this case. Its concrete concern was that unexplained acceptance of management's several objections gave a shareholder no meaningful account of the decision or guidance for future submissions. (673–675 & nn. 13–16.)

The limitation is essential: courts would not supervise resource allocation or require the full Commission to consider every staff response. Here the Commission had already chosen to review the controversy. Judicial review would examine its legal framework and return the matter for a proper exercise of the agency's remaining discretion. The court expressly distinguished compelling initial Commission review or a particular enforcement action on remand. Review of a staff no-action decision depended on the Commission's initial choice to review it. The court neither ordered prosecution nor established reviewability of every staff letter. (675–676 & nn. 17–21, especially n. 19.)

## Proxy-law reasoning and the appellate judgment

Section 14(a)'s corporate-democracy purpose informed interpretation of the exclusions. The court traced the political-cause exception to proposals seeking shareholder consensus on matters unrelated to the company's own affairs. It traced the ordinary-business exception to preserving management's ability to make routine operating decisions, with state corporation law supplying an important reference point. Both exceptions were legitimate, but construing them so broadly that proposals were always either too general or too specific could defeat the inclusion rule. (676–680 & nn. 22–28.)

Dow's ordinary-business argument did not adequately identify controlling Delaware law or satisfy management's burden. Delaware law expressly permitted charter amendments changing the nature of the business. The court found no explanation why this proposed change fell outside that authority. Dow's assertion that protests against the Vietnam War proved an improper political purpose was likewise insufficient: the proposal concerned a product the company itself manufactured, not a demand to change unrelated public policy. Even accepting a socially motivated desire to make less profitable choices, shareholders had a substantial argument that the rules should permit deliberation about use of their own corporate assets. (680–681 & nn. 29–31.)

The record additionally suggested that management continued napalm production for its own moral and political reasons despite limited profits and business costs. The court did not judge the wisdom of those positions; it questioned using proxy exclusions to shelter them from shareholders while allowing management's personal preferences to control. The judgment **remanded for reconsideration within the Commission's proper discretion and a sufficiently detailed explanation**, not an outright judicial direction that Dow must include the proposal. No separate circuit opinion accompanied the decision. (681–682 & nn. 32–33.)

## The distinct 1972 Supreme Court decision

On January 10, 1972, Justice Marshall wrote for six Justices—Burger, Brennan, Stewart, White, Blackmun, and himself. Douglas dissented; Powell and Rehnquist did not participate. By then Dow had included the renewed proposal in its 1971 proxy materials. Less than three percent of voting shareholders supported it, allowing exclusion of the same or substantially similar proposal for three years under another SEC rule. Dow retained its legal position that exclusion was permissible. (404 U.S. at 403–406.)

The majority nevertheless held the controversy moot. A future submission and future rejection after the three-year interval were conjectural, particularly given the proposal's scant support. Voluntary cessation would not moot a dispute if repetition were likely, but the majority found no reasonable expectation of recurrence on this record. Article III therefore barred a merits decision. The Court **vacated the appellate judgment and remanded to the court of appeals for dismissal**. It did not affirm the appellate reviewability theory, reverse that theory on its merits, or decide the proper substantive scope of the proxy exclusions. (406–407.)

Douglas argued that Dow's reserved right to resume exclusion defeated mootness. Voluntary cessation ordinarily did not suffice, and even a promise not to repeat conduct had been insufficient in prior litigation; Dow had made no such promise. The majority's prediction that inclusion would be cheaper than litigation was speculative and irrelevant to whether the dispute remained live. His dissent emphasized the public importance of shareholder participation and the possibility that proposed SEC rule changes would strengthen management's position. His six notes discuss prior voluntary-cessation litigation, scholarly accounts, corporate influence, and competing proposed reforms; they are not majority holdings. (407–411 & Douglas nn. 1–6.)
