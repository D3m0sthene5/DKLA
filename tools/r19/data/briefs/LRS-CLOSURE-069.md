# Davidson v. New Orleans
96 U.S. 97 (1878), October Term 1877 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Drainage assessments and state-court proceedings

New Orleans sought judicial approval of an assessment financing drainage of swamp lands in Carroll and Orleans parishes. Its petition was filed December 7, 1871. The estate of John Davidson faced approximately $50,000 in charges on parcels in different locations. Davidson’s widow and testamentary executrix appeared and objected. The state district court refused approval and set aside the assessment, allowing a revised assessment roll. The Louisiana Supreme Court reversed, dismissed the objections, and approved the roll as a judgment against both the listed properties and their owners. Mrs. Davidson brought a writ of error to the United States Supreme Court. (97–98.)

The many objections included the state’s use of a private corporation to perform the work, a legislatively fixed allegedly exorbitant price, possible surplus revenue going to the city, assessment before the improvements were performed, a previous paid assessment for the same purpose, lack of benefit to some parcels, and personal liability in addition to a charge on land. But counsel identified only one federal issue: whether the judgment deprived the owner of property without Fourteenth Amendment due process. Justice Miller cautioned that the Supreme Court was not sitting to correct every alleged state-law error. (98–100, 106.)

## The historical guarantee did not validate whatever a legislature enacted

Miller traced the guarantee through Magna Carta, the Fifth Amendment’s limitation on federal power, and the Fourteenth Amendment’s application to states. The English guarantee had principally protected against the Crown. An American constitutional restraint on a state also had to limit legislative action: a state could not make any chosen procedure sufficient merely by enacting it. A bare statute transferring A’s land title to B, without more, would deprive A of property without due process. (101–102.)

At the same time, due process did not invariably require an ordinary judicial proceeding. The opinion discussed *Murray’s Lessee*, which sustained summary executive collection of a customs collector’s debt through a historically established distress-warrant procedure. That precedent showed why “law enacted by a legislature” and “ordinary court trial” were neither an automatically sufficient nor an invariably necessary definition. (102–103.)

## Gradual definition and the particular assessment rule

The assigned passage contrasts the relatively rare invocation of the federal due-process restraint during its early history with the numerous challenges to state action after the Fourteenth Amendment. Miller criticized use of the clause as an invitation to reconsider an unsuccessful litigant’s abstract views about the justice of state judgments and legislation. He declined a comprehensive definition that might both encompass every forbidden deprivation and exclude every permissible one. Instead, the Court would identify the guarantee’s application by reasoned inclusion and exclusion in successive cases. That was an interpretive method, not a refusal to apply a constitutional rule in this case. (103–104.)

For the present dispute, the Court stated a sufficient-process proposition. When a state imposed a tax, assessment, servitude, or other public-use burden on property and provided a mode of confirming or contesting it in ordinary courts, with notice or property-directed proceedings appropriate to the case, a judgment rendered through that process did not deny due process merely because other objections remained. A fair trial according to the applicable judicial procedure supplied the relevant protection. The opinion relied on *Kennard v. Morgan* and *McMillan v. Anderson*, as well as its historical discussion. (104–105.)

Louisiana required filing the assessment roll in the appropriate district court before collection or effectiveness. Known owners within reach of process received personal notice and a reasonable opportunity to object; unknown or unreachable owners were addressed through advertisement. Those requirements were followed. Mrs. Davidson actually appeared and received a full and fair hearing, first in the trial court and then in the state supreme court. Her losing the appeal did not turn those opportunities into absence of due process. (105–106.)

## Why the other objections did not authorize reversal

Assuming drainage was a project the state could undertake and finance through assessments, Miller treated the choice of corporate instrument, contract price, potential surplus, and advance collection as method or detail outside the asserted federal restraint. *Slaughter-House* supported use of a private corporation for sanitary regulation. Collecting money before embarking on costly work was not inherently defective procedure. (99–101.)

The Court found no federal constitutional provision, as it then understood the Constitution, preventing repeated assessments or unequal state taxation on the asserted grounds. It found no contract in the prior assessment statute promising exemption from future charges. Whether individual parcels benefited enough was a detail it would not reassess in this posture. Personal liability raised a substantial question that might be considered in a state court or possibly on review of a federal circuit court, but it did not establish the due-process defect presented here, and counsel invoked no other federal provision. These are the opinion’s historical holdings; its discussion of compensation and unequal taxation should not be silently recast as a statement of present constitutional doctrine. (105–106.)

## Judgment and Bradley’s concurrence

The Court found no error within its federal cognizance and **affirmed the Louisiana Supreme Court’s judgment**, leaving the assessment approved against the properties and owners. The report gives no numerical vote or identified dissent. Justice Bradley agreed with the result and general tenor but wrote separately because he considered the majority’s inquiry too narrow. (106–108.)

For Bradley, providing some legal process or a judicial proceeding did not alone establish *due* process. The nature, cause, and object of the taking also mattered: taxation, eminent domain, and local-improvement assessments required consideration of whether the particular burden was suitable or was arbitrary, oppressive, and unjust. He thought uncompensated public taking ordinarily implicated due process, while identifying imminent emergencies such as enemy capture or conflagration as exceptions. *Murray’s Lessee* supported historical summary collection but also expressly restrained legislative invention. Bradley’s approach would examine those substantive circumstances while allowing considerable state discretion over procedural forms. It was a concurrence in the judgment, not a dissent or the majority’s complete rationale. (107–108.)
