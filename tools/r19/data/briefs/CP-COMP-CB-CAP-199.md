# Otto Candies, LLC v. Citigroup, Inc.

**963 F.3d 1331 (11th Cir. 2020), July 1, 2020.** Judge Jordan wrote for a panel including Judge Newsom and District Judge James Randal Hall, sitting by designation. No separate opinion was filed. The assigned account appears in CivPro physical page 237, printed pages 432–433, Note 8. Citations below use the numbered pages of the complete 46-page court opinion, rather than an unverified reporter-page conversion.

Two American and thirty-seven foreign plaintiffs sued one American defendant, Citigroup, over an alleged financing fraud involving a Mexican oil-services company. The district court dismissed for forum non conveniens in favor of Mexico. The Eleventh Circuit **reversed and remanded** because the court improperly reduced deference to the American plaintiffs' choice and accepted unsupported assertions about the location of evidence. The reversal did not adjudicate fraud, RICO liability, or the adequacy of the pleading. (Opinion 1–2, 45–46.)

Oceanografía provided drilling services to Pemex, Mexico's state-owned oil company. Citigroup established cash-advance facilities through its indirectly owned Mexican bank, Banamex. The complaint alleged that Citigroup's New York-based Institutional Clients Group developed and supervised the facilities, knowingly approved advances supported by forged Pemex authorizations, and disregarded required internal review. It further alleged that relevant credit documents and communications were saved on Citigroup's United States network, and that employees overseeing the program were in New York and Miami. Citigroup also advised Oceanografía, acted as trustee and paying agent on a bond issue, and participated in other aspects of its financial business. These were allegations accepted for this motion because affidavits or other evidence had not contradicted them, not adjudicated findings that Citigroup committed the fraud. (3–6.)

The Mexican scandal emerged in 2014 and prompted investigations in Mexico, Citigroup's own review led from Miami, and SEC and Justice Department investigations. Shipping lessors claimed they had been induced to deal with a financially unstable company; American plaintiff Otto Candies alleged fraudulent communications received in Louisiana. Bondholders alleged inducement to purchase United States-issued bonds that defaulted; American plaintiff Waypoint alleged communications in New York. Plaintiffs asserted substantive and conspiracy RICO claims plus fraud, aiding and abetting, conspiracy, and fiduciary-duty claims. They chose to sue Citigroup alone, rather than Banamex, Oceanografía, or their Mexican personnel. (5–7, 18–19.)

Citigroup sought forum non conveniens dismissal or, alternatively, dismissal under Rules 9(b) and 12(b)(6). Plaintiffs requested limited discovery into Citigroup's assertions that relevant witnesses and documents were in Mexico and misconduct had not occurred in the United States. Citigroup opposed discovery, saying its position rested entirely on the complaint. The district court denied discovery and later dismissed the amended complaint. It concluded that Americans investing abroad could not be surprised by litigation abroad and therefore gave their choice only reduced deference. (7–8, 11.)

A defendant seeking forum non conveniens bears the burden on an adequate and available alternative forum, the balance of private and public interests, and the ability to reinstate the action abroad without undue inconvenience or delay. An American plaintiff's domestic choice receives a strong initial presumption of convenience, requiring substantial evidence of oppression or material injustice to overcome it. Foreign plaintiffs receive less deference, but their choices do not receive none. The plaintiffs did not challenge the district court's conclusions on Mexico's adequacy and availability or reinstitution of the litigation; those conclusions remained standing. Review for abuse of discretion did not shield an incorrect legal standard or unsupported factual balancing. (8–11 & nn.1–2, 45.)

The court rejected a categorical rule reducing deference simply because an American plaintiff invested in a foreign enterprise. That fact does not establish where the alleged wrongs occurred or where useful evidence can be obtained. Decisions involving substantially foreign misconduct did not compel the district court's broader rule. The panel reserved whether some intermediate deference might be appropriate in other circumstances. Here the unrebutted complaint alleged American direction, communications, personnel, and evidence. Citigroup's insistence that Mexican entities were really responsible substituted its merits theory for the plaintiffs' allegations. Citigroup's own American presence also made its claim of inconvenience on its home territory require a persuasive evidentiary explanation. (11–20 & n.3.)

The presence of thirty-seven foreign coplaintiffs did not itself dilute the two Americans' presumption. The ratio did not demonstrate added litigation burdens; ordinary deposition arrangements could require plaintiffs to attend in their chosen district, while a corporate defendant generally could be examined at home. The court found no evidence that Americans had been added through blatant forum gamesmanship or were merely nominal claimants. It nevertheless left the district court to consider whether separate analysis or severance of domestic and foreign claims was permissible and warranted. Such division could require Citigroup to defend overlapping litigation in two countries, undermining rather than advancing convenience. This was not a guarantee against a later properly supported dismissal. (20–26 & nn.4–5.)

The second independent error was evidentiary. Even *Piper Aircraft*, involving foreign plaintiffs, required enough information to evaluate relevant interests. It did not require names and detailed testimony for every witness, but the supporting affidavit there identified categories and subjects. Citigroup supplied no comparable evidentiary basis establishing the location and materiality of documents or witnesses. General assertions that former Banamex employees or Mexican records were critical did not identify their roles, expected testimony, or actual obstacles. Plaintiffs, meanwhile, plausibly alleged that relevant materials were already accessible on Citigroup's American servers or had been collected in domestic investigations. Digitization mattered to accessibility; physical origin in Mexico did not establish present inaccessibility. (26–34 & nn.6–8.)

Footnote 8 carefully qualifies the apparent “no evidence” description. Citigroup had supplied an expert affidavit and other materials bearing on Mexico's adequacy, banking secrecy, and corporate organization. Those materials did not establish the necessary premises that material witnesses were abroad or relevant records were unavailable domestically. The court also rejected an attempt to reconstruct the district court's unstated claim-by-claim analysis: it had neither delineated the elements nor received sufficient evidence to assess convenience reliably. (34 n.8; 35–37.)

Public-interest analysis likewise needed more than Mexico's substantial connections. The district court had to account for Miami activities and the United States' sovereign interest in alleged domestic wrongdoing by its corporation against Americans. The panel also discussed practical concerns about discovery conditions and return-jurisdiction clauses, but expressly declined to decide whether the particular conditions were erroneous. Questions about governing discovery rules, material breach, cure, enforcement authority, and parallel foreign proceedings were guidance for reconsideration, not resolved legal holdings. (37–45 & nn.9–10.)

The mandate permitted further evidence, limited discovery, or an evidentiary hearing on remand. Unchallenged findings about Mexico and foreign-judgment enforceability remained, without prescribing the latter factor's weight. Alternative pleading defenses remained undecided. The assigned quotation accurately captures the coplaintiff issue; the complete opinion adds the decisive evidence requirement and limits on the reversal. This brief describes the 2020 judgment, without a later-law certification. Independent-review status appears in the accompanying register.
