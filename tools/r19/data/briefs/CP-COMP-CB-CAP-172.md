# BP P.L.C. v. Mayor and City Council of Baltimore

## Facts, removal, and the disputed appeal

Baltimore’s mayor and city council sued energy companies in Maryland state court in 2018. The complaint asserted state-law causes of action concerning promotion of fossil fuels, alleged concealment of environmental consequences, and resulting injuries. Whether those allegations were true, or supplied a successful substantive claim, was outside this appeal. **Court 1–2; dissent 7.**

The companies removed the suit, invoking multiple grounds for federal jurisdiction. They asserted federal-officer removal under §1442 because some challenged exploration, drilling, and production occurred at the federal government’s behest. They also invoked federal-question, Outer Continental Shelf, admiralty, and bankruptcy grounds. These were removal theories asserted by defendants, rather than Supreme Court findings that the companies actually satisfied those jurisdictional provisions. The district court examined and rejected the grounds and remanded the case to state court. **Court 2.**

Section 1447(d) generally prohibited appellate review of remand orders, but expressly excepted cases removed pursuant to the federal-officer provision, §1442, or civil-rights provision, §1443. The Fourth Circuit accepted that it could examine the rejection of the §1442 theory. It affirmed that rejection but declined to review the other removal theories, interpreting the statutory exception as ground-specific. A circuit disagreement over that appellate scope brought the case to the Supreme Court. **Court 2–4.**

## Issue and holding

When a defendant premises removal in part on §1442 or §1443, does the exception allow appellate review of only that removal ground or of the district court’s entire remand order? The Court held that the entire order is reviewable, including the other grounds for removal addressed in it. The question concerns the scope of appellate jurisdiction after remand; it does not establish the validity of any particular ground for removing Baltimore’s suit. **Court 4–8, 14.**

## Textual reasoning

The word “order” was central. The statute authorized review of an order remanding a case, meaning the district court’s direction returning the case to state court. Here that direction rested on rejection of all the companies’ theories. Because courts ordinarily must exercise jurisdiction they possess, the district court could not simply send the suit away while leaving a valid jurisdictional basis unresolved. Review of the order therefore encompassed each rejected basis supporting the command to remand. **Court 4–5 & n.1.**

A case was removed “pursuant to” §1442 when the defendant complied with §1446’s removal procedure and asserted that statute as a ground. The majority rejected Baltimore’s proposed requirement that a federal court first hold the federal-officer ground valid before appellate review could arise. Filing the requisite removal notice effects removal, after which the state court cannot proceed until remand; the statutory exception does not require defendants first to win their removal argument. **Court 5 & n.2, 7–8.**

Nor did reliance on multiple statutes change the result. Elsewhere Congress expressly spoke of cases removed “solely” under a provision, demonstrating how it could impose that restriction. Section 1447(d)’s exception contained no comparable limitation. The majority also refused to narrow the exception merely because exceptions sometimes receive restrictive constructions: statutory exemptions deserve a fair reading, and courts must respect Congress’s compromises among competing goals. **Court 5–7.**

## Precedent, history, and policy

The Court treated Yamaha as the closest analogy. Section 1292(b) permitted review of a certified “order,” and Yamaha allowed review of issues fairly included within that order rather than only the district court’s identified controlling question. Baltimore distinguished §1292(b)’s word “involves,” but the majority understood Yamaha’s reasoning to turn on “order.” Other precedents limiting review of state judgments or government criminal appeals depended on different statutory and constitutional settings. Decisions interpreting the general bar on remand review did not settle the scope of its explicit exception. **Court 8–11.**

Baltimore also argued that Congress’s 2011 addition of §1442 ratified existing decisions narrowly construing the earlier §1443 exception. The majority found no sufficiently broad and unquestioned consensus to overcome contrary text and structure. It separately reserved whether its reading affected appeals from fee-and-cost awards included in remand orders: such awards might be collateral and independently appealable, and that question was not presented. **Court 11–12.**

Efficiency and manipulation concerns likewise did not override the majority’s textual reading. Congress had already accepted some appellate delay for these specially protected removal categories. Considering all grounds could sometimes simplify an appeal by allowing decision on an easier ground. Section 1447(c)’s cost-and-fee provisions and Rule 11 sanctions offered responses to frivolous removal arguments; any further adjustment belonged to Congress. **Court 12–14.**

## Sotomayor’s dissent

Sotomayor considered the multiple-ground situation ambiguous. She rejected both total denial of review and review of every ground, favoring review limited to §1442 or §1443. That preserved the specified exception while respecting the longstanding policy against delaying state litigation through jurisdictional appeals. She emphasized the decades of appellate decisions adopting the limited interpretation before Congress added §1442 without changing the rest of the provision; her starred note addressed the Tenth Circuit’s earlier unpublished decision. **Dissent 1–5 & n.*.**

She also distinguished Yamaha’s statutory context and warned that defendants could add a weak federal-officer theory to obtain otherwise unavailable review of unrelated grounds. An argument might be too weak to pursue on appeal without being so unreasonable as to justify sanctions. In this case, the companies no longer pressed their §1442 ground before the Supreme Court, yet that ground opened review of other theories. **Dissent 6–8.**

## Exact disposition and limits

The Supreme Court **vacated the Fourth Circuit’s judgment and remanded** for further proceedings. It declined defendants’ request to resolve their other removal grounds itself, leaving those questions to the Fourth Circuit in the first instance. **Court 14.** The ruling therefore did not uphold removal, decide the climate claims’ merits, or determine the reserved fee-appeal question.
