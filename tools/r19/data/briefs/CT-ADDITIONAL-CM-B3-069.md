# ARP Films, Inc. v. Marvel Entertainment Group, Inc.
952 F.2d 643 (2d Cir. 1991) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Judge Mahoney wrote the Second Circuit’s December 24, 1991 opinion on a panel with Judges Cardamone and Miner. ARP Films, Amerex Films, and Claude Hill appealed, and Marvel cross-appealed, after district-court summary rulings, a six-week jury trial, and a completed accounting. The court affirmed all challenged orders and the judgment. No separate writing appears. Although Marvel’s first attempted termination was treated as a repudiation, ARP’s choice to retain contractual benefits while withholding substantial payments and accountings justified a later termination of the distribution license. That conclusion did not erase ARP’s damages for Marvel’s own breach or its rights to revenues from valid licenses created before termination. (643, 645–651.)

## Distribution arrangements and the source of conflict
The relationship began with a 1968 agreement permitting production and exploitation of cartoons embodying Marvel-associated characters, including Spiderman. Litigation over that arrangement ended in a 1976 settlement and new agreement. Amerex was formed to distribute specified films, while ARP supplied distribution services, including Hill’s personal services. Amerex had to remit agreed percentages of gross receipts and furnish periodic accountings to Marvel and ARP. Marvel held half of Amerex’s stock in escrow and had approval rights over its transfer; Hill’s transfer of ARP stock also required approval that could not be unreasonably withheld. Paragraph 8 permitted termination of the distribution license for substantial noncompliance, expressly identifying prompt accounting, while providing that the remainder of the agreement would continue. Separate letters in 1979 and 1980 gave ARP additional commission-based distribution rights. (646–647.)

Footnotes explain the corporate names: Marvel had been a Cadence division before sale and substitution of Marvel Entertainment as a litigant; ARP’s Delaware signatory transferred rights and Amerex stock to a same-named Tennessee corporation. The jury found those ARP transfers breached the agreement but that Marvel’s claims concerning them were time-barred. The opinion used each corporate shorthand without treating those identity changes as nonexistent. (646 nn. 1–2.)

Marvel began licensing home videocassette distributions in 1981. ARP asserted in 1982 that its rights were exclusive for that medium. As the dispute escalated, ARP alleged that Marvel pressured it through actual or threatened termination, expensive litigation, and refusal of approval for a public stock offering. Those allegations are distinct from the jury’s later finding that Marvel did breach the 1976 agreement by licensing videocassettes to others. ARP sued in July 1986; Marvel removed on diversity grounds, and its separate action was consolidated. (647–648.)

## Two termination letters and ARP’s continued exploitation
On November 12, 1986, Marvel purported to terminate all granted distribution rights for alleged violations, offering continued distribution under the agreement’s procedures on a courtesy, at-will basis. ARP continued receiving proceeds from existing licenses and initially paid Marvel its share. Marvel refused approval of new proposed licenses, asserting inadequate information. In February 1987 ARP stopped remitting Marvel’s share; in April it stopped reporting distribution activities. On September 2 Marvel issued a second termination letter, stating that withholding payments was an unjustified material breach independently terminating any rights still outstanding. (647.)

The district court first ruled on partial summary judgment that continued exploitation affirmed the agreement, making indefinite withholding impermissible, but initially reserved materiality for trial. On a renewed motion it concluded that the express accounting condition and withholding of more than $400,000 made the breach material and the September termination effective. The appellate opinion’s third footnote narrows the terminology: the license terminated; the agreement’s other terms expressly continued. (648 & n. 3.)

## Trial outcomes and finality
The jury found that the 1976 agreement covered videocassettes but the side agreements did not. ARP could receive its contractual share from authorized licenses made during the agreement’s life, even if revenues arrived later, but could not receive revenue from unauthorized or post-termination licenses. The jury awarded ARP $1,220,000 for Marvel’s competing videocassette licensing and Marvel $137,000 for other contractual breaches. The district court also awarded ARP $293,480 in commissions under a side agreement and ordered an accounting. Footnote 4 records that some special verdicts were confusing; the district court reconciled them, and the Court of Appeals accepted that reconciliation. (648 & n. 4.)

An earlier appeal was dismissed as premature because the unfinished accounting involved legal questions rather than merely mechanical tasks, so the judgment was not yet final under § 1291. After the accounting concluded and a stipulation and order reflected its results, the parties brought the present appeal and cross-appeal. The 1990 finality decision at 905 F.2d 687 is procedural background here, not a separately read full judgment. (649.)

## Election to affirm foreclosed indefinite self-help withholding
ARP argued that withholding payments and reports would pressure Marvel to repent its initial repudiation and should not count as a material breach. The court rejected that combination of continued benefits and nonperformance. Following repudiation, ARP could cease performance and sue for total breach, or affirm through continued performance and sue for partial breach. Its continued receipt of benefits constituted affirmation. Having made that election, it could not keep exploiting the agreement while refusing its important reciprocal obligations over a substantial period. The court did not hold that Marvel had committed no breach; ARP’s damages for Marvel’s breach remained in the judgment. (649–650.)

Materiality was established as a matter of law here. Paragraph 8 expressly made prompt accounting important to the license and authorized termination for substantial noncompliance. Withholding payments was also a generally material contractual failure, and the amounts withheld were very substantial, as the eventual accounting confirmed. Other factual disputes did not materially affect these central facts. The court therefore upheld termination through the second letter despite the earlier wrongful repudiation and ARP’s claims arising from it. (649–650.)

## Other challenged rulings
ARP requested an instruction that Marvel had to expressly reserve videocassette rights, relying on Bartsch’s construction of a broad grant of motion-picture rights. The side agreements, however, were short letters without comparable grant language. The court refused to turn Bartsch’s context-specific construction into a universal rule granting every unreserved right to a licensee. That issue affected the side agreements only, since ARP had already prevailed on the 1976 agreement’s coverage. (650.)

ARP also challenged denial of revenue from new licenses made without Marvel’s consent after November 12. Marvel’s approval was contractually required, and ARP had not demonstrated that refusal of any particular proposed deal before September 1987 was unreasonable. Thus the absence of approval, not merely the existence of Marvel’s first letter, supported exclusion of ARP’s share. The court also upheld exclusion of a barter-syndication damages estimate exceeding $3 million: stations would have supplied advertising time instead of cash, but the offered testimony was speculative, risked unfair prejudice and confusion, and came from a witness not expert in that subject. Those reasons fell within the trial judge’s discretion. (650–651.)

The mixed outcome also supported the discretionary denial of costs and attorneys’ fees under the copyright statute and the applicable costs rule. On Marvel’s cross-appeal, the court declined to overturn the jury’s findings that the 1976 license covered videocassettes, that deferred side-agreement commissions were payable, and that ARP retained shares of revenues from valid pre-termination licenses received later. Viewed favorably to the verdict winners, the evidence did not compel a contrary result. Continuing revenue rights also fit paragraph 8’s preservation of terms other than the license. (651.)

## Assigned significance and source scope
The assigned account on printed page 865 focuses on ARP’s impermissible self-help after affirming the agreement and contrasts it with a buyer’s withholding in Patel. The complete judgment supplies the prolonged failure of essential accounting and payment obligations, the two different termination dates, the license-versus-agreement distinction, and the simultaneous survival of damages and earned revenue rights. It does not make the first repudiation retroactively rightful or declare all post-termination receipts forfeited.
