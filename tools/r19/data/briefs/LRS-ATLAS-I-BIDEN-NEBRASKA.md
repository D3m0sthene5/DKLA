# Biden v. Nebraska

**600 U.S. 477 (2023) · Supreme Court of the United States · June 30, 2023 · No. 22–506**

## The loan program and statutory authority asserted

The Higher Education Act provided several federal student-loan programs and specified circumstances for discharging loans, including certain public service, death, disability, bankruptcy, and school misconduct or closure. The HEROES Act of 2003 separately empowered the Secretary of Education to “waive or modify” statutory or regulatory provisions applicable to Title IV financial assistance when necessary in connection with war, military operations, or a declared national emergency. Among its conditions, the action had to be necessary to prevent affected recipients from being placed in a worse financial position in relation to their assistance because of the emergency. Congress also required publication of the replacement terms and conditions in the Federal Register. (Court 2–4.)

During the COVID emergency, the Department suspended payments and interest accrual, and Congress temporarily extended the suspension. The Department later continued it. In August 2022, the Department announced a further program canceling specified amounts of principal. Eligible borrowers with adjusted gross income below $125,000 in 2020 or 2021 would receive up to $10,000; those who had received Pell Grants could receive up to $20,000. The income cap was $250,000 for specified joint filers, heads of household, and qualifying widows or widowers. The eligible loans included Direct Loans and specified federally held or collected FFEL and Perkins loans. The Department estimated that about forty-three million borrowers qualified, and the Congressional Budget Office estimated about $430 billion in principal cancellation. These were the proposed program’s coverage and fiscal estimates, not a finding that the injunction-blocked program had already discharged all those balances. (Court 5–6 & n.2.)

The Department’s counsel had concluded in January 2021 that blanket cancellation was unauthorized. Following the change in administrations, the Department rescinded that memorandum and adopted the contrary reading of the HEROES Act. The Secretary’s asserted source of power in this case was that Act’s waiver-and-modification authority. The decision concerns this particular program and source of authority; it is not a ruling on every potential statutory student-loan relief mechanism. (Court 5–6, 12–18.)

## Litigation and Missouri’s standing

Arkansas, Iowa, Kansas, Missouri, Nebraska, and South Carolina challenged the program. The district court dismissed for lack of standing. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit issued a nationwide preliminary injunction pending resolution of the appeal, concluding that Missouri likely had standing and the challenge raised substantial merits questions. The Government asked the Supreme Court to vacate that injunction or grant certiorari before judgment. The Court took the case before judgment and expedited argument. (Court 7; Kagan 4.)

The Court held that Missouri had standing based on anticipated injury to the Missouri Higher Education Loan Authority, or MOHELA. That nonprofit public corporation owned some loans and separately serviced millions of federal loan accounts under contract with the Department. Cancellation would close accounts and reduce servicing fees; Missouri estimated a $44 million annual loss. The Government and dissent accepted that the asserted loss was injury to MOHELA. The dispute was whether Missouri could sue for it. (Court 7–8.)

For the majority, MOHELA was an instrumentality of Missouri by both law and function. The State created it to support higher education, controlled its governance through state officials and gubernatorial appointments, required reports, and could determine its dissolution. Its educational contributions also served a public function. Harm impairing that public function therefore directly injured the State. Drawing on *Arkansas v. Texas* and the Amtrak cases, the Court held that separate corporate personality and the ability to sue in MOHELA’s own name did not bar Missouri from suing for this harm. The Court did not find that the lost servicing fees flowed directly into Missouri’s treasury. It also did not need to decide the other plaintiffs’ standing theories once Missouri had standing. (Court 8–12 & n.3.)

The assigned standing paragraph correctly presents the majority’s instrumentality reasoning and the dissent’s contrary third-party objection. Kagan’s dissent stressed the entity’s financial and legal independence: MOHELA’s assets were not state revenues, the State did not assume its liabilities, and it could litigate itself. It had chosen not to join or assist this lawsuit voluntarily. Kagan distinguished the university’s relation to Arkansas and emphasized that being a state actor for constitutional obligations did not make a corporation interchangeable with its creator for litigation. She also rejected an asserted injury to Missouri students as a route around limits on suing the Federal Government as parens patriae. On her view, MOHELA could sue, but these states could not. (Kagan 4–13 & n.1.)

## The Court’s statutory holding

Chief Justice Roberts wrote for Justices Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett. The Court held that the HEROES Act did not authorize this comprehensive principal-cancellation program. Its ordinary statutory analysis was sufficient; the major questions doctrine supplied an additional supporting ground. It did not strike down the HEROES Act as an unconstitutional delegation. (Court 12–25 & n.9.)

The Court read “modify,” in light of *MCI*, as allowing modest adjustments rather than a fundamental transformation. Existing discharge provisions identified limited qualifying circumstances. The new program instead made income and Pell Grant history the basis for broad cancellation across nearly all borrowers. That was a new regime rather than a moderate alteration. (Court 13–15.)

“Waive” did not solve the problem. Past uses had removed particular requirements, but here the Secretary needed both to remove restrictions and to create new dollar limits and eligibility rules. The Court rejected the argument that combining waiver with modification eliminated the limits of either word. Nor did the publication provision authorizing notice of terms applied “in lieu of” prior provisions independently confer unlimited substantive authority: any replacements still had to remain within the underlying modification power. The Court acknowledged that additions can be permissible, while denying that this wholesale replacement was one. (Court 15–18 & n.4.)

Two express limits are important. The Court did not decide the legality of the earlier temporary payment and interest suspensions, noting that challenges to them could raise different standing and merits considerations. It also did not rest its judgment on the states’ separate argument that cancellation was unnecessary to keep affected borrowers from a worse financial position, although it expressed skepticism about that argument’s factual premise from the Government’s perspective. (Court 18–19 & nn.5–6.)

## Major questions and Barrett’s contextual explanation

The Court regarded the program’s unprecedented scope under the HEROES Act, its fiscal magnitude, and its economic and political significance as reasons to require clear congressional authorization. Prior waivers had not approached this scale. Congress had debated loan forgiveness without enacting this program. For the Court, the central tradeoffs of such a program were ones Congress would ordinarily make itself. It rejected a categorical exemption for government-benefit programs, reasoning that separation of powers includes Congress’s control of expenditures and that *King v. Burwell* had already involved benefits. Because the ordinary reading did not authorize the program, it also lacked the clear authorization needed under the major questions approach. (Court 19–25 & nn.7–9.)

Barrett joined the Court in full and concurred separately. The assigned account develops her distinction between a strong-form substantive canon and contextual interpretation. A substantive canon may require choosing a less natural but tenable reading to protect an external value. Barrett acknowledged the textualist concern with such a rule but denied that this was how she understood the major questions doctrine. For her, it helped identify the text’s most natural meaning in its legal and practical context. (Barrett 1–5 & nn.1–2.)

She illustrated that account with ordinary delegations: an instruction to buy apples does not ordinarily authorize an unprecedented inventory purchase; giving a babysitter a credit card to ensure the children have fun does not normally authorize a multiday amusement-park trip. Further contextual clues, however, could establish that the broader action was intended. Likewise, clear agency authority can come from context, not only microspecific words. Economic importance creates skepticism about an asserted delegation but does not license a court to reject the best reading when text and context genuinely support it. She distinguished her statutory interpretation from a separate constitutional challenge to a delegation. (Barrett 6–11 & nn.3–4.)

Barrett identified familiar clues—mismatch between statutory provision and claimed power, departure from usual agency expertise, and discovery of novel authority in an older law—without treating them as a rigid checklist. She acknowledged that the Secretary was not entirely outside his usual domain here. Nonetheless, the program’s scale and the statutory text supported the majority’s result. Her concurrence thus should not be collapsed into Gorsuch’s Article I substantive-canon justification in the different *West Virginia* case. (Barrett 11–16; assigned PDF 38–39.)

## Kagan’s merits dissent

Kagan, joined by Sotomayor and Jackson, would have dismissed for lack of standing. She also rejected the merits analysis. In her view, the Court isolated “modify” instead of reading “waive or modify,” “any” covered provision, and replacement “terms and conditions” together. If the Secretary could entirely remove a requirement, the paired language naturally permitted intermediate changes and replacement conditions. The emergency declaration, affected-individual limitation, and necessity requirement bounded that authority. (Kagan 13–22 & n.2.)

She treated the HEROES Act as Congress’s deliberate response to the need for speed and flexibility in future emergencies. The pandemic’s greater scope could justify more substantial relief than earlier crises; unprecedented use did not itself prove lack of delegated power. She criticized the majority’s distinction of the payment pause while expressly recognizing that the Court reserved its legality. (Kagan 14–22.)

Kagan further argued that the major questions doctrine displaced Congress’s actual broad delegation with a judicial demand for particularity. She distinguished cases involving ancillary provisions or action outside the agency’s field: emergency student-loan relief was the HEROES Act’s central purpose and within the Secretary’s responsibilities. Her third note directly compares Barrett’s contextual theory with these features. For Kagan, the majority’s approach shifted policy choices from politically accountable officials to the judiciary. That separation-of-powers criticism is the dissent’s position, not the holding. (Kagan 23–30 & n.3.)

## Exact judgment and significance

The Court **reversed the judgment of the Eastern District of Missouri and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion**. It **denied as moot** the Government’s application to vacate the Eighth Circuit’s injunction. The merits holding was **6–3**; Barrett’s concurrence added a distinct explanation without withholding her vote from the Court’s opinion. (Court 26.)

The case combines a contested state-instrumentality standing rule with competing understandings of broad emergency delegation. Keep those questions separate, distinguish ordinary textual analysis from the reinforcing major questions analysis, and preserve the express reservations about other relief programs and the independent statutory necessity argument.
