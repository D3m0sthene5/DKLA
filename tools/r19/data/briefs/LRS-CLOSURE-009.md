# Boumediene v. Bush
553 U.S. 723 (2008) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, question, and result

On June 12, 2008, the Supreme Court decided Boumediene and the consolidated Al Odah case, Nos. 06-1195 and 06-1196, by a 5–4 vote. Justice Kennedy wrote for Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Foreign nationals detained as enemy combatants at Guantanamo Bay could invoke the Suspension Clause's protection of habeas corpus. The Detainee Treatment Act's appellate review was an inadequate substitute, making § 7 of the Military Commissions Act an unconstitutional suspension of the writ. The Court reversed the D.C. Circuit and instructed it to remand to the district court. It decided neither the President's substantive authority to detain these petitioners nor that any petitioner must be released. (732–736, 771–773, 792, 798.)

## Detention and litigation chronology

The Executive relied on the 2001 Authorization for Use of Military Force to detain these petitioners. Some were apprehended in Afghanistan, others as far away as Bosnia and Gambia. All were foreign nationals; none was a citizen of a country then at war with the United States. Each denied membership in al Qaeda or the Taliban. After Hamdi, the Defense Department established Combatant Status Review Tribunals, or CSRTs, to decide whether detainees were enemy combatants under the Department's definition. Each petitioner appeared before a separate CSRT and received an adverse status determination. That determination was contested, rather than an established premise of the Court's decision. (733–734.)

Habeas actions began in February 2002. The district court and D.C. Circuit initially dismissed because Guantanamo lay outside U.S. sovereign territory. In Rasul in 2004, the Supreme Court held that statutory habeas jurisdiction extended to the base without resolving the constitutional question. On return, Judge Leon dismissed one group of cases for lack of enforceable rights, while Judge Green held in the other group that the Fifth Amendment protected the detainees. During those appeals, Congress enacted the DTA in 2005, withdrawing habeas jurisdiction over Guantanamo aliens and providing exclusive D.C. Circuit review of final CSRT determinations. Hamdan held that the withdrawal did not reach pending cases. Congress responded in 2006 with the MCA, expressly making its new jurisdictional withdrawal applicable to pending cases. The D.C. Circuit held that the MCA removed jurisdiction and that the Suspension Clause did not protect petitioners, leaving it unnecessary to assess the adequacy of the substitute. (734–736.)

## The statute really withdrew pending habeas jurisdiction

Petitioners argued that the MCA's effective-date language reached only the other detention-related actions described in § 2241(e)(2), not habeas applications separately identified in (e)(1). Kennedy rejected that reading. The phrase “other action” links the paragraphs, and habeas is itself an action relating to detention. The effective-date provision reached all pending detention-related cases without exception. The legislative response to Hamdan reinforced that meaning. A clear-statement rule facilitates communication between Congress and the judiciary, but when Congress supplies the required clarity the Court must respect its statutory choice and independently decide the constitutional question. It cannot avoid that question by preserving jurisdiction through a contrary statutory construction. (736–739.)

## Constitutional reach: history, control, and practical obstacles

The Court treated habeas as both an individual protection against unlawful confinement and an instrument restraining executive power. Its history—from imperfect common-law checks through the Petition of Right and the Habeas Corpus Act of 1679—explained why the Framers specified limited conditions for suspension. Separation of powers protects persons as well as citizens, and habeas requires the jailer to answer for detention. This structural account informed interpretation of the Suspension Clause. (739–746.)

Founding-era authorities did not conclusively answer the precise question. Alien status was not a categorical bar, but the prisoner-of-war decisions and the writ's reach into exempt jurisdictions, India, Scotland, Hanover, Ireland, and Canada were not straightforward analogies to Guantanamo. English courts sitting in India did not establish what courts sitting in England could do abroad; differences in legal systems, competing courts, and enforceability could explain geographical limits. The Court declined to turn the absence of a precise precedent into proof for either side, particularly given the incomplete historical record and Guantanamo's unusual position. (746–752.)

Cuba retained de jure sovereignty under the lease and treaties; the Court accepted that political-branch determination. The United States nevertheless exercised complete, uninterrupted jurisdiction and control, amounting to de facto sovereignty. Political control of formal sovereign status did not determine the Constitution's reach. The Insular Cases and Reid demonstrated that constitutional application can depend on local conditions and feasible alternatives. Kennedy read Eisentrager's attention to postwar Germany and military difficulties as integral to its decision, rather than treating its territorial language as an exclusive bright-line rule. Allowing the political branches to surrender formal sovereignty while retaining total control would permit them to switch constitutional constraints off through arrangements of their own making. (753–766.)

The operative framework considered at least three factors: citizenship and status, including the adequacy of the process establishing status; the nature of the places of capture and detention; and practical obstacles to resolving entitlement to the writ. Unlike Eisentrager's prisoners, these petitioners disputed their status and had not received a rigorous military trial with lawyers, evidence, and cross-examination. A CSRT personal representative was expressly neither a lawyer nor an advocate; government evidence carried a presumption of validity and the confined detainee could present only reasonably available evidence. Unlike the temporary, shared Allied occupation of Germany, U.S. control of Guantanamo was absolute and indefinite. No Cuban court competed for jurisdiction, and no credible showing established that habeas review would compromise the base's mission. Ordinary resource costs did not overcome those circumstances. An active theater of war or material conflict with a host government's authority could present a different case. The Suspension Clause therefore had full effect at Guantanamo; the MCA neither purported to suspend formally nor was defended as a formal suspension. (766–771.)

## Why the substitute failed

Although the D.C. Circuit had not decided adequacy, the Supreme Court resolved it instead of requiring another remand. Years of detention without meaningful judicial oversight and the serious separation-of-powers issue made these cases exceptional. The parties had briefed the question, and the Court could consider the D.C. Circuit's parallel Bismullah rulings on the review record without waiting for a completed DTA case. (771–773.)

Hayman and Swain had upheld statutes that relocated collateral review to more convenient courts while preserving broad powers and a savings clause for ineffective substitutes. The DTA and MCA instead deliberately narrowed review, contained no such savings clause, and assigned exclusive appellate jurisdiction rather than allowing a transfer to a district court for factfinding. Their text, structure, comparison with § 2241, and legislative history all showed that this was not ordinary habeas under a different name. (773–779.)

An adequate process must provide a meaningful opportunity to show detention rests on erroneous law and empower the court to order conditional release when necessary. Its further requirements depend on context. Historical habeas was adaptable, particularly when detention lacked a prior fair judicial hearing. For executive detention, the court must meaningfully examine both the cause of confinement and the Executive's authority. The rigor of prior proceedings affects how extensive collateral review must be; Kennedy noted that this principle accords with Mathews, but did not convert the case into a due-process holding. (779–783.)

The CSRT's limited access to evidence, lack of counsel, reliance on hearsay, and limited practical ability to confront the accusations created a substantial risk of factual error. That risk mattered particularly when confinement might last a generation or more. Hamdi did not resolve this Suspension Clause question: statutory habeas remained available there, and the cited tribunal language was from a plurality. Even assuming CSRTs satisfied due process, habeas remained independently relevant. Kennedy expressly made no judgment whether the CSRTs met due-process standards. Here the reviewing court needed authority to assess evidentiary sufficiency, correct errors, consider relevant exculpatory evidence not introduced earlier, and issue appropriate relief, including release if necessary. The Court left the precise government showing to later proceedings and did not require a criminal trial. (783–787.)

The Court assumed for argument that the DTA might imply release authority, permit challenges to the AUMF basis for detention, and authorize correction of factual findings. It also assumed Bismullah's broad record construction was right, allowing consideration of government information reasonably available when the CSRT sat. Even those assumptions did not permit evidence becoming available afterward. Nechla, for example, had asked the CSRT to contact an employer who could corroborate his denial of an al Qaeda affiliation; the tribunal found the witness relevant but unavailable, while counsel now represented that he could be heard. The DTA still would exclude that evidence. A request to the Deputy Secretary to convene another CSRT was an insufficient alternative because that decision was discretionary and was not a reviewable final CSRT determination. (787–792.)

Constitutional avoidance could not rewrite the whole scheme to restore the extensive habeas review Congress meant to withdraw. Nor did the Court say that fixing the new-evidence problem alone would suffice, or that every adequate substitute must duplicate § 2241. Taken together, the restrictions made DTA review facially inadequate. The unconstitutional provision was MCA § 7; the DTA and CSRT process themselves remained intact. (787, 792, 795.)

## Exhaustion, remedy, and remaining questions

Petitioners need not complete DTA review before seeking district-court habeas. Some had been held for six years, DTA applications had been pending for over two years without merits decisions, and another exhaustion requirement would cause additional serious delay. This did not establish immediate judicial intervention upon capture. Except where delay is undue, the Executive should ordinarily have reasonable time for a CSRT to assess status before habeas review. A court could also accommodate genuine exigencies and legitimate intelligence interests. (793–796.)

The Court suggested channeling future cases to the D.C. district court through venue mechanisms and protecting intelligence sources and methods within judicial discretion. It left detailed evidentiary and counsel-access issues, the governing substantive detention law, and claims about conditions of confinement unresolved. The precise judgment reversed the D.C. Circuit and remanded with instructions to remand to the district court for consistent proceedings, rather than directing release or declaring any petitioner innocent. (792, 795–798.)

## Separate writings

Souter, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer, joined Kennedy in full and stressed two points. Rasul's historical reasoning had already put the government on notice of habeas's relevance, so the constitutional result was not an unexpected invention. Four years since Rasul and six years of some detentions made charges of judicial haste unpersuasive: the decision was an effort to make long-recognized review meaningful, rather than a judicial victory over the other branches. (798–801.)

Roberts, joined by Scalia, Thomas, and Alito, would dismiss because the statutory system adequately protected whatever rights these detainees possessed. He would first require exhaustion and an actual D.C. Circuit assessment before deciding constitutional reach. He treated the CSRT as a first tier of collateral review modeled on Hamdi, followed by Article III scrutiny, not simply an initial executive designation. Personal representatives, access to witnesses and evidence, appellate counsel, and legal and factual review supplied adequate basic process in the military setting. He argued that remand for new evidence, regulatory reconsideration, annual review, and implied release authority answered the majority's concerns; his note 2 specifically disputed the claim that a refusal to reconvene was unreviewable. He criticized invalidation based on hypothetical defects and predicted that added district-court litigation would delay relief and eventually resemble the displaced system. (801–826, nn. 1–2.)

Scalia, joined by the same three Justices, also joined Roberts but would independently affirm the D.C. Circuit because the Suspension Clause did not reach aliens abroad. He considered Eisentrager's territorial rule controlling and accused the majority of transforming supporting practical considerations into a new test without confronting stare decisis. The Insular Cases concerned U.S. sovereign territory, and Reid's protection of citizens abroad did not establish constitutional rights for aliens abroad. His historical account confined the English writ to the Crown's dominions; restrictions on sending prisoners elsewhere were a distinct legislative response, not proof that the writ itself ran there. He corrected his earlier inclusion of Scotland among places where the writ ran. He argued that even historical ambiguity should favor deference to Congress, and that separation-of-powers abstractions could not enlarge jurisdiction beyond the Clause's original meaning. He warned of military and intelligence burdens and increased danger from mistaken releases. Those predictions and recidivism assertions were dissenting arguments, not majority findings about these petitioners. (826–850, nn. 1–7.)

Its assertion that the Court held CSRT procedures constitutionally inadequate by applying Mathews overstates the actual decision: Kennedy used Mathews as an analogy about procedural rigor, expressly reserved CSRT due-process adequacy, and invalidated the MCA's habeas withdrawal under the Suspension Clause. The account's subsequent Guantanamo population report is later editorial context.
