# Little v. Banks
85 N.Y. 258 (1881) | Contracts

## Holding and judgment
On May 3, 1881, the New York Court of Appeals affirmed a judgment allowing law-book sellers to enforce a state publishing contract's promise to supply them with reports and pay stipulated damages for refusal. The contracting state officials had authority to secure advantageous publication and distribution terms, including enforceable protection of the identified class of dealers. The $100 amount for each refusal was liquidated damages rather than an invalid penalty on these facts, and interest ran from breach. Miller wrote; all participating judges concurred, with Rapallo absent and Andrews not voting. (259, 263–267.)

## Publishing contract and refusals to supply
State officials contracted with A. Bleecker Banks to publish the New York Reports. The contract required retail availability in Albany and New York City, simultaneous sale in both cities, a $1.10 price per copy, and nondiscrimination in supply. Another law-book seller in either city could request up to one hundred copies, with larger quantities at Banks's option; subsequent supplies were to be available at intervals no longer than ten days in quantities up to fifty volumes at a time. The smaller periodic quantity did not negate the separate initial application entitlement. (260–262.)

The contract gave the state officials a cancellation right for default and specified $5,000 payable to the people of the state, recoverable by the attorney general, for breach of its terms. Separately, a failure to keep the books for sale and furnish them at the prescribed price triggered $100 liquidated damages payable to and recoverable by the aggrieved person. The private provision, rather than the state's separate $5,000 remedy, supported this action. Plaintiffs made six applications for copies and Banks refused supply; they sought six sums of $100. The record account identifies a bench-trial judgment affirmed by the General Term before this appeal. (259–262.)

## State authority to confer the enforceable benefit
Banks challenged the officials' authority to impose these terms and the plaintiffs' ability to sue without contractual privity. The publication statute required terms most advantageous to the public and directed the officials to consider proposals. The court treated reasonable distribution protections as within that authority: access through other booksellers promoted circulation to the public, and plaintiffs' holdings of earlier reports gave them a particular interest in supplying the full series. No separate statute expressly authorizing the officials to fix damages was necessary where that reasonable term advanced the authorized contract's purpose. (262–263.)

The court cited the established rule that a promise supported by consideration to pay a third person could be enforced by that person, including Lawrence v. Fox. It also relied on public-contract precedents recognizing private actions by persons injured through a state contractor's nonperformance. Those decisions reflected a broad public-welfare principle. The court concluded that this principle could sustain the suit without depending on Lawrence: the protected distribution provision was a material means of implementing an agreement made for public advantage, and plaintiffs had a specific interest expressly addressed by it. This was not an assertion that any member of the public affected by any government contract automatically has a damages action. (263–264.)

Banks contended that favoring law-book sellers in two cities was not advantageous to the public. The court declined to assume disadvantage where the facts supported distribution through those dealers. It nevertheless reserved a possible defense if an application exceeded ordinary sales needs or was speculative, extravagant, or unreasonable. Reading the retail-availability and dealer-supply clauses together avoided a conflict between them. (264–265.)

## Liquidated damages, proof of injury, and quantities
Banks argued that plaintiffs needed direct evidence of loss and that the stated damages were actually a penalty. The court held that a valid liquidated amount for breach of a material supply duty dispensed with item-by-item proof of lost sales, procurement costs, and injury to trade. The separate state's remedy did not displace the protected dealers' claim. (265.)

Neither the label liquidated damages, the word penalty, nor the term forfeit settled enforceability. The court examined the parties' intention, contractual language, and circumstances. The specified breach was failure to keep, furnish, and deliver the reports as agreed. A refusal could concern a single copy or one hundred; the loss from a large order or interruption of a dealer's ability to supply customers might exceed the fixed amount, while proof could be uncertain and difficult. The $100 stipulation reasonably addressed that uncertainty and was not grossly disproportionate to a probable estimate. The court acknowledged that it could exceed actual loss in some instances without treating that possibility as fatal on this record. (265–267.)

The quantity provision allowed the applicant to choose the number requested up to one hundred. Banks could choose to supply more, but could not use the maximum language to determine whatever smaller quantity he preferred. The court therefore rejected the claim that the applications did not trigger a duty to supply. (267.)

## Interest, exact result, and assigned account
Because the stipulated amounts became liquidated and due at breach, they bore interest from that time as overdue debts. The judgment was affirmed. The opinion identifies the six $100 claims and the interest entitlement but does not print a total judgment with calculated interest; no such aggregate has been invented here. (267.)
