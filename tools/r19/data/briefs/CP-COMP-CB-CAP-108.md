# Lassiter v. Department of Social Services

**452 U.S. 18 (1981) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 1, 1981.** Civil Procedure. The assigned account is CivPro physical PDF page 172, printed page 297, Note 5’s Lassiter paragraph before the Turner discussion. Justice Stewart wrote for Chief Justice Burger and Justices White, Powell, and Rehnquist. Burger also concurred. Justice Blackmun dissented, joined by Justices Brennan and Marshall; Justice Stevens separately dissented. The judgment was 5–4.

## Facts and procedural history

Abby Gail Lassiter’s infant son, William, entered the custody of the Durham County Department of Social Services after a 1975 adjudication of neglect. The court found that she had failed to provide proper medical care. A social worker later described missed medical follow-up, malnutrition, breathing difficulties, and untreated infection. Lassiter did not attend that original custody hearing. Her four other children were living with her mother. In 1976 Lassiter was convicted of second-degree murder and sentenced to 25 to 40 years in prison. The majority’s first footnote recounts the underlying violence and her unsuccessful collateral challenge, including her contention that her mother actually committed the killing. Those details were disputed as relevant to the termination proceeding by Blackmun’s dissent; incarceration alone was not a statutory ground for termination under the North Carolina provisions he examined. 452 U.S. at 20–22 & n.1, 52–58 & n.26.

In 1978 the Department petitioned to terminate Lassiter’s parental rights. It alleged that she had failed without cause to maintain concern or responsibility for William and had willfully left him in foster care for more than two years without substantial progress or a positive response to the Department’s efforts. She received the petition and hearing notice. Although she had spoken with an attorney about her criminal conviction, she did not ask him to handle the termination case. The trial judge declined a continuance, finding ample opportunity to obtain counsel and a failure to do so without just cause. No lawyer was appointed. 452 U.S. at 21–22, 44–45.

At the August 1978 hearing, the Department’s social worker testified about William’s medical condition, the limited visits, and the grandmother’s asserted inability to care for another child. Lassiter tried to respond by making statements rather than asking questions, and the judge repeatedly redirected her. Lassiter and her mother disputed the worker’s account and advocated placing William with the grandmother. The court nevertheless found willful failure to demonstrate concern or make constructive plans and determined that termination served William’s interests. The putative father’s rights were also terminated, but this case concerned the mother’s right to counsel. The North Carolina Court of Appeals rejected her federal constitutional claim, the state supreme court denied review, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari. 452 U.S. at 22–24 & n.2.

## Issue and governing framework

Did the Fourteenth Amendment require appointed counsel for an indigent parent in every state-initiated parental-rights termination proceeding, or at least in Lassiter’s hearing?

The Court rejected an automatic rule. It drew from appointed-counsel precedents a presumption that an indigent litigant has a right to appointed counsel only when an adverse outcome may deprive the litigant of physical liberty. That is a starting presumption, not an absolute exclusion of counsel whenever imprisonment is absent. The Court then balanced the private interest, the government’s interests, and the risk of erroneous deprivation under Mathews v. Eldridge, weighing their combined result against that presumption. A sufficiently strong combination can overcome it. 452 U.S. at 24–27, 31.

## Majority’s reasoning and application

The parental interest was exceptionally important. Termination permanently ends a deeply personal relationship rather than merely reallocating money or temporarily changing custody. The State also had a genuine interest in an accurate decision about the child’s welfare; accuracy could favor representation rather than oppose it. Although public expense remained a legitimate consideration, the Court regarded that fiscal interest as comparatively weak, and the Department acknowledged that counsel’s cost would be small compared with ordinary criminal prosecutions. 452 U.S. at 27–28.

The risk of error could be substantial. Termination proceedings may involve difficult law, medical or psychiatric evidence, and allegations that also expose a parent to criminal prosecution. An uneducated or emotionally overwhelmed parent may be unable to present a defense effectively. The Court examined North Carolina’s procedures, including its prescribed factfinding burden and opportunity for appeal. Crucially, some described protections were enacted after Lassiter’s own hearing: she had not received the benefit of the later clear, cogent, and convincing evidentiary standard, nor counsel at her original custody hearing. The opinion does not treat those later protections as facts about her trial. 452 U.S. at 27–30 & nn.3–6, especially n.4.

Despite these concerns, the majority thought the balance would vary among cases. It adopted a trial-level determination subject to appellate review instead of requiring counsel in every termination proceeding. Where the parent’s interests and danger of error were greatest and the State’s contrary interests weakest, counsel might be constitutionally required. The general importance of parenthood did not itself settle every case. 452 U.S. at 31–32.

For Lassiter, the majority emphasized that the termination petition contained no allegations exposing her to criminal liability, no experts testified, and the case posed no particularly troublesome legal issues. It acknowledged that counsel could have challenged hearsay and developed the defense that the Department had failed to assist the family. Nevertheless, it regarded the evidence of Lassiter’s lack of involvement as sufficiently strong that a lawyer would not have changed the result. It also considered the grandmother-placement argument adequately explored, Lassiter’s refusal to attend the earlier custody hearing, and her failure to seek help from the attorney she knew. Those combined considerations supported its conclusion that this particular hearing did not violate due process. The absence of criminal exposure was one factor, not a complete standalone rule. 452 U.S. at 32–33 & nn.7–8.

The Court separately recognized that legislatures could provide broader protection than the constitutional minimum. It described state practices appointing counsel in termination cases approvingly without converting that policy judgment into an automatic federal constitutional entitlement. 452 U.S. at 33–34.

## Separate opinions

Burger stressed the nonpunitive aim of protecting the child. He viewed the record as strongly supporting termination and joined the Court’s case-specific approach. His concurrence did not establish that every incarcerated parent loses parental rights or that all termination cases dispense with counsel. 452 U.S. at 34–35.

Blackmun, Brennan, and Marshall would require counsel for indigent parents in this category of state-initiated, formal, adversarial termination proceedings. Blackmun rejected a rigid priority for physical confinement: loss of family association could be more grievous than imprisonment. The State was using lawyers and legal procedures to seek an irrevocable deprivation, while the parent had to contest imprecise standards of willfulness, adequate parenting, and constructive planning. The government’s interest in accuracy and relatively modest expense strengthened, rather than defeated, the case for counsel. His proposed rule did not necessarily extend to privately initiated proceedings or ordinary divorce custody disputes. 452 U.S. at 35–49 & nn.9–17.

Blackmun also disputed the majority’s method of measuring counsel’s value after an uncounseled trial. The record cannot readily reveal defenses that a lawyer would have investigated or evidence never collected. In this case, the worker’s testimony included agency records and community opinions not properly tested; Lassiter struggled with cross-examination; and her incarceration explained much of her inability to visit. Counsel could have investigated the grandmother’s care of the other children and whether the Department had met its own obligations. He did not claim that representation necessarily would produce a different custody outcome; he maintained that it was necessary for a meaningful opportunity to contest termination. 452 U.S. at 49–59 & nn.18–26.

Stevens agreed that counsel was required but would rest more directly on fundamental fairness. Permanent deprivation of parental association concerns liberty as well as property and deserves the protection afforded criminal defendants facing loss of liberty. He would not make that protection depend on a calculation of public expense. 452 U.S. at 59–60 & n.*.

## Judgment and assigned significance

The Court **affirmed** the North Carolina Court of Appeals. 452 U.S. at 34. Its constitutional rule permits a case-specific entitlement to counsel in parental-rights termination proceedings; it did not adopt either a universal entitlement or a categorical prohibition. The textbook’s brief account correctly identifies the physical-liberty presumption and the absence of criminal allegations, but the complete opinion supplies the balancing framework, the additional case-specific reasons, and the substantial dissent. This brief describes the historical decision; it does not certify later law.
