International Shoe Co. v. Washington
Citation
International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 66 S. Ct. 154, 90 L. Ed. 95 (1945). Supreme Court of
the United States, argued November 14, 1945, decided December 3, 1945. Chief Justice Stone delivered the
Court’s opinion. Justice Black filed a separate opinion supporting the state’s authority but criticizing the
Court’s test; Justice Jackson took no part. Casebook pages 95-100. Full judicial opinion.
Parties
International Shoe was the appellant challenging Washington’s effort to collect unpaid contributions to its
unemployment-compensation fund. It was incorporated in Delaware and had its principal place of business
in St. Louis, Missouri. The state authorities administering the fund, including the commissioner who
assessed the contributions, were the appellees defending the assessment. 326 U.S. at 311-13.
Material Facts
Washington financed unemployment compensation through employer contributions calculated as a
percentage of wages paid for employees’ services within the state. A commissioner could assess unpaid
contributions, serve notice personally on an employer found within Washington or send it by registered mail
to an employer not found there, and collect an unpaid assessment. The disputed assessments concerned 1937
through 1940. 326 U.S. at 311-13.
International Shoe manufactured and distributed footwear outside Washington. It had no office there, kept
no stock of merchandise there, made no local contracts for buying or selling merchandise, and made no
deliveries in intrastate commerce. It nevertheless employed eleven to thirteen Washington-resident salesmen
whose principal activities occurred there and whose work was supervised from St. Louis. Their annual
commissions exceeded $31,000. That number describes compensation, not the company’s Washington
revenue. Id. at 313-14.
The salesmen displayed single-shoe samples and solicited orders at prices and on terms fixed by the
company. They sometimes rented permanent or temporary display rooms, with the company reimbursing the
rent. Orders went to St. Louis for acceptance or rejection; accepted orders were filled by shipments from
outside Washington. The salesmen could neither enter contracts nor collect payments. These activities
produced a substantial, continuing flow of merchandise into Washington. Id. at 314-15.
Notice of the assessment was personally delivered to one Washington sales solicitor, and a copy was mailed
by registered mail to the company in St. Louis. The controversy thus involved both Washington’s authority
to bind the company and the adequacy of the method used to notify it. Id. at 312.
Procedural History
The company appeared specially before the unemployment office, limiting its appearance to contesting
authority. It argued that the salesman was not a proper recipient of service, that the company was not doing
business or present in Washington, that it had no local agent for service, and that it was not an employer
furnishing employment within the statute. An appeal tribunal heard evidence and stipulated facts, rejected
the motion, and ruled that the commissioner could recover. The commissioner, Superior Court, and
Washington Supreme Court affirmed. 326 U.S. at 312-13.

The Washington Supreme Court regarded regular solicitation producing continuous shipments as sufficient
local business activity. It also relied on additional features: sample displays, rented rooms, and the
salesmen’s continuing residence. International Shoe appealed to the United States Supreme Court,
maintaining both due-process and interstate-commerce objections. The lower court’s solicitation-plus
analysis was its rationale, not the full constitutional test the Supreme Court then articulated. Id. at 313-16.
Issue
Did International Shoe’s Washington activities permit the state, consistently with due process, to require it to
defend proceedings collecting employment-based unemployment contributions? Were service on a local
salesman and registered mailing constitutionally adequate, and could Washington impose the contributions
themselves?
Rule and Holding
An absent defendant may be subjected to a personal judgment when it has minimum contacts with the forum
such that maintaining the particular suit does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial
justice.” Minimum contacts are the defendant’s connections with the state, assessed for their nature, quality,
and relationship to the litigation rather than through a mechanical count. International Shoe’s systematic,
continuous Washington operations and the direct connection between those operations and the assessment
satisfied that standard. 326 U.S. at 316-20.
The Court also sustained notice through the local salesman and registered mail to the home office, and
upheld Washington’s authority to impose the employment-based tax and sue to collect it. Those conclusions
concern this assessment arising from the company’s local sales activities; they do not decide that any
continuous activity supports every possible claim against a corporation. Id. at 320-22.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the Washington Supreme Court’s judgment. The company remained subject to
the assessed contributions and the proceeding to collect them. 326 U.S. at 322.
Reasoning
The Court replaced a conclusory inquiry into corporate presence with examination of what the corporation’s
authorized representatives actually did. A corporation has no physical body, so saying that it is present
merely restates the question whether its activities justify the state’s authority. The relevant inquiry is
whether its connections make it reasonable, within the federal system, to require it to defend this particular
suit. The burden of litigation away from its principal place of business is relevant. 326 U.S. at 316-17.
The Court described several patterns from earlier decisions. Continuous and systematic local activity giving
rise to the liability ordinarily supported suit. Casual or isolated activity ordinarily did not support unrelated
claims. Some continuous operations were insufficient for unrelated litigation, while especially substantial
operations had supported claims arising elsewhere. A single act could sometimes support related litigation
because of its character and circumstances. These examples reject both a simple volume threshold and a rule
that every isolated contact is inadequate. Id. at 317-18.
The controlling assessment concerns the quality and nature of the activity in relation to fair administration of
law. A state could not bind a defendant with which it had no ties or relations. Conversely, a corporation
conducting local activities obtained the benefits and protection of local law, and obligations arising from
those activities ordinarily could be enforced there without unfairness. This reasoning linked the company’s

Applied here, the sales operation was neither irregular nor casual. It continued over the assessed years,
generated substantial interstate business, and received Washington’s legal protection, including access to its
courts. The employment-based obligation arose from those same activities. Requiring defense in
Washington was therefore reasonable and just under the Court’s standard, notwithstanding out-of-state
acceptance of orders and the absence of an ordinary corporate office. Id. at 320.
Notice required its own analysis. The salesman whose activities helped establish the relevant contacts was
an appropriate channel for communicating a proceeding related to those activities. The chosen substitute for
direct corporate service gave reasonable assurance of actual notice. The full report, in a sentence omitted
from the excerpt, also expressly sustains registered mailing to the home office as reasonably calculated to
inform the company. Adequate notice supported this exercise of jurisdiction; notice alone was not the
substitute for contacts. Id. at 320-21.
The state’s taxing authority rested on the same local activity. The Court accepted the Washington Supreme
Court’s construction of the contribution as a tax on employing salesmen in the state, measured by their
commissions. Employment there supplied both the taxable event and an activity supporting suit to collect the
resulting obligation. The Court did not treat state authority over taxation and adjudication as abstract power
over every aspect of the company’s business. Id. at 321-22.
The full opinion also resolves the interstate-commerce objection in a passage the casebook leaves out.
Congress had expressly provided that engaging in interstate or foreign commerce would not excuse
compliance with state unemployment-fund requirements. That federal authorization disposed of the asserted
commerce objection; it was separate from the minimum-contacts analysis under due process. Id. at 315;
casebook pp. 96-97.
Separate Opinions
Justice Black agreed that Washington could tax and proceed against this business but objected to the
majority’s flexible constitutional criteria. In the passage printed in the casebook, he argued that states could
protect their citizens through suits against corporations conducting business through local agents and
criticized conditioning that power on judicial assessments of fairness or corporate convenience. Those
objections were Black’s position, not qualifications adopted by the Court. 326 U.S. at 324-25 (Black, J.,
separate opinion); casebook p. 100.
The omitted portions of Black’s opinion explain his preferred procedural disposition: he would dismiss the
appeal as unsubstantial rather than announce unnecessary broad constitutional rules. He also regarded the
actual notice as plainly sufficient and warned that open-ended standards could give judges excessive power
over legislation. Justice Jackson took no part in consideration or decision. Id. at 322-26.
