# Grigerik v. Sharpe
247 Conn. 293, 721 A.2d 526 (Conn. December 22, 1998) | Contracts

## Facts and professional engagement
Grigerik negotiated to buy Lang's undeveloped Killingworth land in 1985. He offered $9,000 as it stood, or $16,000 if Lang obtained approval as a building lot. Because the property lay in a reservoir watershed, the town sanitarian required an engineer's drainage plan. Lang retained Sharpe and his engineering firm for soil testing, a site plan, and design of a compliant subsurface sewage-disposal system. On facts the jury could reasonably find, Lang told Sharpe he needed building-lot approval and had a buyer contingent on approval; Sharpe disputed having been told of the intended sale. The work was completed on October 16, 1985. The wetlands commission approved the submitted site plan, and Lang sold to Grigerik for $16,000 on November 19. Approval of that plan did not establish that all later building and septic permits would issue. (299–300 & n. 8.)

When Grigerik sought construction permits in 1986, a new sanitarian questioned the soil conditions and testing. Further tests in March 1987 indicated a need for a curtain drain. Grigerik installed it, but the health department found the land unsuitable for a septic system in May. Additional 1989 testing again showed that minimum standards could not be met. Grigerik served Sharpe in September 1989 and later added the firm, served in June 1991. (300–301.)

## Proceedings and competing positions
The remaining claims alleged negligent professional services and breach of the engineering contract as a third-party beneficiary. A jury awarded $44,024, including interest, on both theories. Its interrogatory answers found Grigerik neither an intended nor a contemplated beneficiary, but a foreseeable one. The trial court had permitted contract recovery on any of those alternatives. (296–301 & nn. 4–5, 15.)

The Appellate Court held negligence barred by the general two-year limitation and ordered judgment for defendants on that count. It found the contract instruction flawed both by allowing foreseeable-beneficiary recovery and by requiring both contracting parties' intent. It read Stowe v. Smith as making the promisee's intent sufficient and ordered a contract retrial. Both sides obtained certified review: Grigerik contested limitations, while the defendants contested the promisee-only standard and need for a retrial. (297–299 & nn. 6–7.)

## Beneficiary holding and contractual intention
The Supreme Court reaffirmed that both contracting parties must intend the promisor to assume a direct obligation to the third party. That intention is determined from the contract's terms in light of the circumstances, motives, and purposes attending its making. An express clause creating the obligation is not indispensable. But intent simply to confer a benefit, or foreseeability that someone will benefit, is different from intent to create an enforceable obligation. Both parties' intention limits the persons who may enforce the bargain and protects certainty about voluntarily assumed duties. (310–313.)

Stowe did not change that rule. Its complaint expressly alleged that the testatrix and drafting attorney both intended a direct obligation to her beneficiary. The issue there was whether the attorney's performance had to be delivered directly to the beneficiary, and the court rejected that proposed restriction. Stowe's footnote discussing Corbin and Williston did not adopt a promisee-only test. The Grigerik court also disagreed with Corbin's criticism that intent was too elusive: intent already served in formation and interpretation, and was no less usable here. (313–316 & n. 16.)

Restatement (Second) § 302 likewise referred both to effectuating the intention of the parties and to the promisee's intent to give a benefit. The court did not read the latter reference as eliminating the former requirement. Because the jury had conclusively found that Grigerik was not an intended beneficiary, it had already resolved the necessary factual issue against him. A foreseeable benefit was a tort concept and could not substitute for voluntarily created contractual rights. Judgment for defendants, rather than another trial on the contract count, was required. The court had no need to classify Grigerik as a creditor or donee beneficiary. (316–318 & nn. 15, 17.)

## Negligence limitations ruling
The court separately held that § 52-584a's seven-year period for relevant actions against architects and professional engineers governed. The 1986 legislature had removed the language on which an earlier decision relied to treat that period only as a repose ceiling while preserving shorter limitations. Text and legislative history showed an intention to supply a seven-year limitation for the covered claims, rather than superimpose the general negligence period. (301–305 & nn. 1–2, 9–11.)

The defendants contended there had been no completed improvement because no building or septic system resulted. The court rejected both that excessively narrow approach and the plaintiff's broad suggestion that every professional engineering service automatically qualified. An improvement ordinarily entails a physical addition or alteration enhancing use of real property. Where the contemplated improvement cannot be completed precisely because of the defective professional design, § 52-584a still applies; otherwise discovering the defect early and stopping construction would irrationally shorten the claimant's time and encourage wasteful building. (305–308 & nn. 12–13.)

The court left open precisely when that period begins if the improvement is never completed. This action commenced less than seven years after the allegedly defective work was finished, so it was timely under any relevant reading. Its note mentioning possible laches in a much-delayed hypothetical did not decide that hypothetical. (308–309 & n. 14.)

## Exact judgment and vote
The Supreme Court reversed the Appellate Court on both counts, with different directions. On negligence, the Appellate Court had to consider defendants' previously unaddressed claim that permitting an amendment after jury selection was an abuse of discretion; if not, it was to affirm the trial judgment on negligence. On contract, it had to remand to the trial court for judgment for defendants. The Supreme Court therefore did not simply restore the whole jury judgment or finally eliminate all further negligence issues. Borden wrote, and Callahan C.J., Berdon, Norcott, and Katz concurred: five-to-zero, with no separate writing. (318.)
