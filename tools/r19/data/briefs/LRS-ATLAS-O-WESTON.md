# Weston v. Cassata
37 P.3d 469 (Colo. App. 2001) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and procedural setting

On July 5, 2001, the Colorado Court of Appeals affirmed a judgment for a class of Adams County welfare recipients challenging deficient sanction notices. Judge Davidson wrote the opinion; Judges Metzger and Roy concurred, with no separate writings. The court held that qualified participants already receiving available welfare funds had a protected interest in continued benefits despite federal and state “no entitlement” provisions. It also sustained the conclusion that the county’s notices denied due process and rejected the county’s exhaustion defense to the state-law claims. The case concerns conditional benefits and meaningful notice, not an unconditional promise that government must fund every eligible person forever. (469; 472–479.)

Frances Weston, Christine Kneubehl, and Shauna Disher represented persons who had received sanctions under the Colorado Works Program. They sued the county department director and social-services board under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, Colorado’s Administrative Procedure Act, and its declaratory-judgments law. They sought declaratory and injunctive relief, reversal of sanctions, restoration of withheld benefits, and attorney fees under § 1988. Denver defendants settled before trial, and the state defendants were not parties to this appeal. (472–473.)

After a bench trial, the trial court found that none of the five versions of notices used between July 1997 and November 1999 complied with statutory or regulatory requirements. It declared the notices inadequate, enjoined further use, ordered reversal and removal of records of prior sanctions based on them, and directed equitable restoration of cash assistance improperly withheld. That is the judgment affirmed; the appellate decision does not quantify an individualized award of damages or an attorney-fee award. Statutory construction was reviewed de novo. (473, 479.)

## Welfare reform and the county’s role

The federal Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 replaced AFDC with TANF. The opinion describes the new program’s sixty-month limit for eligible adult care providers and its federal block-grant funding conditioned on state implementation of the new system. Colorado enacted its Works Program in 1997. Participants entered individual responsibility contracts and could lose part of their cash assistance for noncompliance. Before sanctioning a participant, however, the county had to send notice describing the basis for the action, the available county conference and state-level appeal, and the way to pursue review. (472.)

The state department developed standardized forms and maintained a database of codes describing sanctionable activities. Counties administered the program, completed those forms, and gave notices of adverse actions. Adams County therefore could not avoid responsibility by pointing to the state’s oversight and form-development authority. The statute expressly assigned counties increased administrative responsibility and specific duties affecting benefit distribution. (473–474.)

The county argued it was not a “person” subject to § 1983. The court distinguished the rule excluding the state and certain official-capacity defendants from monetary-damages actions from official-capacity proceedings for prospective declaratory and injunctive relief. Because these defendants implemented and enforced the statutory program, the court held them proper defendants for the relief at issue. It did not announce that every arm of the state is generally liable for damages under § 1983. (473–474.)

## The property-interest dispute

Both federal and Colorado constitutions protect property against deprivation without due process. The court first asked whether the substantive statutory scheme created a protected interest and then whether the notice process adequately protected it. Following Roth, it required a legitimate claim of entitlement grounded in an independent legal source, rather than need or unilateral expectation alone. (474–475.)

The county relied on federal language declaring that the TANF provisions did not entitle any individual or family to assistance under a funded state program and Colorado language stating that the Works Program created no legal entitlement to assistance. The court accepted that welfare reform modified the former program’s entitlement framework. It rejected the further inference that the disclaimers eliminated every form of protected interest. The rest of Colorado’s scheme contained mandatory directions: subject to available appropriations, a participant shall receive a basic grant; participants shall continue receiving cash assistance during an appeal; and applicants were entitled to written notice of actions affecting benefits. Those provisions had to be read with, rather than discarded because of, the disclaimer. (474–475.)

## Conditional funding versus discretion over eligible recipients

The opinion distinguished several uses of “entitlement”: an enforceable absolute benefit claim, the political and fiscal obligation to fund an entitlement program, and a protected interest arising from standards that constrain discretion within an operating regulatory program. A property interest could be conditional and still receive constitutional procedural protection. The important issue was the discretion remaining after the relevant conditions were fulfilled. (475–476.)

Government retained discretion over funding and could withhold funds from a state failing federal conditions. Before funds were available, the individual’s expected benefit had not matured into a personal entitlement. But when the state received money for the program, and recipients fulfilled the governing eligibility requirements and had begun receiving benefits, the county’s distribution role did not include discretion to deny those recipients the money for reasons outside the statutory standards. State and county officials conceded the obligation to pay eligible recipients when funds were available. (476–477.)

The court distinguished Barry, where officials retained discretion to choose which eligible families would receive scarce shelter. It also adopted Professor Farina’s explanation that due process did not guarantee obtaining the benefit in all circumstances; it guaranteed a decision process reasonably likely to apply the governing substantive criteria correctly once the benefit was granted. Under Loudermill, the legislature could not confer such an interest and then eliminate appropriate procedural safeguards for taking it away. The court accordingly held that these recipients had a conditional property interest in continued benefits. The holding is not that a “no entitlement” clause can never matter, or that appropriations limits disappear. (476–477.)

## Why the notices failed

Adams County argued that Goldberg required only timely and adequate notice of the reason for termination and that its forms sufficed. The court applied the regulatory minimum requirements: written and understandable notice of the action and reason; specific authority for it; its effective date; review rights, deadlines, and steps; continued-benefit and repayment consequences; representation rights; and, where practicable, the recipient’s language, with verbal explanation for an illiterate recipient. Substantial failure to satisfy the governing notice standards could not support a deprivation of benefits. (477–478.)

The trial court identified defects separately for each of five phases. All failed to state the sanction amount and a sufficiently specific reason. Early notices also failed to explain effects on later benefit levels, cite the governing rule, and state the correct review period: twenty-six days appeared where ninety were permitted. Subsequent versions removed some mistakes, including the appeal-period error and eventually a missing rollover date, but retained central deficiencies. The appellate court found these defects supported by the record and held that the notices denied due process. It did not treat any minor typographical mistake, without regard to its effect, as automatically unconstitutional. (478.)

## Exhaustion and the scope of relief

The county also argued that failure to exhaust administrative review deprived the trial court of jurisdiction over the state-law claims. The court recognized futility and practices avoiding judicial review as exceptions. The evidence showed that the county routinely restored benefits when sanctioned recipients appealed, while continuing to use the defective notices and refusing to revise them even after administrative findings of inadequacy. Restoration resolved individual disputes without producing review of the recurring notice system. (478–479.)

The administrative framework was designed to decide individual benefit appeals and could not provide the classwide correction sought here. The lawsuit addressed the systemic inadequacy of notices, beyond the scope of individual administrative relief. On this record, exhaustion was therefore unnecessary. This does not excuse exhaustion in every benefits dispute merely because the claimant hopes for broader relief. (479.)
