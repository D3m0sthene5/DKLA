# Phillpotts v. Evans
5 M. & W. 475, 151 Eng. Rep. 200 (Exch. of Pleas 1839) | Contracts

## Sale, refusal, and the damages verdict

In early January 1839, Gloucester corn merchants contracted to sell wheat to Evans, a Birmingham miller. Delivery was to be made at Birmingham as soon as vessels could be obtained to carry it. After the market began to fall, Evans notified the sellers on January 26 that he would not accept the wheat if delivered. It was already traveling by canal to Birmingham. When it arrived, the sellers tendered it and Evans again refused acceptance; they then brought assumpsit for the refusal. The report does not specify the quantity, contract price, or arrival date. Those omissions should not be filled with assumptions about the transaction. (151 Eng. Rep. at 200–201; 5 M. & W. at 475–476.)

At the Gloucester Assizes before Alderson, the disputed question was when to value the damages, rather than whether Evans was liable for the ultimate refusal. Evans argued for the difference between contract price and the market on January 26. The sellers relied on Leigh v. Paterson and argued for the market at the last time the contract could have been performed, which the report identifies as the tender for acceptance. Alderson directed the latter measure, and the jury returned £248 9s. The defendant reserved leave to move to reduce the award to £93 12s., the earlier-date calculation. The subsequent proceeding was that motion; it was not a new damages trial or an appeal reversing the verdict. (151 Eng. Rep. at 201.)

## The buyer’s argument for an earlier valuation

R. V. Richards argued for Evans that the January notice conclusively refused performance, discharged the sellers’ obligation to deliver, and entitled them to sue immediately. On that premise, the sellers could have sold the wheat by sample while it was still in transit. Continuing to send it to Birmingham despite knowing that Evans would refuse it should not, he argued, entitle them to benefit from an additional fall in the market. Their recoverable loss should have been fixed when they first could have disposed of the goods after receiving his notice. This was the defendant’s account of the notice’s legal effect, not the court’s finding that the contract had already ended. (Id.)

Alderson asked how this could be distinguished from Leigh. His quotation from that decision emphasized that a mutually made contract remained open unless the plaintiff assented, expressly or inferentially, to putting it at an end. Richards answered that Leigh required reconsideration and that the sellers should not have an option to increase the damages by continuing after notice. The exchange exposed the disagreement underlying the choice of valuation date: whether the buyer’s declaration alone released the contract before delivery became due. (Id.)

## Reasons for retaining the delivery-date measure

Lord Abinger, Chief Baron, found no ground to reduce the damages. The relevant time was when the buyer ought to have received the wheat. The original contract had not been modified by his notice, and the sellers were not bound to sell on receiving it to reduce damages. Abinger considered Leigh directly applicable and adopted its reasoning. (Id. at 201.)

Parke gave the fuller explanation. If the sellers had been entitled to bring their action immediately after notice, the notice-date measure might have followed. But he held that no such action lay before the delivery time. Evans’s undertaking was to be ready and willing to receive and pay for the goods when the contract required it. A previous declaration that he would refuse did not itself break that undertaking under the rule Parke applied; Evans could still accept the wheat when due and commit no breach. The sellers therefore had to wait to see whether he would receive it. (Id. at 202; 5 M. & W. at 477.)

Parke expressly qualified the proposition. The notice was ineffective before the receipt date unless the seller acted on it in the meantime and rescinded the contract. Thus the reasoning does not make an earlier declaration incapable of participating in an actual termination by the other party’s intervening conduct. Here, however, the sellers had not ended the contract on that basis, and the appropriate damages date remained the time for acceptance. Alderson again said that Leigh governed; Gurney concurred. The four reported judges agreed in refusing the rule. (151 Eng. Rep. at 202.)

## Result and historical significance

The Exchequer refused the requested rule, leaving the £248 9s. verdict unreduced. It did not impose a notice-date mitigation obligation on these sellers. The assigned textbook account uses the case to illustrate waiting for performance despite a refusal and compares later authorities, including Roehm v. Horst. The complete 1839 report also reveals a historically important qualification: Parke denied that a premature refusal itself supplied an immediate action before performance was due. That reasoning preceded the anticipatory-breach doctrine associated with Hochster; it should not be restated as a modern, universal prohibition on an action for anticipatory repudiation. (Id. at 201–202; assigned textbook printed page 908.)

The report’s bracketed later references to Ripley, Cort, Hochster, and Frost are editorial subsequent-history material, rather than authorities the judges considered in 1839. The complete judicial treatment comprises Abinger’s and Parke’s explanations, Alderson’s response to counsel and adoption of Leigh, Gurney’s concurrence, and the terminal refusal of the rule. (151 Eng. Rep. at 201–202.)
