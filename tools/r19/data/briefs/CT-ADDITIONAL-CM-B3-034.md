# North Pacific Lumber Co. v. Oliver
286 Or. 639, 596 P.2d 931 (1979) | Contracts

## Court, result, and procedural limits
The Oregon Supreme Court decided this appeal June 19, 1979, after argument January 5. Justice Holman wrote for the court, with Chief Justice Denecke and Justices Howell and Lent also participating; the report supplies no dissent or concurrence. The court affirmed dismissal of the employer’s complaint, modified the decree to delete the employee’s $105,000 attorney-fee award, and allowed neither party costs. Rehearing was denied September 5. The noncompetition period had already expired, making the request for an injunction moot, but the employer’s damages and fee claims and the employee’s fee award remained live. The court therefore reviewed the underlying equitable controversy de novo, treating the trial findings as persuasive rather than binding. (286 Or. at 639, 640-a, 643–644, 667.)

## Employment and litigation
North Pacific wholesaled lumber, principally through telephone trading from Portland. It hired Oliver in February 1967 as a hardwood trader and promoted him to assistant manager in 1969. His contract prohibited defined competition after departure for the shorter of his employment period or two years. The detailed restrictions covered specified wholesaling activity within one hundred miles of Portland and dealings with defined customers and suppliers, limited by department and transaction volumes. The company could seek an injunction and damages, and the contract gave it a right to attorney fees in an enforcement action. Footnotes 1–4 reproduce those provisions. (Id. at 641–642 & nn. 1–4.)

Oliver left voluntarily in April 1976 and soon worked for competing Tree Products. North Pacific sued both in May, but never served Tree Products, which did not become an actual party. The company sought to stop prohibited employment, use of confidential information, and solicitation of customers and suppliers, and demanded damages and fees. Oliver stopped actively soliciting sales when he learned of the suit. He challenged the covenant as unreasonable, asserted unclean hands, and counterclaimed for attorney fees. (Id. at 642–643.)

After a lengthy trial, the court found the contract valid but refused enforcement because of North Pacific’s misconduct and dismissed the complaint with prejudice. It rejected Oliver’s claim that the contract was oppressive and denied quantum-meruit wages. Applying the reciprocal-fee statute, ORS 20.096, it nevertheless awarded Oliver $105,000 in fees. North Pacific appealed dismissal and that award; Oliver cross-appealed the amount. The statute’s operative language is reproduced in footnote 5. (Id. at 643 & n. 5.)

## Claimed misconduct and the governing relationship
Oliver advanced numerous accusations: deceptive names and product descriptions, improper settlements and charges, secret monitoring, unauthorized hauling, underpayment, and oppressive employee practices. Those allegations were not all accepted as reasons to deny enforcement. The trial findings did not establish general underpayment, and Oliver received a salary and percentage of departmental profit; footnote 6 states that he earned as much as $80,000 annually. Other practices were characterized as minor deceptions or activities that had largely ceased. The principal issues became telephone monitoring and profits extracted from settlement of customer claims. (Id. at 644–649 & n. 6; 660.)

The court distinguished a covenant’s validity from its enforceability in equity. A prior decision had sustained a similar covenant, and Oliver did not press the validity challenge on appeal. Clean hands can nonetheless deny relief on a valid contract. It protects the integrity of the court, rather than punishing the plaintiff or rewarding a deserving defendant. Misconduct need not itself invalidate the agreement or violate a statute, but it must be serious enough and sufficiently related to the transaction. General bad character or unrelated wrongdoing does not suffice. (Id. at 649–654.)

North Pacific urged an especially narrow relationship: the covenant itself must have arisen from the wrongdoing. The court declined to confine the relevant transaction to contract formation. The employment relationship’s later performance could create the necessary connection. Decisions involving fraudulent business operation, illegal use of property, and employer breaches illustrated that the relationship may be present at the outset or develop later. An employer’s misconduct toward outsiders could also matter if it used the employee’s position to involve him in that conduct. (Id. at 654–659.)

The critical question was whether continuation of the employment relationship made the employee’s participation in the wrongful practices necessary. The court rejected an employee’s automatic escape whenever an employer committed an unrelated indiscretion or damaged its general reputation. Conversely, the defense could remain available even when the employee had neither protested nor avoided participation: withholding the court’s assistance to the employer did not depend on the employee’s moral innocence. (Id. at 659–660.)

## Why monitoring did not suffice but settlement profits did
The record showed equipment and practices for listening to other employees’ calls, sometimes as training and sometimes to investigate suspected activity. One monitoring episode sought information about Oliver after he left. But the court found no showing that North Pacific actually monitored Oliver’s own conversations or required him to monitor others. Possible violations of the telephone statute did not establish the necessary connection to his employment responsibilities. The court did not authorize criminal violations or prevent public enforcement; it held that these circumstances did not justify withholding equitable relief under clean hands. (Id. at 660–661.)

The settlement-profit practice had a different connection. Traders handling complaints about defective lumber would minimize the deficiency to induce a buyer to accept a small settlement, then exaggerate it to the supplier and obtain a larger payment. North Pacific retained the difference. The company acknowledged improper profits and encouragement by lower management, but claimed the frequency and total were small, senior management had been unaware, and refunds and corrective measures followed disclosure. The court was unpersuaded by the company’s nonindependent audit. It found that the practice occurred and was knowingly encouraged in departmental meetings and the instruction manual. It did not adopt every witness’s numerical estimate as an established fact. (Id. at 661–662.)

Oliver supervised those departmental activities and benefited through profit-based compensation. He could not remain assistant manager while avoiding responsibility for carrying out and overseeing that policy. The practice therefore sufficiently affected the parties’ equitable relations. Enforcing the employment bargain would require the court to settle accounts arising from a relationship involving the employee in wrongdoing against customers and suppliers. That justified affirming refusal of North Pacific’s claims. (Id. at 662–663.)

## Why the reciprocal-fee statute did not reward Oliver
A successful defense did not entitle Oliver to affirmative relief free of the same equitable objection. The court found that he had knowingly participated in and profited from the improper settlements. He left because of dissatisfaction with pay and personnel, rather than because he repudiated those practices. His misconduct did not remove the employer’s unclean-hands problem, but it barred his own request for affirmative contractual relief. (Id. at 663–665.)

ORS 20.096 made one-sided contractual fee rights reciprocal. The court acknowledged that equity was bound by the statute, then construed its purpose: equalizing the parties’ contractual rights did not give a disfavored party greater rights than an express bilateral fee provision would supply. Such a provision still would be subject to clean hands in equity. Earlier fee decisions supported treating the statute as dependent on the contract, rather than an independent superior entitlement. The court consequently deleted the $105,000 award and left the parties where it found them. Footnote 7 rejected an asserted concession below because the employer had preserved its objection to affirmative fee relief in a filed memorandum. (Id. at 664–667 & n. 7.)

## Assignment and source scope
The clean-hands account at C&M printed pages 753–754 correctly contrasts monitoring with the settlement-profit scheme and describes the different results on enforcement and fees. Its shorthand that Oliver went on his own is refined by the actual record: he joined Tree Products. The reference to eavesdropping on clients concerns monitoring employees’ business calls, not a finding that every alleged monitoring practice occurred or directly involved Oliver. The complete actual judicial opinion, all seven notes, and report identification were read, and both assigned pages were read in native text and original scans. Original report scans were checked selectively for caption, panel, contract and statutory notes, the relationship inquiry, the profit findings, fee preservation, and final judgment.
