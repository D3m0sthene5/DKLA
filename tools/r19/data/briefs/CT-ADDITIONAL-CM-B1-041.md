# Geismar v. Abraham & Straus
109 Misc. 2d 495, 439 N.Y.S.2d 1005 (N.Y. Dist. Ct., Suffolk County 1981) | Contracts

## Court, proceeding, and disposition
Judge Francis G. Caldeira decided this small-claims action on June 8, 1981. Judith Geismar appeared without counsel and sought $280 from a department store that refused to sell advertised china at its stated sale price. The court rejected recovery for breach of contract and found no proved actual damages, but entered judgment for Geismar for the $50 statutory minimum under the then-operative false-advertising provisions of New York’s General Business Law. This is the trial court’s actual small-claims decision, not an appellate affirmance. There is no separate opinion or collegial voting division. (495, 499–500.)

## Facts and claim
On February 11, 1981, Abraham & Straus placed a full-page advertisement in Newsday. A bold heading advertised discounts of forty to fifty percent on Mikasa, Sango, and Yamaka china. Four sets appeared with regular and sale prices. One twenty-piece set was reduced from $100 to $59.95; a forty-piece set from $140 to $59.95; a service for eight from $120 to $69.95; and the disputed service for twelve from $280 to $39.95. Geismar tried to purchase the last set for $39.95, but the store refused. (495, 497.)

She sued for the entire stated regular price of $280. She did not prove that she had bought substitute dishes elsewhere, did not seek a demonstrated difference between an actual purchase price and the advertised price, and established no actual damages. The opinion does not supply a detailed account of arguments by each side; indeed, the court observed that the statutory questions controlling recovery had not been fully explored or argued in this small-claims proceeding. Its discussion of whether the price was an obvious mistake is the court’s consideration of a possible objection, not a reported admission that the store intentionally deceived anyone. (495–496, 498.)

## Contract formation and the separate statutory question
The advertisement did not create a contract merely because Geismar expressed willingness to buy. Applying the authorities it cited, the court characterized it as an invitation to negotiate rather than an offer that a shopper could accept to bind the seller. That disposed of the breach-of-contract theory. It did not, however, dispose of statutory liability for misleading advertising. A seller’s freedom from contractual liability on these facts did not make its public representations legally irrelevant. (495–496.)

Article 22-A of the General Business Law defined and prohibited false advertising through sections 350 and 350-a. A provision effective June 19, 1980, then contained in subdivision 3 of section 350-d, authorized an injured person to recover actual damages or $50, whichever was greater. The quoted provision also authorized a discretionary enhanced award for a willful or knowing violation, subject to stated limits. The court awarded only the $50 minimum and did not find or award the statutory enhancement. The questions were whether this advertisement was materially misleading and whether Geismar could be an injured person despite failing to prove contract damages. (496.)

## Materially misleading advertising without proved intent to deceive
The court interpreted the statute to require no proof of an intent to deceive for ordinary liability. The definition of false advertising turned on whether an advertisement was misleading in a material respect, not the advertiser’s intent. The separate authorization to increase damages for willful or knowing conduct reinforced the distinction between basic liability and aggravated misconduct. Prior decisions construing advertising restrictions also recognized that material misrepresentations could result from lack of care rather than dishonesty. The court’s sole judicial footnote distinguished the affirmative defense then available under the current Penal Law provision; that criminal-law qualification was not converted into an intent element for this civil claim. (496–498 & sole note at 497.)

The unusually low price did not eliminate misleadingness as an obvious mistake. The court emphasized both the protective purpose identified in its precedents and the actual advertisement’s inconsistencies. Two listed discounts already exceeded the headline’s fifty-percent ceiling. A shopper could therefore reasonably regard the headline percentages as inaccurate rather than assume that the disputed $39.95 price must be erroneous. The store’s refusal to sell at that advertised price was undisputed. On the limited record the court found materially misleading advertising, without finding deliberate baiting or dishonesty. (498–499.)

## Injury and the $50 recovery
The court distinguished statutory injury from proved monetary damages. As it read the newly enacted language, someone misled by a materially misleading advertisement could be an injured person even without establishing an actual financial loss of $50. The provision expressly offered actual damages or a minimum $50 recovery; limiting all recovery to demonstrated monetary damages would frustrate that minimum’s operation. Legislative and gubernatorial explanations identified increased liability and encouragement of private suits as purposes of the enactment. Geismar’s inability to buy at the price that had misled her therefore supported the minimum award. (499.)

The judge acknowledged the breadth of this construction. Corrective advertising would not itself defeat liability except insofar as it showed that a particular person had not been deceived, and the dissemination of erroneous advertisements could create extensive exposure. He suggested legislative reconsideration but applied the provision as he understood it, entering judgment for $50. Those observations are interpretive commentary accompanying this individual judgment, not an actual class-wide award or a declaration that every reader of any mistaken advertisement automatically recovered damages. (499–500.)
