# Commonwealth v. Welosky
276 Mass. 398, 177 N.E. 656 (1931), decided September 9, 1931 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Criminal proceeding and questions presented

A complaint sworn in the Chelsea District Court on July 9, 1930 charged Genevieve Welosky with keeping and exposing intoxicating liquor with intent unlawfully to sell it. On appeal, the complaint was tried in the Superior Court before Judge Hayes, a district judge sitting there under statutory authority. As the jury was about to be empaneled, Welosky challenged the array because the lists from which jurors were drawn contained no women. The Commonwealth joined an issue of law by replication, which the trial judge sustained. Welosky was convicted and took exceptions to that ruling and to evidentiary and instructional decisions. (399–400.)

The Supreme Judicial Court addressed whether state law required otherwise-qualified women to be included on jury lists and whether excluding them violated Welosky’s federal equal-protection rights. It also considered the state constitutional guarantee of trial by the judgment of one’s peers. Earlier justices had answered the statutory and federal questions adversely to women’s eligibility in an advisory opinion to the legislature. Chief Justice Rugg emphasized that such opinions were individual constitutional advice, not adjudications carrying stare decisis effect. The court had to reexamine the questions in actual litigation with the parties’ arguments, guarding against influence from the earlier advice. (400–401.)

## Statutory text and competing readings

General Laws chapter 234, § 1 made a person qualified to vote for representatives to the General Court liable for jury service, subject to exceptions not material here. Welosky offered two routes to inclusion. The general voter-based language should automatically include women once constitutional voting disqualifications disappeared; alternatively, reenactment of that language in December 1920, after the Nineteenth Amendment’s ratification, should be understood to include women who could then vote. (401–402, 409.)

The court acknowledged that an abstract literal reading could include women: an unrestricted reference to persons normally encompassed women. But it treated text as the principal source of legislative purpose, to be read alongside its development, prior legislation, contemporary conditions, constitutional background, and common law. General language could accommodate future conditions within the enactment’s purpose without extending to radically different circumstances outside that purpose. In its view, the voter-based jury statute and its predecessors had always referred to men because the state constitution then confined voting to men. Constitutional limits made that legislative intention unavoidable when the language was chosen. (401–404, 406.)

The Nineteenth Amendment became effective in August 1920 and itself displaced the state constitution’s male-only voting qualification. Massachusetts’s 1924 amendment formally deleting that word did not create women’s voting rights anew; those rights already followed from the federal amendment. The court nonetheless separated voting eligibility from the jury statute’s historically intended class. It contrasted earlier removal of property and tax qualifications, which had enlarged eligibility within the existing class of male voters and thereby jurors, with women’s enfranchisement, which it characterized as adding a new class outside the jury enactment’s original meaning. On that reasoning, neither constitutional amendment automatically expanded the jury statute. (402–403, 406–409.)

Rugg supported the contextual approach with examples in which “person” included corporations or other entities in some statutes but not in others, and with British decisions rejecting attempts to derive women’s voting or parliamentary eligibility from general language adopted against an earlier legal background. Those authorities served the opinion’s claim that the word’s legal operation depended on context rather than carrying a fixed application to every subsequently enfranchised person. The court did not claim that women were literally not human persons. Its holding instead imposed a historically restricted construction on a facially general statutory description. (403–406.)

## Why post-amendment reenactment did not change the result

The December 22, 1920 reenactment presented the harder chronology: women could vote before the General Laws became law. The court applied a presumption that reenactment in substantially unchanged words continued the earlier meaning, then checked the revision process rather than relying on that presumption alone. The consolidating commissioners submitted their proposed body of existing law on June 1, before federal ratification. They were to suggest changes rather than silently incorporate them into the consolidated text. The proposal left jury lists unchanged. (409–410.)

The subsequent special legislative committee expressly revised suffrage provisions to conform to the Nineteenth Amendment, while leaving other provisions alone. Its reported chapters altered election laws but made no jury-list change and retained an express jury-of-men provision elsewhere. The court inferred that legislators had not intended the fundamental change of requiring women’s jury service. It also reasoned that such a change would have required consideration of exemptions and courthouse accommodations. A commission appointed in 1923 investigated women’s jury eligibility, but no favorable enactment followed. These were reasons the court gave for its historical statutory conclusion, not independent requirements for jury eligibility created by the judicial decision. (410–411.)

The court thus held women ineligible under the existing statutes and approved preparation of all-male jury lists. It acknowledged that other states’ decisions on comparable voter-based jury provisions were divided, citing authority on both sides. The result was not presented as an inevitable literal implication of the Nineteenth Amendment nationwide. (411.)

## Constitutional arguments

Welosky relied on Supreme Court decisions, including Strauder and Neal, rejecting racial exclusions from juries under the Reconstruction Amendments. The court recognized the supremacy of federal constitutional law and acknowledged that the Fourteenth Amendment protected persons beyond the formerly enslaved race. It nevertheless distinguished the historical purpose of the Reconstruction Amendments from the Nineteenth Amendment’s explicit protection of voting. It treated the racial-jury cases as establishing a protection that did not compel women’s jury eligibility and relied on Strauder’s statement permitting states to confine jury selection to males. Its discussion also invoked contemporary precedents permitting other sex-based legal restrictions. Those propositions are the court’s 1931 reasoning, not a statement of present constitutional law. (411–415.)

Neal involved a similarly worded Delaware statute that the state court had interpreted to encompass Black citizens after the Fifteenth Amendment. Rugg read the Supreme Court’s approval as accepting that state-law construction, rather than requiring Massachusetts to give its own statute the same treatment for women. He likewise rejected the argument under the 1875 federal statute prohibiting jury disqualification for race, color, or previous servitude. Other federal grounds not argued were waived. The court concluded that Welosky had received equal protection and a jury selected according to valid state law and common-law principles, sufficient under Massachusetts’s guarantee of trial by peers. (415–417.)

## Remaining exceptions and judgment

The full opinion also rejected trial objections omitted from the assigned paragraph. Police observations of Welosky’s premises and the numbers and sobriety of people coming and going were relevant to the liquor charge. Questions about why officers had not arrested departing intoxicated men, charged manufacturing, or sought to convey a particular impression were properly excluded as irrelevant to guilt or credibility. A competent but nonresponsive answer required a motion to strike if that was the objection; merely requesting the court’s judgment did not establish reversible error. (417.)

There was sufficient evidence to support guilt. Officers’ motives in conducting raids were immaterial to guilt, and any bearing on credibility was adequately covered in the charge. The requested rulings were either unnecessary or sufficiently addressed by instructions the court found complete, accurate, and impartial. The exact disposition was “Exceptions overruled,” leaving the conviction undisturbed. Rugg wrote the sole reported opinion; Rugg, Crosby, Carroll, Wait, and Field were listed as present. No dissent, separate writing, numerical division, or numbered judicial footnote appears in the report. (398, 417–418.)

## Assigned purpose and reading scope

The Parrillo–Shane supplement’s paragraph on PDF 41, printed page 39, uses Welosky to contrast historical legislative intent with literal and dynamic interpretation. The editors propose that changed civic conditions and women’s enfranchisement could support updating the statute’s application. That alternative is editorial analysis; the court took the historical construction described above. The whole assigned page and original scan were read, together with the complete judicial report, including the constitutional and criminal-procedure rulings. The brief preserves the 1931 decision’s terms and limits and does not certify its subsequent treatment.
