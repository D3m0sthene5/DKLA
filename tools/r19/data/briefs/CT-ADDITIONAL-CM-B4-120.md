# Macedonia Church v. Lancaster Hotel Limited Partnership (2010 class certification)
270 F.R.D. 107 (D. Conn. September 30, 2010) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural position

District Judge Alvin W. Thompson granted the plaintiffs’ renewed motion for class certification under Rule 23(b)(3). He rejected mandatory limited-fund certification under Rule 23(b)(1)(B) and certified no punitive-damages class. The certified group comprised Macedonia Church, its members, and members’ relatives or friends who participated in the July 9–10, 2004 church trip to Lancaster, Pennsylvania, and were denied accommodations at Lancaster Host. This order determined how the action could proceed; it neither adjudicated racial discrimination nor awarded damages. (107, 111, 119–122.)

## Facts and chronology

Macedonia Church was a predominantly African-American congregation in South Norwalk, Connecticut. Congregants and affiliates alleged that Lancaster Hotel Limited Partnership, MASSPA Realty Corporation, and Fine Hotels Corporation denied the group lodging because of race, violating § 1981. The judge drew certification findings from depositions, affidavits, and exhibits, while identifying disagreements about communications. (111.)

In February 2003, church member Merle Rumble visited Lancaster Host and met sales representative Bonnie Skagen. In April 2004, Reverend Dewitt Stevens, his wife Addie, and Merle and assistant pastor Michael Rumble—all African-American—inspected the hotel for a proposed church excursion. They discussed approximately forty rooms for July 8–9. Skagen said a signed contract was necessary to confirm a reservation. The church announced the proposed lodging; participants would pay the church, which would pay the hotel. The signup sheet referred to the musical excursion and dates rather than naming Lancaster Host. (111–112.)

Skagen’s April 21 document was characterized by defendants as promotional information and by plaintiffs as a written proposal. Rumble believed it locked in reservations. On May 10 she requested arrival on July 9 and an additional Sunday, but a revised proposal still listed twenty rooms for July 8–9 and expressly said no space was being held because the function remained tentative. On May 17, she requested corrected dates and forty rooms; the parties disputed whether she asked for a tentative contract or a contract. After a May 26 voicemail and further calls, Skagen said on June 3 that forty rooms were unavailable. The hotel explained that reconciling its sales and reservation departments’ definite bookings exposed a booking problem. (112–113.)

Stevens enlisted Judith Addington, a Caucasian church friend, to inquire about comparable lodging. On June 11 she requested thirty rooms for the same weekend for Connecticut church members. Skagen confirmed availability and sent a tentative contract quoting thirty-five rooms. On June 21 she also indicated availability for fifteen additional rooms. In late June and early July, she called Addington to welcome future business but made no equivalent calls to Macedonia Church. The group instead stayed at a Ramada Inn, reporting fewer amenities and observing what members believed were prostitution, drug activity, and a street fight. Defendants maintained that the hotel had no vacancies on the relevant dates and explained that tentative bookings could exceed capacity, with cancellations estimated through netting. These circumstances framed the common discrimination claim, rather than establishing discrimination conclusively. (113–114.)

## Certification standard and beneficiary rights

Plaintiffs had to establish numerosity, commonality, typicality, and adequate representation, plus a permissible Rule 23(b) category. Following In re IPO, the judge had to resolve factual disputes relevant to those requirements, even where they overlapped the merits, under a preponderance standard. A mere showing that the requirements might be satisfied was insufficient. Certification did not authorize a trial of unrelated merits issues, and the class could be modified or decertified before final judgment. (114.)

Macedonia Church was the proposed contracting party; individual travelers were intended third-party beneficiaries. Connecticut law asked whether the contracting parties intended the promisor to assume a direct obligation to the beneficiary. Providing accommodations to the group’s travelers supplied the relevant obligation. The judge relied on the earlier ruling and Second Circuit recognition of beneficiary rights under § 1981. He treated Domino’s Pizza as having reserved, rather than resolved, the question whether an intended beneficiary could have protected contractual rights. Footnote 1 incorporated the earlier proposed-contract analysis. (115 & n. 1.)

This theory did not require each traveler independently to negotiate or possess contractual capacity. Whether travelers organized the trip, signed up before or after announcements, knew the hotel’s identity, or had particular lodging preferences did not alter their common beneficiary position. The judge distinguished a restaurant case involving plaintiffs who had not attempted to obtain service on their return visit: these travelers asserted beneficiary rights rather than rights as individual proposed promisors or promisees. Different responses to the church’s lodging choices similarly did not displace the common question. (116–117 & n. 2.)

## Rule 23(a) findings

The proposed class contained approximately 112 people. Inconsistent estimates and scratched-out names did not make this finite group unidentifiable: relatively few likely membership disputes could be resolved through a simple proceeding. More than forty members were clearly present, and exact identification of every member was unnecessary to establish numerosity. (115–116.)

All alleged injuries arose from one wrongful group denial at one time. The court found commonality and typicality despite differences among organizers and other travelers, minors and adults, and individuals’ ability to quantify damages. White or Hispanic friends could pursue discrimination allegedly suffered through association with African-American participants, an objection the earlier ruling had addressed. Individual damages remained an issue to manage. (116–118.)

Counsel were qualified. Rumble’s disputed organizational missteps did not make her inadequate. Dancy’s earlier third-degree larceny conviction did not require disqualification in light of subsequent church and community work. Stevens’ leadership position did not create the employment-management conflict defendants suggested: he had never worked for defendants or participated in their challenged practices. The assertion that the claim was certain to lose also failed to establish inadequate representation. (118–119.)

## Limited fund, predominance, and damages

Plaintiffs identified a $5 million liability insurance policy as a proposed limited fund. Ortiz required proof of the maximum available fund and aggregate claims sufficient to establish inadequacy, supported by evidence subject to challenge. Insurance did not prove defendants lacked other assets; they denied insolvency or bankruptcy. The record therefore could not support mandatory Rule 23(b)(1)(B) certification. (119–120.)

Rule 23(b)(3) was satisfied. Predominance was more demanding than commonality, but liability concerned one alleged discriminatory event affecting the group in its shared beneficiary capacity. This differed from classes combining discriminatory encounters at different times or locations. The judge considered actual-injury proof and individualized damages, which did not outweigh common liability proof here. Management possibilities included bifurcation, individual damages proceedings, subclasses, amendment, and later decertification. He identified these devices without ordering a particular damages-trial structure. (120–122.)

A class action was superior because neither side identified a substantial interest in separate suits, repeating liability proof would waste resources, and collective litigation would save expense without apparent unmanageable difficulties. Nevertheless, the judge refused a punitive-damages class seeking assessment before actual compensatory damages had been determined. He rejected the Rules Enabling Act objection because plaintiffs proposed no collective damages fund with individual members merely claiming shares, as in McLaughlin. (121–122.)

## Disposition and source scope

Document 207 was granted within the opinion’s limits: the defined class could proceed under Rule 23(b)(3), while mandatory limited-fund certification and a punitive-damages class were unavailable. No final finding of unlawful discrimination, executed contract, individual injury, or damages amount followed. (122.)
