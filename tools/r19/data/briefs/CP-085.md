# Hancock Oil Co. v. Independent Distributing Co.

**Civil Procedure · CP-085**  
24 Cal. 2d 497, 150 P.2d 463 (1944) · Supreme Court of California · July 13, 1944  
Full reported caption: *Hancock Oil Company of California v. W. L. Hopkins*

## Facts and proceedings

Hancock Oil and R.R. Bush Oil leased real property from W.L. and Gertrude Hopkins in 1936. About $1,500 in landowner royalties had accrued. In 1941, Independent Distributing, a partnership, sued claiming that the Hopkins parties held the property in trust for it and demanding an accounting of rents. The oil companies then sought interpleader, alleging that the Hopkins group and the partnership group both claimed the royalties accrued and to accrue under the lease. The companies disclaimed collusion and sought to discharge their obligation without choosing between rival recipients. The casebook's caption names the competing partnership; the complete report names Hopkins. Both identify this same interpleader dispute. 24 Cal. 2d at 501–02.

The partnership group answered that it owned the property and was entitled to the rents. The Hopkins group filed general and uncertainty demurrers, which the trial court sustained without leave to amend. Its stated reason was the rule barring a tenant from questioning the landlord's title as it existed when the tenancy began. The lessees appealed the resulting judgment. Thus, the Supreme Court reviewed the sufficiency of their interpleader complaint, rather than determining which claimant owned the land or royalties.

## Issue and competing rules

Could tenants use California's broadened interpleader statute to require their lessors and another claimant to litigate entitlement to the rent, notwithstanding tenant estoppel? Justice Edmonds's majority held that this complaint stated an interpleader claim.

Traditional equitable interpleader required competing claims to the same thing, debt, or duty; claims derived from a common source; a disinterested stakeholder; and no independent liability to either claimant. The 1881 amendment to Code of Civil Procedure § 386 expressly allowed claims without a common origin and claims that were not identical. It removed the common-source requirement and relaxed rigid identity of demands, but it did not authorize combining unrelated debts simply because one stakeholder owed both. 24 Cal. 2d at 503–05.

## Majority's application

As pleaded, both groups demanded the same lease royalties. If the partnership instead demanded reasonable use value, mesne profits, or damages for waste, those demands would concern a different obligation and would not support this interpleader. But when all claimants asserted rights to the contractual royalties, payment of the accrued amount could discharge the lessees' competing obligation once the claimants litigated entitlement. The lessees also stood as disinterested stakeholders. 24 Cal. 2d at 504–05.

The lease itself was not an independent liability defeating interpleader. An independent liability would remain unresolved by deciding the rival claimants' entitlement to the fund or obligation. Here, deciding that dispute would determine the lessees' responsibility. Treating every contractual relationship with a claimant as disqualifying would defeat ordinary interpleader by banks, insurers, and escrow holders as well. 24 Cal. 2d at 505–06.

The difficult remaining obstacle was § 1962(4), which barred a tenant from denying the landlord's title at the tenancy's commencement. The majority acknowledged the traditional estoppel rule and the accepted exception for claims derived from the landlord after the lease began. The complaint did not clearly establish that exception, so the court addressed the broader statutory question. It construed § 386 liberally to fulfill its remedial purpose. Abolishing the common-source restriction undermined the associated common-law restriction on tenant interpleader. Moreover, a neutral tenant could recognize the landlord's title while acknowledging another person's asserted claim to the rent, without adopting that competing claim as its own. 24 Cal. 2d at 506–09.

Otherwise, the tenant could face forfeiture or repeated payment while competing entitlement was litigated separately. Interpleader could protect the competing claimant too: payments to a landlord later becoming insolvent might otherwise defeat effective recovery. The landlord's delay in receiving rent was a real disadvantage, but a tenant had to allege facts showing a reasonable probability of competing demands, rather than mere pretext or suspicion. The remedy protected against double litigation over one liability; the tenant did not have to profess uncertainty about who would ultimately prevail. 24 Cal. 2d at 509–10.

## Carter's dissent

Justice Carter dissented in a writing omitted from the assigned excerpt. He viewed interpleading a landlord with someone claiming an antecedent adverse title as a denial of the landlord's original title, incompatible with § 1962(4). For him, § 386's removal of two technical interpleader restrictions did not expressly or impliedly repeal this separate landlord–tenant protection. The statute mentioned personal property and obligations, whereas a contrasting Indiana statute expressly included real property. 24 Cal. 2d at 511–15.

Carter also emphasized the practical cost of impounding rent. A landlord might need it for mortgages, taxes, upkeep, and insurance, and a clouded title could impair refinancing or cause foreclosure. An unscrupulous tenant could delay both rent collection and possession remedies. He preferred requiring the adverse claimant to seek an injunction or receiver and furnish security protecting the landlord if the claim failed. He would have affirmed the general-demurrer ruling. His three footnotes qualify California precedents, reproduce the Indiana provision, and discuss priority and holdover-possession rules. 24 Cal. 2d at 515–17.

## Judgment and source scope

The court **reversed the judgment**. The complaint survived a general demurrer, and denying amendment was an abuse of discretion even if the uncertainty demurrer had merit. On reconsideration, the trial court could require clarification of the essential allegations. Edmonds wrote for himself, Gibson, Shenk, Curtis, and Traynor; Carter dissented; Schauer did not participate. This was a 5–1 reported division, not a judgment establishing either group's ownership. 24 Cal. 2d at 510–11.
