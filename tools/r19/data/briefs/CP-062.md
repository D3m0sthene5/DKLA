# Jones v. Winsor

**22 S.D. 480, 118 N.W. 716 (1908) · Civil Procedure · CP-062**

The South Dakota Supreme Court reversed an order overruling a demurrer because the complaint combined contract recovery with an inadequately pleaded conversion theory. Abolition of common-law forms of pleading did not, in the court's view, permit one ambiguous count to be sustained on whichever theory later suited the proof. The plaintiffs could not ask the court to disregard their repeated conversion allegations as surplusage and preserve the pleading solely as money had and received. The decision illustrates the restrictive theory-of-the-pleading approach under this historical code; it does not apply modern federal alternative-pleading rules.

The plaintiffs alleged that they hired Winsor as an attorney to obtain a Sioux Falls city-railway franchise in April 1907. They gave him $2,500 for the required city-treasurer deposit and a further $130 for efforts to secure the franchise. When the franchise was not granted, the treasurer returned the deposit to Winsor for the plaintiffs' benefit. Winsor furnished an account listing disbursements, charged $1,250 for professional services, and remitted a $1,012.25 draft. Plaintiffs alleged that reasonable compensation was no more than $250, that $1,000 remained due, and that the excess charge was unjust, unlawful, and fraudulent. They repeatedly alleged wrongful conversion and demanded $1,000 with interest from April 17. These are allegations described on demurrer, not findings that Winsor committed fraud or charged an unreasonable fee. (22 S.D. at 482–83.)

Winsor demurred for failure to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. The circuit court overruled the demurrer, and he appealed. His principal argument was that a conversion complaint needed an allegation that plaintiffs owned or possessed the property at the time of the alleged conversion. The court agreed that, if construed as conversion, this complaint lacked that necessary allegation. An earlier description of the deposit as money belonging to plaintiffs did not supply the allegation of ownership or possession at the legally relevant time that the court required. (Id. at 483–84.)

The complaint also appeared to state a contract-based demand for money had and received. Plaintiffs argued that their references to fraudulent conversion could be disregarded as unnecessary language. The court considered this proposed rescue objectionable because the count had been framed with a double aspect: its general financial account resembled assumpsit, while its repeated accusations and requested judgment presented conversion. In the court's reasoning, a defendant should be able to identify the legal controversy from the complaint instead of waiting until trial to discover which of several inconsistent theories the plaintiff's proof would support. (Id. at 484–86.)

The opinion relied on Wisconsin decisions, Pierce v. Carey and Supervisors of Kewaunee County v. Decker. Their quoted reasoning distinguished simplification of forms from uncertainty about the nature of an action. Plain and concise pleading was supposed to make administration of justice definite, rather than allow tort, contract, and equity theories to be fused into an uncertain count. The South Dakota court endorsed that reasoning as applicable under its code despite abolition of traditional pleading forms. The consequence was a demanding choice: a complaint or count had to be assessed according to the cause of action it was designed to state, and failure to allege that cause's elements could not be repaired merely by pointing to facts supporting a different kind of claim. (Id. at 484–86.)

For this complaint, the conversion language was pervasive and deliberate. Plaintiffs alleged wrongful and fraudulent retention, repeated that Winsor converted the sum to his own use, and requested judgment specifically for wrongful conversion. The court therefore would not treat those allegations as accidental words that could be ignored. Doing so, it reasoned, would encourage ambiguity and mislead both the court and opposing party. The ruling concerned the particular fused pleading; its discussion should not be read as deciding the merits of a separately and adequately pleaded fee-recovery claim. (Id. at 485–86.)

The complete opinion contains an additional argument omitted from the assigned excerpt. Plaintiffs invoked Revised Code of Civil Procedure §157(2), governing arrest in designated actions involving an attorney's receipt or misuse of money in a fiduciary capacity. They argued that their demand concerned money Winsor received as their attorney. The court refused that statutory characterization: it saw no allegation of embezzlement or fraudulent misapplication of the relevant funds, and regarded the actual dispute as one over the reasonable value of professional services. Calling the excess fee unlawful and fraudulent did not, by itself, plead embezzlement or misapplication. This separate analysis reinforces the difference between using an accusatory label and stating the facts required for the statutory theory being asserted. (Id. at 486–87.)

The court also said it had examined the cited New York decisions and found them distinguishable, without developing those distinctions. It reversed the circuit court's order overruling the demurrer. The opinion did not determine the reasonable fee, order repayment of $1,000, or establish tort liability; it found this complaint insufficient. Justice Corson wrote the opinion. The complete report contains no judicial footnotes or separate writing and does not state a numerical vote. (Id. at 487.)

The assigned principal passage and both scans were read through the reversal, together with the full reported opinion including the omitted statutory argument and extended quoted reasoning. Court headnotes and counsel's printed argument summaries were distinguished from the court's reasoning. The case's procedural lesson is historical: a code could simplify forms while judges continued to insist on a definite substantive theory. That insistence drove the refusal to salvage the complaint as contract after its conversion theory failed, rather than any finding that the underlying fee controversy lacked a possible remedy.
