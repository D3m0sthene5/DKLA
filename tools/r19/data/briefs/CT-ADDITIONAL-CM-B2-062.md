# Jewish Center of Sussex County v. Whale — Supreme Court
86 N.J. 619, 432 A.2d 521 (1981) | Contracts

## Holding and procedural posture
The New Jersey Supreme Court unanimously affirmed rescission of the congregation's contract with its rabbi, together with the injunction entered on summary judgment. Justice Clifford wrote for all seven justices on July 23, 1981. The court relied on affirmative misrepresentation of the applicant's employment history, amounting to actual equitable fraud. It expressly declined to decide the broad duty to disclose a criminal record and disbarment adopted below. Affirmance of the result therefore did not constitute adoption of every lower-court rationale. (619, 621–624, 627.)

## Undisputed record and chronology
In late 1977 the Jewish Center advertised for a rabbi in the Anglo-Jewish Press. Whale responded with a brief resume listing his education, ordination, and experience. For 1970–1977, it represented that he had worked as an administrator in the Israeli Ministry of Education's Foreign Student Department in Jerusalem. It offered references on request; the congregation made no request. After he conducted services and met members, the parties negotiated an employment agreement dated January 1, 1978. The record contained no allegation that his performance of his duties was unsatisfactory. (622.)

In April the congregation discovered that he had been convicted of federal mail fraud in 1975 and disbarred from law practice in New York. The opinion recounts that he had actually left Israel in 1972, remained a fugitive until surrendering on February 14, 1974, and was later confined in federal prison following his conviction. His disbarment involved multiple acts of professional misconduct, including attempted bribery of a police officer, deception of a judge, and alteration of affidavits. These facts contradicted the resume's account of his activities throughout 1970–1977. (622–623.)

The trustees resolved on April 17, 1978 to rescind and sent a letter that day. The congregation then sued for rescission and an injunction barring him from entering its property or acting as its rabbi. Its verified complaint alleged both affirmative misrepresentation and concealment. A temporary restraining order became permanent when summary judgment was entered for the congregation. The Chancery Division relied on a disclosure duty, and a divided Appellate Division affirmed substantially on that rationale. The Supreme Court describes Judge Antell's appellate dissent as suggesting misrepresentation as a sounder basis while questioning summary resolution of the policy issues surrounding nondisclosure. That is the Supreme Court's account of the intermediate dissent, not a representation that its complete separate text is incorporated here. (623.)

## Defendant's objections and the governing distinctions
On his appeal as of right, Whale argued that fraud was inherently factual and required trial. He challenged materiality, fraudulent intent, injury, and the breadth of the duty imposed below. He emphasized that the congregation had made no inquiries into his past and had received four months of satisfactory service from a qualified rabbi. The court answered these arguments through the narrower affirmative misrepresentation already established by the undisputed record. (623–626.)

The opinion separates two classifications that should not be collapsed. Legal versus equitable fraud depends on the relief sought; actual versus constructive fraud concerns whether the wrong includes an untruth between the parties. A material false representation of existing or past fact can therefore constitute actual equitable fraud. For actual legal fraud, the court described knowing falsity and intent to induce detrimental reliance. Equitable fraud permits relief without the same scienter requirement. Because the congregation sought equitable remedies, the absence of a triable issue about scienter did not preclude its judgment. (624–625, 627.)

## Application and remedy
The resume contained a false statement about the applicant's rabbinical experience. That experience was plainly material to evaluating a candidate for the position. Submitting the resume in response to the advertisement demonstrated that the congregation was intended to rely on its contents. Reliance was supported by Whale's own admission that it had made no further inquiry into his past. His contention that the congregation should have investigated did not defeat the claim; the first footnote explains that a person engaging in fraud cannot insist that the victim should have been more circumspect. This evidentiary discussion of intended reliance did not transform the equitable claim into one requiring proof of legal-fraud scienter at trial. (625–626 & n. 1.)

Satisfactory service and the absence of quantified financial loss likewise did not bar rescission. Equity considered the unfair retention of a benefit obtained through misrepresentation, rather than making actual monetary loss an indispensable prerequisite. The inaccurate history obstructed discovery of an episode that would have adversely affected the employment opportunity, given the distinctive moral and spiritual relationship involved. The agreement called for religious, educational, spiritual, and pastoral service; the second footnote reproduces that description. The court concluded that legal damages could not make the congregation whole in light of the qualitative services contracted for, making rescission appropriate. It did not award monetary damages or punish the defendant for the earlier conviction. (626–627 & n. 2.)

## Disposition and limits
With no genuine material factual dispute over the false resume and equitable fraud, summary judgment was appropriate. The Supreme Court affirmed. Chief Justice Wilentz and Justices Sullivan, Pashman, Clifford, Schreiber, Handler, and Pollock all voted for affirmance; there was no separate Supreme Court writing. The court expressly reserved what duties of disclosure might apply absent the misrepresentation. Thus the trial court's broader nondisclosure rule and the Supreme Court's narrower ground remain distinct judicial stages. (627.)
