# Berk v. Choy
607 U.S. 187 (2026), No. 24-440, decided January 20, 2026 | Civil Procedure | Federal Rules and state affidavit requirements

## Facts, the screening statute, and the procedural dismissal

Harold Berk alleged that treatment for a fractured ankle made his injury worse. While visiting Delaware, he fell out of bed and was transported to a hospital owned by Beebe Medical Center. An X ray revealed a fracture, and Dr. Wilson Choy recommended a protective boot. According to Berk's complaint, hospital employees twisted his fractured ankle while forcing his leg into the boot. Choy did not immediately order another X ray, instructed Berk not to put weight on the ankle, and scheduled a follow-up two weeks later. A second X ray at that appointment showed a severe deformity requiring surgery. These were allegations supporting a malpractice claim, not findings establishing negligence. Majority slip op. pp. 1–2; assigned PDF pp. 4–5.

Berk sued Choy and Beebe under Delaware malpractice law in federal court, invoking diversity jurisdiction because the parties were citizens of different states. Delaware's statute, Del. Code tit. 18, §6853, ordinarily requires a medical professional's affidavit of merit to accompany a malpractice complaint. The affidavit must identify reasonable grounds to believe each defendant committed health-care negligence. A plaintiff showing good cause may obtain one sixty-day extension, but the extension motion must be filed before or with the complaint. Without the affidavit or a timely extension motion, the clerk must refuse to file or docket the complaint. Defendants need not act on the complaint until twenty days after the affidavit is filed, and may request an in camera review of the affidavit's statutory sufficiency. Majority slip op. p. 2.

Berk obtained an extension but could not find a doctor willing to supply the affidavit before the deadline. He filed his medical records under seal instead. Defendants sought an in camera examination of those materials, while Berk argued that the Federal Rules displaced the state affidavit requirement. The district court rejected that argument and dismissed for noncompliance. The Third Circuit affirmed, reasoning that the Federal Rules did not address whether an affidavit had to accompany a complaint. It therefore applied the ordinary Erie inquiry and concluded that the state requirement was substantive: failure to apply it would affect outcomes and encourage forum shopping and inequitable administration of law. Majority slip op. pp. 2–3.

The Supreme Court reviewed the applicability of the affidavit requirement in federal court. It did not review medical evidence to determine whether either defendant had actually injured Berk negligently.

## The governing framework and the precise question

The Rules of Decision Act directs federal courts to use state substantive law unless the Constitution, a treaty, or federal legislation otherwise requires or provides. The Rules Enabling Act supplies statutory authority for Federal Rules governing procedure. Accordingly, when a valid Federal Rule answers the disputed question, it displaces contrary state law even if the state rule would qualify as substantive under ordinary Erie analysis. The Court first identifies a conflict by determining whether the Federal Rule answers the question at issue, then asks whether that Rule is valid under the Rules Enabling Act and Congress's rulemaking power. Majority slip op. pp. 3–4.

Justice Barrett's majority framed the disputed question as whether Berk's lawsuit could be dismissed because his complaint was not accompanied by an expert affidavit. Rule 8, the Court held, answered that question. Justice Jackson agreed with the judgment but regarded Rules 3 and 12, rather than Rule 8, as the relevant conflicting Rules. Their disagreement concerned the scope of particular Federal Rules and the appropriate framing of the conflict, not whether Delaware's affidavit requirement should govern this federal action.

## Why Rule 8 displaced the requirement

Rule 8(a)(2) requires a short and plain statement showing entitlement to relief. For the majority, this prescription establishes what information about the merits a plaintiff must supply at the outset; it does not additionally demand evidentiary proof. Rule 12 reinforces the distinction between alleging a plausible claim and proving it. A Rule 12(b)(6) motion tests failure to state a claim, and considering material outside the pleadings requires the conversion procedure in Rule 12(d). A well-pleaded claim may proceed even when actual proof seems improbable. Majority slip op. pp. 4–6.

The Court placed Delaware's additional demand within that same field. Under federal pleading rules, sufficient allegations can permit the case to proceed; under the state statute, those allegations cannot suffice without an expert's supporting document. The majority rejected the formal answer that the affidavit sits on a separate sheet outside the complaint. A state cannot avoid the collision merely by requiring the same additional information about the merits beside, rather than within, the pleading. The Court drew a negative implication from Rule 8's specified requirements and relied on decisions rejecting additional pleading requirements for particular categories of claims. It emphasized that the Federal Rules themselves may prescribe special treatment, as Rule 9 does. Majority slip op. pp. 5–7 and n. 2.

Defendants relied on Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp., which had permitted a state security-for-expenses requirement in a shareholder derivative suit. But Cohen did not exempt every state precondition to litigation. The state statute there created potential liability for litigation expenses and required security against that liability; the Federal Rule concerned disclosure and notice. They addressed different questions. Delaware's affidavit statute and Rule 8, by contrast, addressed the same initial information about a claim's merits. Majority slip op. pp. 7–8.

Defendants next proposed adapting Delaware's requirement to federal practice by demanding the affidavit sometime early in the case. That approach would turn a single extension for good cause into a routine opportunity to supply the document later, and would have to abandon the state's different response timetable. It would produce a free-standing evidentiary requirement rather than enforce the statute as written. Even such a rewritten requirement lacked a permissible dismissal mechanism: defendants conceded that an absent affidavit was not a Rule 12(b)(6) ground, Berk was actively litigating rather than failing to prosecute, and inherent authority could not replace the Federal Rules' mechanism for testing proof. Rule 56 supplies summary judgment, with adequate time for discovery. The state screening rule could not obtain its desired protection from discovery through a different early evidentiary demand. Majority slip op. pp. 8–9.

Finally, Rule 11(a) says a pleading need not be verified or accompanied by an affidavit unless a rule or statute specifically says otherwise. Defendants treated that proviso as importing Delaware's requirement. The Court declined to decide whether the proviso incorporates any state affidavit statutes. It held that it did not incorporate this one: Rule 11 addresses the certifications of attorneys and parties, including circumstances requiring a represented party to verify a pleading, rather than an independent third-party expert's affidavit. Majority slip op. pp. 9–10.

## Validity under the Rules Enabling Act

Identifying the conflict was not the final step. Rule 8 also had to satisfy the Rules Enabling Act's prohibition on abridging, enlarging, or modifying substantive rights. Applying Sibbach's test, the Court asked whether the Federal Rule really regulated procedure—the manner and means of enforcing rights. Rule 8 determines what plaintiffs must present at the outset, rather than the underlying rights, remedies, or substantive rules of decision. Its practical effect on litigants did not invalidate it. Majority slip op. pp. 10–11.

Defendants proposed a second inquiry into whether the displaced state law was substantive. The majority rejected that approach. For the validity of this Federal Rule, the inquiry focused on what Rule 8 regulated; Delaware's substantive purpose or the state statute's characterization did not supply a separate veto. Thus, the Third Circuit's ordinary Erie characterization could not rescue the state requirement after a collision with a valid Federal Rule had been identified.

## Jackson's concurrence in the judgment

Justice Jackson agreed that the affidavit requirement was inapplicable but objected to the majority's expansive reading of Rule 8. In her view, Rule 3 independently settled commencement: filing a complaint starts a civil action, whereas Delaware would require an additional affidavit or extension motion before the complaint could be filed and docketed. Rules 3 and §6853 answered the same commencement question differently. Jackson concurrence slip op. pp. 1–7.

She distinguished Walker v. Armco Steel Corp., where state law controlled commencement for the state's limitations period. Rule 3 did not govern that limitations question. Here, the filing and docketing requirement operated in the same procedural domain as the Federal Rules and changed when associated response and service obligations began. She likewise distinguished Woods v. Interstate Realty Co., which concerned substantive conditions on a foreign corporation's access to state courts. She also argued that the Federal Rules should be interpreted with sensitivity to state policies, invoking Gasperini and the alignment of five Justices in Shady Grove. Concurrence slip op. pp. 2–3 n. 1, 6–7 and n. 3.

Jackson regarded Rule 8 as prescribing the contents of a pleading, rather than everything that may be required concerning the merits at the beginning of litigation. An affidavit outside the pleading therefore did not conflict with Rule 8 merely because it supplied additional material. The problem was the breadth of the majority's inference, not negative implications as a general interpretive technique. Concurrence slip op. pp. 7–10 and nn. 4–6.

Rule 12(d), however, supplied an additional direct conflict. Delaware's highest court had construed the absent affidavit as a ground for dismissal. An affidavit filed separately in a sealed envelope was outside the pleadings; it was neither an authorized pleading under Rule 7 nor a document incorporated into the complaint. Its content was also not discoverable or admissible under the state statute. A merits dismissal accounting for that outside material could not bypass Rule 12(d). Jackson believed the majority unnecessarily strained Rule 8 when Rules 3 and 12 already yielded the correct result. Concurrence slip op. pp. 10–13 and n. 7.

## Judgment, votes, and practical consequence

The Court reversed the Third Circuit and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. Berk won the federal-procedure issue and could continue without Delaware's affidavit-of-merit requirement; he did not obtain a finding of malpractice or a damages award. The defendants remained able to contest his factual allegations and proof through the Federal Rules.

Barrett wrote for eight Justices: Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas, Alito, Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh joined her opinion. Jackson concurred only in the judgment. The result was unanimous, with an 8–1 division concerning the rationale; there was no dissent. The decision's specific treatment of §6853 should not be generalized into a holding that every state condition, expert requirement, or procedural-looking rule is automatically displaced. The required analysis still identifies the particular federal-state collision and tests the relevant Federal Rule's validity.
