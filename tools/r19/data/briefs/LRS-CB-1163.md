# Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife

**Course:** Legislation and the Regulatory State
**ID:** LRS-CB-1163
**Review:** Complete source comparison; affidavit fact and Stevens rationale repaired; complete assigned notes added.

## Citation and Court

Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992). Supreme Court of the United States. Argued December 3, 1991; decided June 12, 1992. Justice Scalia announced the judgment and wrote for the Court in Parts I, II, III-A, and IV, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices White, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Part III-B had only Rehnquist, White, and Thomas joining Scalia. Justice Kennedy, joined by Souter, concurred in part and in the judgment. Justice Stevens concurred in the judgment. Justice Blackmun, joined by O'Connor, dissented. Assigned casebook excerpt begins at printed p. 1163. Full opinion: [Justia reproduction](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/504/555/). Original report: [U.S. Reports scan](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep504/usrep504555/usrep504555.pdf).

## Parties

Secretary of the Interior Manuel Lujan was petitioner. Defenders of Wildlife and allied environmental organizations were respondents. Defenders challenged an Interior regulation limiting the Endangered Species Act's §7 consultation requirement to federal actions within the United States or on the high seas. Members Joyce Kelly and Amy Skilbred supplied affidavits intended to establish injury to Defenders through its members.

## Material Facts

ESA §7(a)(2) requires federal agencies, in consultation with the appropriate wildlife Secretary, to ensure that actions they authorize, fund, or carry out are not likely to jeopardize listed species or adversely modify critical habitat. Interior and Commerce initially interpreted consultation to extend abroad, but in 1986 promulgated a rule limiting it to actions in the United States or on the high seas. 504 U.S. at 558-559. Kelly visited Egypt in 1986 and observed the Nile crocodile’s traditional habitat, hoping to observe the crocodile directly on a later visit; Skilbred visited Sri Lanka in 1981 and observed habitat of the endangered Asian elephant and leopard, but had been unable to see any of the endangered species. Each alleged that federally assisted projects threatened those species and said she intended to return. Neither identified a concrete return date or ticket. Defenders also advanced ecosystem-nexus, animal-nexus, vocational-nexus, and procedural-right theories. Id. at 563-567, 571-578.

## Procedural History

The District Court dismissed for lack of standing. A divided Eighth Circuit reversed. On remand, the Secretary again raised standing at summary judgment; the District Court treated the appellate ruling as controlling, granted Defenders summary judgment on the merits, and ordered a new regulation. The Eighth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court reversed for lack of Article III standing. Id. at 559-560, 578. The summary-judgment posture was critical. Standing is part of the plaintiff's case. General allegations may suffice at pleading, but at summary judgment the plaintiff must set forth specific facts by affidavit or evidence. Id. at 561.

## Issues

Had Defenders shown concrete, particularized, actual or imminent injury? Was the asserted injury fairly traceable to the challenged regulation and likely to be redressed where projects depended on other agencies and foreign actors? Could Congress's citizen-suit provision create Article III standing based solely on a generally shared interest in lawful administration?

## Rule and Holding

Article III standing requires (1) injury in fact that is concrete and particularized and actual or imminent; (2) causation; and (3) likely redressability. The plaintiff bears the burden at every litigation stage. Id. at 560-561. Defenders failed. Past visits plus indefinite intentions to return did not establish imminent injury. The broader nexus theories lacked the required personal connection. A citizen-suit provision cannot eliminate Article III's concrete-injury requirement. Id. at 562-567, 571-578. Scalia's Part III-B redressability analysis did not command six votes. Four Justices reasoned that an order directed to Interior would not necessarily bind funding agencies and that it was speculative whether changes in U.S. funding would halt foreign projects. Kennedy and Souter did not join. Id. at 568-571.

## Judgment

The Court reversed the Eighth Circuit and remanded. It did not decide whether ESA §7(a)(2) applies to federal actions abroad. Id. at 578.

## Reasoning

### Injury and imminence

The Court accepted that destruction of species a member personally observes can cause aesthetic or recreational injury. The defect was imminence. Past visits showed past exposure, while an intention to return "some day" without concrete plans did not establish threatened future harm. Id. at 562-564. The ecosystem-, animal-, and vocational-nexus theories would have permitted plaintiffs with an interest in a species or ecosystem to challenge projects without a sufficiently specific connection to the affected place. The Court rejected that expansion. Id. at 565-567.

### Redressability and third parties

When the plaintiff is not the object of the challenged government action, causation and redressability depend on choices by third parties. Part III-B concluded that the record did not establish that funding agencies would follow an injunction against Interior or that consultation would stop the projects. Id. at 568-571. Because only four Justices joined this part, a brief must not present it as the same six-Justice holding as Part III-A.

### Procedural injury

A procedural right can relax the immediacy and redressability showing for a person whose concrete interest is threatened; the Court's example was a person living next to a proposed federally licensed dam. But procedure cannot substitute for the underlying concrete interest. Defenders had not shown that its members personally faced imminent harm. Id. at 572 n.7.

### Citizen suits and separation of powers

Scalia distinguished concrete injury from a universal right to have the Executive obey the law. Allowing Congress to authorize any citizen to sue solely to enforce lawful administration would, in the majority's view, move the Take Care function from the President toward continuing judicial supervision. Id. at 573-578.

## Separate Opinions

### Kennedy, joined by Souter

Kennedy agreed that the record lacked concrete injury but emphasized Congress's power to define injuries and causal chains. Congress can define injuries and articulate causal chains creating a case or controversy where none existed before, but must at least identify the injury to be vindicated and relate it to the persons entitled to sue. The ESA's authorization for "any person" was too indiscriminate by itself. Id. at 579-581. Kennedy and Souter did not join Part III-B; Kennedy would not reach redressability given the injury defect. He also expressly reserved whether a similar nexus theory could work on different facts. Id. at 579–580.

### Stevens

Stevens believed respondents had standing and expressly disagreed with both the Court’s imminence conclusion and the plurality’s redressability conclusion. He concurred in the judgment because, on the statutory merits, he read §7(a)(2) as not applying to activities in foreign countries. Id. at 581–589.

He would measure imminence by the timing and likelihood of destruction of the species and habitat to which genuine study or observational interests attach. Past visits, professional backgrounds and sworn intentions could establish that genuine interest. He presumed federal agencies would follow this Court’s authoritative construction and regarded consultation and threatened withdrawal of assistance as capable of influencing foreign projects. Id. at 582–585.

On statutory scope, Stevens applied the presumption against extraterritoriality. The critical-habitat clause’s reference to affected States, the lack of a reason to give the species and habitat clauses different geographic reach, the ESA’s express international provisions elsewhere, and its domestic findings persuaded him that §7(a)(2)’s consultation duty did not extend abroad. His statutory rationale was his own concurrence; the Court resolved the case on standing. Id. at 585–589 & nn.3–7.

### Blackmun, joined by O'Connor

The dissent argued that the Court demanded excessive specificity about future travel and failed to draw summary-judgment inferences for Defenders. He distinguished specific-fact production under Rule 56(e) from whether those facts create a genuine issue under Rule 56(c), and challenged a universal geographic-proximity rule for environmental harm. Id. at 589–595. It also believed agencies would likely conform to an authoritative ESA interpretation, and their participation could bind them through collateral estoppel. The value and mitigating purpose of United States assistance could affect project conduct despite its minority share of total funding. Id. at 595–601 & nn.2–4.

Blackmun also disputed the majority’s procedural-injury and separation-of-powers discussion. Congress often implements substantive goals through consultation, reporting, and other procedural duties; judicial enforcement can protect the legislature’s design rather than transfer executive power to courts. Some procedural breaches may be sufficiently bound up with prevention of concrete harm to demonstrate injury, and no categorical bar should exclude procedural injuries. These are dissenting arguments, not a holding that every procedural omission gives everyone standing. Id. at 601–606. His hypotheticals about property sales, job applicants and consortium illustrate his objection to evidentiary formalism; they are not additional claims adjudicated here. Id. at 592–593.

## Doctrinal Significance and Limits

The three-element standing formulation and rejection of indefinite "some day" intentions had six votes. Scalia's fuller redressability analysis had four. The case does not make environmental, aesthetic, or procedural injury categorically insufficient; it requires a concrete personal connection and adequate imminence. Kennedy's concurrence is important when Congress creates statutory rights.
