# Baldwin v. Iowa State Traveling Men's Association

283 U.S. 522 (1931) | Civil Procedure | Supreme Court of the United States

## Identity and assigned scope

No. 445, argued April 22 and decided May 18, 1931. Justice Roberts delivered the Court's opinion. The supplied CivPro textbook develops the case in its discussion of challenging personal jurisdiction, PDF page 142, printed page 235. The complete reported opinion supplements that account. It concerns the finality of a jurisdictional issue actually contested in an earlier action.

## Parties and relief

Baldwin, the petitioner, sought enforcement in the federal court for Southern Iowa of a judgment previously entered against the Iowa State Traveling Men's Association in the federal court for Western Missouri. The association resisted enforcement on the ground that the Missouri court lacked personal jurisdiction. The Supreme Court reviewed the rejection of Baldwin's enforcement action; it did not independently try the underlying claim or determine a new damages award. (523–524.)

## Material facts and procedural history

The original action began in a Missouri state court and was removed to the federal district court. The association appeared specially and moved to quash service and dismiss. The court initially quashed service but refused dismissal. After an alias summons was returned served, the association again appeared specially, asking that the service and return be set aside and the action dismissed for lack of personal jurisdiction. The court heard the question on affidavits and briefs, denied the motion, and allowed thirty days to plead. The association filed no plea, did not seek to set aside the eventual judgment for the amount claimed, and did not seek appellate review. (523–524.)

When Baldwin later sued on that judgment in Iowa, the association renewed the same jurisdictional objections: it was an Iowa corporation, had never been present in Missouri, and the person served there was not an agent upon whom service could bind it. The Iowa district court admitted the jurisdictional challenge despite Baldwin's objection that the issue had already been adjudicated. After a hearing without a jury, it sustained the defense and dismissed the action. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, 40 F.2d 357. (523–524 & nn. 1–2.) These assertions were the association's grounds of objection; the Supreme Court did not resolve them afresh in its favor.

## Issue

May a defendant that specially appeared, fully contested personal jurisdiction, lost that contest, and allowed the resulting judgment to become final relitigate the same jurisdictional question when the judgment is sued upon in another federal court?

## Rule and holding

No. A full adjudication of the defendant's personal-jurisdiction objection can itself become conclusive. A special appearance preserves an objection for direct review; it does not entitle the defendant to repeat a fully heard jurisdictional contest through a later collateral attack. Finality applies, in the absence of fraud, where the party voluntarily submitted the issue and was fully heard. (524–526.)

## Judgment

The Court reversed the judgment rejecting Baldwin's enforcement action and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. The Court did not simply affirm the Missouri judgment on a fresh examination of its jurisdictional merits. (527.)

## Reasoning

The constitutional arguments did not answer the dispute. Baldwin invoked Article IV's Full Faith and Credit Clause, but neither court concerned was a state court. The association invoked Fourteenth Amendment due process; the Court explained that due process does not provide a right to litigate the same question twice. The controlling question was the preclusive effect of the prior adjudication of jurisdiction. (524.)

The special appearance mattered for the manner of obtaining review, rather than creating immunity from the eventual adjudication. Under the authorities the Court cited, the association could have preserved its objection while proceeding on the merits and challenged the jurisdictional ruling on appeal. It instead obtained a full hearing of the objection and then took no further steps. Its deliberate submission of that issue justified treating the resulting determination as settled. (525.)

The Court distinguished a defendant that never appears. Such a defendant has never had its day in court on jurisdiction and can raise that issue in an action on the default judgment. The association's later failure to plead did not place it in that category: it had already appeared for the specific purpose of contesting jurisdiction and had been heard. Thus, the fact that the eventual merits judgment followed nonparticipation did not erase the earlier jurisdictional adjudication. (525–526.)

Two precedents did not require a different result. In National Exchange Bank v. Wiley, an unauthorized attorney had purported to appear and confess judgment; the defendant itself had not appeared and contested jurisdiction. In Bank of Jasper v. First National Bank, the special appearance sought to quash a statutory notice that the state court construed merely as an invitation to participate. The later power over the property was not an issue the bank had litigated. Baldwin's association had actually submitted the personal-jurisdiction issue now being renewed. (526–527.)
