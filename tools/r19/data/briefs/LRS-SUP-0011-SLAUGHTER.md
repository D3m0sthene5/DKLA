# Trump v. Slaughter
609 U.S. 422 (2026), No. 25-332, decided June 29, 2026 | Legislation and Regulation; Class 11 supplement

## Facts and agency structure
The Federal Trade Commission has five Commissioners appointed by the President with Senate confirmation. Their ordinary terms are seven years and staggered, and no more than three Commissioners may belong to the same political party. The President designates the Chair. The Federal Trade Commission Act permits the President to remove a Commissioner only for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office. The Commission administers and enforces approximately eighty statutes, makes substantive rules, investigates regulated businesses, conducts administrative adjudications, and brings civil enforcement suits. The constitutional dispute concerned Congress's restriction on removal of the agency's leadership, rather than the validity of a particular FTC rule or enforcement action. (Court opinion, 609 U.S. at 431–432, 454–455; dissent, id. at 481–482.)

Rebecca Slaughter, a Democrat, was nominated by President Trump and confirmed in 2018; she was confirmed for a second term in 2024. When Trump began his second presidential term in January 2025, the Commission had two Republicans and three Democrats. He replaced the Chair with Andrew Ferguson, and the former Chair, Lina Khan, resigned her Commission seat shortly afterward. Pending confirmation of a replacement, the Commission had two Republicans and two Democrats. In March, Trump removed Slaughter and Alvaro Bedoya without alleging any of the statutory causes. His notice instead stated that their service conflicted with his administration's priorities and invoked Article II. These removals left the two Republican Commissioners. (Id. at 431–432, 482.)

## Procedure and parties' arguments
Slaughter and Bedoya sued the President and other executive officials for declaratory and injunctive relief restoring them to office. They alleged that removal without statutory cause was ultra vires, violated the Administrative Procedure Act, and violated the Constitution. Bedoya later formally resigned, and the District Court dismissed his claims as moot. Only Slaughter's claims reached the Supreme Court. The District Court granted her summary judgment, declared the purported removal ultra vires, and permanently enjoined interference with her performance of lawful duties. It considered Humphrey's Executor v. United States, 295 U.S. 602 (1935), which upheld the same FTC removal provision, controlling. (609 U.S. at 432–433 & n. 1.)

A divided D.C. Circuit panel denied the government's request for a stay pending appeal, explaining that Humphrey's Executor foreclosed success. Judge Rao dissented. The Supreme Court stayed the District Court's order and granted certiorari before judgment. Consequently, its final disposition directly reversed the District Court; the D.C. Circuit had ruled on interim relief, not finally resolved the merits appeal. (Id. at 433, 464.)

The government argued that the FTC exercises executive power which Article II places under presidential control and that the President must be able to remove its leaders at will. It sought rejection or overruling of Humphrey's Executor and also asserted that courts could not provide reinstatement relief even if Slaughter's statutory protection survived. Slaughter defended Humphrey's Executor, congressional and public reliance on independent commissions, historical examples of partially independent administration, and Congress's authority to impose reasonable removal limits consistent with the President's constitutional duties. She accepted a default presidential removal power and accepted Seila Law's result, while disputing that this required unrestricted removal of FTC Commissioners. (Id. at 445–453, 456–464; dissent, id. at 511–512 n. 13.)

## Issue, holding, and exact disposition
The question was whether the FTC's for-cause removal restriction could constitutionally prevent the President from removing Slaughter, a principal officer exercising executive power, without one of the statutory grounds. The Court held the restriction contrary to Article II's separation of powers. The FTC's rulemaking, administrative enforcement, adjudication, and civil litigation powers placed it within the President's general administrative control, requiring presidential ability to remove its Commissioners at will. The Court overruled Humphrey's Executor to the extent it permitted a contrary arrangement. It reversed the judgment of the United States District Court for the District of Columbia and remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion. Slaughter's statutory removal restriction could not sustain the judgment reinstating her. (Id. at 450, 454–455, 464.)

Chief Justice Roberts wrote for six Justices on the dispositive analysis: himself and Justices Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett. Thomas joined all but Part III-B, the discussion reserving questions about the Federal Reserve and other entities and officials not before the Court. Five Justices joined that part. Gorsuch concurred separately without additional joiners. Sotomayor dissented, joined by Kagan and Jackson. The judgment was 6–3, with a partial nonjoinder by Thomas rather than a separate Thomas opinion. (Id. at 428.)

## Text, structure, and the majority's historical account
The majority began with Article II's vesting of executive power in one President and his duty to take care that the laws are faithfully executed. It understood these clauses to establish a hierarchy: subordinate officers assist the President in carrying out his responsibilities, and removal makes those officers accountable to him. Without that power, the President could be constitutionally responsible for executive conduct while unable to replace the officials conducting it. The constitutional choice of a single President also made it possible for voters to identify responsibility for executive decisions. (Id. at 433–438.)

The historical argument contrasted the Constitution with early state systems in which governors shared executive authority with councils. The Framers, on the majority's account, sought energetic and identifiable executive leadership while retaining constitutional limits on presidential power. Congress could establish departments and officers to assist the President, but those assistants were not his constitutional equals. The majority inferred removal authority both from the founding understanding of executive power and from the practical need for subordinates subject to presidential supervision. (Id. at 434–438.)

The majority treated the Decision of 1789 as confirmation of a constitutional removal power that Congress could not diminish. The First Congress debated Senate participation, congressional control, and presidential control when establishing departments. It ultimately used statutory language presupposing presidential removal rather than language appearing to confer that power. The majority relied on Madison's account, contemporary and later commentary, and early presidential practice to conclude that the executive hierarchy had been settled through governmental practice. It rejected the dissent's distinction between a default removal authority and an authority Congress could not limit: if the power belongs constitutionally to the President, Congress cannot abridge it merely by creating an office. (Id. at 438–442, 457–461.)

These historical propositions are the majority's interpretation, contested at length by the dissent. The brief does not present either side's characterization of the Decision of 1789 as an undisputed historical fact.

## Precedent and the overruling of Humphrey's Executor
The majority read Ex parte Hennen, Parsons, and particularly Myers v. United States, 272 U.S. 52 (1926), as supporting presidential control through removal. Myers invalidated a requirement of Senate consent to removal of a postmaster. For this Court, its reasoning established that the President must be able to replace officers for whose execution of the laws he remains responsible. The majority also relied on Myers's account of executive resistance to the Tenure of Office Act. The dissent would confine Myers to its Senate-consent holding. (609 U.S. at 442–445; dissent, id. at 507–509.)

Humphrey's Executor upheld statutory removal protection for an FTC Commissioner whom President Roosevelt dismissed without specifying cause. That Court described the FTC as predominantly quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial and exercising no part of executive power. The present majority considered that classification untenable. Buckley treated litigation to enforce federal law as executive; Chadha and Bowsher treated administration of legislatively delegated standards as execution of law. Morrison itself acknowledged that FTC functions would now be regarded as executive at least in part. Free Enterprise Fund and Seila Law emphasized presidential control and declined to extend Humphrey's Executor to, respectively, a second layer of removal protection and the sole head of a powerful agency. (Id. at 445–450.)

The majority accordingly concluded that Humphrey's Executor could no longer justify insulating an agency exercising executive power. To the extent anything more remained, it expressly overruled the precedent. Its stare decisis analysis considered reasoning, consistency with later cases, workability, and reliance. It found the quasi-power classifications unsound, the decision inconsistent with later doctrine, and its boundaries uncertain. It rejected reliance on congressional creation of independent agencies as a sufficient justification for reallocating constitutionally vested executive power. Independence from the President does not eliminate political influence and may increase congressional influence; in the majority's view, public accountability through the President protects the relevant constitutional reliance interests. (Id. at 450–453.)

The footnote discussing severability did not decide to abolish the FTC or invalidate its substantive programs. It invoked earlier decisions severing removal restrictions while leaving agencies intact, and stated that broader questions of congressional preference could be addressed in a case actually presenting severability. Treating this case as dissolution of the Commission would go beyond its judgment. (Id. at 453 n. 3.)

## Application to the FTC and matters reserved
The Court identified three concrete sets of FTC authority. The Commission promulgates binding substantive regulations implementing broad statutory standards; investigates and enforces requirements through administrative proceedings and orders; and brings federal civil suits seeking injunctions, penalties, and consumer redress. Each is executive power on the majority's account. Their combined breadth made Slaughter's position a straightforward application of its removal rule. The Commission's multimember and bipartisan design did not rescue its removal protection. (Id. at 454–455.)

Part III-B limited the reach of the decision. Creation by Congress does not itself make an entity an executive instrument: an institution might exercise no sovereign power or merely assist Congress's investigative and informational work. The Court also left room for functions historically handled outside the ordinary executive hierarchy. It identified the Federal Reserve's relationship to the First and Second Banks of the United States and cautioned that this opinion should not itself be read to determine the validity of that arrangement. It expressly reserved the permissibility of tenure protection for judges of non-Article III courts, such as the Tax Court and Court of Federal Claims, because those questions were neither presented nor briefed. Thomas did not join this part. Slaughter should therefore not be described as itself invalidating every removal protection, abolishing the Federal Reserve's independence, or resolving the status of every administrative adjudicator or civil servant. (Id. at 455–456.)

The majority rejected a general reasonableness test for congressional limits. Such a standard, it reasoned, could allow Congress to insulate most ordinary departments and leave the President unable to supervise the execution of laws. It also answered Slaughter's early-agency examples by maintaining that their executive members were removable by the President and rejected the claim that later widespread practice could constitutionalize an impermissible arrangement. (Id. at 456–464.)

## Gorsuch's concurrence
Gorsuch agreed that principal officers exercising executive power must answer to the President through removal. His concern was what remained after that conclusion. Congress had given independent agencies extensive powers to make rules and decide disputes, apparently relying in part on their insulation from presidential control. Removing that insulation transfers effective control over those powers to the President; it does not diminish the powers themselves. Gorsuch described the risk of coordinated regulation, enforcement, and adjudication across numerous fields being used against disfavored businesses or individuals. (Id. at 464–476.)

He questioned whether Congress would have delegated so much authority on that premise and whether it could readily reclaim it, because a President can veto legislation reducing delegated power. He urged judicial enforcement of constitutional limits on delegated lawmaking and adjudication through nondelegation, major questions, vagueness, Article III, due process, and jury-trial principles. This was an invitation to future doctrinal action; his concurrence did not hold existing FTC delegations unconstitutional or grant relief against an agency adjudication. Unlike the dissent, he regarded the removal holding as correct but insufficient by itself to restore the allocation of powers. (Id. at 476–479.)

## Sotomayor's dissent: congressional design, history, and precedent
Sotomayor would have sustained the FTC's removal protection and upheld relief for Slaughter. She emphasized that Humphrey's Executor addressed the same statute and issue, and that later decisions—including Wiener and Morrison—preserved or extended its functional approach. Free Enterprise Fund and Seila Law distinguished novel structures while leaving the established multimember commission intact. The FTC had investigative, enforcement, and rulemaking functions in 1935 as well; changes in scope did not require abandoning its core constitutional treatment. If particular added powers were constitutionally incompatible with the design, severing those powers might be a more appropriate remedy than eliminating independence altogether. (Id. at 480–488.)

The dissent read constitutional silence about presidential removal alongside Congress's express powers to create offices and enact necessary and proper laws. A duty to faithfully execute laws does not automatically create unlimited power to disregard their tenure provisions. Presidential appointment is itself shared with the Senate and depends on congressionally established offices, undermining the claim that appointment necessarily entails unrestricted removal. The dissent relied on English and state practices, Federalist Nos. 39 and 77, and the absence of explicit Convention discussion to dispute the majority's originalist account. (Id. at 492–497.)

Its central reading of the Decision of 1789 distinguished rejecting Senate participation in particular removals from forbidding Congress to prescribe grounds for removal. The adopted statute assumed that the President could remove without an express grant, but did not resolve whether Congress could impose for-cause tenure. The dissent cited continued disagreement among major nineteenth-century commentators and statesmen, early structures such as the Sinking Fund Commission and Revolutionary War Debt Commission, and later independent commissions established with presidential participation. It treated this long political practice as significant constitutional evidence where the text did not expressly settle the question, contrasting Chadha's explicit bicameralism and presentment requirements. (Id. at 488–491, 497–509.)

Sotomayor also rejected removal as the exclusive measure of accountability. Appointment, selection of the Chair, budgets, and removal for genuine misconduct allow presidential influence while preserving some impartiality and continuity. Congress's design balanced accountability with those other constitutional values. Courts have less institutional knowledge than Congress and the President about how those mechanisms function. (Id. at 509–511.)

## The dissent's stare decisis and remedies objections
For the dissent, reliance was unusually strong: Congress expressly built agencies and delegated powers in reliance on Humphrey's Executor, while regulated firms and ordinary people organized their affairs around partially independent administration. At-will dismissal could defeat bipartisan membership and lengthy terms by removing opposition members or reducing a commission to a single member. Labeling legislative reliance illegitimate because the prior precedent was wrong would defeat the function of stare decisis, which can preserve even a decision a later Court disputes. (Id. at 512–518.)

The dissent found the established multimember-agency rule workable, with occasional litigation reflecting ordinary boundary disputes. It attributed much recent uncertainty to the Court's changing treatment on the emergency docket. In contrast, the new rule left unexplained exceptions and uncertainties involving the Federal Reserve, non-Article III adjudicators, inferior officers, and career employees. These were the dissent's predictions and objections, not holdings that those protections had already fallen. (Id. at 518–526.)

In footnote 13, the dissent addressed the government's alternative remedies arguments. If Congress may lawfully confer protection, Article II does not independently bar its enforcement. Limits on historic equitable reinstatement coexisted with mandamus and declaratory remedies; precedent had also approved reinstatement injunctions. The dissent rejected reliance on the Civil Service Reform Act, which excludes principal officers like Slaughter, and noted that this theory had not been properly raised below or in the initial application. The majority's constitutional holding made it unnecessary to decide these alternative remedial questions in order to reverse. (Id. at 511–512 n. 13.)

## Course significance and source boundaries
Slaughter changes the doctrine of presidential control over independent commissions by removing Humphrey's Executor as a basis for FTC leadership's for-cause protection. It links removal to the character of the officer's power and makes the historical, structural, stare decisis, and institutional arguments central to the disagreement. Gorsuch and Sotomayor both recognize increased presidential control over delegated agency powers, while drawing different conclusions about the proper response. The reserved questions must be analyzed separately; the Class 11 supplement's editorial discussion of Trump v. Cook is not a merits holding in Slaughter.
