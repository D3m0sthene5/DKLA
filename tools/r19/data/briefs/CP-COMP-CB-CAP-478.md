# Grupo Mexicano de Desarrollo, S.A. v. Alliance Bond Fund, Inc.

**527 U.S. 308 (1999) · Supreme Court of the United States · June 17, 1999 · Civil Procedure**

By a 5–4 vote on the merits, the Court held that a federal district court lacked authority to freeze an unsecured debtor's assets before judgment in a suit seeking contract damages, where the plaintiffs claimed no lien or equitable interest in those assets. All nine Justices agreed that a potential recovery on the injunction bond kept the challenge alive. The Court reversed the Second Circuit and remanded. The assigned account is CivPro Note 4, physical PDF page 603, printed page 1193.

## The debt and the asset-preservation order

Grupo Mexicano de Desarrollo, a Mexican holding company involved in toll-road construction, issued $250 million in unsecured notes in 1994. Four subsidiaries guaranteed payment. The respondent investment funds held approximately $75 million of these notes, which ranked equally with other unsecured, unsubordinated debt. Economic problems undermined the toll-road program and left contractors unpaid. A Mexican government rescue program offered replacement notes for road concessions; Grupo expected approximately $309 million of those toll-road notes. **527 U.S. at 310–311.**

Grupo nevertheless faced serious financial distress and missed the August 1997 interest payment on the unsecured notes. Negotiations with creditors followed. Grupo announced transfers of rights to toll-road notes for employee compensation and to the Mexican government, apparently for taxes, while negotiations with the respondent funds failed. On December 11 the funds accelerated their debt; on December 12 they sued in federal court in New York, a forum to which Grupo had consented, demanding $80.9 million in contract damages. **Id. at 311–312.**

The funds alleged that Grupo was insolvent or approaching insolvency and was preferring Mexican creditors through disposition of its principal assets. They sought a freeze to preserve assets from which a future judgment might be collected. The district court entered a temporary order and then a preliminary injunction against transfers of the toll-road notes. It found near-certain success on the contract claim, irreparable injury, and a serious risk that dissipation would frustrate collection. It required the funds to post a $50,000 bond. The Second Circuit affirmed. **Id. at 312–313.**

While the interlocutory appeal proceeded, the district court granted the funds summary judgment, dismissed Grupo's counterclaims, ordered payment of approximately $82.44 million through assignment or transfer of toll-road assets, and converted the temporary restraint into a permanent injunction. Grupo ultimately abandoned its appeal from the permanent injunction, although its appeal from the payment order remained pending when the Court described the proceedings. **Id. at 313.**

## Why the preliminary-injunction challenge remained live

Justice Scalia's Part II spoke for a unanimous Court. Ordinarily, a permanent injunction supersedes a preliminary injunction, making an appeal from the preliminary order moot. Here, however, Grupo asserted a potential claim on the bond for harm caused by an unauthorized pre-judgment freeze. That claim concerned the court's power to impose a restraint before adjudicating the debt, rather than whether the funds eventually proved their contract claim. **Id. at 313–318 & n.1.**

Winning the contract suit did not necessarily show that the debtor's earlier asset dispositions were unlawful. A restraint designed to secure a future judgment could therefore be wrongful even if the eventual judgment and post-judgment injunction were valid. For the same reason, abandoning the permanent-injunction appeal did not forfeit the distinct challenge to the preliminary freeze. **Id. at 314–318.** Note 2 leaves room for a defense to a later bond claim if the note contract actually prohibited the restrained conduct. The Court preserved the possibility of recovery; it did not award bond damages or determine their amount. **Id. at 315–316 n.2.**

## The majority's historical limit on federal equity

On the merits, Scalia wrote for Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas. The federal equity power conferred by the Judiciary Act derives from the traditional principles and remedies of English Chancery at the founding. Rule 65 regulates injunction procedure; it does not independently enlarge the substantive equitable remedies available. **Id. at 318–319.**

The historical creditor's bill generally required a creditor first to obtain a judgment establishing the debt. An unsecured claimant had no cognizable interest in particular debtor property and could not interfere with the debtor's disposition of it simply because collection might later prove difficult. This requirement protected substantive property rights as well as enforcing the procedural exhaustion of legal remedies. Combining law and equity in one system did not eliminate that substantive protection. **Id. at 319–324 & n.6.**

The United States, supporting the funds, suggested possible historical exceptions. The majority declined to speculate about uncertain exceptions not analyzed by the lower courts or definitively applied by the parties. Notes 4 and 5 explain why insolvency or an asserted admitted debt did not establish an applicable exception here. The Court likewise did not decide the applicability of Rule 18(b), which had not been briefed, and note 7 expressly reserved the effect of state fraudulent-conveyance statutes because this case included no fraudulent-conveyance claim. **Id. at 320–324 & nn.4–7.**

The majority distinguished earlier asset-preservation cases. Deckert concerned equitable rescission and restitution, making interim relief related to the final equitable relief sought. First National City Bank involved a tax-enforcement statute, public interests, and a governmental equitable lien. De Beers warned against using an unrelated asset freeze to secure whatever decree a claimant might later obtain. The All Writs Act did not avoid the same inquiry into traditional equitable authority. **Id. at 324–327 & n.8.**

England's modern Mareva injunctions did not show that this remedy belonged to historical federal equity. Those freezes emerged in 1975 and represented a substantial departure from earlier practice; later statutory recognition did not transform them into a founding-era remedy. **Id. at 327–329 & nn.9–10.**

Finally, the majority treated expansion as a legislative policy choice. Asset freezes might protect enforceable judgments in an economy of mobile capital, but could also bypass state attachment requirements, encourage races among creditors, disrupt struggling businesses, and alter the balance embodied in bankruptcy and debtor-creditor law. The funds represented only some noteholders, not every creditor; note 11 disputes the dissent's suggestion that their requested relief necessarily benefited all creditors. The Court did not decide which policy arguments were ultimately stronger. It concluded that Congress should authorize a substantial new remedy of this kind. **Id. at 329–333 & n.11.**

## Ginsburg's partial concurrence and dissent

Justice Ginsburg, joined by Stevens, Souter, and Breyer, agreed with Part II's rejection of mootness but would have affirmed the preliminary injunction. Her concern was practical: compelling evidence established the debt, imminent asset dissipation, and the probable worthlessness of a money judgment without interim protection. The district judge had permitted bankruptcy proceedings and expressed willingness to modify the order to keep Grupo operating. The freeze created no security interest or priority among creditors. **Id. at 333–335 & nn.1–2.**

She read the founding-era grant as incorporating adaptable principles of equity rather than freezing the permissible remedies at eighteenth-century practice. Modern asset transfers could make an otherwise adequate damages remedy useless. In her view, a temporary restraint preserving effective adjudication was a modest application of equity's established function. Her precedent analysis and historical account therefore differed from the majority's, rather than supplying an exception the majority adopted. **Id. at 335–339 & nn.3–5.**

Ginsburg also emphasized safeguards: a strong merits showing, substantiated danger of noncollection, a bond compensating a wrongly restrained debtor, and limits matching the expected judgment. She disputed the majority's creditor-race account, noting the funds' proposals for broader creditor protection. Absent congressional restriction, she would allow this carefully controlled equitable remedy. **Id. at 339–342 & n.6.**

## Exact result and assigned-source qualification

The Court **reversed the Second Circuit and remanded**, holding this preliminary freeze beyond the district court's authority. It did not disturb the debt judgment in this interlocutory review or decide liability on the injunction bond. **Id. at 333.** Note 3 separately declined to consider a late-raised Erie argument about forum-state law. **Id. at 318 n.3.**

The textbook describes the freeze as pending adjudication of the plaintiffs' “right to a lien.” The actual case presents a damages claim in which **no lien or equitable interest was claimed**, a premise central to the majority's reasoning. The holding does not extend automatically to suits asserting equitable ownership, rescission, a lien, statutory asset-restraint authority, or the fraudulent-conveyance issue expressly reserved. **Assigned physical 603, printed 1193; actual 527 U.S. at 310, 324 n.7, 325–326, 333.**
