# Kremer v. Chemical Construction Corp.

**456 U.S. 461 (1982)** — May 17, 1982. Justice White wrote for a five-Justice majority joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justices Powell, Rehnquist, and O’Connor. Justice Blackmun dissented with Justices Brennan and Marshall; Justice Stevens separately dissented. Civil Procedure. Assigned: CivPro PDF 690, printed 1372, complete Note 3.

Rubin Kremer emigrated from Poland in 1970 and began working for Chemical Construction Corporation as an engineer in 1973. He was laid off with other employees in 1975. Some were subsequently rehired, but his repeated applications were unsuccessful. In May 1976, he charged the employer before the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission with discrimination based on national origin and his Jewish faith. Title VII required an initial opportunity for the competent state agency to address the matter, so the EEOC referred it to the New York State Division of Human Rights. His state complaint alleged age and religion, rather than separately pleading national origin. The agency investigated and found no probable cause: one rehired worker had greater seniority and another filled a lesser position than Kremer’s former job, and the agency rejected discrimination as the explanation for his exclusion. These were the agency’s findings, not fresh Supreme Court factfinding. (463–464 and nn.1–2.)

An administrative appeal upheld the decision as neither arbitrary nor capricious nor an abuse of discretion. Kremer then sought review in New York’s Appellate Division, which unanimously affirmed on February 27, 1978. He did not seek further review in the state Court of Appeals. The EEOC subsequently found no reasonable cause, issued a right-to-sue letter, and declined reconsideration. Kremer brought a federal Title VII suit asserting religion and national-origin discrimination. The district court initially distinguished existing circuit preclusion precedent because Title VII provided federal de novo adjudication. After a later circuit case extended that precedent to Title VII, it dismissed his case. The Second Circuit affirmed and rejected his objection to applying the intervening precedent retroactively. The Supreme Court reviewed and affirmed that judgment, rather than determining the discrimination facts through a new trial. (464–466 and nn.3–5.)

The question was whether Title VII displaced 28 U.S.C. §1738 sufficiently to permit relitigation after a state court upheld rejection of the same employment-discrimination grievance. Section 1738 requires federal courts to give a state judicial proceeding the preclusive effect it receives in that state’s own courts. New York law barred another action based on the same grievance after the completed state proceeding. Kremer argued both that Title VII guaranteed a fresh federal determination and that the earlier procedures or the issue actually resolved were inadequate to support preclusion. The Court addressed statutory displacement and procedural adequacy separately. (466–468.)

For the majority, the starting point was the longstanding congressional command, rather than a federal court’s independently preferred preclusion policy. There was no express repeal. Implied repeal required a clear legislative intention established by irreconcilable conflict or a later statute intended to replace the earlier scheme. Title VII’s limited deferral to state agencies, subsequent EEOC consideration, and opportunity for federal suit could coexist with finality for an intervening state judicial judgment. A claimant had to allow the agency an initial opportunity; Title VII did not require appealing an unfavorable agency determination to state court. The federal de novo trial recognized in earlier cases followed administrative proceedings, a different matter from a completed state-court judgment. (468–470.)

That distinction limits the holding. An **unreviewed state administrative determination** did not preclude a de novo Title VII suit, even if state law treated that administrative determination as conclusive. An adverse EEOC determination likewise did not foreclose the federal trial. Giving state findings substantial weight during the EEOC process did not mean Congress limited every later state judicial judgment to merely persuasive evidence. The majority read the 1972 substantial-weight amendment as increasing respect for state decisions, rather than implicitly repealing the judicial full-faith-and-credit obligation. (470–471 and nn.7–8.)

The Court examined the 1964 and 1972 legislative history in detail. It recognized concern about inadequate state discrimination remedies but found no clear intention to eliminate the ordinary effect of state judicial adjudication. The federal and state enforcement systems were meant to cooperate, while statements about avoiding repetitive litigation supported finality. Allen v. McCurry’s application of §1738 in federal civil-rights litigation reinforced this reading. Alexander v. Gardner-Denver did not resolve the question: a private arbitrator’s contractual ruling was outside §1738, and contractual arbitral competence differed from state authorities’ responsibility to apply antidiscrimination legislation. The majority also thought finality encouraged serious participation and effective state adjudication. (471–478.)

Kremer’s remaining objection concerned what had actually been determined and whether he had received a fair opportunity. Although the claims arose under different statutes, they challenged the same conduct and had virtually identical substantive elements. The majority understood the state affirmance as sustaining a determination that the discrimination grievance lacked merit. It did **not** require deciding definitively whether claim preclusion or issue preclusion supplied the precise label: at minimum, the same discrimination issue could not be relitigated. Note 22 expressly leaves that doctrinal choice open while explaining that either route required dismissal here. Note 4 explains that Kremer never argued his national-origin theory was distinct from his religion claim; failing to raise a matter within the prior proceeding’s scope would not independently avoid preclusion. The Court also reserved whether federal courts had exclusive Title VII jurisdiction. (465 n.4, 479 n.20, 479–482 and nn.21–22.)

Preclusion remained subject to due process. A constitutionally infirm judgment could not acquire binding force merely because a state purported to make it final. Applying §1738, however, federal courts could not demand their preferred procedural model beyond the minimum constitutional requirements. New York afforded an opportunity to present evidence and exhibits, rebut the employer’s evidence, obtain counsel and request subpoenas; a warranted probable-cause finding led to a public hearing, and administrative and judicial review protected procedural rights. A summary rejection required a sufficient investigation and a complaint lacking merit under the applicable state standards. The majority found the combined process constitutionally adequate. Kremer’s failure to use all available procedures did not establish their deficiency. This was not a finding that he actually received a full trial or that every administrative process necessarily satisfies due process. (480–485 and nn.21–26.)

Blackmun challenged the separation between agency decision and judicial affirmance. Title VII’s references to state proceedings encompassed administrative processing and its judicial review. Giving substantial weight to those proceedings preserved federal adjudication; making the affirmance conclusive effectively gave an agency rejection the very force the majority admitted it lacked on its own. Deferential review tested whether the agency acted rationally, not whether the employer actually discriminated. He read the legislative history as preserving overlapping remedies, without forcing claimants into exclusively state relief. Minimal due process was not equivalent to the vigorous federal enforcement Congress intended. (486–503.)

His policy objection was concrete: an unsuccessful claimant who skipped judicial review could pursue a federal trial, while one who tried to correct agency error through the state courts could lose that opportunity. He thought the rule discouraged state review, undermined the state system’s quality, and trapped unrepresented claimants. He distinguished a full state trial on the merits from deferential review of an administrative decision, rather than claiming that every prior state merits judgment must be ignored. These objections and the response to the majority’s federalism rationale occupy the dissent’s final sections and notes. (494–508, especially nn.10, 18–23.)

Stevens agreed that Title VII did **not** implicitly repeal §1738. He instead disputed what the state judgment settled. An equivalent de novo state trial would bar another suit, but an arbitrary-and-capricious affirmance established only that a rational adjudicator could support the agency decision. Accepting that proposition in federal court would still leave the discrimination merits open for trial. His extended starred note disputed the majority’s reading of New York cases: agency discretion and deferential review did not convert every dismissal into a judicial merits determination. His dissent thus rested on the limited character of the prior judgment, not a general rejection of full faith and credit. (508–511 and starred note.)

The Court **affirmed**, 5–4. The assigned note describes the majority’s result and Blackmun’s contrast between deferential review and federal merits adjudication. Its general reference to procedural quality should be read with the opinion’s specific constitutional minimum and pivotal state-judgment distinction, not as an unrestricted invitation to repeat any case whose procedures were less extensive than federal procedures.
