# Tolan v. Cotton

**572 U.S. 650 (2014) · Supreme Court of the United States · May 5, 2014**  
**Civil Procedure · Summary judgment, qualified immunity, and disputed factual context**

## Facts and the competing accounts

At approximately 2 a.m. on December 31, 2008, Officer John Edwards saw Robert Tolan and his cousin Anthony Cooper park a black Nissan sport utility vehicle outside the home where Tolan lived with his parents in Bellaire, Texas. Edwards entered the wrong license-plate character into his computer. The erroneous number matched a stolen vehicle of the same make and color, prompting an automatic notification to other police units. The actual vehicle belonged to the family. Edwards drew his pistol, accused the two men of stealing the vehicle, and ordered them to the ground. Tolan complied by lying facedown on the porch. 651–652.

Tolan’s parents came outside in their pajamas and tried to explain the mistake. His father identified the house, his son, and his nephew; his mother explained that the family owned the vehicle. After Edwards requested assistance, Sergeant Jeffrey Cotton arrived, drew his pistol, and was told the men had exited a stolen vehicle. Cotton directed Tolan’s mother toward the garage door. The evidence about the ensuing encounter was disputed. 652–653.

According to the mother, Cooper, and Tolan, Cotton pushed or slammed her against the garage, causing her to fall. Testimony and photographs supported bruising that lasted for days. Cotton described her pulling her arm up and objecting as he escorted her; he believed he had not caused bruises. Tolan testified that he rose to his knees when he saw his mother being pushed, while the officers said he rose to his feet. From approximately 15 to 20 feet away, he told Cotton, in profane terms, to take his hands off his mother. Cotton fired three shots; one entered Tolan’s chest, collapsed his right lung, and pierced his liver. Tolan survived with lasting pain and an injury that disrupted his developing baseball career. He and his mother testified that the shots came without a verbal warning. 653–654.

Those accounts supplied evidence, not a final judicial finding about everything that occurred. For summary judgment, the Supreme Court described the facts in the light most favorable to Tolan because he opposed Cotton’s motion. A jury’s ultimate assessment could differ.

## Procedural history and parties’ positions

In May 2009, Tolan, Cooper, and the parents sued under 42 U.S.C. §1983 in the Southern District of Texas. The issue before the Supreme Court concerned Cotton’s alleged excessive force against Tolan in violation of the Fourth Amendment. Dismissed equal-protection claims and the excessive-force claim concerning Tolan’s mother were not before the Court. Cotton had separately been indicted for aggravated assault by a public servant and acquitted; testimony from that criminal trial formed part of the civil record. The acquittal did not resolve the summary-judgment question in this civil action. 653 n.1; 654 & n.2.

After discovery, Cotton sought summary judgment on qualified-immunity grounds. The district court concluded that his force was reasonable and therefore did not violate the Fourth Amendment. The Fifth Circuit affirmed on a different ground: even assuming a constitutional violation, Cotton had not violated a clearly established right. It relied on the perceived threat to the officers from a dim porch, an agitated mother, Tolan’s allegedly threatening words, and his movement toward the encounter. Rehearing en banc was denied, with three judges voting to grant it. 654–655.

Tolan argued that the appellate court had resolved disputed evidence against him. He also objected that it analyzed the reasonableness of Cotton’s beliefs under the wrong prong of the qualified-immunity inquiry. The Supreme Court resolved only the summary-judgment error and expressly declined to decide that additional argument. 655 n.3.

## Issue, rule, and disposition

The issue was whether the Fifth Circuit could uphold summary judgment on the clearly-established-law prong using disputed factual propositions favorable to the moving officer. It could not. Under either prong of qualified immunity, courts must credit competent evidence of the nonmoving party and draw reasonable inferences in that party’s favor. The need to define the right in a specific factual setting does not permit a court to select a setting that rests on genuinely disputed facts resolved against the plaintiff. 655–657.

The Court **granted certiorari, summarily vacated the Fifth Circuit’s judgment, and remanded** for further proceedings. It also granted the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund’s motion to file an amicus brief. All nine Justices agreed with the judgment: the Court issued a per curiam opinion, and Alito, joined by Scalia, concurred in the judgment separately. The Court did **not** hold that Cotton violated clearly established law or award damages. 660–662.

## Reasoning: the legal inquiry depends on a properly constructed record

Qualified immunity ordinarily asks two questions: whether the facts, taken favorably to the person asserting injury, show a violation of a federal right; and whether that right was clearly established when the conduct occurred. In an excessive-force case, the constitutional question involves balancing the intrusion on Fourth Amendment interests against the asserted governmental justification. The second question concerns fair warning to a reasonable official. Courts may choose which prong to consider first. That flexibility does not relax the ordinary summary-judgment standard. 655–656.

Rule 56 permits judgment without trial only when there is no genuine issue of material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. A court determines whether a genuine dispute exists; it does not weigh evidence to determine whose account is true. Although clearly established law must be considered in the case’s specific context, that context must honor the plaintiff’s competent evidence and favorable reasonable inferences. 656–657.

The Court identified four concrete errors:

- **Lighting:** The Fifth Circuit accepted Cotton’s description of a fairly dark porch and a decorative gas lamp. It failed to credit the father’s contrary description, Tolan’s testimony that floodlights illuminated the driveway and that he was not in darkness, and Cotton’s acknowledgment of motion-activated lights. 657–658.
- **The mother’s demeanor:** The court relied on Cotton’s characterization of her as agitated and refusing to remain calm. Her testimony denied agitation. Repeatedly correcting the mistaken theft accusation did not conclusively establish the manner in which she did so. 658.
- **Threatening words:** The parties agreed on the words Tolan used, but their meaning and delivery were not fixed merely by the presence of profanity. He denied screaming. A jury could understand his statement as a plea to stop assaulting his mother rather than an intention to inflict harm. 658–659.
- **His movement:** The court relied on the officers’ description of a standing, charging position. Tolan testified that he remained on his knees, was not going anywhere, and did not jump up; his mother corroborated his position. The court could not resolve that dispute in Cotton’s favor. 659.

Together, these errors showed that the appellate court credited the moving party’s evidence and neglected contrary evidence on facts central to its analysis. The Court characterized the decision as a clear misapprehension of summary-judgment standards warranting intervention, rather than simply announcing new substantive excessive-force law. 659–660.

The remedy remained limited. The identified facts were not necessarily the only relevant considerations, and other facts might support a legal conclusion about reasonableness. On remand, the Fifth Circuit had to assess clearly established law using Tolan’s properly credited evidence and favorable reasonable inferences. The Supreme Court neither selected the ultimate winner on the constitutional claim nor declared that every factual dispute defeats qualified immunity. 660.

## Alito’s concurrence in the judgment

Alito, with Scalia, agreed that genuine issues of material fact made summary judgment inappropriate. He did not necessarily agree with every aspect of the Court’s description of the evidence. His main concern was the Court’s decision to review an ordinary question of whether a particular record contained enough evidence to avoid summary judgment. The Fifth Circuit had stated the correct standard; disagreements about its application were routine appellate matters. Taking such cases could substantially change the Supreme Court’s usual practice, reflected in Rule 10’s caution against certiorari for factual errors or misapplication of a correctly stated rule. He did not register a dissent from the grant of certiorari and concurred in the disposition. 661–662.
