# Ruxley Electronics & Construction Ltd. v. Forsyth
[1995] UKHL 8, [1995] 3 W.L.R. 118, [1996] A.C. 344 (H.L.) | Contracts

## Court, result, and source locators
On June 29, 1995, all five Law Lords—Keith of Kinkel, Bridge of Harwich, Jauncey of Tullichettle, Mustill, and Lloyd of Berwick—allowed Ruxley’s appeal and restored Judge Diamond QC’s judgment. Forsyth could not recover the £21,560 cost of demolishing and rebuilding his usable swimming pool merely because that was the only way to attain the specified depth. Restoration preserved the trial judge’s £2,500 loss-of-amenity award. The speeches differ in how fully they address that award: Jauncey reserved its propriety because the builder had not challenged it, while Bridge, Mustill, and Lloyd supplied affirmative reasons for recognizing such compensation. Locators below identify the numbered paragraphs in the preserved NADR reproduction, not purported original WLR paragraph numbers. (NADR ¶¶ 1, 6–10, 25–32, 87–90.)

## Transaction and litigation history
In autumn 1986, Stephen Forsyth contracted with Ruxley, trading as Home Counties Swimming Pools, for a pool adjoining his house in Kent. Lloyd states a pool price, including extras and VAT, of £17,797.40. The original specified depth was six feet six inches; Hall, who owned or controlled Ruxley, later agreed without extra charge to increase it to seven feet six inches. There was no provision for a diving board. Work began in June 1987, but defective subcontracted work caused the bottom to crack. Hall agreed to replace the pool without charge and reimburse professional charges. The replacement was completed in June 1988. Forsyth later demanded a £10,000 disturbance reduction, which Hall reluctantly agreed to, but still did not settle the invoice. (¶¶ 34–37.)

In March 1989, Forsyth discovered that the deep end was six feet nine inches rather than the promised seven feet six inches. He complained through his architect, including about corrosion. Ruxley did further work but remained unpaid and sued in January 1990 for a £10,330 balance. An associated enclosure company brought another action for its separate balance; that contract’s details were not the substantive concern of the appeal. Forsyth’s original and amended counterclaims listed defects and remedial costs without mentioning depth. He first raised depth during trial in May 1993, after dismissing his advisers and proceeding personally. (¶¶ 38–42.)

Judge Diamond found the pool safe for diving, no diminution in its value from the shortfall, no intention or likely future desire for a diving board, and no practicable way to obtain the specified depth short of total reconstruction for £21,560. He found reconstruction wholly disproportionate to the benefit and was not persuaded that Forsyth’s professed rebuilding intention would persist after litigation. The speeches refer to a depth of six feet at the relevant diving location as well as Lloyd’s six-foot-nine-inch deep-end measurement; they should not be reduced to a single inconsistent measurement. The judge rejected both a rebuilding award and Forsyth’s claim that an entire, uncompleted contract left him owing nothing: staged payments and substantial completion defeated that latter argument, which was not revived in the House. The judge awarded £2,500 for lost amenity and a separate £750 for general inconvenience. (¶¶ 9, 42–49.)

## Competing approaches on appeal
The Court of Appeal majority, Staughton and Mann LJJ, awarded reconstruction costs and set aside the amenity award; Dillon LJ dissented. Staughton considered reasonableness a mitigation principle that could not defeat the only remedy achieving the promised depth. Mann emphasized the bargain’s personal-preference objective. Dillon thought it illogical to make an expensive cure automatically recoverable simply because a much smaller loss of value happened to be zero. (¶¶ 10, 50–54.)

In the House, Forsyth’s counsel argued that cost of cure and diminution in value were the only permissible measures. Since the judge had recognized some real loss but diminution yielded nothing, reconstruction supposedly followed. He opposed the amenity award in principle as part of that argument. Ruxley emphasized both the unreasonableness of reconstruction and the lack of a genuine intention to perform it. The House rejected the forced choice between no compensation and excessive reconstruction costs. (¶¶ 4–7, 15, 55–56.)

## Reasonableness and the loss actually suffered
Jauncey linked reasonableness directly to identifying the loss rather than treating it merely as a choice among remedies after loss was conclusively established. A usable pool falling short of one specification did not necessarily produce the loss associated with receiving no usable pool at all. Reconstruction costs are often the proper measure for defective building work, but the required work must be both necessary to produce conformity and reasonable to undertake. The trial finding that demolition was unreasonable was justified; it meant this loss did not extend to the full cost of reinstatement. Lloyd likewise held that absence of a cheaper physical cure did not make disproportionate expenditure reasonable. (¶¶ 13–25, 58–72, 89–90.)

Neither speaker treated the owner's preferences as irrelevant or allowed contractors simply to substitute their own taste. Reasonableness concerns the particular contract. A collapsed ornamental folly might justify rebuilding even though it adds nothing to market value. Personal preference is therefore a factor, but does not create a special class exempt from reasonableness. Here the judge had considered Forsyth’s preferences and nevertheless found reconstruction disproportionate; the appellate majority should not have displaced that factual assessment. (¶¶ 21–25, 73–75.)

Intention to reconstruct could bear on whether the claimed cost represented a genuine loss and on the reasonableness of that measure. It did not give the defendant continuing control over how an established damages award was spent. Jauncey declined to decide whether a rebuilding intention was always required. Lloyd concluded that Forsyth’s later undertaking to spend a recovery on reconstruction could not create a loss that otherwise did not exist. This distinction answers the casebook’s intention variation without making every damages recipient prove eventual expenditure. (¶¶ 26, 79–83.)

## Amenity and the separate speeches
Mustill rejected the premise that there were two exhaustive and opposing measures. The governing aim was compensation for true loss. A promise can matter to a householder beyond the financial enhancement measurable on sale: comfort, convenience, and personal tastes may generate a genuine nonmonetary benefit. He described this as consumer surplus and held that its imperfect monetary precision was not a reason to erase it. Recognizing that intermediate loss avoided both undercompensation and an unjustified windfall. (¶¶ 28–32.)

Bridge agreed with the principal speeches, explained the flaw in converting an intangible disappointment into reconstruction costs not incurred, and endorsed the power to award amenity damages despite the absence of a cross-appeal against their amount. Keith agreed with Jauncey, Mustill, and Lloyd. Jauncey found it unnecessary to express a view on the £2,500 award. Lloyd justified it as an application or adaptation of the established exception for contracts affording pleasure; he thought the amount generous but unchallenged in quantum. He expressly left unanswered a broader question about modest compensation for disappointed expectations in ordinary, non-amenity building cases. The decision is therefore not an unrestricted award of emotional-distress damages for every contractual breach. (¶¶ 1–8, 27, 84–89.)

## Assigned problem and reading limits
The full assigned problem “The Deep End,” physical 315–316, printed 972–973, changes the actual transaction into a $100,000 fully paid pool, seven-foot-nine-inch specification, six-foot-six-inch performance, unexpected rock, $180,000 cure, and a $10,000 jury amenity award with both parties appealing. Those are pedagogical variations, not historical findings of this bench-tried case. Its further $50,000 cost-saving question is also hypothetical; the House did not find that saving or award disgorgement. The actual case’s reasonableness and amenity reasoning informs the variations without supplying findings or a procedural disposition the House never made.
