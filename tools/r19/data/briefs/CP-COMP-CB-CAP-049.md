# Ratliff v. Cooper Laboratories, Inc.

**Civil Procedure · CP-COMP-CB-CAP-049**  
**444 F.2d 745 (4th Cir. 1971)** · June 29, 1971 · No. 14788  
**Craven, Circuit Judge, for a panel comprising Winter, Craven, and District Judge Miller. No separate writing is reported.**  
**Assigned:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 123, printed page 196, the Ratliff account under “Consent by Registration in State.” The reported decision also resolves Nichols v. Sterling Drug Company in the same consolidated appeal.

## Two drug claims, two different sets of forum activities

Annie and Henry Ratliff, Florida residents, sued Cooper Laboratories over injury allegedly caused by drugs Annie purchased in Florida and did not consume in South Carolina. Cooper was incorporated in Delaware and headquartered in Connecticut. Its South Carolina activities consisted of mailed solicitation to dealers and wholesalers and promotional mailings to approximately 650 physicians. CAP opinion paragraphs 3–4.

Alice Nichols, an Indiana resident, sued Sterling Drug over a drug manufactured in New York. The parties stipulated that purchase, consumption, injury, treatment, and hospitalization occurred in Indiana. Sterling was incorporated in Delaware, headquartered in New York, and had more South Carolina activity than Cooper. It obtained authority to do business there, appointed a service agent, and employed five resident promotional representatives called detail men. They contacted doctors and drugstores and occasionally took orders, but their primary function was promotion rather than sales. Id. ¶ 5.

These distinctions matter because the abbreviated textbook account can sound as if one manufacturer combined all the described conduct and faced both families' claims. The actual opinion assigns registration, the service agent, and the five detail men to Sterling, the defendant in Nichols's companion case. Cooper's activities were principally mailings. Neither injury-producing drug was manufactured or consumed in South Carolina. Id. ¶¶ 3–5.

The plaintiffs selected South Carolina solely for its six-year limitations period. The opinion says the periods had expired in every other state connected to the claims: Florida, Indiana, New York, Connecticut, and Delaware. Neither plaintiff had another connection with South Carolina. Thus, the forum-selection incentive did not establish a connection between the particular injuries and the defendants' South Carolina activities. Id. ¶ 6.

## Procedure and the question presented

The South Carolina federal district court denied the companies' motions to set aside service and dismiss the complaints. The Fourth Circuit heard a consolidated interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. §1292(b). Cooper had been served under the state long-arm procedure, and Sterling through its appointed state agent. The procedural sufficiency of those acts of service was not disputed; the antecedent issue was whether the court possessed adjudicatory power over these out-of-state claims. Id. ¶¶ 2, 7.

South Carolina's service law had been construed to reach the outer limits allowed by International Shoe. The panel therefore concentrated on constitutional amenability: were the companies' forum activities extensive enough to permit adjudication of claims brought by nonresidents, arising elsewhere, and unconnected with those activities? The companies additionally relied on South Carolina's separate “door-closing” statute restricting some claims by nonresidents. The jurisdictional ruling made it unnecessary to decide that statutory defense. Id. ¶¶ 11, 19.

## Holding and the relationship between service and power

The panel held that these forum activities did not supply sufficient contacts for the unrelated claims and **reversed**. Both motions to set aside service and dismiss should have been granted. The ruling concerned the court's power to hear the claims, not whether either drug was defective or caused compensable injury. Id. ¶¶ 18–20.

Compliance with service procedures did not itself establish constitutional jurisdiction. The panel applied International Shoe's inquiry into the quality and nature of the activities in relation to fair administration of the laws, rather than relying on a mechanical count of visits or on a corporation's supposed physical presence. Expanding interstate business did not eliminate jurisdictional limits. Id. ¶¶ 8–13.

The opinion's third judicial note explains why this diversity court used limits governing state jurisdiction. The incorporation of state law into federal amenability had generally been understood to include constitutional restraints on the state's authority. It also distinguishes the state-law decision to authorize jurisdiction from the later federal constitutional inquiry; a state was not compelled to exercise every power constitutionally available. Here, because the state statute reached the constitutional boundary, that second inquiry controlled. Id. concluding note 3.

## Why these contacts were insufficient

The claims lacked a rational connection to South Carolina. For an injury unconnected with forum conduct, the panel required other contacts extensive enough to justify the burden of defending there. Neither company maintained an office, warehouse, bank account, or directory advertising in the state. Neither held property there except the samples and materials used by Sterling's promotional employees. National medical-journal advertisements reaching South Carolina did not transform those activities into the substantial forum relationship needed for these claims. Id. ¶¶ 14–15.

Sterling's registration and service-agent appointment did not change the result. The panel distinguished obtaining permission to do business from actually exercising that privilege to a constitutionally sufficient degree. In this factual setting, mere compliance with the domestication statute carried no special weight and the five representatives did not tip the balance. The opinion used this reasoning together with the full forum-activity record; it did not decide that the content of every possible registration statute, or every form of expressly given consent, must produce the same result. Id. ¶ 15.

The panel found persuasive the First Circuit's similar Seymour case. Its discussion distinguished an enterprise that had made the forum a major business center or substantially entered its business life from the more limited pattern of advertising and sales solicitation at issue. The similarity was not merely that the plaintiffs hoped to benefit from a longer limitations period: both cases involved unrelated out-of-state claims and inadequate corporate forum relationships. Id. ¶¶ 16–17.

## Exact judgment, assignment, and historical limits

The decision ends **“Reversed.”** It says the jurisdictional dismissal motions should have been granted and expressly leaves the door-closing statute undecided. The panel report contains no separate concurrence or dissent. It does not adjudicate the plaintiffs' tort claims on their merits. Id. ¶¶ 18–20.

The textbook places Ratliff in a later debate about consent through corporate registration, referring to decisions and questions well after 1971. That surrounding commentary is distinct from this panel's historical analysis. The brief preserves the actual consolidated identities and does not convert the excerpt into a current blanket rule for all registered corporations.
