# Tatman v. Collins
938 F.2d 509 (4th Cir. 1991) | Civil Procedure | Using a discovery deposition at trial

## The missing physician and the causation problem

Monte Tatman's car was struck from behind by a tractor-trailer driven by Bobby Wayne Collins near Winfield, West Virginia, on April 15, 1986. Dr. Joseph Amico, practicing in Columbus, Ohio, treated Tatman's neck, back, shoulder, and head complaints. Almost a year later, Tatman suffered a cerebral aneurysm and died when it ruptured on March 12, 1987. His wife Rebecca, individually and as administratrix, sued Collins and his employer in the Southern District of West Virginia. She alleged that the collision caused blood-pressure fluctuations that led to rupture of the aneurysm. That causal chain was contested; the appellate opinion does not establish it as medical fact. 938 F.2d at 510.

Defendants deposed Amico during discovery. He testified about treatment and expressed an opinion linking the accident to the subsequent rupture. A scheduling conflict prevented him from attending trial in Charleston, so the plaintiff sought to introduce his deposition. The trial court excluded it for two reasons: defendants had taken it early for discovery rather than trial use, and Columbus was within one hundred miles of the district's northern boundary. Columbus was nevertheless more than one hundred miles from the Charleston courthouse. Id.

The ruling affected more than one witness. Dr. Cyril Wecht, the plaintiff's other causation expert, relied on Amico's testimony. The court struck Wecht's testimony after excluding the deposition and directed a verdict for defendants on the wrongful-death claim. A jury separately found Collins negligent but found no injury caused by the collision on the survivor's claim. The plaintiff appealed the exclusion and resulting adverse disposition. Id.

## Rule 32 did not create a separate discovery category

Judge Niemeyer rejected a distinction between a “discovery” deposition and one taken *de bene esse* for use as trial evidence. Rule 32 governed use of depositions without making admissibility depend on the initiating party's purpose. The historical discussion from *United States v. IBM* explained that earlier language authorizing depositions for discovery, evidence, or both did not create separate admissibility rules; the 1970 revision removed that potentially confusing language. Id. at 510-511.

When a deposition was duly noticed, parties had the opportunity to attend and did attend, and the witness was unavailable within the circumstances described by then-Rule 32(a)(3), the deposition could be used for any purpose subject to the rules of evidence. It did not matter which party initiated it, whether that party intended discovery only, or whether other discovery remained incomplete. Counsel could address the deposition's timing and format through examination, objections, motions, and appropriate stipulations under the procedural rules. Id. at 511.

That conclusion establishes eligibility under Rule 32, not mandatory admission of everything in a transcript. The district court retained substantial discretion to apply the rules of evidence. It could not, however, exclude testimony merely because defendants expected a discovery deposition to remain outside trial. The difference between procedural eligibility and evidentiary admissibility is central to both the majority and dissent. Id.

## One hundred miles from the courthouse

Then-Rule 32(a)(3)(B) referred to a witness more than one hundred miles from the “place of trial.” The majority held that this meant the courthouse where trial occurred. Measuring from district borders would make the standard of convenience vary with the size and shape of a district and with the location of the witness and trial. The rule's text supplied a fixed trial location instead. Id. at 511-512.

The court contrasted the historical subpoena provision of Rule 45(e), which expressly distinguished the district from the distance around the place of trial. That distinction reinforced the conclusion that the place of trial was a particular locus, not the entire district. Other decisions also used “courthouse” when describing Rule 32's distance requirement. These references describe the rules discussed in this 1991 decision; their old subsection numbers should not be treated as current rule numbering. Id.

Amico was beyond one hundred miles from the Charleston courthouse, and there was no showing that a party had procured his absence. His professional commitment prevented attendance. His location within one hundred miles of a district boundary therefore supplied no proper ground for exclusion. The court did not announce that every scheduling conflict, standing alone, automatically permits deposition use; the distance condition and absence of procurement were the relevant facts here. Id. at 512.

## Judge Hall agreed with the rules but would affirm

Judge K. K. Hall dissented from the result while expressly agreeing with both legal propositions: discovery depositions were not categorically excluded, and the trial court had misstated the one-hundred-mile rule. He emphasized the integrated pretrial order. The plaintiff had listed Amico as a witness but had not disclosed an intention to offer his deposition, leaving defendants without notice of that intended use. In Hall's view, the court could prevent unfair last-minute surprise through its authority over evidence and case management. Rule 32 permitted admission without invariably requiring it. Id. at 512.

The complete dissent, largely omitted from the assignment, also questioned the expert evidence's foundation. The parties agreed the aneurysm itself was congenital. Hall stressed Tatman's preexisting hypertension, obesity, and the limited support for a theory that the collision caused the later rupture. Amico had not treated the high blood pressure at issue, did not identify examples or significance of the alleged fluctuations, and did not know the medication being taken. Wecht lacked important medical records and information about other sources of stress. Hall considered Rules of Evidence 702 and 403 pertinent to whether expert testimony would assist the jury and whether its apparent authority might overstate weak support. Id. at 512-513.

The dissent's sole footnote adds its account of the impact: $55.28 in vehicle damage and defense reconstruction testimony comparing the force with ordinary braking in the opposite direction. Those details supported **Hall's reasoning**; they should not be silently attributed to the majority as an adjudication of causation. Hall would have affirmed because he considered the admission ruling within permissible discretion and the causation evidence insufficient even with the proposed experts. Id. at 513 & dissenting note.

## Judgment and coverage

The majority recognized that the deposition might have been excluded on other proper grounds, but the grounds actually given were erroneous. Because those errors also removed Wecht's critical testimony, the court **reversed and remanded for a new trial of both the wrongful-death and survivor's claims**. This was a 2-1 panel decision, with Niemeyer writing for the majority and Hall dissenting. The plaintiff obtained another opportunity to litigate, not a finding that the collision caused death or an award of damages. Id. at 512.
