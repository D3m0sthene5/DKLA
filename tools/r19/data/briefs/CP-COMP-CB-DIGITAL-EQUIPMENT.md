# Digital Equipment Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc.

**511 U.S. 863 (1994) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 6, 1994. Civil Procedure.** Justice Souter delivered a unanimous opinion. Refusal to enforce a private settlement asserted to protect a party from suit did not permit an immediate collateral-order appeal under § 1291.

## Facts and procedural history

Desktop Direct sold computers and similar equipment under that trade name. Digital operated in the same field and began marketing a service as “Desktop Direct from Digital” in late 1991. Desktop sued in federal court in Utah, alleging unlawful use of its name. After negotiations between corporate officers, the parties reached a confidential settlement on March 25, 1992. Digital agreed to pay for rights to the name and trademark, waiver of damages, and dismissal. Desktop filed its notice of dismissal that day. 511 U.S. at 865–866.

Months later, Desktop requested that the dismissal be vacated and the settlement rescinded, alleging material misrepresentation during negotiations. The district court granted the motion, stating that a factfinder could determine that Digital had failed to disclose material facts that would have caused Desktop to reject the offer. That formulation did not establish a final Supreme Court finding of fraud. The district court denied reconsideration and a stay, and Digital appealed. Id. at 866.

The Tenth Circuit dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. It concluded that the order neither ended the merits litigation nor qualified as an appealable collateral order. Although Digital described its contractual benefit as a right not to stand trial, the circuit found that private entitlement insufficiently important to justify immediate review, distinguishing immunities rooted in public law or compelling public policy. The Supreme Court granted review to resolve conflicting circuit approaches. It had granted a stay pending disposition of the certiorari petition after the circuit denied a stay. Id. at 866–867 & n. 1.

## Issue, holding, and reservations

Does a settlement’s asserted protection against suit make an order refusing to enforce it immediately appealable? No. Rights under this category of private settlements are adequately vindicable after final judgment and through other mechanisms. Their claimed freedom from trial does not have the significance needed to displace the final-judgment rule. Id. at 869, 878–884.

The Court did not resolve whether this order was conclusive or separate from the merits. The circuit had accepted those elements, but the Supreme Court noted possible doubts and left them untouched because failure of the effective-review requirement sufficed. Nor did it decide whether Digital’s settlement actually conferred the precise immunity it asserted, whether rescission was substantively correct, or whether a privately conferred right could never support any collateral appeal. Id. at 868–869 & nn. 2–3, 876–879.

## Finality and categorical decisionmaking

Cohen provides a practical construction of § 1291, rather than a license to evade it. The narrow collateral category requires conclusiveness, an important question wholly separate from the merits, and effective unreviewability after final judgment. Most errors must be presented in one appeal at the end of litigation. Appealability concerns the category of orders, not a court’s estimate that immediate review might avert injustice or save expense in the individual dispute. Id. at 867–868.

Footnote 3 confirms that the requirements concern appellate subject-matter jurisdiction, so party agreement cannot dispense with an element. The Court’s decision not to examine all elements here followed from the dispositive failure of one; it did not convert jurisdiction into the parties’ choice. Id. at 869 n. 3.

## Why describing a right against trial was insufficient

Digital relied on double-jeopardy and governmental-immunity cases recognizing that certain rights would be lost by the trial itself. It argued that its settlement similarly protected both against liability and against being tried. The Court rejected the proposition that labeling an interest a right not to stand trial necessarily established collateral jurisdiction. Almost any potentially valid pretrial dismissal argument—including limitations, claim preclusion, personal jurisdiction, or failure to state a claim—could be framed that way. If the characterization alone controlled, the statutory rule would disappear. Id. at 869–875.

Ordinary appeals cannot undo every cost, disclosure, or burden of trial. Even erroneous rulings that effectively end a claim or impose irreparable practical costs often remain unappealable until final judgment. The relevant inquiry therefore requires closer examination of the protection’s nature and value, rather than whether history can literally be undone. Id. at 871–874.

Digital emphasized that this particular agreement expressly conferred immunity. The Court declined to base a categorical appeal rule on how clearly one settlement was drafted. Almost every settlement could include some freedom-from-trial aspect, and parties could readily add explicit immunity language if it manufactured appellate jurisdiction. The Court assessed the asserted category on the assumption that accepting Digital’s position would permit immediate appeals whenever a court denied effect to a settlement. Id. at 875–877.

Footnote 6 also rejected the significance of Digital’s contention that the district court used the wrong legal standard. A claimed legal error did not make the protected interest more irretrievable than a factual or ordinary application error. Nor did allocation of a high settlement price to dismissal and a damages waiver prove the special importance of avoiding trial, especially given accounting and business reasons for contractual pricing. Id. at 877 n. 6.

## Importance, later remedies, and express legislative choices

Digital argued that importance was irrelevant to finality. The Court answered that Cohen itself requires it, and that effective review cannot be evaluated without considering the value of what delay would lose. Constitutional and statutory immunities embody public judgments about protection from litigation; an agreement between private parties does not automatically outweigh the public policies served by finality. The Court did not need to rule out every private-right basis for collateral review. Id. at 878–880.

Footnote 7 particularly distinguishes Congress’s express authorization of appeals concerning arbitration under 9 U.S.C. § 16. Courts must honor that legislative decision, but it does not license them to create a parallel appeal category for settlement agreements. The general public policy favoring settlements also did not persuade the Court that postponing review would materially undermine willingness to settle. Id. at 880–881 & nn. 7–8.

Even apart from importance, settlement rights usually remain vindicable later. Limiting liability is generally a central settlement benefit and can be protected through final-judgment review. A party with a special confidentiality concern could seek trial-court protection. Digital’s asserted promise not to sue could also support a state-law contract remedy, while an improper attempt to rescind might be addressed through Rule 11. These were potential remedies, not findings that Desktop owed damages or deserved sanctions. Id. at 881–883.

A serious legal question about enforcement could also support certification and discretionary appeal under § 1292(b). Footnote 9 recognizes that this is no universal remedy, since both certification and appellate acceptance matter, but rejects the concern that requesting it would prejudice an otherwise valid claim to appeal under § 1291. Id. at 883–884 & n. 9.

## Judgment and assigned reading

The Supreme Court **affirmed the Tenth Circuit’s jurisdictional dismissal**. It did not adjudicate trademark liability or affirm rescission on its merits. Id. at 884.
