# Thiel v. Southern Pacific Co.

**328 U.S. 217 (1946) — Supreme Court of the United States, May 20, 1946.** Justice Murphy wrote for the Court; Frankfurter dissented, joined by Reed. Jackson did not participate. The report does not state a numerical majority tally. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical 556, printed 1095–1096, with all five attached editorial notes completed on physical 557, printed 1097.

The Court reversed a railroad’s favorable negligence judgment because federal jury officials intentionally excluded everyone paid by the day from the jury lists. Individual financial hardship could justify excusing a particular prospective juror; it did not justify excluding an entire wage category without investigating actual hardship. The Court acted through its supervisory authority over federal judicial administration, without requiring proof that the litigant belonged to the excluded class or that the exclusion prejudiced his case. 328 U.S. at 224–225.

## Facts and procedural history

Thiel was a passenger who jumped from the window of a moving Southern Pacific train. He sued in California state court, alleging that railroad personnel knew he was not in his normal mental condition and should either have refused him passage or guarded him. He also alleged failure to stop the train before he fell. Those are the allegations underlying the negligence suit, not Supreme Court findings that the railroad was liable. Southern Pacific, a Kentucky corporation, removed the action to federal district court in San Francisco on diversity grounds. Thiel unsuccessfully sought remand and was enjoined from proceeding in state court; the earlier injunction appeal is recorded in the first footnote. Id. at 218–219 & n.1.

After demanding a jury, Thiel moved to strike the entire panel. He alleged selection favoring business executives and employers while excluding employees and poorer citizens. The court heard testimony and denied the motion. Thiel attempted to withdraw his jury demand, but the railroad refused consent. After twelve jurors were selected, he challenged them again on the panel-selection grounds and additionally asserted connections between six jurors and the railroad. That challenge failed, and the jury returned a defense verdict. Id. at 219.

In denying postverdict relief, the district judge found at least five jurors closely associated with the working or employee class and potentially sympathetic to its experiences. The Ninth Circuit affirmed. Supreme Court review was expressly limited to whether the motion to strike the panel had been properly denied; the Court did not undertake a merits review of the negligence verdict or resolve the alleged individual railroad affiliations. Id. at 219–220.

## What the selection officers admitted

The clerk and jury commissioner testified that they deliberately omitted all people working for daily wages. Using city-directory occupational entries, the clerk would leave out a longshoreman or other apparent day laborer because experience suggested that such a person would seek exemption rather than serve for the $4 daily jury payment. The commissioner similarly excluded ironworkers, bricklayers, carpenters, and machinists, explaining that judges generally accepted financial-hardship excuses from them. Id. at 221–222.

Weekly and monthly wage earners, and wives of daily wage earners, were included. Businessmen and their wives comprised at least half the lists, although the officers denied consciously choosing by wealth or occupation. The Court therefore identified the admitted discriminatory practice precisely: exclusion of daily wage earners. It did not find that every employee or all poor people had been omitted, or that the officials had proved a scheme to secure victory for this railroad. Id. at 222.

## Rule and holding

Civil as well as criminal jury traditions contemplate an impartial jury drawn from a cross-section of the community. That principle does not require each particular jury to contain representatives of every economic, racial, religious, political, social, or geographic group. It does forbid officials from systematically and intentionally excluding eligible groups from the source of prospective jurors. Competence belongs to individuals, rather than entire economic classes. Id. at 220.

The pay period bore no relevant relationship to jury eligibility or ability. A daily wage earner could be as competent as a person paid weekly or monthly. Neither the then-applicable federal statutes nor California’s qualification and exemption provisions authorized the categorical exclusion. Although federal selection generally drew on state qualifications and exemptions, an unlawful state qualification would not become acceptable merely because federal officials borrowed it. Id. at 221–223 & n.2.

The Court accordingly held that the panel should have been struck. Its remedy was reversal and a new trial with a properly selected panel. Its stated basis was supervision of the administration of justice in federal courts, rather than an adjudication requiring particularized prejudice under a constitutional test. Id. at 225.

## Why hardship did not validate the blanket practice

The Court accepted the practical burden created by low jury pay. The fourth footnote reproduces a judicial-administration discussion explaining that long periods of service at $4 daily could impose extreme hardship. But the selection officers had made no effort to determine which individual day workers actually would experience undue financial difficulty. They had eliminated the whole category in advance. Id. at 224 & n.4.

The distinction was between individualized hardship and presumed inability based on a pay classification. Jury service was a civic duty as well as a privilege, so lower earnings or inconvenience did not automatically excuse a person. A real burden could justify release; benevolent motives or familiarity with prior excusals could not justify treating every prospective daily worker alike. Otherwise the jury system could become an institution of economic privilege even without an openly hostile purpose. Id. at 223–225.

The presence of at least five laboring-class jurors did not cure the source-list defect. Nor did Thiel have to prove membership in the omitted class or demonstrate that the verdict was tainted by the omission. The Court’s supervisory response addressed the wholesale exclusion itself and protected the selection system’s standards. Those reservations are express; the opinion does not find that Thiel actually suffered economic-class prejudice on the merits. Id. at 225.

## Frankfurter’s dissent, joined by Reed

Frankfurter agreed that jury pools should broadly represent the community and that economic, racial, or religious prejudice was unacceptable. He regarded this case, however, as judicial administration rather than a constitutional controversy. In his view, the lower courts had upheld an otherwise sound verdict and no case-specific discriminatory manipulation had been sustained. The challenged longstanding practice was designed to relieve people especially vulnerable to lost daily income, not to favor the propertied. Id. at 225–229.

He emphasized the inclusion of weekly and monthly workers and daily workers’ wives. From that record he rejected the inference of designed economic bias and doubted that daily workers necessarily possessed a distinct outlook affecting their verdicts. He compared recognized occupational exemptions and argued that judges needed substantial room to balance representation, hardship, and competing public obligations. Id. at 227–230.

Frankfurter also gave a detailed institutional argument. The Judicial Conference had already studied jury administration, appointed a committee, developed legislative proposals, and held hearings addressing pay and service burdens. The problem warranted comprehensive administrative and legislative adjustment, rather than invalidation of a judgment he considered intrinsically untainted. An admonition or coordinated Conference action could improve future practice without upsetting this verdict. He would affirm. Id. at 231–234.
