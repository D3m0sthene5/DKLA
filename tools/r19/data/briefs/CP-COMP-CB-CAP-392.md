# McLean Contracting Co. v. Waterman Steamship Corp.

**Citation:** 277 F.3d 477 (4th Cir. 2002). **Court/date:** Fourth Circuit, January 14, 2002. **Opinion:** Williams, joined by Wilkinson and Gregory. **Course:** Civil Procedure—pretrial waiver and proof burdens. **Assigned location:** CivPro.pdf, physical pages 511–512, printed page 1002, Note 2.

## Facts

McLean was replacing the Newport River Railroad Bridge at Morehead City, North Carolina. Waterman’s vessel discharged cargo barges there on August 22, 1998, including CG-5151. Waterman hired James River Towing to supply tugs and labor and Captain Glander to oversee securing the barges. During Hurricane Bonnie on August 27, CG-5151 broke loose and struck the bridge. McLean repaired the damage, incurring approximately $17,562.33 in out-of-pocket expenses. It brought this maritime claim against Waterman in the Eastern District of Virginia. (478–479.)

## Procedural posture

The parties consented to trial before a magistrate judge under 28 U.S.C. § 636(c)(1). A final pretrial order entered February 23, 2001 identified trial issues and factual contentions. Waterman stipulated that it operated CG-5151. Its disputed issues concerned McLean’s standing, economic-loss recovery, an act-of-God defense, and reasonable securing of the barges. The order did not dispute agency or identify the towing company or Glander as independently responsible. (478 n.1, 479–480.)

A few days before trial, Waterman’s proposed findings and conclusions first asserted that it could not be liable for those parties because they were independent contractors. On McLean’s objection, the magistrate excluded evidence supporting that defense. Following a bench trial, judgment favored McLean. Waterman appealed both the exclusion and application of the maritime presumption of fault. (479–481.)

## Issues and holdings

**Pretrial exclusion:** Was it an abuse of discretion to bar the independent-contractor defense omitted from the pretrial order? **No.** Waterman knew agency was integral to McLean’s liability theory and failed to identify it as disputed. (479–480.)

**Maritime liability:** Could the court apply the presumption of fault to Waterman on this record? **Yes.** After proper exclusion of the unpreserved defense, the magistrate reasonably assumed the parties securing the barge were Waterman’s agents and could impute their fault to it. (480–481.)

## Rule and reasoning

The burden to identify an issue for trial differs from the burden to prove it once disputed. McLean ordinarily would bear the burden of proving agency. Requiring Waterman to disclose that it disputed agency did not shift that evidentiary burden. It instead served Rule 16’s function of defining the actual controversy so parties and court could prepare efficiently. A party that fails to identify a trial issue at the pretrial conference or in the order can waive its right to litigate that issue. The court examined the order as a whole, rather than finding waiver from one isolated omission. (479–480.)

Here, the pleadings and pretrial order made McLean’s imputed-liability theory clear, and Waterman conceded its awareness. Listing other issues and stipulating operation without disputing agency supported exclusion. The court reviewed that decision for abuse of discretion. Importantly, Waterman never sought modification of the pretrial order; whether a requested modification would have been proper was expressly reserved. (480 & n.2.)

Under The Oregon presumption, a moving vessel striking a stationary, visible object is presumed at fault. Waterman supplied no evidence identifying why its barge escaped its moorings. The presumption does not ordinarily impose personal liability on a barge owner for independent contractors’ fault. Waterman was correct about that rule, but could not use it to revive its waived agency defense. Personal liability can arise from agents’ maritime torts. An established act-of-God cause could also defeat the presumption; the magistrate rejected that defense, and Waterman did not appeal that finding. (480–481 & n.3.)

## Exact disposition and study significance

**Affirm the magistrate judge’s judgment for McLean.** There was no remand, and the appellate court did not decide whether an independent-contractor relationship would have been proved if its evidence had been admitted. The textbook accurately presents pretrial waiver; the full opinion supplies the distinction between disclosure and proof, the never-requested modification reservation, and the second admiralty ground for affirmance. (481.)
