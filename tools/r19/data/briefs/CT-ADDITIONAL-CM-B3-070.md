# Guerini Stone Company v. P. J. Carlin Construction Company
248 U.S. 334 (1919) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Justice Pitney delivered the Supreme Court’s January 7, 1919 opinion on the second review of this construction dispute. Following the Court’s earlier reversal, a new trial had produced a substantial damages verdict for subcontractor Guerini. The First Circuit then reversed for asserted instructional and evidentiary errors. The Supreme Court rejected those grounds, examined the remaining assignments, reversed the First Circuit judgment, and affirmed the district court judgment for Guerini. The report contains no separate writing or numerical voting statement. The result restores the trial judgment; it does not remand for another trial. (334, 336–337, 349.)

## Contract and events leading to termination
Carlin was the federal government’s general contractor for a post office and court building at San Juan, Puerto Rico. Carlin would complete the foundation to basement level; Guerini would build the principal concrete structure, including walls, floors, and roof, for $64,750. Carlin also exercised an option for Guerini to set supplied granite at forty cents per square foot. Paragraph 12 required monthly payments of up to eighty-five percent of the cost of work erected, upon a prescribed written requisition at least twelve days before payment was required. The contract did not specify how the cost of the non-granite work would be measured. (337–338.)

Guerini submitted unpaid requisitions in December 1911 and January 1912. Its evidence was that the parties agreed around February 2 to a unit-price schedule, including $1.07 per cubic foot for concrete walls, arches, and cement work. On March 9 it submitted a requisition showing $11,735.95 due. Except for a later $674 payment, that demand went unpaid. Meanwhile, the government found serious settlement of the foundation and ordered construction stopped. Carlin communicated the shutdown to Guerini on March 9 and confirmed it by letter two days later, without answering Guerini’s request for instructions concerning its work force. (338.)

By May 22, more than two months later, Guerini had received neither meaningful further payment nor permission to resume, and its evidence indicated that no one could say when work might restart. Its termination letter cited both nonpayment and indefinite delay, the refused arbitration offer, and the lack of assurance of a reasonable opportunity to finish. It announced that it would perform no further work and would claim resulting damages. The complaint asserted total breach but also contained a general quantum-meruit claim for labor and materials. (338–339.)

## Delay: an extension clause did not require indefinite readiness
The trial judge allowed the jury to find justified termination if, by May 22, a long or indefinite delay was apparent or it was impossible to determine when work could resume. The First Circuit regarded the contract’s extension-of-time clause as requiring continued readiness despite owner-caused delay, leaving only damages for delay. The Supreme Court rejected that construction. Paragraph 11 expressly required Carlin to supply other labor and materials without delaying Guerini’s material progress and to reimburse resulting loss. As applied here, it required a foundation upon which Guerini could build without delay. This obligation did not depend on proving Carlin’s fault, or on the government’s right to stop work under a main-contract clause not incorporated into the subcontract. (340–341.)

Paragraphs 6 and 7 established a 300-day completion period and extensions for specified delays. Their protection against late completion did not strip Guerini of the right to finish sooner or require it to remain indefinitely available after a total shutdown with no reasonable prospect of resumption. The instruction was evaluated in light of the undisputed two months already elapsed, rather than as permission to terminate over any brief interruption. (340–341.)

## Payments: substantial default could excuse further work
The trial court instructed that a substantial failure to make contractual progress payments—substantially withholding the bulk of the payment—could justify stopping work and recovery of damages. The Supreme Court approved the principle that these payments were material in a lengthy project requiring large outlays. The parties could not reasonably expect the subcontractor to finance the whole undertaking without the stipulated installments. Substantial compliance with advance-payment obligations was therefore a condition precedent to the subcontractor’s further performance. A defense instruction limiting the remedy to interest unless Carlin positively refused all payment was not required. (344–345.)

The First Circuit nevertheless thought Guerini’s requisitions improper because they included granite setting, which it treated as a separate contract payable only on completion. The Supreme Court held that exercising the granite option added work to the existing contract and subjected it to the contract’s appropriate general payment terms. Even on the alternative separate-contract view, evidence would permit the jury to find that the granite work was substantially complete in February and that Carlin agreed no more blocks should be set because the remaining blocks arrived intermittently at unreasonable installation cost. If that work was thus ended, Guerini could seek its full granite payment, not merely eighty-five percent. That remained a jury-supported alternative, not an appellate declaration of every contested fact. (345–346.)

## Materials, variance, and the damages instructions
Evidence showed that materials, machinery, and tools worth $3,500 were left at the site and taken for Carlin’s use. That item was included in the bill of particulars and admissible on the unwaived quantum-meruit claim. It also mattered under the accepted damages approach of United States v. Behan: a deduction for materials remaining on hand presupposed that they remained Guerini’s property available for its use. Articles retained and appropriated by Carlin with Guerini’s consent could instead count as Guerini’s contribution to performance. The evidence did not compel a separate tort-for-conversion remedy. (342–344.)

The court also rejected exclusion of evidence of the February unit-price arrangement merely because the complaint described payment demands as complying with the contract. The allegation concerned performance of a condition precedent; the earlier Supreme Court opinion had already discussed that evidence, and the second trial supplied supporting detail missing before. Any variance was unimportant and nonprejudicial; even if an amendment were necessary, the pleading could be treated as amended rather than reversing the judgment. (346–347.)

A separate damages instruction listed expenditures, losses, performed work, contract-price entitlement, and profits. The First Circuit viewed it as duplicative. The Supreme Court did not endorse that potentially overlapping formulation on the merits: the issue might have been difficult if Carlin had preserved it. The prior Supreme Court discussion had assumed the similar instruction’s propriety only for argument, not decided it. On retrial this instruction came from the court itself, and Carlin had not specifically excepted to it; a blanket exception to all requested plaintiff instructions did not identify a precise error. A different, preserved objection addressed an instruction limited to outlay and lost profits, which contained no duplication and was not erroneous. (347–349.)

## Final ruling and assigned significance
After rejecting the First Circuit’s grounds, the Court also considered the other assignments of error—101 in total, aided by the respondent’s extensive brief—and found none warranting reversal. It reversed the appellate judgment and affirmed the district judgment. The opinion calls the verdict substantial but does not print its dollar amount; no award figure is inferred from the $11,735.95 requisition or the $3,500 materials evidence. (337, 349.)

The assigned account on printed page 865 emphasizes the progress-payment condition and asks how suspension differs from final termination. The complete opinion provides the actual interval, the independent foundation-and-delay promise, and the contractor’s unsuccessful attempts to obtain payment and assurance. It also separates approval of the material-breach principle from the unpreserved question about possible duplication in damages. Those distinctions prevent treating every late installment as automatically authorizing an immediate walk-off.
