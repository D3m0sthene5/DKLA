# Sherwood v. Walker
66 Mich. 568, 33 N.W. 919 (1887) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
The Michigan Supreme Court decided the case on July 7, 1887. Justice Morse wrote for a three-justice majority, joined by Chief Justice Campbell and Justice Champlin. Justice Sherwood dissented; he was the judge, distinct from plaintiff Theodore C. Sherwood. The buyer had won this replevin action in a justice’s court and again after an appeal and jury trial in the Wayne County Circuit Court. The sellers sought review, assigning twenty-five errors. The Supreme Court reversed and ordered a new trial, with costs in that court to the defendants. It did not enter a final judgment awarding the cow to the sellers. Its controlling instruction depended on what the new jury found about the parties’ beliefs when they contracted. (66 Mich. at 568, 578, 585.)

## Facts and formation of the sale
The Walkers bred imported Polled Angus cattle and kept a farm at Greenfield. Sherwood, a banker living in Plymouth, approached them about buying an animal. They told him their available cattle were probably barren and would not breed. He visited the farm on May 5, 1886, and later negotiated by telephone for Rose 2d of Aberlone, identified as lot 56 in the catalogue. The agreed price was five and one-half cents per pound of live weight, less fifty pounds for shrinkage. A May 15 written confirmation provided for weighing and delivery at King’s cattle yard, a halter, and payment to the farm manager or by a check mailed to the sellers. The specified animal and weight-based bargain were important: this was not simply an agreement to buy some cow for a flat eighty dollars. (Id. at 569–570.)

On May 21 Sherwood presented the delivery order to the manager, Graham, who said he had been instructed not to deliver. Sherwood tendered eighty dollars, which the sellers refused. He obtained possession through the writ in this action, and the constable weighed the cow elsewhere at 1,420 pounds. The majority rejected an argument that Sherwood’s May 19 reference to “Rose or Lucy” showed uncertainty about the animal: he had written hastily without the order at hand, and the surrounding correspondence identified Rose as the already purchased cow. His request that she receive no water before weighing also concerned performance of this particular sale. (Id. at 570–572.)

The sellers’ evidence was that both sides had believed Rose incapable of breeding. They had paid approximately $850 for her, and a breeding cow of her kind was worth $750 to $1,000. After the May 15 letter, Graham informed the sellers that he believed Rose was with calf. They instructed him not to deliver her and, on May 20, telegraphed Sherwood about the suspicion and their resulting refusal to sell. Rose delivered a calf the following October. These events supplied the sellers’ mistake theory, but the majority’s eventual direction left the shared belief to the jury. The dissent read the record differently: Sherwood thought the cow could be made to breed, while the sellers believed she was sterile. Present pregnancy, permanent fertility, and each party’s expectation about future breeding were therefore distinct propositions. (Id. at 571, 578–581.)

## Arguments and rulings below
Sherwood claimed that title had passed and that he was entitled to possession. The sellers argued that the sale remained executory, including because the agreed weighing and payment had not occurred, and that the apparent agreement could be avoided because the cow was not barren. The trial court allowed the jury to determine the parties’ intention from their letters, instructed that weighing need not precede possession or take place at the named yard, and treated whether Rose was with calf as immaterial. It declined the sellers’ requested instructions concerning weighing as a condition precedent and the barren-cow condition. (Id. at 569, 572.)

The Supreme Court agreed with much of the trial court’s treatment of ordinary formation. The signed correspondence supplied a sufficient memorandum under the statute governing sales above fifty dollars. The words did not necessarily make payment or weighing a condition precedent to passage of title; neither was physical delivery invariably required. The parties’ intention could determine whether title to the identified animal had passed before those acts. The majority thus did not accept the sellers’ weighing and delivery objections as a sufficient basis for reversal. Its reason for a new trial was the excluded mistake issue. (Id. at 573–575.)

## Majority rule and reasoning
A party’s apparent assent may be avoided, before or after completion, when the bargain rests on a mutual mistake of material fact. The majority distinguished a difference in the substance of what the parties bargained for from a difference merely in quality or an accidental attribute. A mistake about quality could leave the contract binding even when it motivated the transaction. (Id. at 576.)

The difficult step was applying that distinction to the same physical cow. The majority acknowledged that the animal’s identity had not changed. It nevertheless considered a cow permanently useless for breeding and a breeding cow materially different in their nature and the purposes for which each could be used. The difference lasted beyond the current pregnancy or season: the former was principally useful for beef, whereas the latter had enduring breeding value. Under that analysis, the very basis on which both parties allegedly priced and exchanged the animal had failed, rather than merely their estimate of one advantageous quality. (Id. at 576–577.)

The necessary instruction was conditional. If the jury found that both parties believed Rose barren and useless for breeding when they contracted, but that she was actually capable of breeding, the sellers could rescind and refuse delivery, and the verdict should be for them. Because the jury had been told pregnancy was immaterial and was not given that instruction, its verdict could not settle the relevant factual issue. The majority ordered a new trial. No finding that the buyer knowingly concealed pregnancy was necessary to that mutual-mistake rationale, and the opinion should not be read as establishing such concealment. (Id. at 576, 578.)

## Justice Sherwood’s dissent
Justice Sherwood accepted that the written bargain could transfer title without prior weighing or payment. He rejected the central factual premise of the majority’s mistake analysis. In his account, the buyer exercised his own judgment that Rose might breed; the sellers exercised their contrary judgment. Their agreement concerned the same existing animal, with neither side warranting the unknown quality. Discovery that one judgment was better than the other did not establish a common mistaken assumption authorizing rescission. He also said the evidence supplied no basis for imputing fraud to the buyer. (Id. at 578–581.)

The dissent used a hypothetical sale of a horse later revealed to be exceptionally fast to illustrate the risk of turning mistaken valuations into grounds for avoidance. It then examined the majority’s authorities individually. Those cases involved, among other things, a nonexistent interest, property hidden in a purchased machine that was not itself sold, an assignment of the wrong judgment, or delivery of a different grade or article. In his view, they did not authorize a seller to reclaim the precise animal both parties had intended to exchange simply because its unguaranteed breeding quality made it more valuable. These accounts are the dissent’s treatment of those authorities, rather than separate source reviews of each cited case. (Id. at 580–584.)

For the dissent, a general sale did not imply a warranty of every quality, and reliance on one’s own judgment did not create an implied confidence that the other side would surrender the benefit of better judgment. If anyone had superior information about this animal, it was the breeders. The sellers’ error concerned quality and value, not the existence or identity of what was sold. He would have affirmed the buyer’s judgment. (Id. at 585.)
