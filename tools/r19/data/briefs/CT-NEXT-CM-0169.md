# Taylor v. Caldwell
3 B. & S. 826, 122 Eng. Rep. 309 (Q.B. May 6, 1863) | Contracts

## Agreement and destruction
On May 27, 1861, Caldwell and Bishop agreed to give Taylor and Lewis use of the Surrey Gardens and Music Hall for concerts and day-and-night fêtes on June 17, July 15, August 5, and August 19. Taylor and Lewis would pay £100 at the close of each day and retain the entrance receipts. The owners were to supply an organized military and quadrille band, other amusements, fireworks and illuminations, while the promoters provided concert artists and publicity. The agreement specified advertising access, the time dancing could begin, and the latest fireworks hour. This coordinated entertainment venture depended on the particular hall, not merely access to an outdoor site. (3 B. & S. 826–830.)

The hall was destroyed by an accidental fire on June 11, before the first concert. The destruction was complete enough to prevent the contemplated concerts, and the court accepted that neither side was at fault. The agreement did not expressly address that event. The promoters sued for expenses incurred advertising and preparing the performances, alleging the owners failed to supply the promised facilities. (Id. 827–830,832–833.)

## Litigation and questions
At trial before Blackburn, a verdict went to the plaintiffs with leave reserved to enter a verdict for the defendants on the second and third issues. The defendants obtained a rule seeking entry of a defense verdict; counsel argued it in January 1863 before Cockburn C.J., Wightman, Crompton, and Blackburn JJ. The court delivered judgment through Blackburn on May 6. (Id. 830–832.)

The plaintiffs argued that the arrangement was not a demise transferring possession, notwithstanding its use of “let” and “rent,” and that an absolute contractual undertaking was not discharged by accidental destruction. Their argument invoked Paradine v. Jane. Defense counsel contended that it was a demise and that the agreement’s words “God’s will permitting” qualified the whole arrangement. The decisive question was whether the contract itself, read according to its nature, carried a condition of the hall’s continued existence even without an express destruction clause. (Id. 830–833.)

## Rule and holding
An absolute promise to do a lawful act ordinarily requires performance or damages despite unforeseen accidents that make performance burdensome or impossible. That rule does not eliminate express or implied conditions. Where the nature of a bargain makes continued existence of a particular person or thing fundamental to performance, and there is no express or implied warranty of its existence, the law implies a condition excusing performance when that person or thing perishes without the contractor’s default before breach. (Id. 833–834.)

The particular Music Hall’s continued existence was essential to this bargain. Its faultless destruction excused both sides: the promoters from taking the gardens and paying the price, and the owners from giving use of the hall and gardens and providing the related services. The promoters could not recover their preparation losses as damages for the owners’ nonperformance. (Id. 839–840.)

## Contractual construction and reasoning
Blackburn first rejected the notion that “let” and “rent” necessarily created a lease. The owners retained possession, and the arrangement granted use on specified days; there was no demise. He said nothing ultimately depended on that characterization. The agreement taken as a whole established that performances could not occur as intended without the hall. Its destruction therefore eliminated an assumed foundation, rather than merely making delivery of a promised generic performance inconvenient. (Id. 832–833.)

The court explained implication as furthering the intentions of persons entering this kind of transaction: if the contingency were brought to their minds, they would ordinarily provide such a condition. This is the opinion’s historical construction method. It is not a finding that these parties actually negotiated or considered the fire risk; the court expressly observed that the disaster was not in their contemplation. Nor did its holding rest on defense counsel’s proposed construction of “God’s will permitting.” (Id. 833–834.)

The court then developed a broad analogical foundation omitted in part from the assignment. Roman-law obligations concerning a specific subject and Pothier’s treatment of destruction without the debtor’s act, neglect, or preexisting default furnished guidance, while the opinion expressly denied that civil law was itself authoritative in an English court. Its Roman examples concern enslaved persons; that historical language belongs to the authorities the court used, not to a modern endorsement or a different factual transaction in Taylor. (Id. 834–835.)

English examples extended the same implication to personal performance: an author’s death before completing a work, loss of necessary personal capacity, and death of an apprentice whose father promised the apprentice’s service. The underlying assumption could concern someone other than the promisor, and neither an unqualified promise nor the absence of an express death clause foreclosed it. (Id. 835–837.)

The court also considered specific goods destroyed after property had passed and bailment obligations to return a particular chattel. Rugg v. Minett illustrated that the consequences for payment could depend on whether title to particular goods had passed; Taylor therefore does not erase every preexisting allocation of risk or entitle every party to a refund. Williams v. Lloyd and the bailment discussion supplied the narrower proposition that the innocent loss of the particular thing can excuse promised return, provided the promisor did not assume the risk or cause the loss. Coggs v. Bernard and earlier authority supported treating this as a principle of English law rather than an import confined to bailments. (Id. 837–839.)

The final inference united those examples: the law can imply continued existence from the performance’s nature even when the promise uses positive language. This contract’s dependence on the particular hall made the implication appropriate. Faultless destruction before any performance date consequently relieved both sides’ prospective obligations.

## Exact result and source limits
The court made the rule absolute to enter a verdict for the defendants, displacing the conditional plaintiffs’ verdict. Blackburn delivered the court’s judgment. The report identifies the four judges who heard argument but does not give a numerical vote on judgment or a separate judicial opinion; no separate writing should be invented. The complete report has one lettered reference footnote to Pothier at 837, read with the opinion. (Id. 840.)
