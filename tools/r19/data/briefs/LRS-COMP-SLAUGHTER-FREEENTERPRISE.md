# Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board

**Citation and court:** 561 U.S. 477 (2010), Supreme Court of the United States, No. 08–861, decided June 28, 2010. Chief Justice Roberts wrote for five Justices, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Alito. Justice Breyer dissented, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg, and Sotomayor. The complete official report includes thirteen Court notes and Breyer's four appendices, including their substantive starred and dagger notes; all were read before drafting.

**Facts and regulatory design.** Congress created the five-member PCAOB in the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 after major accounting failures. Its members were appointed by the Securities and Exchange Commission to staggered five-year terms. Accounting firms auditing public companies had to register, pay fees, and comply with Board rules and oversight. The Board could inspect firms, investigate violations, conduct disciplinary proceedings, and impose serious sanctions. Although the Act described it as outside the federal government, the parties agreed that it was governmental for constitutional purposes and its members were officers exercising significant authority. 561 U.S. at 484–486.

The SEC approved or could alter Board rules and sanctions and had additional budgetary and supervisory authority. But it could remove individual Board members only for good cause under unusually restrictive grounds and procedures: willful violations, willful abuse of authority, or unreasonable failure to enforce compliance, determined on a record after notice and an opportunity for a hearing. Id. at 486–487, 502–503. Crucially, the SEC's own statute did not expressly give commissioners for-cause protection. The parties agreed that such protection existed, and the Court decided the case on that understanding. This was an assumption, not a statutory holding creating express SEC tenure protection. Id. at 487.

The Board inspected Beckstead and Watts, a Nevada accounting firm, criticized its auditing procedures, and opened an investigation without yet imposing a sanction. The firm and Free Enterprise Fund, an organization of which it was a member, sued to challenge the Board's constitutionality and sought declaratory and injunctive relief. They asserted both separation-of-powers and Appointments Clause objections. The United States intervened. The district court found jurisdiction but granted summary judgment to the government; a divided D.C. Circuit affirmed, with Judge Kavanaugh dissenting. Id. at 487–488.

**Questions and holdings.** The district court could hear this structural constitutional challenge notwithstanding the securities laws' ordinary appellate-review scheme. The two layers of for-cause removal protection violated Article II, but the Board-member removal restrictions were severable. With those restrictions removed, Board members were inferior officers validly appointed by the full SEC, which qualified as a department and collectively as its head. Id. at 489–514. The Court preserved the Board and its substantive regulatory powers.

**Jurisdiction.** Section 78y permits appellate review of SEC rules and final orders, but did not channel this suit exclusively into that process. Under Thunder Basin, meaningful review, the collateral nature of the claim, and the agency's expertise mattered. Petitioners had no sanction to appeal. They did not have to deliberately incur one merely to obtain constitutional review. Nor was an appeal from an unrelated Board rule a meaningful route to challenge the Board's existence. These constitutional issues lay outside the Commission's technical expertise and were collateral to ordinary agency orders. Id. at 489–491. Note 2 recognizes established equitable relief against unconstitutional official action and rejects treating the Appointments Clause and separation-of-powers theories differently for that purpose. Id. at 491–492 n.2. The decision did not generally eliminate statutory exhaustion or review-channel requirements for every regulatory claim.

**Article II reasoning.** Executive power and the obligation to ensure faithful execution require a President able to supervise executive officers and answer to the electorate for their conduct. The majority relied on the Decision of 1789 and Myers while expressly declining to reexamine the precedents permitting certain single-level tenure protections, including Humphrey's Executor, Perkins, and Morrison. Id. at 492–495. Thus, it did not overrule those cases or announce that every executive officer must be removable at will.

With a single protected level, the President or a directly accountable subordinate determines whether cause exists to remove the protected officer. Here, protected commissioners themselves controlled whether protected Board members met a rigorous removal standard. The President could review the commissioners' conduct, but could not ordinarily overturn their reasonable judgment that cause was absent. The added layer reduced accountability for the Board's exercise of executive power. Id. at 495–498 & n.4. The Court feared that accepting the structure would permit further layers and diffuse responsibility beyond an effective chain of command.

Efficiency and technical expertise did not excuse a structural violation. Congress could design a varied bureaucracy, but its control over offices, duties, and salaries made presidential supervision important to preserving constitutional balance. Id. at 498–502 & nn.5–6. The Court also rejected broader constructions of the Board's removal grounds and the suggestion that supervisory control over functions substituted for control over members. Revising rules, budgets, or jurisdiction was cumbersome; the majority found that those tools did not confer effective control over individual investigations. Id. at 502–505 & nn.7–8.

**Reserved questions.** The Court emphasized the Board's substantial executive authority and unusual tenure restrictions. It did not decide other employees' officer status, invalidate the civil-service system, resolve the status or removal protections of administrative law judges, or determine presidential removal authority over military officers. Id. at 505–508 & nn.9–10. These express reservations matter: Breyer's predictions about other institutions were objections to the rule's implications, not holdings invalidating those institutions. Note 11 likewise reserves whether the SEC is an executive department for the Opinions Clause or Twenty-Fifth Amendment. Id. at 511 n.11.

**Severability and appointments.** Sections 7211(e)(6) and 7217(d)(3), the substantive Board-member removal restrictions, were severed. Ordinary appointment authority carried removal authority, leaving the SEC able to remove Board members at will. The Act remained workable, and neither text nor historical context established that Congress would prefer no Board to a Board without those protections. The Court declined more extensive judicial redesign of the Board's duties or appointment structure. Id. at 508–510.

Once the SEC's at-will removal power was recognized, that power and its other oversight made Board members inferior officers under Edmond. The SEC was a freestanding, self-contained executive component and therefore a department for appointments purposes. Its commissioners jointly held its governing powers; the chair did not command the other commissioners. A multimember body could constitutionally be a department head. Id. at 510–513. Note 12 rejects the standing objection based on the chair's agreement with past appointments: the Court could not assume the same choices if the chair had acted alone. Note 13 explains that commission approval could satisfy the clause for other appointments initially made by the chair; it did not invalidate those appointments. Id. at 512 nn.12–13.

**Breyer's dissent.** Breyer agreed that Board members were inferior officers but disputed the constitutional violation. The Constitution's removal provisions were not specific enough, and history and precedent were not conclusive enough, to justify the majority's mechanical treatment of two layers. He urged functional examination of the President's actual ability to perform constitutional responsibilities, with deference to Congress's administrative expertise. He also stressed that Congress had not appropriated removal power to itself. Id. at 514–523.

In his view, removal was one among many levers of presidential influence. Eliminating the Board's protection would not require independent commissioners to carry out a President's wishes, while the SEC already had pervasive control over rules, investigations, sanctions, budgets, and assigned responsibilities. The change therefore offered little demonstrated practical gain. Technical expertise, impartial adjudication, and public confidence supplied substantial reasons for Board independence. Perkins and Humphrey's Executor, read together with the Court's functional precedents, supported the structure. Id. at 524–536.

Breyer challenged the uncertainty surrounding civil servants, administrative adjudicators, and military officers and doubted that severability would readily solve every future application. His appendices supplied historical illustrations: 48 agencies or offices with statutory protection; an illustrative list of 573 senior protected appointees; 1,584 administrative law judges; and 27 agencies with other independence characteristics but no express removal protection. These were the dissent's 2010 data and legal characterizations, not current counts or Court findings. The senior-position table itself warns that source dates and temporal lag make it illustrative rather than exact. Id. at 536–545, 549–592, especially 556–557. Finally, because the SEC statute was silent, he criticized assuming an extra protective layer and using that assumption to invalidate another statute. He would uphold the Board's protection whether or not commissioners had implied tenure protection. Id. at 545–549.

**Exact disposition.** The D.C. Circuit's judgment was **affirmed in part and reversed in part**, and the case was **remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion**. Id. at 514. Petitioners were entitled to declaratory relief sufficient to ensure regulation by a constitutionally accountable agency, but not the broad injunction dismantling the Board's operations. Id. at 513. This was neither wholesale invalidation of Sarbanes-Oxley nor presidential power to remove Board members directly.

**Course significance.** Separate the constitutional defect, the severed provisions, and the surviving agency. The assigned passage emphasizes accountability and Breyer's functional critique; the full decision also establishes the jurisdictional and appointment holdings. Its limited result preserves the Board while removing one level of tenure protection, leaving the broader removal precedents and expressly reserved institutions for other cases. The supplement's later historical observations are editorial context, not part of the 2010 holding.
