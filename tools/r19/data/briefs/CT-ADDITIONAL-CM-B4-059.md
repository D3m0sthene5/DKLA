# 1140 Broadway LLC v. Bold Food, LLC
2020 N.Y. Slip Op. 34017(U), 2020 WL 7137817 (Sup. Ct., N.Y. County Dec. 3, 2020) | Contracts

## Court and precise procedural result
Justice Arlene P. Bluth decided the landlord's summary-judgment motion in the New York Supreme Court, New York County, index 652674/2020, on December 3, 2020. The court granted judgment as to liability only and ordered a trial to determine damages, directing the landlord to file a note of issue by December 15. The decision is marked non-final. It did not determine an exact monetary award or excuse either defendant's existing liability. The motion date printed on the order is November 24, distinct from the decision and filing date. (Decision 1, 5–6.)

## Office lease, guaranty, and the pandemic's effect
Bold Food leased part of the twelfth floor of a Manhattan office building. Its business provided management and consulting services to restaurants, including help with human resources, payroll, and accounting. KBFK Restaurant Corp. furnished a good-guy guaranty. The landlord described the lease as running through February 2022 and alleged that rent payments stopped in February 2020, with the tenant remaining until June 30. The court ultimately treated the lease of office space and cessation of payments as undisputed. (1–2, 4–5.)

The defendants attributed their default to the pandemic. Restaurants' curtailed operations or closures eliminated demand for their services, making the consultant's business unprofitable and leaving it unable to afford rent. They asserted both objective impossibility and frustration of purpose. Alternatively, they demanded an inquest to establish damages. The landlord argued that neither excuse applied, that the guarantor also remained liable, and that its damages were not disputed. (1–2.)

The distinction between the tenant and its customers mattered. The tenant was an office-based service provider, one step removed from the orders limiting restaurant operations. It was not itself shut down by those public-health directives. Moreover, restaurants were restricted in activities such as indoor dining rather than uniformly forbidden to conduct all operations; some closed because of the financial consequences. The court did not hold that every restaurant physically remained open or that every restaurant lease was enforceable regardless of its terms. (4 & n. 1.)

## Summary-judgment standard and frustration
The landlord had to present evidence establishing entitlement to judgment as a matter of law and the absence of a material factual dispute. If it failed its initial burden, the motion had to be denied regardless of the opposition's sufficiency. Once that burden was met, defendants needed evidence of a triable issue. Alleged facts were viewed favorably to the nonmovants; the court's task was identifying factual issues rather than resolving credibility. (2–3.)

Frustration is narrow. The disappointed purpose must have been so completely the shared basis of the bargain that, without it, the transaction would make little sense, and the frustration must be substantial. The court found that leasing office space while a tenant's industry later suffers a precipitous decline does not by itself satisfy that rule. Here the office had not been destroyed or lost a unique capacity for an agreed specific purpose; the problem was that the consulting enterprise had lost clients and could not afford the agreed rent. (3.)

The court expressly reserved the broader question of which other circumstances might support frustration under a generic office lease. Its contrast with a condemned factory or costumes rented for a particular performance in a theater that burns down explained why these facts failed; those hypotheticals were not facts of this dispute or a comprehensive list of permissible excuses. (3.)

## Impossibility, casualty, and the contractual allocation
The court applied Kel Kim's requirement of objective impossibility through destruction of the subject matter or means of performance, caused by an unanticipated event that could not have been foreseen or guarded against in the agreement. The office space remained the subject of the contract. Lack of clients and funds did not destroy it or the means of rendering the contractually promised performance. The consultant's indirect exposure to the restaurant orders was insufficient. The sole judicial footnote expressly declined to decide whether an actual restaurant could succeed on impossibility or frustration. (4 & n. 1.)

The pandemic also did not establish the asserted casualty-based abatement. That lease provision addressed physical damage, not the loss of the tenant's clientele. Nor had the landlord made rent contingent on the tenant's ability to afford it. The court declined to revise that allocation simply because both landlord and tenant faced hardship. (3, 5–6.)

The good-guy guaranty supplied a contractual protection for the guarantor if the business failed, provided its conditions were fulfilled. The court described the guarantor as responsible for rent while the tenant actually remained in possession, with power to return the space. Instead, the tenant kept possession for five months after payments stopped. That bargain did not authorize occupying the premises without payment merely because business had become unprofitable. The decision does not hold that the guarantor owes all rent through February 2022 regardless of the guaranty's possession limitation. (5.)

## Why damages still required trial
Although liability was established, the landlord had not adequately documented the amount sought. Defendants disputed whether the security deposit had been deducted. The landlord conceded in reply that any award should be reduced by that deposit, highlighting the need to determine the net amount. It supplied neither a ledger nor documentation showing how it calculated rent, additional rent, reasonable attorneys' fees, other damages, or interest. Its agent's affidavit did not establish that arithmetic, and the agent alternatively requested referral to a special referee. (5.)

The final operative order chose a trial to assess damages, rather than itself appointing a referee or entering a sum-certain award. Accordingly, the result is summary judgment on liability with damages unresolved. The court's rejection of the defendants' excuses does not supply missing damages evidence. (5–6.)

## Assigned hypothetical and reading scope
The complete assigned Problem at printed pages 1139–1140 adapts the case to a consultant named Hannibal and expressly adds initial lack of workplace access and a two-year lease entered just before COVID-19. The actual opinion describes the office lease and its February 2022 expiry but does not specify that formation chronology or find initial physical inaccessibility. Those adapted premises belong to the exercise, not to the judicial findings. The actual court emphasized this consultant's loss of restaurant customers and reserved other possible frustration scenarios.
