# Stewart Organization, Inc. v. Ricoh Corp.

**Course:** Civil Procedure
**ID:** CP-050
**Review:** Complete source comparison and separate independent review; corrected legacy pinpoint.

## Citation

Stewart Organization, Inc. v. Ricoh Corp., 487 U.S. 22 (1988). Supreme Court of the United States; argued February 29, 1988; decided June 20, 1988. Justice Marshall wrote the eight-Justice majority opinion. Justice Kennedy, joined by Justice O’Connor, concurred; Justice Scalia dissented. Assigned casebook pp. 486–491. Full opinion: Complete official report, 487 U.S. 22–41, including all eleven Court notes, Kennedy’s concurrence, and Scalia’s complete dissent with its sole starred note. The brief follows the assigned excerpt; the distinction between a statute and a promulgated Rule, the reservation concerning the antitrust jurisdictional ground, and portions of Scalia’s contract-validity discussion are confirmed or supplemented from the full opinion and footnotes.

## Parties

Stewart Organization was an Alabama corporation that agreed to market Ricoh copier products. It and individual shareholder petitioners sued Ricoh after their business relationship deteriorated. Ricoh, a nationwide manufacturer with its principal place of business in New Jersey, was the defendant requesting transfer and the Supreme Court respondent. Stewart sought to keep the action in Alabama despite the contractual designation of Manhattan courts. 487 U.S. at 24–25.

## Material Facts

The dealership agreement required disputes arising under or connected with the agreement to be brought in an appropriate state or federal court in Manhattan. After relations broke down, Stewart filed in the Northern District of Alabama in September 1984. Its central allegation was breach of the dealership agreement, with additional warranty, fraud, and antitrust claims. The Supreme Court did not resolve those allegations; the merits of the commercial dispute were unnecessary to deciding the governing transfer standard. 487 U.S. at 24 & n.1.

The district court understood Alabama policy to disfavor contractual forum-selection clauses. That policy would deny the clause the effect Ricoh sought, whereas Ricoh invoked federal transfer law and the parties’ negotiated preference. The Supreme Court treated the asserted conflict as sufficient to decide the federal-law question but expressly found it unnecessary to determine the exact contours of Alabama’s policy. The opinion therefore should not be read as an independent, definitive construction of Alabama contract law. 487 U.S. at 24, 30 & n.9.

## Procedural History

Ricoh moved to transfer under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a), alternatively seeking dismissal for improper venue under § 1406. The district court denied relief because it considered Alabama law controlling. It certified its ruling under § 1292(b), allowing an interlocutory appeal rather than requiring the parties to finish the merits litigation first. 487 U.S. at 24–25.

A divided Eleventh Circuit panel reversed and instructed transfer to Manhattan. On rehearing en banc, the court largely adopted that result and reasoning. It considered venue a matter of federal procedure and applied The Bremen’s forum-clause standards, originally developed in admiralty. 487 U.S. at 25.

The Supreme Court affirmed the order reversing the district court’s reliance on Alabama law, but adopted a different method and remanded for the district court to decide the clause’s appropriate effect under § 1404(a). Its judgment did not simply endorse the court of appeals’ premise that a generalized body of federal forum-clause law conclusively required transfer. 487 U.S. at 28–32.

## Issue

Does federal transfer legislation govern whether a federal court should give effect to a contractual forum-selection clause when contrary state policy would disregard the clause? Is the relevant federal authority § 1404(a) itself, or a judge-made rule borrowed from admiralty? 487 U.S. at 24–29.

## Rule and Holding

When a federal statute is invoked in a diversity case, the court first determines whether the statute fairly covers the disputed issue and then whether it is constitutionally valid. A statute satisfying both conditions governs. The ordinary Erie inquiry applicable in the absence of controlling federal legislation does not permit a contrary state policy to displace that statute. 487 U.S. at 26–27.

Section 1404(a) covers the decision whether to transfer this action in light of the forum-selection clause. Its individualized consideration of convenience and fairness includes the contractual preference as a significant factor, along with other relevant interests. A categorical state policy denying that factor any effect conflicts with the federal statute’s prescribed analysis. Section 1404(a) is a valid exercise of Congress’s authority over the federal judiciary. 487 U.S. at 29–32.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court affirmed the Eleventh Circuit’s order reversing the district court’s application of Alabama law and remanded so that the district court could determine the appropriate effect of the clause under federal transfer law. The Supreme Court itself neither resolved the contract claims nor made the final case-specific transfer determination. 487 U.S. at 32.

## Reasoning

Marshall first identified the source of the proposed federal rule. The issue involved a congressional statute, which required statutory interpretation followed by constitutional analysis. This differed from choosing between state law and uncodified federal judicial practice. A valid Act of Congress covering the issue supplies an authoritative instruction, rather than one interest to be balanced against state policy. 487 U.S. at 26–27.

The court of appeals had started too broadly by surveying federal interests in venue and importing The Bremen’s standards. Those standards could be informative, but admiralty common law does not automatically become governing law in diversity. The immediate motion asked for a transfer under § 1404(a). The proper first question was whether that statute governed how the forum clause affected the requested transfer. 487 U.S. at 28–29.

The statute’s flexible terms reached the question. Congress instructed district courts to consider the parties and witnesses, fairness, and the interest of justice. The parties’ own agreement about where disputes should be heard necessarily bears on that inquiry. The Court identified convenience, the expressed preference, fairness, and relative bargaining power as matters the district court could consider in evaluating the agreement’s role. The clause could not be removed from the federal analysis merely because state policy rejected it categorically. 487 U.S. at 29–30.

The conflict did not require identical wording or perfectly overlapping subject matter. Alabama’s asserted policy focused on whether a contractual clause should receive effect, while § 1404(a) considered that and additional matters. But a state instruction to give the clause no weight would still obstruct the federal statute’s operation within the disputed transfer decision. Conversely, a state rule automatically enforcing a clause could not eliminate the statute’s consideration of other interests. 487 U.S. at 26 n.4, 30–31.

That is why the Court distinguished Walker. In Walker the Federal Rule and state statute operated on separate questions after proper construction. In Stewart, the categorical state approach and federal multifactor method directed conflicting treatment of the same transfer motion. The federal statute’s broader scope did not weaken its supremacy; Congress’s decision to require multiple considerations was part of what the state approach would defeat. 487 U.S. at 30–31.

Constitutional authority presented little difficulty. Regulating transfers among federal district courts is a form of federal judicial administration supported by Article III and the Necessary and Proper Clause. The Court relied on Hanna’s account of congressional authority over federal practice. Because Congress itself enacted § 1404(a), the Court did not test that statute as though it were a subordinate rule promulgated under the Rules Enabling Act. 487 U.S. at 27 n.5, 31–32.

The Court left actual weighing to the district court. Under Stewart’s formulation, the clause deserved neither automatic disregard nor invariably dispositive treatment. It was to receive the importance appropriate under the statute. That explains the remand and the difference between selecting the governing law and deciding the transfer motion on its merits. 487 U.S. at 31–32.

## Separate Opinions

Justice Kennedy, joined by Justice O’Connor, joined the majority fully but emphasized enforcement of legitimate negotiated expectations. He thought valid forum clauses generally should control absent exceptional circumstances. Enforcing them could reduce litigation costs and preliminary disputes over forum, and The Bremen’s reasoning could guide the statutory inquiry even though its original setting was admiralty. Kennedy’s position pressed more strongly toward enforcement than the majority’s general description of individualized balancing. 487 U.S. at 33 (Kennedy, J., concurring).

Justice Scalia agreed that statutory scope was the first question but framed the disputed issue differently. He separated the validity of a contractual allocation between the parties from the weight a concededly valid agreement receives in a federal transfer decision. Section 1404(a), he argued, regulates present convenience and future litigation, without clearly creating federal contract-validity law. A clause invalid under governing state law could receive no contractual weight, though transfer could still be justified on other grounds. 487 U.S. at 33–37 & 35–36 n.* (Scalia, J., dissenting).

Scalia then applied Erie because he found no governing federal statute. Divergent state and federal treatment of a forum clause would strongly influence filing and removal choices and could give parties materially different litigation opportunities based on diversity. He therefore would use state law on validity. His dissent did not deny federal authority to administer § 1404(a); it disputed the statute’s reach into the antecedent contract question. 487 U.S. at 37–41.

## Notes

The case illustrates why identifying the level of federal authority matters. A congressional statute must cover the issue and be constitutional. A promulgated Federal Rule additionally must satisfy its enabling legislation. Judge-made practice where neither applies raises the ordinary Erie inquiry. Stewart, 487 U.S. at 26–27 & nn.4–6.

The complaint also included a federal antitrust claim. The Court noted that jurisdiction had been alleged on both federal-question and diversity grounds but expressly declined to decide whether that combination independently changed the choice-of-law analysis, because § 1404(a) already resolved the issue. It is inaccurate to describe the holding as dependent on the presence of antitrust allegations. 487 U.S. at 26 n.3.

The later Atlantic Marine decision gives more specific instructions for an undisputedly valid forum clause, including disregarding contrary private convenience and adjusting the choice-of-law consequence of transfer. Those later instructions should be distinguished from Stewart’s own 1988 formulation. They build on the statutory enforcement route, while the two cases reached the Court with differently framed questions about clause effect and validity. Atlantic Marine, 571 U.S. 49, 62–66 (2013); Stewart, 487 U.S. at 29–33.
