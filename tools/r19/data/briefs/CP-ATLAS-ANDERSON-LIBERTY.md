# Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.
477 U.S. 242 (1986) | Civil Procedure

## Court, question, and judgment
Justice White wrote for a six-Justice Supreme Court majority on June 25, 1986. The Court asked whether the heightened trial burden of proving actual malice by clear and convincing evidence must inform the summary-judgment inquiry in a public-figure libel action. It held that it must. A judge asks whether the evidence could support a reasonable jury finding under the applicable burden, while leaving credibility and legitimate competing inferences to the jury. The judgment vacated the D.C. Circuit's decision and remanded for application of that standard; it did not itself decide that the publishers deserved summary judgment on every disputed statement. (477 U.S. at 244, 254–257.)

Marshall, Blackmun, Powell, Stevens, and O'Connor joined White. Brennan dissented separately. Rehnquist dissented with Chief Justice Burger, producing a 6–3 division. The complete report includes seven Court footnotes, three Brennan footnotes, both dissents, and an opening amici note. (Id. at 243, 257–273.)

## Publication, evidence, and proceedings
Liberty Lobby was a nonprofit corporation describing itself as a citizens' lobby; Willis Carto was its founder and treasurer. The October 1981 issue of *The Investigator* published two longer articles concerning Carto and Francis Yockey, introduced by a shorter article about an alleged neo-Nazi underground. The articles portrayed Liberty Lobby and Carto as neo-Nazi, anti-Semitic, racist, and Fascist. They sued in the federal district court in the District of Columbia under diversity jurisdiction, alleging that 28 statements and two illustrations were false and derogatory. Defendants included publisher Jack Anderson, corporate president and chief executive Bill Adkins, and Investigator Publishing Co. The opinion recites accusations in the articles rather than adopting their truth. (Id. at 244–245.)

After discovery, defendants moved for summary judgment. They contended that the plaintiffs were public figures subject to the constitutional actual-malice requirement and could not prove that defendants knew the statements were false or recklessly disregarded their truth. Charles Bermant, the employee who wrote the two longer articles, submitted an affidavit describing substantial research, numerous sources, and his continuing belief in the articles' accuracy. A supporting appendix identified sources for the challenged statements. Anderson's shorter introduction relied exclusively on Bermant's information. (Id. at 244–245 & n.2.)

The plaintiffs pointed to alleged inaccuracies and sources they considered patently unreliable, arguing that the defendants had inadequately verified the articles. They also offered evidence that editor William McGaw had described the articles to Adkins before publication as terrible and ridiculous. That was evidence bearing on possible malice, rather than an adjudicated finding that the articles were false or their authors acted knowingly. (Id. at 246.)

The district court treated the plaintiffs as limited-purpose public figures and granted defendants summary judgment, reasoning that Bermant's research and reliance on numerous sources precluded actual malice. The plaintiffs did not challenge their public-figure status on appeal. The D.C. Circuit affirmed as to 21 challenged items but reversed as to nine. It believed that applying clear-and-convincing proof at summary judgment would improperly turn a search for minimum supporting facts into an evaluation of their weight. It therefore asked whether a jury could find the statements false, defamatory, and malicious without incorporating the heightened proof requirement. The Supreme Court reviewed that legal standard. (Id. at 246–247 & n.3.)

## Material facts and genuine disputes answer different questions
A factual disagreement defeats summary judgment only if it concerns a material fact and is genuine. Substantive law identifies material facts: a dispute matters when its resolution could affect the outcome under the elements of the governing claim. Disagreement over an irrelevant detail does not require trial. The burden of persuasion does not determine whether a fact belongs to an element; it determines what evidence can support the necessary finding about that fact. (Id. at 247–248.)

Genuineness asks whether the evidence permits a reasonable jury to return a verdict for the nonmoving party. The judge does not resolve the competing accounts or decide which witnesses tell the truth. But a bare allegation, a merely colorable showing, or an evidentiary scintilla that could not support a verdict does not become a triable dispute simply because an opponent denies it. A properly supported motion calls for specific supporting facts, rather than resting on the pleading. The threshold question is whether a trial is needed to resolve an issue that reasonable factfinders could decide for either side. (Id. at 248–250.)

White connected that inquiry to a directed verdict under Rule 50. The timing and material differ: summary judgment generally concerns a pretrial documentary record, while a directed verdict concerns evidence admitted at trial. The central sufficiency question remains whether reasonable jurors could disagree or whether only one legally permissible conclusion exists. The rule does not require the judge to make factual findings, although explanatory findings can aid appellate review. (Id. at 250–252 & n.6.)

## Why the trial burden informs evidentiary sufficiency
One cannot identify a reasonable plaintiff's verdict without knowing what the plaintiff must prove. In an ordinary civil case, the evidence must permit the jury to find the relevant fact by a preponderance. In a public-figure libel case, actual malice must be established with convincing clarity. The judge therefore asks whether the record, favorably viewed for the opponent, could allow a reasonable jury to find the requisite knowledge or reckless disregard by clear and convincing evidence. Evidence adequate under a lesser trial burden is not necessarily adequate under the greater one. (Id. at 252–255.)

The majority used criminal acquittal and directed-verdict decisions to illustrate the relationship between trial proof and threshold sufficiency. A criminal trial judge assessing sufficiency asks whether a reasonable jury could find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. The judge does not use the ordinary civil burden simply because the judge, rather than the jury, decides the screening motion. Similarly, a public-figure libel plaintiff cannot avoid the actual trial standard at the summary-judgment stage. This reasoning concerns the governing evidentiary standard generally, rather than establishing a procedural privilege confined to newspaper defendants. (Id. at 252–254; Brennan dissent, 257–258 n.1.)

This holding does not authorize weighing evidence on affidavits. The opponent's evidence is to be believed and all justifiable inferences drawn in that party's favor. The court assesses the legal sufficiency of that favorable version under the actual burden; it does not choose a more credible version. White also retained caution in granting summary judgment and acknowledged that a judge may consider a full trial the better course in an appropriate case. (Id. at 254–255.)

## State of mind, discovery, and issues the Court reserved
The plaintiffs argued that summary judgment should rarely, if ever, be available where the defendant's state of mind is disputed, because a jury might disbelieve the defendant's professions of innocent intent. White rejected a categorical exception. A plaintiff needs affirmative evidence supporting a verdict; mere possible disbelief of a denial ordinarily does not supply proof of the contrary proposition. That requirement applies even when evidence is held by the defendant, provided the plaintiff has had a full opportunity for discovery. Concrete circumstantial evidence can qualify; an assertion that cross-examination might reveal malice does not automatically suffice. (Id. at 256–257 & n.7.)

The Court expressly did not decide the movant's initial production burden, an issue associated with the separate *Celotex* decision. It assumed the initial burden had been satisfied. It likewise assumed ample discovery and preserved the former Rule 56(f) protection where essential opposition information was unavailable. These qualifications prevent converting Anderson into a rule that any unsupported motion immediately compels a complete merits presentation by the plaintiff. The former subsection references describe the rule applied in 1986. (Id. at 250 nn.4–5.)

## Brennan's dissent
Brennan believed the majority changed summary-judgment law while presenting the change as a straightforward restatement. In his reading, the cited conspiracy decisions asked whether substantive law permitted the particular inferences asserted, rather than whether the judge considered the plaintiff's evidence sufficiently strong or one-sided. His second footnote also contrasted White's warnings against expansive judicial weighing in the contemporaneous *Matsushita* dissent with the majority's language here. (Id. at 257–264 & nn.1–3.)

Brennan's practical objection was that directions to consider evidentiary quality and quantity were difficult to reconcile with the prohibition on weighing evidence. A documentary proceeding could become a complete paper trial, increasing preparation costs and threatening the civil jury's role. His hypothetical contrasted one plaintiff's eyewitness with 100 opposing witnesses: the judge still must not resolve credibility merely by counting witnesses or assigning evidentiary weight. Once a plaintiff offers evidence or permissible inferences supporting every necessary element, Brennan would leave whether that showing is clear and convincing to the factfinder. He rejected the majority's threshold incorporation of the persuasion burden. (Id. at 265–268.)

## Rehnquist and Burger's dissent
Rehnquist characterized the new approach as an added procedural requirement, rather than simply application of substantive law. He faulted the majority especially for failing to apply its formulation to this record, leaving lower courts with abstract instructions. His hypothetical involved a witness claiming the reporter admitted serious doubts, despite that witness's perjury conviction and several contradictory witnesses. If credibility genuinely remains the jury's task, he asked, when would the higher proof standard change the screening result? (Id. at 268–271.)

He also questioned the criminal analogy, noting that criminal pretrial screening and trial sufficiency are different procedures. Burden formulations may influence a factfinder's judgment but need not supply workable rules for a pretrial judge. He predicted inconsistent summary-judgment decisions without corresponding benefits. These objections supported dissent from the Court's judgment and standard; they were not a holding that the publishers had actually acted maliciously. (Id. at 271–273.)
