# Dodson v. Shrader
824 S.W.2d 545 (Tenn. 1992) | Contracts

## Decision and setting
Tennessee’s Supreme Court changed the restitution consequences of a minor’s disaffirmance of a purchase in a fair transaction. It adopted a qualified rule permitting reasonable deductions for use, depreciation, and willful or negligent damage rather than automatically returning the entire purchase price. It remanded for factual determinations concerning the truck and responsibility for its damage. Justice O’Brien wrote; Chief Justice Reid and Justices Drowota, Daughtrey, and Anderson concurred, making the decision unanimous. No separate Supreme Court opinion appears. The decision was issued January 27, 1992. (545–546, 549–550.)

## Purchase, damage, and attempted return
Sixteen-year-old Joseph Dodson bought a used 1984 pickup truck from Burns and Mary Shrader’s dealership in April 1987. He paid $4,900 cash borrowed from his girlfriend’s grandmother. The sellers did not ask his age, and he did not misrepresent it; Mr. Shrader testified that he believed Dodson was eighteen or nineteen. That belief was not a finding that Dodson deceived the sellers. (546.)

In December the truck developed mechanical problems. A mechanic suspected a burnt valve but would have needed to inspect the engine to be certain. Dodson either did not want to pay for repairs or lacked the money. He continued driving, and the engine failed in January. He parked the truck at his parents’ home, where he lived, and asked the sellers to rescind and refund the purchase price. They refused the tender and refund. After he appealed the initial judicial dismissal, the sellers again declined to accept the truck without compensation for depreciation. Before the circuit-court trial, a hit-and-run driver struck the parked truck’s left front fender. Mr. Shrader estimated its trial value at $500 because of engine and body damage; the Supreme Court did not adopt that estimate as a final valuation. (546.)

## Procedure and competing remedial positions
Dodson sued in general sessions court to rescind and recover his payment. That court dismissed the warrant, and he took a de novo appeal to circuit court. In November 1988 the circuit judge, reluctantly following earlier common-law decisions, ordered the sellers to refund $4,900 upon tender and delivery of the truck. The Court of Appeals affirmed through Judge Todd’s opinion, with Judge Cantrell concurring separately and Judge Koch dissenting. Those were lower-court writings, not separate votes in the Supreme Court’s unanimous decision. The Supreme Court addressed whether recovery must be the full price or could account for the truck’s loss in value while with the minor. (546.)

## Evolution of minority protection
The court traced Tennessee law from distinctions among beneficial, prejudicial, and uncertain contracts toward the more modern conception of a minor’s contract as voidable rather than inherently void. Voidability allowed the minor to retain advantageous bargains or disaffirm injudicious ones. The doctrine protected immature judgment against exploitation, but equitable principles also resisted converting the protective shield into a means of injuring good-faith counterparties. (546–547.)

The court compared two minority approaches among other states. One reduced recovery by the benefit actually received through use; another allowed deductions for use, depreciation, or deterioration of what the minor had possessed. It selected a modified version of the Oregon approach. Its extended discussion of the common law’s capacity to adapt explained why inherited doctrine could respond to modern minors’ commercial activity. The court reasoned that minors often work, earn, and spend money independently, and that merchants should be able to transact fairly with them without invariably refunding the entire payment after an article has been used or destroyed. That reasoning changed restitution, rather than generally declaring minors fully capable of binding contracts. (547–550.)

## Qualified deduction rule
The adopted rule requires a fair and reasonable contract, no overreaching or undue influence, payment toward the purchase price, and the minor’s receipt and use of the purchased article. In those circumstances the minor may not recover the money paid without allowing the seller reasonable compensation for use, depreciation, and willful or negligent damage while the article was in the minor’s hands. The rule does not apply if the seller practiced fraud or imposition, the contract was unfair, or an unfair advantage induced the purchase. Whether the seller overreached and the fair market value of returned property are questions for the trier of fact. Thus the decision does not automatically authorize the full difference between price and a seller’s asserted current value, or apply a deduction despite an unfair transaction. (549.)

## Matters reserved and exact disposition
The Supreme Court left to the trial court whether continuing to drive after learning of the engine problem involved gross negligence or intentional conduct. The record did not establish whether the sellers had asserted a tort-damage counterclaim. It also left the hit-and-run damage’s amount and allocation between buyer and sellers to the trier of fact, recognizing that this damage occurred after the first tender was refused. Fair market value at tender remained unresolved. The court therefore did not set Dodson’s net refund, decide that he owed the entire engine-loss amount, or charge him automatically for the later collision. (550.)

The case was remanded for further proceedings under the new rule, displacing the lower courts’ unconditional full-refund approach; appellate costs were divided equally. The textbook labels the result a reversal, while the actual opinion’s final directive expressly states remand and equal costs.
