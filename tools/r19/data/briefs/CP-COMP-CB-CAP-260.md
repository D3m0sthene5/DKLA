# Conaughty v. Nichols

**42 N.Y. 83 (1870) · New York Court of Appeals · March 17, 1870 · Civil Procedure**

**Assigned passage:** CivPro.pdf, physical pages 316–317, printed pages 598–599. The account begins below the separator on 316; its judicial quotation and the complete attached Note and Questions end before Garrity on 317.

**Decision in one sentence.** A plaintiff who pleaded and proved the facts establishing a contract right to sale proceeds could recover despite additionally alleging conversion: the surplus tort allegation did not justify a nonsuit where defendants knew the operative facts and claimed no surprise. 42 N.Y. at 85–89.

## Concrete dispute and procedure

During December 1860 and January 1861, Hugh Conaughty consigned dried fruit and butter to New York City commission merchants Lemuel Nichols and Finch Van Zandt for sale. They sold the goods for $690.82. After sales expenses, $618.43 remained payable to Conaughty. His complaint alleged the consignment, sale, balance, demand, and refusal to pay, then added that defendants had converted the proceeds to their own use. He demanded the balance, interest, and costs. These detailed facts appear in the report's procedural statement, not merely in an abstract headnote. Id. at 83–84.

After Conaughty presented his evidence, defendants sought a nonsuit. They argued, among other grounds, that commission agents could be liable on contract but that no conversion had been shown, and that the plaintiff's proof therefore failed to establish the cause of action pleaded. They also asserted a joint-ownership objection concerning John Conaughty; the reported judicial reasoning resolves the pleading-theory problem rather than separately analyzing that objection. Id.

Conaughty asked to strike the conversion allegation and maintained that the action rested on contract. The referee refused the amendment because it would change the form of action from tort to contract, then nonsuited him because the pleaded theory was tort while the proof established contract. The General Term reversed. Defendants appealed that reversal to the Court of Appeals. Id. at 84–85. Thus the final court's affirmance favored the plaintiff; it did not affirm the referee's nonsuit.

## Issue and governing code provisions

The issue was whether an unnecessary tort allegation could defeat recovery when the existing complaint already contained, and the evidence established, the facts needed for a contractual claim. Judge Ingalls explained that deleting the conversion language would leave a complaint plainly adapted to the demonstrated contract claim. The court refused to let the unnecessary addition undo those otherwise adequate allegations. Id. at 85.

The court grounded that conclusion in the New York Code's replacement of former pleading forms with a concise statement of operative facts. It invoked sections 140 and 142, liberal construction under section 159, the ability to strike irrelevant or redundant matter under section 160, and sections 169–170's treatment of variance according to actual prejudice and provision for corresponding findings or amendment. Taken together, these provisions sought to prevent procedural form from sacrificing a substantiated right. Id. at 85–87. The decision applied historical state code pleading, not the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure.

## Reasoning and limits

**Facts alleged and proved supplied the required foundation.** The complaint fully described defendants' receipt and sale of the consigned property and their unpaid obligation to remit the net proceeds. Those allegations supported contract recovery and were borne out by proof. Conversion added a potentially different legal characterization, but it was unnecessary to that established basis for relief. Under the Code, unnecessary allegations could be disregarded at trial or removed earlier by motion. Id. at 86–87.

**Defendants had notice and no claimed surprise.** Because the facts were already stated, defendants knew what they had to meet. They could have moved before trial to strike the extra language, obtain a more definite pleading, or require an election of theories. Having accepted the pleading without those measures, they could not use its surplus allegation to prevent recovery on the facts disclosed and proved. The court found no claimed surprise. Id. at 88.

**The contrary precedent involved a different defect.** Defendants relied on Walter v. Bennett, where the plaintiff alleged wrongful detention of a draft or money and failed to establish the alleged wrong. Ingalls read that decision as requiring a claimant to allege and prove a basis for recovery. It did not require dismissal merely because a complaint with adequate contract facts also contained unnecessary tort language. Id. at 87–88.

The court expressly distinguished a hypothetical attempt at trial to add the **missing facts** of a contract claim to a complaint that had alleged only wrongful detention. It said that different application should be denied. Here Conaughty did not need to introduce an unpleaded factual foundation; it was there from the outset. Id. at 88. The holding therefore does not authorize recovery on any theory regardless of the facts actually pleaded or the prejudice of a late change.

Even if Conaughty originally intended to recover for conversion, a mistaken legal theory did not require dismissal when the stated and established facts independently justified contract recovery. Making him bring another action would produce avoidable delay, expense, and multiple suits without advancing substantial justice. The objection that the summons sought relief rather than the particular recovery claimed likewise belonged in a motion rather than as a trial trap. Id. at 88–89.

## Judgment, assignment, and source distinctions

The court **affirmed the General Term with costs and directed judgment absolute for the plaintiff**. The report expressly records that all concurred in affirmance; it supplies no separate judicial writing or numerical tally. Id. at 89. Judge Ingalls wrote the opinion.

The report's final note describes the answer's denials of any obligation to remit the specific proceeds and of conversion, then points to another case about whether conversion allegations were traversable. It is explicitly signed by the **reporter**, not a separate judicial footnote or holding. That note was read in full and distinguished from the court's reasoning.

The assignment contrasts Conaughty with Jones v. Winsor and quotes Whittier's criticism of treating failure to seek clarification as evidence of adequate notice. Those are editorial questions and criticism, not additional Conaughty holdings. They help isolate the tension between requiring a definite chosen theory and allowing proved facts already pleaded to support a different legal characterization. The source's procedural statement places the amendment request at the close of plaintiff's evidence; the assignment compresses that sequence as after trial, which should not be understood to mean after a completed final judgment.
