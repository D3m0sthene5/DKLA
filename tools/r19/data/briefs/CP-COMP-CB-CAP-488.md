# Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Mackey

**351 U.S. 427 (1956).** The Supreme Court decided the case on June 11, 1956. Justice Burton wrote the Court’s opinion. Justice Frankfurter concurred in this judgment, joined by Justice Harlan, while dissenting from the same-day Cold Metal decision in a shared separate opinion at 439–444. The assigned Sears account is CivPro physical pages 616–617, printed pages 1219–1221.

## Facts and procedural path

Mackey and his company, Time Saver Tools, sued Sears in federal court in Illinois in 1953. The operative controversy involved four claims. Count I alleged Sherman Act violations damaging three ventures: a nursery-lamp business, a commission arrangement for selling another company’s tools, and Time Saver’s carbide-tipped lathe-bit and other products business. Mackey sought $570,000 in trebled damages on alleged losses totaling $190,000, as well as equitable relief. Count II invoked diversity and sought ordinary damages for destruction of the lamp business and Mackey’s losses on guaranteed obligations. Count III asserted unlawful inducement of breach of the commission agreement; Count IV asserted unfair competition and patent infringement damaging Time Saver. Count V was abandoned and Count VI duplicated others, so the Court disregarded them. These were allegations, not findings that Sears had committed the conduct. (429–431.)

The district court dismissed Counts I and II without leave to amend while leaving Counts III and IV unresolved on the answers. It expressly determined there was no just reason for delay and directed entry of judgment. When the plaintiffs appealed, Sears moved to dismiss for lack of appellate jurisdiction. The Seventh Circuit denied that motion, relying on §1291 and amended Rule 54(b). The Supreme Court reviewed jurisdiction, not whether the dismissed claims were legally sufficient or Sears was liable. (428–431.)

## Issue and holding

Did §1291 permit an appeal of the finally dismissed claims in a multiple-claims action, supported by a proper Rule 54(b) certification, while other claims continued? The Court held that it did. The amended rule preserved the requirement that an individual claim be finally adjudicated while permitting the district judge to release that final decision for appeal before completion of the entire action. Both genuine finality and certification were necessary; neither could be replaced by merely labeling an order final. (434–438.)

## Why the certification framework was needed

Earlier practice generally treated an entire controversy as the unit for appeal. A decision could conclusively dispose of an individual claim yet await the rest of the action before appellate review. Liberal federal joinder increased the range of claims that could coexist in one proceeding. Original Rule 54(b) responded by allowing review of sufficiently separate claims, but determining whether a claim was independent enough could be uncertain. Parties risked letting the time for appeal expire while assuming that the rest of the case had to finish, or filed protective appeals whenever the answer was doubtful. (431–434 & nn.3–7.)

The amended rule provided a more definite trigger. To release fewer than all claims for appeal, the district court had to make an **express determination that there was no just reason for delay** and an **express direction for entry of judgment**. Without those acts, partial dispositions remained subject to revision before the entire case was resolved. The Court described the trial judge as a dispatcher, selecting when an already final individual decision was ready for appeal. The rule’s negative effect gave an adversely affected party assurance that appeal time would not start merely because a partial claim appeared finally decided without the required certification. (434–436 & n.8.)

That benefit did not mean a district judge could create appellate jurisdiction by fiat. The Court identified three possible objections: no cognizable claim had been decided, its disposition was not truly final, or certification was an abuse of discretion. An incomplete disposition could not become final through certification. Conversely, a final individual claim could be released at an appropriate time subject to appellate review of the district court’s exercise of discretion. (436–437.)

## Application and the statutory objection

The dismissed claims were cognizable claims for relief and were fully disposed of. The Court also found no abuse in permitting their appeal before Counts III and IV ended. The claims could be decided independently despite partial factual overlap. Note 9 specifically distinguished the basis of liability: Count I invoked the Sherman Act while Counts III and IV largely relied on common-law rights, although some facts overlapped; Count II was clearly independent of those remaining claims. The Court therefore did not require factual isolation in every detail. (436–437 & n.9.)

Sears argued that amended Rule 54(b) impermissibly enlarged the appellate jurisdiction supplied by §1291. The Court rejected the premise. The rule did not relax the finality required of an individual decision or supersede a jurisdictional statute. It administered that statutory requirement in the setting of multiple claims, replacing uncertain judicial-unit distinctions with supervised timing. Certification’s affirmative effect—allowing some final claims to be appealed earlier than before—did not make it invalid. Its negative effect also curtailed premature appeals by requiring a clear judicial determination before review of fewer than all claims. Both aspects fell within the rulemaking authority discussed in note 5. (433 n.5, 436–438.)

The majority declined to reach an alternative assertion of jurisdiction under the injunction-appeal provision then identified as §1292(1). Note 12 expressly reserved it because the §1291 and Rule 54(b) grounds sufficed. That reservation becomes important when comparing the separate opinion, which relied on the alternative route for one count. (438 n.12.)

## Frankfurter and Harlan’s different route to the same judgment

Frankfurter, joined by Harlan, agreed that this appeal could proceed but strongly criticized the broader rationale. He viewed separateness and independence as the established statutory boundary, not merely factors in district-court timing discretion. Rule 54(b) could give certainty and permit a district judge to withhold early review; it could not turn a connected portion of an unresolved organic controversy into a final decision within Congress’s statute. Statutory jurisdiction also required independent appellate judgment, not the narrow review ordinarily associated with an abuse-of-discretion standard. (439–444.)

On his account, Count II could be appealed because its transactions were independent of those in Counts III and IV. Count I shared transactions with those counts but could be reviewed as an interlocutory denial of injunctive relief under §1292(1). He therefore concurred in Sears’s judgment. In Cold Metal, by contrast, the unresolved counterclaim substantially arose from the transactions involved in the main claim, leading him and Harlan to dissent. Their six-part shared explanation warned that the majority’s approach could invite inconsistent and excessive piecemeal appeals. That was the separate Justices’ criticism, not a holding that the majority admitted unrestricted interlocutory review. (439–444, especially 443.)

## Judgment and assigned discrepancy

The Supreme Court **affirmed the Seventh Circuit’s denial of Sears’s motion to dismiss the appeal**. The circuit retained appellate jurisdiction to review the partial judgment. This was not an affirmance of the dismissal of Counts I and II on their merits, and it did not resolve Counts III and IV. (438.)
