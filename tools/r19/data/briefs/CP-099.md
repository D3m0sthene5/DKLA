Parties
Robert L. Schlagenhauf, the Greyhound bus driver, was a defendant in the passengers’ negligence action and
the petitioner challenging an examination order. Respondent Holder was the district judge named in the
mandamus proceeding. The underlying defendants also included Greyhound, Contract Carriers as owner of the
tractor, its driver Joseph McCorkhill, and National Lead as owner of the trailer. Contract Carriers and National
Lead requested the examinations while litigating responsibility for the collision with Greyhound and
Schlagenhauf. The caption reflects an extraordinary-writ proceeding against the judge, not an underlying tort
claim against Holder. 379 U.S. at 106–109 & n.1.
Material Facts
A Greyhound bus struck the rear of a tractor-trailer, injuring passengers. Greyhound alleged that the truck had
moved too slowly, failed to remain in its lane, and lacked adequate rear lights. Contract Carriers denied its own
negligence and charged that the bus driver’s negligence caused Greyhound’s loss. In a letter treated as part of
its answer, it also claimed that Schlagenhauf lacked the mental or physical capacity to drive. National Lead
later specifically alleged deficient eyesight in seeking damages for its trailer. These were litigants’ allegations,
not adjudicated medical findings. 379 U.S. at 106–108.
The request sought one examination in each of four specialties: internal medicine, ophthalmology, neurology,
and psychiatry. It listed alternative physicians for the judge to choose from, totaling nine names. Counsel’s
supporting affidavit stated that Schlagenhauf admitted seeing red lights for ten to fifteen seconds before the
collision without slowing or changing course, that another witness could see the trailer’s lights from one-half to
three-quarters of a mile away, and that Schlagenhauf had been involved in a similar prior collision. No specific
mental, neurological, or internal medical illness was identified. 379 U.S. at 107–108, 120–121.
Schlagenhauf did not seek damages for a medical condition or rely on a medical condition as a defense. Other
parties were attempting to make his condition an issue through their account of his driving. That distinction
determined what evidence they needed to supply before obtaining intrusive examinations. 379 U.S. at 119–120.
Procedural History
The passengers sued in federal court on diversity jurisdiction. After answers and Greyhound’s
property-damage cross-claim, Contract Carriers and National Lead sought the Rule 35 order. National Lead’s
own cross-claim against Greyhound and Schlagenhauf followed the initial examination request. The district
court ordered all nine listed examinations without a hearing, although only four had been requested. A second
materially identical order followed supplementary petitions. 379 U.S. at 106–109, 115–116.

Schlagenhauf sought mandamus to set the examination order aside. The Seventh Circuit denied relief, with one
judge dissenting. It treated the application of Rule 35 to a defendant and the in-controversy requirement as
reviewable, but did not decide good cause. After that denial, the district court corrected the order to four
examinations. The nine-examination error was therefore moot as a demand for specific relief, although it
remained relevant to whether the judge had carefully scrutinized the request. 379 U.S. at 109–112 & nn.3, 7.
The Supreme Court granted certiorari to review the unresolved questions concerning Rule 35. It sustained the
Rule’s availability against a defendant but vacated the appellate judgment and required reconsideration of the
examination order under the standards it articulated. This was review of discovery before final adjudication of
negligence or damages. 379 U.S. at 112–114, 120–122.
Issue
Does Rule 35 validly authorize mental or physical examination of a defendant, including on a codefendant’s
motion without an opposing pleading? What must establish that each particular condition is genuinely in
controversy and that each requested examination has good cause? Could those questions be addressed through
mandamus in the unusual posture of this first-impression challenge? 379 U.S. at 109–120.
Rule and Holding
Rule 35 applies to defendants as well as plaintiffs and, under Sibbach, is constitutional and authorized by the
Rules Enabling Act. The examinee must be a party to the action under the Rule then before the Court, but need
not be an opposing party in relation to the movant. Schlagenhauf’s status as a named defendant in the
passengers’ complaint satisfied that requirement. 379 U.S. at 112–116 & n.12.
The related in-controversy and good-cause conditions impose requirements beyond ordinary relevance. The
movant must affirmatively show that each condition sought to be examined is genuinely disputed and that each
specific examination is justified. Pleadings can themselves suffice when a litigant affirmatively relies on an
injury or medical defense; general accusations by another party did not suffice here. Availability of the
information by other means also matters. 379 U.S. at 118–120.
The record did not justify the internal-medicine, neurological, or psychiatric examinations. The specific vision
allegation and affidavit might support an eye examination, but that examination also required reconsideration
on remand. The Court did not hold that every requested examination was permanently forbidden. 379 U.S. at
120–122.
Judgment
The Supreme Court vacated the Seventh Circuit’s judgment and remanded the case to the district court for
reconsideration of the examination order and further proceedings consistent with its guidelines. It neither
determined accident liability nor approved the existing four-examination order. The district judge was to
reassess the vision examination along with the rest of the request. 379 U.S. at 121–122.
Reasoning
The Court first addressed why interlocutory review was available. Mandamus normally cannot replace an
appeal from final judgment, and ordinary disagreement with a judge’s good-cause determination is insufficient
absent clear abuse. Here, however, the petition raised a substantial and apparently unprecedented challenge to
the district court’s power to order examination of a defendant at all. The Court allowed the appellate court to
resolve the connected construction questions to avoid piecemeal resolution of the new problem. It expressly
cautioned that its ruling did not authorize mandamus whenever a later litigant alleged misapplication of the
resulting guidelines. 379 U.S. at 109–112.

On validity, the Rule’s reference to parties naturally included defendants. Sibbach had sustained compelled
examination of a plaintiff as a procedural measure, and its reasoning did not depend on a voluntary waiver of
bodily privacy through filing suit. An injured plaintiff’s need to seek redress can be as involuntary in practical
terms as a defendant’s need to defend. A waiver theory would also create difficulty when a defendant removes
a plaintiff’s state action to federal court. The Court therefore found no principled basis for making the Rule
available against one side only. 379 U.S. at 112–114.
The Court next rejected an additional opposing-party requirement. Schlagenhauf was already a party to the
action. Requiring a direct cross-claim between movant and examinee would encourage unnecessary pleadings
without serving the Rule’s discovery function. The later cross-claim made him an opposing party under even
the Seventh Circuit’s narrower interpretation, but the Supreme Court did not adopt that interpretation as a legal
prerequisite. 379 U.S. at 115–116.
The breadth of discovery did not erase the Rule’s explicit restrictions. If relevance alone supplied good cause,
that requirement would add nothing to the general discovery standard. The in-controversy requirement
similarly demands a real dispute about the particular condition, rather than a general allegation that some
unfitness might explain an accident. A showing sufficient for eyesight would not necessarily justify a
psychiatric examination. The inquiry therefore concerns the condition, the requested method, and the need for
that information, not merely the litigant’s status as a negligent-driver defendant. 379 U.S. at 117–119.
An affirmative showing does not require the movant to prove the merits before obtaining discovery. Nor must a
judge hold an evidentiary hearing in every case; affidavits or other appropriate materials may supply adequate
information. When a plaintiff puts an identified injury forward as a basis for compensation, the pleadings may
establish both the controversy and the need for examination of that injury. The same reasoning applies when a
defendant relies on a medical condition as a defense. Schlagenhauf had done neither, leaving the requesting
parties responsible for a more particularized showing. 379 U.S. at 119–120.
The affidavit described accidents and an apparent failure to react, but it did not identify a mental or
neurological illness or any internal medical condition requiring examination. An assumption that an accident
must reflect an underlying illness would make broad examinations routine in negligence cases. The specific
allegation about vision was stronger, especially when combined with the differing accounts of visibility, yet the
Court left that question for a careful reassessment rather than independently ordering the examination. 379 U.S.
at 120–122.
The order also needed to delimit conditions and scope. Broad specialty labels could authorize blood tests,
imaging, and other investigations with no demonstrated connection to the dispute. The initial selection of all
nine alternative doctors reinforced the concern that the request had not received the discriminating review the
Rule demanded, even after that number was corrected. 379 U.S. at 109 n.3, 121 n.16.
Separate Opinions
Justice Black, joined by Clark, agreed that the Rule applied equally to plaintiffs and defendants and required
controversy, good cause, and notice. He thought the record did show a controversy warranting some
examination: the account of two similar collisions, visible lights, and the absence of an express denial of
impairment supported inquiry. He criticized treating the pleadings as insufficient merely because they were
conclusory. Nevertheless, he agreed the order was overbroad and joined the remand so the judge could
formulate a properly limited order after receiving relevant facts. His opinion thus disagreed with the majority’s
assessment of the showing while accepting the remedial need for revision. 379 U.S. at 122–124 (Black, J.,
concurring in part and dissenting in part).

Justice Douglas supported setting the order aside but would deny the requested Rule 35 relief entirely on
remand. He distinguished examination of an injury affirmatively claimed by a plaintiff from an open-ended
search for conditions that might explain a defendant’s conduct. In his view, adversarial medical investigation
could expose defendants to coercion and could distort the jury’s evaluation. He would refer expansion of the
Rule to the rulemaking process for carefully designed safeguards. His position was not that Congress lacked
constitutional power to authorize such examinations. 379 U.S. at 124–127 (Douglas, J., dissenting in part).
Justice Harlan would affirm the denial of mandamus. Once the courts concluded that the district judge
possessed Rule 35 authority over a defendant, he saw no basis for using an extraordinary writ to review
ordinary application questions without clear abuse of discretion. Novelty and a desire to establish guidelines
did not, in his view, justify bypassing the final-judgment rule. The casebook omits this dissent; it is included
here from the full opinion because it explains the disagreement over the route of review. 379 U.S. at 127–129
(Harlan, J., dissenting).
Notes
A useful application separates four questions: the Rule’s authority over the person, whether the specified
condition is genuinely in controversy, good cause for the particular examination, and a sufficiently limited
order. The legal authority to examine a defendant answers only the first. A driver’s involvement in an accident
cannot automatically answer the remaining questions. 379 U.S. at 112–122.
The opinion’s comparison with a good-cause requirement for document production describes the Rules as they
then stood. It should not be used as an unqualified statement of present Rule 34. Likewise, the opinion’s
footnote on nonparty agents and proposed amendments is historical. This brief addresses the holding and its
reasoning rather than purporting to reproduce every current discovery provision. 379 U.S. at 115 n.12,
117–118.
