# Miami Nation of Indians of Indiana, Inc. v. United States Department of the Interior

255 F.3d 342 (7th Cir. 2001) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, posture, and decision

Posner wrote for a unanimous panel with Bauer and Coffey on June 15, 2001. The Miami Nation appealed the district court’s judgment sustaining Interior’s refusal to acknowledge it as a tribe. The circuit affirmed. It held that the recognition regulations supplied judicially manageable criteria and therefore permitted review, but the challenges to Interior’s decision failed. There are no judicial footnotes or separate writings. The reporter records denial of rehearing and rehearing en banc on September 6, but includes no additional reasoned order. (345–351.)

## Petition, regulatory requirements, and evidence

Congress authorized administration of Indian affairs through 25 U.S.C. §§ 2 and 9 without itself prescribing detailed tribal-recognition criteria. Interior’s 1978 regulation supplied seven: historical identification as Indian, a distinct community with relevant historical ancestry, continuing autonomous political influence or authority, a governing document, membership demonstrating historical tribal descent, predominantly nonoverlapping membership with other tribes, and absence of a congressional bar. In 1980 the Indiana Miami petitioned; in 1992 Interior denied recognition because the community and political-authority criteria were not met. The district court upheld that decision in 2000. (345–346.)

The court assumed for purposes of decision that the Indiana group was the tribe recognized in an 1854 treaty describing Indiana and Western Miamis. It nevertheless distinguished historical recognition from continuing existence. The roughly 4,700 Indiana Miamis were dispersed nationally; only about a third lived within five contiguous Indiana counties and were scattered among the population there. The record showed limited social interaction and reunion attendance. Their council performed little meaningful governance, although activity increased around the renewed recognition effort and included welfare and development programs. The court considered those charitable programs insufficient to establish political governance. These are the court’s account of the record, not a claim that common ancestry was disputed. (346–347.)

## Authority and political-question concerns

The group argued that Congress had not authorized Interior’s regulation. The court rejected that argument under the cited Indian-affairs provisions and relevant precedents. It accepted the prevailing view, uncontested by these parties, that Congress could set recognition criteria directly or through delegation, while explaining why tribal recognition differed from recognition of a foreign sovereign. (345–347.)

Posner separated two political-question rationales. One concerns questions beyond courts’ capacity to gather, evaluate, and resolve facts through law. The other concerns matters constitutionally committed exclusively to the political branches, regardless of whether judges could analyze them. Recognition of an Indian tribe implicated the former concern, but this case did not fall within an exclusive constitutional assignment barring judicial authority. (347–348.)

## Regulations that channel discretion and supply law to apply

The assigned quotation addresses what changes when the executive adopts legal criteria to channel otherwise broad discretion. Regulations can bring recognition within APA review by binding the agency until properly changed and supplying standards courts can apply. The requirements of reasoned decisionmaking and consistency prevent unexplained departures from governing rules. This principle does not depend on treating every agency policy choice as independently reviewable. (348–349.)

The opinion states two qualifications. A regulation may remain too open-ended to supply judicially usable law. And regulations cannot give courts authority over an area that the Constitution commits exclusively to nonjudicial branches. Neither exception barred this case: Interior’s seven criteria broke recognition into legal and factual questions of a kind courts could answer, and the exclusive-authority branch did not govern. (349.)

Reviewability therefore allowed the Nation to challenge interpretation and application of the regulations. It did not require the agency to accept the recommendation of the anthropologist it had hired. Hiring an adviser did not delegate decisionmaking authority to her. Interior examined her report, identified errors and unsupported conclusions, and obtained additional information undermining it. The court found no arbitrary disregard of her work. (349.)

## Earlier recognition and the asserted abandonment standard

The Nation further argued that a historically recognized tribe could not be evaluated as an applicant never recognized before. It claimed that principles governing abandonment should control. Interior had withdrawn recognition in 1897, but the court expressly declined to decide whether that withdrawal was valid, stating that it could ignore the episode for the present analysis. (349–350.)

The court explained that even wholly unchanneled discretion must actually be exercised: if an agency wrongly applies a binding rule and thereby fails to exercise discretion belonging to it, remand may be necessary. The court did not stop there, however. It concluded that the acknowledgment regulation itself contemplated previously recognized groups, through historical federal identification. A 1994 amendment also addressed previously recognized tribes. These provisions undercut the premise that only a tribe’s voluntary abandonment could end relevant recognition. (350.)

The court distinguished rights of an existing tribe from the prior question whether a tribe continued to exist as an organized entity. It found common descent, without meaningful political structure, territory, or substantial social ties, inadequate on this record. The cited cases protecting existing tribes against involuntary loss of rights presupposed an existing rights holder; the panel considered that premise absent here. (350–351.)

Finally, it held that even if Interior had erred by failing expressly to consider abandonment principles, any error would be harmless given the record. The opinion expressly rejected the belief that such an error actually occurred. That alternative holding should not be converted into an affirmative finding of an agency mistake. (351.)

## Exact result and assigned-source qualifications

The circuit affirmed the district judgment upholding denial of recognition. It did not dismiss the case wholesale as a political question, order recognition, or remand for a fresh exercise of discretion. Its reasoning couples reviewability under agency-created standards with rejection of the merits challenges. (351.)
