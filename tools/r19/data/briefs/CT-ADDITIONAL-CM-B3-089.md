# Indiana State Highway Commission v. Curtis
704 N.E.2d 1015 (Ind. 1998), December 18, 1998 | Contracts

## Court, underlying dispute, and settlement terms
Justice Boehm wrote on transfer for the Indiana Supreme Court; Chief Justice Shepard and Justices Dickson, Sullivan, and Selby concurred. The five judges agreed, with no separate opinion. The principal interested plaintiffs were Carl and Lorraine Sutton; previous landowners Billy and Virginia Curtis also appeared as plaintiffs. The Highway Commission had been replaced by the Indiana Department of Transportation, or INDOT, in 1990. (1015–1016 & nn. 1–2, 1020.)

In 1985 the Suttons granted the State an easement over commercial property for drainage work along State Road 10. In 1988 they sued the State, the Highway Commission, and contractor Brown, alleging that the work destroyed their septic system, restricted property access, and made the parking lot unusable, causing lost business. Those allegations supplied the underlying tort dispute; the transfer decision addressed the later settlement’s enforceability, rather than deciding liability for the drainage damage. (1016–1017.)

Four days before the scheduled January 1997 trial, the State’s private lawyer Michael Blaize discussed settlement with the Suttons’ lawyer Gary Matthews. Blaize expressly said that money required the Governor’s approval and that a septic-system easement across State property required INDOT’s approval. A deputy attorney general, consulted during negotiations, said there was no reason to believe the Governor would disapprove; that reassurance was relayed to Matthews. The lawyers agreed on a monetary amount, an easement to install a new septic system, and payment within 45 days. Matthews wrote and faxed the agreement, which Blaize signed and returned. Paragraph seven expressly conditioned access through the existing guardrail and the associated driveway on INDOT’s approval. The parties told the trial court they had settled, apparently without identifying the conditions. (1017.)

## Enforcement proceedings and issues on transfer
After 45 days the State had neither paid nor furnished the easement, and neither required approval had occurred. The Suttons moved to enforce. Following a September hearing, the trial court found a binding settlement and ordered payment, an easement, and attorney’s fees. The Governor approved the monetary settlement between trial and appeal, so the outstanding approval issue concerned the easement. The Court of Appeals affirmed enforcement but reversed the fee award. The Supreme Court granted the State’s transfer petition. (1017.)

Trial Rule 52 required deference to supported factual findings unless clearly erroneous. But the conclusion that those findings established an enforceable settlement was a legal conclusion. The trial court itself found that approval was required, while neither the record nor a finding showed that INDOT had approved or relinquished the condition. (1017–1018.)

The State argued that its lawyers could not eliminate statutory or contractual approvals. The plaintiffs asserted that Blaize and the deputy attorneys general had authority to bind their client, and the trial court considered lengthy delay inequitable. The Supreme Court separated the two restrictions: the express INDOT condition was enforceable under ordinary contract principles, while the Governor’s authority over compromise of a tort claim also rested on statute. (1018, 1020.)

## Express condition, waiver, and forfeiture
An express condition generally must occur before the duty it qualifies becomes enforceable. The parties themselves included the INDOT condition. Waiver required voluntary, intentional relinquishment of a known right; the mere expiration of 45 days, without another relevant fact, did not show waiver or estoppel. The trial court’s uncertainty whether the settlement had been submitted for approval did not supply the missing evidence that approval was given or intentionally dispensed with. (1018–1019.)

Failure to obtain approval was not necessarily repudiation; it could be insistence on the agreed bargain. A condition’s harsh operation alone did not excuse it. Although extreme forfeiture or penalty might justify excusing a nonessential condition, no extreme forfeiture was shown here and approval was essential. An easement affecting a highway safety rail presented obvious public-safety concerns, making INDOT review and specificity about location and use reasonable. (1019.)

## Good-faith consideration is not a promise to approve
The prevention doctrine does not mean every unfulfilled condition estops a contracting party from relying on it. Parties have an implied duty to make reasonable, good-faith efforts to satisfy conditions, and cannot sabotage the agreement or cause failure in bad faith. But where the condition reserves approval to an internal division or component, the duty is to consider approval in good faith, rather than to guarantee approval. Rejection for sound reasons or newly discovered information remains possible when the contract preserves that choice. (1019.)

The passage of time alone did not establish bad faith, and the record contained no other evidence of bad-faith evaluation. The court therefore permitted the State to rely on the absent approval. The approval requirement protected the State; the 45-day limit protected the Suttons by allowing revival of their original lawsuit when approval did not occur. The limit did not itself convert the conditional settlement into an enforceable one. This decision does not authorize deliberate refusal to undertake good-faith consideration; it finds no evidence of that refusal here. (1019.)

Preserving real approval conditions can facilitate settlements. A negotiator may lack final authority or need technical review that cannot occur during last-minute courthouse discussions. Expected approval is still different from assured approval, and parties can agree provisionally without losing the protection of the express condition. (1019–1020.)

## Governor’s statutory authority and exact judgment
The Tort Claims Act independently required the Governor’s approval for compromise of a tort claim against the State. The court interpreted the provision authorizing the Governor to compromise as reserving ultimate settlement authority to that office. Ordinary private-client attorney principles could not override the applicable statute. The opinion speculated that the requirement focused accountability and protected public assets, but its validity did not depend on proving that policy rationale. (1020.)

The court reversed the trial court’s enforcement order as to the easement provisions and summarily affirmed the Court of Appeals’ decision concerning fees—which had reversed the fee award. It did not undo a still-unapproved monetary settlement: the Governor had approved that component by the appellate stage. Nor did it decide the merits or damages of the original drainage tort suit. Curtis limits cooperation duties by the contractual allocation of approval authority, preserving a good-faith-review obligation without transforming it into a promise of a favorable outcome. (1017, 1019–1020.)
