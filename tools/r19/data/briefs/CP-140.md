Parties
Cromwell sued the County of Sac, Iowa, to collect on county bonds and attached interest coupons. He was the
plaintiff below and the party seeking reversal in the Supreme Court. The county defended on the ground that a
prior judgment against recovery on other coupons barred his present claim. Samuel C. Smith had been the
named plaintiff in that first suit, but the county offered evidence that Smith had pursued it solely for
Cromwell's use and benefit. The identity of the beneficial litigant and the scope of what had been decided were
separate issues. 94 U.S. at 352, 359–360.
Material Facts
The county's voters had authorized $10,000 in bonds to finance a courthouse. The county judge issued the
bonds to a contractor, Meserey. Immediately after receiving them, the contractor gave one bond to the judge as
a gratuity. The courthouse was never built under the contract. These circumstances had been held sufficient to
show fraud and illegality in the bonds' inception, requiring a holder seeking enforcement to prove acquisition
for value before maturity. Id. at 359.
The current suit concerned four $1,000 bonds and four $100 interest coupons. The bonds had been issued in
1860, were payable to bearer in New York in successive years from 1868 through 1871, and carried annual
interest of ten percent. A coupon was a separate promise to make an interest payment and could be the subject
of its own collection action. The prior Smith suit concerned 25 earlier-maturing coupons, rather than the exact
demands presented in Cromwell's later action. Id. at 352, 359–362.
In the first action, the claimant had not proved that value was given for the coupons. The prior judgment
therefore denied recovery in light of the established irregularity. In the later action, Cromwell offered evidence
that he had acquired the particular bonds and coupons now sued upon before maturity for value. Whether that
evidence could be heard was decisive because a qualifying bona fide holder could enforce these negotiable
obligations despite defects in their original issuance. Id. at 359–360.
Procedural History
Smith first sued the county on earlier interest coupons. The court made findings concerning the courthouse
transaction and the absence of proof of value. Judgment was entered for the county, and the Supreme Court
affirmed in that prior proceeding. That judgment remained final; Cromwell's later suit was not a motion to
reopen it. Id. at 359.
When Cromwell brought the second action on other instruments, the county introduced the earlier judgment
and evidence connecting him to the Smith suit as its beneficial owner. The trial court treated the judgment as
preclusive and excluded Cromwell's offered proof of acquisition for value before maturity. It again entered
judgment for the county. Cromwell sought Supreme Court review by writ of error. Id. at 352, 359–360.

The Court upheld admission of the evidence showing Cromwell's beneficial interest in the earlier litigation. It
nevertheless held that the earlier judgment did not decide his acquisition for value of these different
instruments. Excluding his evidence on that question was reversible error. Id. at 360.
Issue
When a prior action between the same real parties has ended in a judgment on the merits, does that judgment
bar every ground that could have been presented in a later suit on different bonds or coupons? Specifically, did
failure to prove value in the earlier coupon action prevent Cromwell from proving that he gave value for the
distinct obligations now in suit? Could the county also show that the earlier named plaintiff had litigated for
Cromwell's benefit? Id. at 352–353, 359–360.
Rule and Holding
A final merits judgment on the same claim or demand bars a later action on that claim, including grounds of
recovery or defense that could have been presented. In a later action on a different claim, preclusion extends
only to matters actually litigated and determined as a basis of the earlier judgment. The Court distinguished the
broader claim bar from the narrower effect now commonly called issue preclusion. Id. at 352–353.
The prior judgment conclusively established the original fraud or illegality affecting the bonds. It did not
establish that Cromwell had failed to give value for every other instrument in the series. Because the later
action concerned different demands, he could offer evidence of value and timely acquisition of those
instruments. The evidence identifying him as the beneficial party to the earlier action was admissible despite
Smith's legal title. Id. at 359–360.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the judgment for the county and remanded for a new trial. Cromwell obtained an
opportunity to prove the facts necessary to enforce the instruments; he did not receive a Supreme Court money
judgment. The first judgment on the earlier coupons remained undisturbed, and the original issuance defect
remained established. Id. at 359–360.
Reasoning
Field first separated two questions often obscured by the broad expression res judicata. When the same claim
returns, the original merits judgment settles the demand as a whole. A party cannot relitigate it by producing a
defense or ground of recovery that was available but omitted. The opinion illustrates this with a judgment on a
promissory note: later assertions of payment, forgery, or lack of consideration cannot ordinarily reopen the
same claim. Id. at 352–353.
Different claims require a narrower inquiry. The court must identify the actual point disputed and decided in
the first action. A fact common to both suits may be settled even though the second demand differs, but its
possible relevance to the earlier pleadings is insufficient. Otherwise, a judgment would determine hypothetical
controversies that no tribunal actually resolved. Id. at 353–356.
The cited trespass and contract examples show how the distinction works. A prior judgment about a particular
trespass bars another recovery for that trespass. A determination of title necessary to that judgment can also
settle title in a later action for a different trespass. Conversely, a general verdict resting on several possible
counts does not necessarily decide the validity of a contract alleged in just one count. Evidence about what was
actually submitted and decided may be necessary to establish the judgment's narrower effect. Id. at 353–356.

The Court also gave a practical reason for limiting preclusion across different demands. A litigant might refrain
from presenting a particular argument because the first claim was small, evidence was difficult to obtain,
litigation was expensive, or personal circumstances discouraged a complete contest. Those choices do not
ordinarily justify treating every unpresented proposition as conclusively determined for another claim. Finality
still governs the original demand; the limitation concerns the reach of that finality into distinct litigation. Id. at
356–358.
Applying those principles required careful reconstruction of the first judgment. It had established that the
issuance circumstances were fraudulent or illegal and that the coupons could not be enforced by a claimant
who failed to establish protected holder status. That finding affected the entire series and was not open to
contradiction in Cromwell's second suit. The Court did not permit him to start over on whether the courthouse
transaction was regular. Id. at 359.
But the first failure of proof concerned acquisition of the particular coupons then sued upon. Whether value had
been paid for another bond or coupon was a different factual matter. The earlier failure did not even
presumptively establish nonpayment for those other instruments. Because they were negotiable obligations
issued under voter authorization and carried the relevant recitals, proof of bona fide acquisition for value before
maturity could produce an enforceable claim. Excluding that proof treated a distinct question as though it had
already been adjudicated. Id. at 359–360.
This conclusion did not depend on treating Cromwell as a stranger to the original suit. The county was entitled
to prove that Smith sued for his sole benefit. The earlier finding that Smith held and owned the coupons
established legal title sufficient to maintain that action; it was compatible with an equitable or beneficial
interest in Cromwell. Thus party identity could be established while the scope of issue preclusion remained
limited. Id. at 360.
Separate Opinions
Justice Clifford dissented. He first explained his view that the original proof of irregularity had been
insufficient to defeat the normal presumption that a bearer acquired a negotiable instrument for value. Even so,
the prior judgment remained unreversed and binding. His disagreement in this case concerned how far that
judgment reached, rather than permission to ignore a final judgment thought wrong. Id. at 360–363 (Clifford,
J., dissenting).
Clifford considered the bonds and coupons dependent on the same title and the same underlying transaction.
He assembled authorities in which a determination concerning title, a common defense, or a transaction
controlled later litigation over another instrument or item of property. In his analysis, the later suit presented
the same essential controversy about the claimant's enforceable title, despite involving a different portion of the
indebtedness. Id. at 363–370.
He also stressed that the claimant in the first suit had alleged acquisition for value and could have offered
evidence supporting that allegation. A mistaken litigation strategy about the burden of proof should have been
corrected by objection or a request for a new trial. Allowing proof to be held in reserve for a later action
threatened repeated litigation. The majority instead treated acquisition of each distinct bond or coupon as a fact
not settled merely by failure of proof concerning the earlier coupons. Id. at 370–371; majority at 359–360.

Notes
The case supplies the conceptual distinction between claim preclusion and issue preclusion; it does not
announce that every obligation arising from a common transaction is always a separate claim under later
procedural law. The Court treated these particular negotiable instruments as different demands. The first step in
applying its framework is to determine whether the second suit presents the same claim under the governing
claim-definition rule. Id. at 352–353, 359–360.
A failure of proof can end a claim conclusively without establishing the same negative proposition about a
different transaction. Here the earlier claimant's loss remained final. Cromwell's right to offer evidence in the
second case did not reopen that loss or erase the common finding of fraud. Keeping both effects visible avoids
converting the decision into a general license for unsuccessful parties to try again. Id. at 359–360.
