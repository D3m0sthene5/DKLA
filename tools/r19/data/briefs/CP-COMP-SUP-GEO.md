# GEO Group, Inc. v. Menocal

**Citation and court:** 607 U.S. 438 (2026), Supreme Court of the United States, No. 24–758, decided February 25, 2026. Justice Kagan wrote for seven Justices throughout; Justice Thomas joined Parts I and III and concurred separately; Justice Alito concurred only in the judgment. All nine agreed on the outcome. The complete official report includes five Court footnotes and one substantive asterisk note in Alito's concurrence, all read for this brief.

**Assigned reading:** Civil Procedure Supplement, printed page 519, physical PDF page 348: the complete captioned account, verified against the scan. The complete official preliminary report, pages 438–464, supplies the underlying procedure and both complete concurrences. Justia separately corroborates the identity, date, opinion lineup, and disposition. Pinpoints below follow the official report.

**Material facts.** GEO operated a private immigration detention facility in Aurora, Colorado, under a contract with Immigration and Customs Enforcement. Former detainee Alejandro Menocal brought a class action challenging two work policies. The Sanitation Policy required unpaid cleaning of common areas and imposed escalating sanctions, up to seventy-two hours of solitary confinement, for failure to work. The Voluntary Work Program offered one dollar per day for other work. Plaintiffs alleged that the first violated the federal forced-labor prohibition and the second constituted unjust enrichment under Colorado law. Those were claims awaiting adjudication; the Supreme Court did not determine their merits. 607 U.S. at 442.

**Procedural history.** After discovery, GEO sought dismissal under Yearsley, contending that ICE had authorized and directed the challenged policies. The district court rejected that reading of the contract: GEO had independently developed the policies and exceeded its contractual obligations, so trial would be needed. GEO immediately appealed. The Tenth Circuit dismissed for lack of appellate jurisdiction, reasoning that denial of Yearsley protection failed Cohen's second requirement because it was not completely separate from the merits. The Supreme Court granted review of immediate appealability and focused instead on Cohen's third requirement. Id. at 442–443. The district court had not used § 1292(b)'s certification route. Id. at 452 n.5.

**Issue and holding.** Is a district court's pretrial denial of Yearsley protection to a federal contractor immediately appealable under the collateral-order doctrine? No. Yearsley affords a potential defense to liability, rather than immunity from suit. A denial can be effectively reviewed after final judgment and fails the third collateral-order requirement. Id. at 443–452. This is an appellate-jurisdiction holding, not a final rejection of GEO's defense on its merits.

**Governing framework.** Section 1291 generally permits appeals only from final decisions ending the case. Cohen permits immediate review of a narrow category of orders that conclusively determine a disputed question, resolve an important issue completely separate from the merits, and are effectively unreviewable after final judgment. All three requirements must be met, assessed categorically rather than through the circumstances of an individual litigant. Id. at 443–444.

**The Court's reasoning.** A merits defense asserts that the defendant acted lawfully and should not be held liable. An immunity can protect even unlawful conduct and provides, in its essence, entitlement to avoid trial and litigation burdens. The right not to stand trial disappears once trial occurs; the right to a finding of nonliability can be vindicated by reversal of an adverse final judgment. That distinction generally controls Cohen's third requirement here. Id. at 445–447. Note 1 recognizes a further category—nonmerits defenses that lack sufficiently weighty public objectives to count as immunities—and note 2 explains qualified immunity's protection even when conduct was unlawful but the law was not clearly established. Id. at 445–446 nn.1–2.

Yearsley itself protected a contractor that flooded land while performing work lawfully authorized and directed by the federal government. Liability could attach if the contractor exceeded its authority or the authority was not validly conferred. Because protection depended on lawful authorization and compliance, it supplied a merits defense rather than entitlement to escape suit. Id. at 448–449. GEO's characterization as derivative sovereign immunity also conflicted with decisions denying transfer of the government's sovereign immunity to its agents. An agent may instead defend by showing lawful authority for its acts. Id. at 449–451.

Note 4 addresses loose uses of “immunity” in Brady and Campbell-Ewald: in context those passages concerned protection from liability, not an exemption from litigation. Id. at 451 n.4. Properly characterized, Yearsley can be reviewed through reversal of an erroneous liability finding after final judgment. GEO therefore must wait for that stage to obtain ordinary appellate review. Id. at 451–452. Note 3 relates the defense/immunity distinction to Cohen's first and second requirements without making them the Court's principal basis for decision. Id. at 447 n.3.

**Thomas's concurrence.** Thomas agreed that Yearsley establishes a liability defense and joined Parts I and III. He declined Part II because, in his view, courts should not expand Cohen beyond orders already held immediately appealable. Judge-made exceptions bypass Congress's selection of statutory exceptions and the rulemaking process. Since this order was unlike recognized immunity denials and no other statute or rule authorized appeal, dismissal followed without further application of Cohen's general test. Id. at 452–453.

**Alito's concurrence in the judgment.** Alito agreed that Yearsley is not immunity from suit and that this appeal fails, but rejected the Court's emphasis on whether a defense turns on legality as a sufficient explanation of immunity doctrine. He traced the doctrine from orders that would become moot, through constitutional protections against trial, to a public-interest inquiry. Immediate appeal can be warranted where delaying review imperils important constitutional or policy interests, including some defenses involving legality. Id. at 453–460, 462–464.

For Alito, Yearsley did not meet that threshold: delayed review posed no sufficiently serious separation-of-powers or sovereign-dignity concern, and qualified immunity already accommodates many concerns about distraction and deterrence in government service. Id. at 460–462. His asterisk note expressly qualifies the latter point. Private prison guards lack qualified immunity in specified § 1983 cases; the Court has not decided whether corporate contractors such as GEO may invoke it. The collateral-order inquiry remains categorical, and the analysis might differ if most Yearsley defendants could never claim qualified immunity. Id. at 462 n.*. This concurrence does not establish qualified immunity for GEO or all contractors.

**Exact disposition.** The Supreme Court **affirmed the Tenth Circuit's dismissal for lack of appellate jurisdiction and remanded for further proceedings**. Id. at 452. It did not affirm a final judgment imposing liability on GEO, award damages, or conclusively decide the contractual scope of the Yearsley defense.

**Course significance.** A defense that could defeat liability does not necessarily provide a right to avoid trial. Identify the asserted protection and ask whether final appellate review can vindicate it. Also preserve the alternative review route: note 5 expressly allows an otherwise eligible Yearsley denial to be reviewed through § 1292(b) certification and discretionary appellate acceptance. The holding bars the attempted collateral-order appeal; it does not eliminate that statutory route.
