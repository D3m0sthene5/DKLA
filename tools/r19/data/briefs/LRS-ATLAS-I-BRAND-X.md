# National Cable & Telecommunications Association v. Brand X Internet Services

**545 U.S. 967 (2005) — Supreme Court of the United States — June 27, 2005**  
**Course:** Legislation and the Regulatory State  
**Assigned materials:** November part a, PDF 156–157, printed 936–937, and part b, PDF 22, printed 966. These are developed editorial accounts with judicial quotations. The second uses Brand X to pose a later stare decisis question following Loper Bright.

## Facts, regulatory setting, and procedure

Broadband cable-modem service supplied fast Internet access over cable companies’ television networks. Its classification mattered because the Communications Act imposed mandatory common-carrier obligations on telecommunications carriers, including reasonable and nondiscriminatory rates, interconnection, and universal-service contributions. Information-service providers were not automatically subject to those Title II requirements. The question was whether cable companies offered a telecommunications service in addition to an information service when they sold an integrated Internet-access package. That distinction concerned the service offered to customers, rather than simply whether transmission wires existed. 545 U.S. at 974–978.

The statutory categories developed against the FCC’s earlier Computer II distinction between basic and enhanced services. Basic service meant essentially transparent transmission; enhanced service involved processing or storage affecting the subscriber’s information. The 1996 Act separately defined telecommunications, telecommunications service, and information service. Internet access involved transmission, but also capabilities such as browsing, e-mail, file transfer, domain-name lookup, and other information processing. Id. at 976–978, 987–988.

Following a proceeding initiated in September 2000, the FCC issued its March 2002 declaratory ruling classifying cable-modem service solely as an information service. It viewed transmission and processing as integrated in the product offered to the subscriber. Its earlier Universal Service Report had treated ISPs that leased transmission facilities as information-service providers, and the FCC found no statutory reason to change that classification merely because cable providers owned their facilities. It left open whether to require cable companies to give independent ISPs access through its ancillary Title I authority. Id. at 977–979.

Numerous parties sought review. A judicial lottery placed the challenge in the Ninth Circuit, which partly vacated the ruling. Rather than assess the FCC’s interpretation under Chevron, that court followed its earlier AT&T Corp. v. Portland decision, 216 F.3d 871, treating cable-modem service as telecommunications service. Portland had not reviewed an FCC administrative proceeding, and the agency had not been a party. The FCC and cable-industry petitioners brought separate certiorari petitions, Nos.04–281 and 04–277, resolved through this joint opinion. Id. at 967 n.*, 979–980.

## Questions, holding, and judgment

The Court considered whether Chevron applied despite the Ninth Circuit’s prior construction, whether the FCC’s classification was permissible under the Communications Act, and whether its different treatment of cable and DSL was arbitrary and capricious. It upheld the FCC’s construction and explanation, **reversed the Ninth Circuit’s judgment, and remanded the cases**. Id. at 980–1003.

Justice Thomas wrote for six Justices: Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Breyer, and Thomas. Stevens and Breyer also wrote concurrences. Scalia dissented, with Souter and Ginsburg joining **only Part I**, which disputed the cable-service classification. Scalia’s Part II objections about precedent and agency power were his alone. The result was 6–3. Id. at 972, 1003–1020.

## The historical relationship between Chevron and precedent

Under the then-operative framework, an agency administering an ambiguous statute could adopt a reasonable interpretation even if a court independently preferred another. Here Congress had empowered the FCC to issue binding rules, and the order used that authority. A change from earlier administrative practice did not automatically disqualify an interpretation from Chevron review. An unexplained change could instead be arbitrary and capricious under the APA; a reasoned change remained possible. Id. at 980–982.

The Court held that a prior judicial construction displaced an otherwise Chevron-eligible agency interpretation only when the precedent held that the statute unambiguously required the judicial reading. Portland had identified what it considered the best reading, without holding that the statute admitted no other permissible interpretation. The Ninth Circuit therefore should have applied Chevron to the FCC’s later construction. Otherwise, the allocation of interpretive authority would turn on whether a court or an agency happened to address the question first. Id. at 982–985.

The majority distinguished changing a permissible interpretation within delegated discretion from declaring a prior judicial judgment legally wrong. It also distinguished decisions such as Neal, in which the Court understood the relevant earlier precedent to have established an unambiguous statutory meaning. Although the Supreme Court could evaluate the FCC’s rule without treating Portland as binding on itself, it resolved the precedent issue to settle lower-court confusion and prevent the Ninth Circuit from using Portland to invalidate the same rule again. Id. at 983–986. This is the historical Brand X rule; the later overruling of Chevron must be kept separate from it.

## Why the integrated classification was permissible

The FCC’s conclusion that cable-modem service was an information service was not challenged. The dispute concerned whether the same package also necessarily offered telecommunications service. The majority accepted the FCC’s distinction between using telecommunications as an input and offering a separate transmission service to the public. From the agency’s perspective, subscribers bought Internet access in which the transmission path was integral to information-processing capabilities. Id. at 987–989 & n.1.

The statutory word “offering” did not unambiguously require describing every component of an integrated product as an additional offered service. The majority illustrated this with cars: a dealership offers the finished vehicle without necessarily offering its engine or chassis as distinct products. It acknowledged that some packages contain separate offers, but considered the degree of integration a technical issue on which the FCC could reasonably act. Congress’s use of parallel basic/enhanced-service concepts supported the agency’s reading, as did the longstanding exclusion of non-facilities-based enhanced providers from mandatory common-carrier regulation. Ownership of the underlying facilities did not itself settle the statutory service definition. Id. at 989–997 & n.2.

The Court also rejected the contention that this reading let any carrier evade regulation by adding an information feature. Ordinary telephone service bundled with voice mail still supplied a transparent transmission path independent of the storage feature. By contrast, the FCC reasonably treated Internet access as involving information processing even when users visited third-party websites. Domain-name service matched requested addresses to host addresses, and caching improved access to stored content. The Court’s treatment of domain-name service differed from Scalia’s characterization of it as incidental routing; footnote 3 responds specifically to that objection. Id. at 997–1000 & n.3.

## The APA explanation and unresolved classifications

MCI argued that exempting cable while imposing common-carrier duties on DSL providers was inconsistent. The Court accepted the FCC’s explanation that the older telephone-network rules responded to a bottleneck, while the cable ruling evaluated a newer market with multiple transmission platforms. Applying a fresh analysis to cable was rational. Policy inconsistency concerned the adequacy of the explanation, not automatically whether the statutory interpretation was permissible. Id. at 1000–1002 & n.4.

The FCC could reconsider its broader regime incrementally rather than immediately apply the cable ruling to every provider. The Court **expressly declined to decide how DSL should or lawfully could be classified**, or how ancillary authority should be used to require cable access. Thus the judgment did not establish a single classification for every broadband technology or decide every possible access obligation. Id. at 1002–1003.

## Separate opinions

Stevens joined the majority but cautioned that its explanation concerning a court of appeals’ reading would not necessarily apply to a Supreme Court interpretation, which he presumed would remove earlier ambiguity. That was his concurrence’s caveat, not an express limitation adopted by the Court opinion. Id. at 1003.

Breyer thought the FCC’s decision fell within its delegated authority, although barely. He defended Mead against Scalia’s characterization that formal process was required for Chevron deference. In his account, congressional delegation could appear through several indicators; formal rulemaking was neither invariably necessary nor sufficient. Id. at 1003–1005.

Scalia’s Part I, joined by Souter and Ginsburg, argued that the transmission component retained its separate identity. His pizza-delivery and puppy-and-leash analogies challenged the majority’s finished-product account. Subscribers could perceive both access and applications as offered services, and the underlying transmission remained distinguishable. He contested the majority’s regulatory-history, domain-name, and caching explanations, and suggested that statutory forbearance supplied a more honest means of reducing regulatory burdens. Id. at 1005–1014 & nn.1–7.

Scalia alone then attacked the historical precedent rule. He saw it as permitting executive officers to displace definitive judicial statutory constructions, raising Article III concerns and destabilizing past decisions that had not expressly labeled their readings unambiguous. He also criticized the majority for reaching that issue unnecessarily. The majority rejected his account of agency reversal; the competing positions should not be conflated. Id. at 1014–1020 & nn.8–14.

## Assigned significance and later-law boundary

The assignment uses Brand X to illustrate how Chevron’s distinction between a permissible reading and the best reading affected precedent. Its later note asks how holdings grounded in permissibility relate to independent interpretation after Loper Bright. That question is textbook analysis, not a holding of the 2005 Court. Assigned printed 936–937, 966.

Loper Bright overruled Chevron’s deference framework while preserving statutory stare decisis for earlier holdings upholding particular agency actions. Those precise later propositions are verified in Roberts’s official slip pp.34–35 and the assigned materials; this brief does not certify current broadband classification or resolve every post-Loper Bright effect of Brand X.
