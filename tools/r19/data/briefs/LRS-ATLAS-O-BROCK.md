# Brock v. Roadway Express, Inc.
481 U.S. 252 (1987) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and procedural setting

The Supreme Court affirmed in part and reversed in part a federal district court judgment invalidating the procedures used to order a truck driver's temporary reinstatement. The employer had received notice of the retaliation complaint and an opportunity to answer, but had been denied the substance of the evidence supporting it. That omission violated due process. The district court went further and required an evidentiary hearing with confrontation and cross-examination before reinstatement; the Supreme Court rejected that categorical requirement. This was a direct appeal from the Northern District of Georgia's summary judgment, 624 F. Supp. 197, under the then-existing 28 U.S.C. § 1252. It was not review of the Secretary's ultimate determination that the discharge violated the statute. (257, 268.)

Justice Marshall announced the judgment in an opinion joined by Blackmun, Powell, and O'Connor. Brennan and Stevens agreed that withholding the supporting evidence was unconstitutional, but would have affirmed the district court's requirement of a prior adversarial hearing. White, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Scalia, agreed that a trial-type hearing need not precede temporary reinstatement, but would also have upheld the withholding of the supporting information. Thus six Justices supported the conclusion that the disclosure here was inadequate, and seven rejected an automatic requirement of a prior trial-type hearing. Marshall's explanation and particular package of minimum procedures were a four-Justice plurality, not an opinion joined in full by those seven. (255, 268–278.)

## Facts, administrative proceedings, and litigation

Roadway Express was a large interstate trucking company subject to § 405 of the Surface Transportation Assistance Act of 1982. On November 22, 1983, it discharged driver Jerry Hufstetler, asserting that he had disabled lights on his truck to obtain extra pay while waiting for repairs. Hufstetler denied a contractual act of dishonesty and alleged retaliation for previous safety complaints. The grievance went to arbitration under the collective-bargaining agreement. On January 30, 1984, the arbitrators upheld the discharge. (255–256.)

On February 7, 1984, Hufstetler filed a statutory complaint with the Labor Department. An OSHA field investigator notified Roadway and interviewed the driver and other employees, obtaining statements that supported retaliation. Roadway could meet with the investigator and submit a written explanation. It repeated the dishonesty ground accepted by the arbitrators. It was not given the other witnesses' names or the substance of their statements. On January 21, 1985, the Department's regional administrator found reasonable cause to believe the discharge retaliatory and ordered immediate reinstatement with backpay. The order called Roadway's explanation conjectural without detailing the evidence underlying that conclusion. (256.)

Roadway obtained a preliminary injunction against enforcement, followed by summary judgment declaring § 405 unconstitutional insofar as it permitted reinstatement without a prior evidentiary hearing. It separately objected within the administrative proceeding and requested the statutory hearing. An administrative law judge conducted that hearing in March 1985; the Secretary issued a final reinstatement and backpay order on August 21, 1986. Roadway's appeal from that final administrative decision was pending in the Eleventh Circuit when the Supreme Court decided this case on April 22, 1987. The Supreme Court therefore addressed the constitutional prerequisites for the preliminary order, while the merits of the final statutory order remained in a separate proceeding. (257.)

## Statutory design and competing positions

Section 405 protected commercial transportation employees from retaliation for reporting safety violations or refusing to operate vehicles that failed applicable safety requirements. Congress regarded employees as particularly well situated to detect violations, but vulnerable to losing their livelihoods if they reported them. Backpay following a lengthy proceeding might not overcome that deterrent. Consequently, after an initial investigation and a reasonable-cause finding, the Secretary was to order temporary reinstatement; the employer could request an expeditious hearing and final decision, but that request did not stay reinstatement. (255, 258–259.)

The statute itself specified notice of the complaint but little about employer participation in the preliminary investigation. The OSHA practices used here provided notice of the allegation and an opportunity to explain the discharge. New implementing rules, effective December 22, 1986, similarly permitted a written statement, affidavits, documents, a meeting with counsel, and statements by supporting witnesses. Neither set of procedures required preliminary cross-examination or disclosure of the adverse witnesses' names and statements. The Secretary nevertheless represented that investigators' established practice was to convey the substance of supporting evidence. The plurality accepted that representation as an official procedure of which employers were made aware, while emphasizing that the procedure had not been followed here. (259–261 & n.1, 265.)

Roadway contended that a meaningful defense required access to the adverse evidence and a hearing testing its credibility before reinstatement. It also argued that a preliminary hearing would add little burden because an expeditious hearing had to occur afterward anyway. The Secretary relied on highway safety, protection against retaliation, the reasonable-cause screen, the employer's initial response, and the later full hearing. The contractual right to discharge for cause was conceded to be a property interest protected by the Fifth Amendment. The plurality accepted that concession but rejected a separate claimed property entitlement to have arbitration exclusively determine the discharge's validity. Whether arbitral findings had collateral-estoppel or res judicata effect could be raised in the administrative proceeding and reviewed by the court of appeals; arbitration's existence did not displace the statutory process by constitutional entitlement. (260–261 & n.2, 266–267.)

## Plurality reasoning: meaningful response without a preliminary trial

The plurality applied the contextual approach of Mathews v. Eldridge: the public interest, affected private interests, risk of error, and likely value of additional safeguards. Roadway's substantial interest in controlling its workforce mattered, but the district court had failed to weigh another private interest—Hufstetler's continued livelihood. Losing a job could seriously damage both finances and prospects for alternative work. That interest reinforced the public interest in keeping employees willing to report unsafe conditions. (261–263.)

An adequately reliable preliminary check, followed by complete and expeditious review, could accommodate those competing interests without requiring a full evidentiary hearing before reinstatement. The plurality specified notice of the allegations, notice of the substance of relevant supporting evidence, an opportunity for a written response, and an opportunity to meet with the investigator and present rebuttal witnesses' statements. Those statements could be informal; confrontation and cross-examination were not required at that stage. Loudermill and the relevant Arnett opinion supported separating an initial opportunity to respond from the later full hearing, but also demonstrated why an explanation of the supporting evidence was essential. An employer ignorant of that evidence could not prepare a meaningful answer. Roadway's inability to obtain it made this preliminary order unconstitutional. (263–265.)

The plurality considered informal rebuttal sufficiently reliable for the preliminary reasonable-cause determination. Cross-examination might improve credibility testing, but would introduce delay and opportunities for dilatory tactics before the worker recovered a livelihood. The investigator's initial function was to assess reasonable cause, while final credibility judgments belonged to the administrative law judge conducting a full hearing. The fact that a later hearing must be expeditious did not eliminate the extra delay created by placing that hearing before reinstatement. The plurality also rejected making the Department's already lengthy investigation a reason to prolong it further: even bureaucratic inefficiency did not diminish the driver's interest in reinstatement. (266–267 & n.3.)

The judgment did not approve unlimited delay after reinstatement. The new rules contemplated a hearing within 30 days of objections, an administrative decision within another 30 days, and a final Secretary's order within 120 days, with qualifications and an agency characterization of the periods as directory. Excessive delay could itself violate due process. Here, however, the district court's injunction meant the preliminary reinstatement never took effect. The record did not explain the 19 months before the final decision; litigation, employer incentives, or administrative inefficiency might have contributed. The plurality declined to decide whether this delay, or the periods authorized by the regulations, was constitutionally excessive on that undeveloped record. (267–268.)

## Mootness and separate opinions

The final administrative order did not moot the appeal. Although Roadway's current reinstatement obligation now rested on that final order, the preliminary-order dispute was capable of repetition yet evading review. The preliminary order's duration was too short for full litigation, and this large interstate carrier could reasonably encounter similar orders again. The plurality therefore reached the procedural challenge. (257–258.)

Brennan agreed on the evidence-disclosure defect, but regarded the speed of final review as inseparable from the adequacy of initial process. Where material facts were contested and prompt final resolution was not assured, an opportunity merely to argue or deny was inadequate. He would require confrontation, cross-examination, and the employer's own testimony before reinstatement. A genuinely prompt final hearing might permit the plurality's less formal approach, but the rules allowed roughly six months and treated the periods as directory. His footnote acknowledged that the employer's interest was less weighty than some protected interests and had to be balanced against safety and the worker's job, yet maintained that a factual dispute required either adversarial initial testing or final resolution following closely behind. He would affirm the district court's judgment. (269–271 & unnumbered note at 270.)

White, Rehnquist, and Scalia would permit even less initial disclosure. They accepted notice of the charge, an opportunity to respond, a reasonable-cause finding, and an adequate subsequent hearing at a meaningful time. They declined to transplant procedures from public-employee cases mechanically into this whistleblower setting. Protecting corroborating employees could supply a strong governmental reason not to disclose names and supporting statements before the full administrative hearing. They therefore disagreed with the disclosure-based partial affirmance. (271–272.)

Stevens would affirm the district court entirely. The public interest justified the statutory right, but did not justify secret, untested evidence in this case. An eleven-month investigation and an order entered fourteen months after discharge undermined the asserted need for immediate action. His notes discussed narrow emergency exceptions, differences between government administering its own employment or benefits programs and regulating private relationships, alternative investigation deadlines and adequate agency resources, and the comparatively objective or institutionally distinctive contexts in which cross-examination had been relaxed. Conflicting witnesses here presented an ordinary credibility problem. He favored a strong presumption of a meaningful public adversarial hearing before deprivation, understood disclosure of the evidence's substance to include witness names and testimony summaries, and regarded the unexplained 19-month final-decision delay as an additional defect. That last understanding of mandatory witness identification was Stevens's position; it should not be silently added to Marshall's formulation. (273–278 & nn.1–7.)
