# Fiege v. Boehm
210 Md. 352, 123 A.2d 316 (1956) | Contracts

## Court and posture
Judge Delaplaine delivered the opinion of the Maryland Court of Appeals, the state's highest court under its historical name. Fiege appealed a judgment entered on a jury verdict awarding Boehm $2,415.80 for breach of a support agreement. The court affirmed with costs. The reproduced judicial opinion contains no separate writing or judicial footnote; it does not identify the participating judges, so no numerical vote is asserted. (210 Md. at 355–358, 364.)

## Facts and disputed evidence
Boehm alleged that she became pregnant after intercourse with Fiege early in 1951, that he acknowledged responsibility, and that before the child's birth he agreed to pay medical and miscellaneous expenses, compensate her lost wages, and pay $10 weekly until the child reached twenty-one. In exchange, she agreed not to institute proceedings under Maryland's bastardy statute while he performed. The child was born September 29, 1951, and was placed for adoption July 13, 1954. Boehm claimed $2,895.80 in specified expenses, lost earnings, and support, credited Fiege's $480 payments, and sought the $2,415.80 balance. These were the pleading's allegations, rather than appellate findings that Fiege was the father. (Id. at 355–356.)

At trial, Boehm testified to intercourse on January 21, 1951, following a movie and dinner, and to the agreement and payments. Fiege denied both intercourse and any agreement, while admitting the $480 payments. His father testified that Fiege had said he would take care of the matter if it were kept away from his mother, the public, and the courts. The evidence therefore presented an actual dispute about the agreement and the circumstances of the claim. (Id. at 356–357.)

In May 1953, Fiege sought blood testing through Boehm's physician. A hematologist reported that Fiege had type O blood, Boehm type B, and the child type A; under the blood-group inheritance principles explained in the opinion, those results excluded Fiege as the father. He stopped paying, and Boehm then initiated proceedings. The Criminal Court acquitted him October 8, 1953, solely on the blood-test testimony. The civil jury heard that testimony as well. The appellate court assumed the laboratory results were accurate when deciding the contract issue; it did not overturn them or adjudicate paternity. (Id. at 356–357, 362–363.)

## Procedural history and arguments
The trial court initially sustained Fiege's demurrer with leave to amend because the declaration had omitted the prosecution and acquittal. Boehm amended to allege that she brought the charge after his breach and that the acquittal rested on the blood tests. The court overruled a second demurrer, denied a directed verdict, and instructed the jury that the criminal acquittal did not bind it. After the jury awarded the entire requested balance, the court denied judgment notwithstanding the verdict or a new trial. (Id. at 355–358.)

Fiege argued that, even if the alleged agreement existed, forbearance of an invalid claim supplied no consideration. He consequently challenged the amended declaration, the submission to the jury, and the post-verdict rulings. He also attacked the jury instructions, emphasizing the scientific evidence underlying his acquittal. Boehm's evidence supported enforcement of a settlement made before the blood testing, based on her good-faith claim and his undertaking, rather than recovery on a judicial finding of biological paternity. (Id. at 358, 362–363.)

## Rule and reasoning
The court first distinguished an unsupported promise resting only on a putative father's moral obligation from an agreement made in exchange for refraining from statutory proceedings. Maryland's statute imposed support obligations through proceedings formally treated as criminal but civil in purpose: protecting the public from support costs and benefiting the mother and child. A fair and reasonable support compromise was consistent with that statutory policy and was a compromise of civil injuries, rather than an unlawful agreement to compound a criminal prosecution. The statutory support provision discussed by the court extended to eighteen, while the agreement alleged here extended to twenty-one; the contractual and statutory terms should not be conflated. (Id. at 358–359.)

Forbearance still had to constitute sufficient consideration. Drawing on Hartle v. Stahl and Restatement of Contracts § 76(b), the court required both subjective honesty and an objective reasonable basis for believing the claim possibly valid. An invalid claim could support a compromise if asserted with that honest and reasonable belief. A person could not manufacture consideration by threatening a claim known to have no factual or legal foundation merely to obtain payment for relief from annoyance. The court's discussion of reasonable intelligence and factual support makes good faith a substantive limitation, rather than acceptance of every sincerely asserted demand. (Id. at 359–361.)

The supporting authorities showed that uncertainty or subsequent failure of a paternity claim did not necessarily defeat a settlement honestly made without fraud or oppression. Here there was no proof of fraud or unfairness, and Boehm's testimony indicated good faith when she made the charge. Even assuming the blood tests accurately excluded Fiege, that later evidence did not require dismissal of her contractual claim. The court therefore upheld both the amended pleading and denial of the directed verdict. The exchange was settlement of an honestly and reasonably disputed claim; the decision did not hold that scientific exclusion was false or that Fiege owed support merely because of a moral duty. (Id. at 361–363.)

## Instructions and exact result
The court declined to make a specific determination of paternity. Fiege's only preserved exception to the charge was the general objection that it did not refer to a legally valid binding agreement. The judge then added an instruction addressing duress and left the existence of an agreement to the jury. Counsel made no further exception before announcing readiness to argue. Maryland's procedural rule confined appellate review to the particular instruction or omission distinctly identified, with its specific grounds, before the jury retired. The more specific appellate instruction challenge was therefore not preserved. Finding no reversible error, the court affirmed the $2,415.80 judgment with costs. (Id. at 363–364.)
