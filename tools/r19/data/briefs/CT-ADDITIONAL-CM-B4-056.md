# United States v. Winstar Corp.
518 U.S. 839 (1996) | Contracts

## Court, decision, and procedural posture

The Supreme Court decided this federal-government contract case on July 1, 1996, after argument on April 24. Seven Justices affirmed the Federal Circuit’s determination that the United States was liable for breaching accounting commitments made to three thrift acquirers. The Court remanded because the Court of Federal Claims had not yet determined the appropriate measure or amount of damages. This was an interlocutory liability adjudication, not a final monetary award or an exemption from the new banking statute. (839, 842–843, 858–860, 910.)

Justice Souter announced the judgment. Justices Stevens and Breyer joined his entire opinion; Justice O’Connor joined except Parts IV–A and IV–B. Breyer also concurred separately. Justice Scalia, joined by Justices Kennedy and Thomas, concurred in the judgment on different reasoning. Chief Justice Rehnquist dissented; Justice Ginsburg joined Parts I, III, and IV of his dissent, but not Parts II or V. Thus the principal opinion’s particular conclusion that FIRREA was not a public and general sovereign act had three adherents, while its alternative impossibility analysis had four. The seven votes for liability do not make every proposition in Souter’s opinion a majority holding. (842–843, 910, 919, 924.)

## Facts and the regulatory bargain

Rising interest rates in the early 1980s increased thrifts’ deposit costs while their existing mortgage portfolios continued earning comparatively low fixed returns. Many institutions became insolvent, and the Federal Savings and Loan Insurance Corporation lacked sufficient reserves to close and liquidate all failing institutions. Federal regulators encouraged supervisory mergers: private acquirers assumed failed thrifts’ liabilities in exchange for financial assistance and favorable regulatory accounting. (844–850.)

Purchase accounting generated supervisory goodwill representing the excess of the acquisition price over the fair value of identifiable net assets. Regulators allowed this intangible to count toward required capital and permitted long amortization periods. These permissions mattered because the acquired liabilities otherwise could leave the merged institution below its capital requirements immediately. Other arrangements allowed capital credits associated with federal cash assistance. Generally accepted accounting principles and regulatory accounting were not identical; the 1983 accounting changes particularly restricted advantageous amortization and capital-credit treatment. The promises therefore concerned valuable, potentially changeable regulatory permissions, rather than routine assurances that the law would remain unchanged. (850–856.)

Glendale’s 1981 acquisition of Broward involved a roughly $734 million asset shortfall. Its assistance agreement incorporated a Bank Board resolution and an accountant’s letter permitting forty-year goodwill amortization. Without the favorable treatment, even Glendale’s preexisting capital would have left the merged institution insolvent. Winstar’s 1984 acquisition of Windom involved private investment of $2.8 million, FSLIC assistance of $5.6 million, and thirty-five-year goodwill amortization. Its accounting-principles provision gave the negotiated documents priority over conflicting regulatory changes. Statesman’s 1988 transaction expanded at the Government’s insistence to include additional failing institutions. It involved $21 million of private investment and $60 million in federal assistance, including a $26 million capital credit, along with twenty-five-year goodwill amortization and related capital-maintenance documents. (861–869.)

Congress enacted the Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act of 1989, or FIRREA, reorganizing the federal thrift regulators and imposing stricter capital requirements. Goodwill could not count as tangible capital; a limited transitional allowance for qualifying supervisory goodwill under the core-capital requirement ended by 1995. Winstar and Statesman were seized and liquidated after failing the new requirements; Glendale survived through private recapitalization. The thrifts nevertheless remained subject to the statutory requirements. Their contract suits sought compensation for the lost bargain. (856–858, 870.)

## Litigation and the competing positions

The Claims Court recognized Winstar’s implied-in-fact contract in 1990 and determined liability in 1992. It also granted the corresponding liability rulings for Statesman and Glendale. The consolidated interlocutory appeals first produced a Federal Circuit panel reversal, based on the absence of an unmistakable promise limiting future regulation. The en banc Federal Circuit vacated that panel result and affirmed the liability determinations. (858–860.)

The Government argued that the documents supplied existing accounting permissions rather than enforceable protection against future legal change. It invoked four special government-contract defenses: unmistakability, reserved sovereign powers, absence of express delegation to surrender sovereign authority, and the sovereign-acts doctrine. The thrifts relied on the incorporated resolutions, forbearance letters, assistance agreements, and circumstances of transactions that transferred otherwise dangerous liabilities to them. Their position was that the Government had undertaken the accounting commitments as contractual consideration and bore the risk when subsequent legislation prevented performance. (860–870.)

## Contract construction and unmistakability

Souter’s opinion treated the documents as contractual commitments, not merely statements of current regulatory policy. Integration provisions incorporated the specific accounting promises; general duties to obey applicable law did not erase those negotiated commitments. Ordinary contract construction also made economic sense of the bargain: the acquirers had accepted liabilities whose accounting treatment was essential to their institutions’ solvency. The resulting promise allocated the risk of regulatory change to the Government. It did not authorize the institutions to operate contrary to FIRREA. (861–870.)

The unmistakability doctrine ordinarily requires unmistakable terms before construing a contract as a surrender of sovereign authority. Souter distinguished a contract that legally restricts sovereign power from one that leaves regulation effective while requiring compensation for the Government’s failure to provide promised treatment. The latter risk allocation did not surrender Congress’s regulatory authority. He nevertheless rejected a categorical rule that requesting damages always avoids the doctrine: tax reimbursements or equivalent compensation can effectively recreate an exemption. The inquiry concerns the asserted obligation’s effect, rather than the remedy’s label alone. (871–885.)

Souter also rejected the contention that compensation necessarily impermissibly deters legislation. Sovereign decisions routinely impose financial costs, and treating every such cost as a surrender would undermine the Government’s capacity to make credible contracts. The textbook’s economic reference at printed 1117 corresponds to this analysis and to the later discussion of honoring negotiated risk allocation. Those passages explain the principal opinion’s reasoning; they do not establish that every Justice adopted the same economic rationale. (884 n. 27; 908–909 & n. 56.)

The reserved-powers and express-delegation objections likewise failed under this understanding. Essential sovereign power had not been contracted away. FSLIC possessed general authority to make contracts and specific authority to guarantee an acquiring institution against losses from supervisory mergers; the statutory framework also recognized goodwill treatment. The relevant promise was within that contracting authority, rather than a supposed agency power to bind Congress not to legislate. (888–891.)

## Sovereign acts and ordinary impossibility

The sovereign-acts doctrine separates the Government’s capacity as contractor from its capacity as lawgiver when public and general governmental action interferes with performance. It can remove the ordinary obstacle that a party cannot invoke impossibility caused by its own act. It does not automatically establish every element of an impossibility defense. (891–898, 904–905.)

Parts IV–A and IV–B, joined only by Souter, Stevens, and Breyer, concluded that FIRREA did not have the exculpatory public-and-general character required here. They reasoned that the legislation’s substantial effect included releasing the Government from its own accounting commitments, and that Congress expressly focused on those supervisory-merger bargains. A broad public-welfare purpose or uniform application did not by itself negate this substantial contractual self-relief. This was the plurality’s approach, not a seven-Justice determination about FIRREA’s character. (896–904.)

Part IV–C supplied an alternative analysis joined by O’Connor as well. Even assuming public and general action, ordinary impossibility did not excuse the Government. Nonoccurrence of regulatory change was not a basic assumption of these agreements: regulatory rules had changed repeatedly, and the reason for obtaining the particular commitments was to allocate the consequences of change. The opinion did not turn foreseeability into an absolute test for all contracts; its discussion emphasized risks central to the transaction that the parties could readily allocate. More decisively, the agreements assigned the relevant risk to the Government. An agency’s inability to control Congress did not prevent it from assuming a financial obligation contingent on Congress’s action. No court ordered illegal accounting performance. (904–910, particularly 905–906 n. 53 and 908–909 & nn. 56–58.)

## Separate writings and their disagreements

Breyer joined the principal opinion but separately explained that contractual language and circumstances can establish a binding promise to hold a counterparty harmless from legal change. The Government’s identity affects the inference of intent, but does not categorically forbid that promise. Earlier cases involved materially different undertakings, including asserted exemptions from sovereign powers or an express statutory reservation of amendment. A narrow compensation obligation differs from a broad promise not to legislate. Breyer accepted the contractual findings in these transactions; his concurrence did not impose an express indemnity-clause requirement. (910–918.)

Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas disagreed that labeling the agreements as risk shifting could make the sovereign defenses inapplicable. Virtually any contract imposes potential liability for nonperformance. They instead applied unmistakability and found it satisfied: promised accounting treatment over specified future periods was essential consideration for taking over the failing institutions. A promise operative only for as long as the Government chose would render that bargain illusory. The remaining defenses did not permit repudiation of the essential bargain or eliminate the monetary risks the Government had assumed. (919–924.)

Rehnquist challenged the principal opinion’s division between risk allocation and surrender of sovereignty, stressing that damages could function like an injunction and had not yet been calculated. He maintained that unmistakability must guide interpretation before finding the compensable promise. In Part II, which Ginsburg did not join, he regarded FIRREA as broad industry legislation satisfying the sovereign-acts doctrine and criticized reliance on legislative motivation or contractual effects. In Parts III and IV, joined by Ginsburg, he also disputed Scalia’s and Breyer’s approaches: short-term regulatory benefits could supply consideration without a protected long-term promise, and the lower courts’ findings did not resolve the heightened unmistakability inquiry. His final Part V, unjoined by Ginsburg, would reverse outright or remand for reconsideration under his tests. (924–937.)

## Result, assigned account, and source scope

The judgment affirmed federal contractual liability and remanded for damages proceedings. It did not invalidate FIRREA, award a quantified recovery, or hold that all governmental changes affecting contracts produce liability. The case illustrates the importance of identifying the promised performance, the allocated risk, and the relationship between special sovereign defenses and ordinary excuse doctrines. (843, 909–910.)
