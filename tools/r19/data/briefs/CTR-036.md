# Austin Instrument, Inc. v. Loral Corporation

29 N.Y.2d 124, 272 N.E.2d 533, 324 N.Y.S.2d 22 (N.Y. 1971) | Contracts

## Citation

Austin Instrument, Inc. v. Loral Corp., 29 N.Y.2d 124, 272 N.E.2d 533, 324 N.Y.S.2d 22 (1971), argued May 12 and decided July 6, 1971. Court of Appeals of New York. Chief Judge Fuld wrote for a four-judge majority; Judge Bergan dissented with two judges. Assigned excerpt: C&M pp. 487–492. The official full opinion supplies the precise disposition and complete separate opinion. The case concerns recovery of coerced price increases.

## Parties

Austin Instrument manufactured precision gear components and was Loral’s subcontractor. It sued for an unpaid balance under a second subcontract and was the respondent on appeal. Loral manufactured radar sets for the Navy. It separately sued to recover price increases paid under the first subcontract and was the appellant. The actions were consolidated. Loral made no claim in this action challenging the second subcontract itself. 29 N.Y.2d at 128–130; 129 n. 2; C&M pp. 487–489.

## Material Facts

In July 1965, the Navy awarded Loral a $6 million contract to produce radar sets. The prime contract required scheduled deliveries, provided liquidated damages for delay, and permitted cancellation for default. Loral requested bids for approximately forty precision gear components and awarded Austin a subcontract for twenty-three. Austin began deliveries in early 1966. 29 N.Y.2d at 128–129.

Loral obtained a second Navy contract in May 1966 and again sought component bids. Austin bid for all forty gear parts. On July 15, Loral told Austin that it would receive only those items for which it was the low bidder. Austin refused an award for fewer than all forty. The next day it threatened to stop deliveries already owed under the first subcontract unless Loral both awarded it all forty parts under the second and accepted substantial price increases under the first. The demanded increases applied retroactively to parts already delivered and prospectively to parts still due. Austin then stopped delivering. Id. at 129.

Loral contacted ten manufacturers, its entire approved-vendor list for precision gears. None could supply the needed components in time for the Navy schedule. The best response was that deliveries could begin sometime in October; it was not a promise to supply all the necessary parts by then. In a July 22 letter, Loral explained that it had searched urgently for another source and was left without a practical alternative to accepting Austin’s conditions. It agreed to the price increases and awarded the second subcontract for all forty parts. Id. at 129, 132–133 & nn.1, 6.

Loral formally gave Austin until September to resume deliveries, but Austin supplied some parts in August. Components for September and October production arrived around late August and the beginning of September, and Loral worked around the clock to meet its commitments. The interruption therefore mattered even though the Navy’s delivery dates had not yet arrived when Austin applied pressure in July. Manufacture required lead time. Id. at 129, 131–132.

Austin completed its last delivery under the second subcontract in July 1967. Three days later, Loral demanded repayment of the increases paid under the first. Loral explained that an earlier demand would have risked another delivery stoppage while it remained dependent on Austin. The continuing second subcontract was thus important to the timing of disaffirmance even though Loral sought no recovery on that subcontract. Id. at 130, 133.

## Procedural History

On September 15, 1967, Austin sued for more than $17,750 still owed under the second subcontract. The same day, Loral sued to recover approximately $22,250 in price increases under the first. After consolidation and trial, Austin obtained its requested recovery, and Loral’s complaint was dismissed because it had not, in the trial court’s view, shown that replacement parts could not be obtained in time. A closely divided Appellate Division affirmed. 29 N.Y.2d at 130; 35 A.D.2d 387.

The Court of Appeals reversed the portion of the order sustaining dismissal of Loral’s claim. The majority found economic duress as a matter of law, treating the issue as application of law to established facts. The dissent considered practical alternatives and Austin’s conduct disputed factual issues already resolved against Loral. 29 N.Y.2d at 131 n.5, 134–135.

## Issue

Did Austin’s threatened and actual interruption of existing deliveries induce a price modification through economic duress when Loral could not obtain suitable replacement components in time and a damages action would not protect its Navy performance? Did Loral have to seek a Navy extension or investigate unapproved vendors before accepting the demand? Did it lose the right to recover by waiting until three days after Austin finished the second subcontract? 29 N.Y.2d at 130–134.

## Rule and Holding

A contract is voidable for duress when assent is obtained through a wrongful threat that deprives the threatened party of a meaningful voluntary choice. In this supply-contract setting, a threat to breach by withholding needed goods is not alone sufficient. The threatened party must also show that it could not obtain the goods from another source within the relevant time and that an ordinary action for breach would be inadequate. Austin’s conduct, Loral’s unsuccessful reasonable search, and the Navy production deadlines established those circumstances as a matter of law. 29 N.Y.2d at 130–133.

Loral was not required to seek an uncertain government extension or buy critical precision components from vendors it reasonably regarded as unqualified or unsuitable. Its disaffirmance was timely because the delivery-related pressure continued until Austin completed the second subcontract. Loral could recover the coerced increases under the first subcontract, with the amount to be determined on remand. Id. at 132–134.

## Judgment

The order was modified, with costs, by reversing the part affirming dismissal of Loral’s claim; otherwise it was affirmed. The case was remanded for computation of Loral’s damages. Austin’s recovery of the unpaid balance on the second subcontract was not reversed. The court did not itself enter a final award of the approximately $22,250 Loral had claimed, and it did not rescind the second subcontract. 29 N.Y.2d at 134; 129 n. 2.

## Reasoning

Austin used performance already owed as leverage for new concessions. A seller is ordinarily free to negotiate whether to accept a new order, but Austin linked the new order to stopping shipments required by an existing contract. It also demanded more for both past and future deliveries under that contract. The majority treated the threatened breach as wrongful. The legal question then became whether Loral had a reasonable way to resist it. 29 N.Y.2d at 129–131.

The emergency was measured against production requirements, not simply against the next nominal Navy delivery date. Radar sets could not be assembled instantaneously after receipt of gears. Loral needed the withheld components in order to meet staggered commitments during September, October, and November. Its exposure to liquidated damages, cancellation, and damage to its prospects for future government business was concrete. The majority rejected the lower court’s treatment of those concerns as merely subjective or self-imposed. Id. at 131.

The September extension given to Austin did not show that October delivery from someone else would suffice. Austin had indicated that some components could arrive in August, and the actual late-August and early-September shipments still required exceptionally intensive work by Loral. A competing vendor’s offer merely to start in October would have been too late for the earlier production commitments and did not assure complete supply for later ones. The alternatives had to be commercially usable at the relevant time. Id. at 132.

The majority rejected an obligation to seek an extension from the Navy. Loral could not know how long an extension would be needed when replacement vendors promised only the start of delivery and delays were common. An extension also would not necessarily protect Loral’s government relationship, and subcontractor nonperformance did not automatically excuse failure under the prime contract. The majority did not hold that asking a customer for more time is never reasonable; it held that failure to pursue this uncertain option did not defeat duress on this record. Id.

Loral’s search was sufficient because it exhausted its approved-vendor list. These were inspected and satisfactory sources capable of meeting exacting engineering standards for military radar components. The law did not require Loral to treat every name in a trade directory as an equivalent alternative. Reasonableness depended on production capability, quality, and timing, not simply on the existence of other companies in the industry. Id. at 132–133 & n.6.

An ordinary damages action could not solve the operational problem. Loral could sue after Austin breached, but it would still lack the gears needed to fulfill its Navy contracts. A later monetary recovery would not supply timely performance or reliably prevent damage to the government relationship. Accepting the demanded prices, obtaining the components, and then seeking return of the excess was therefore a legally available response to the duress rather than a conclusive admission that the new prices were freely accepted. Id. at 133.

Loral’s delay in demanding a refund was evaluated from the end of the pressure, not from the first compelled agreement. Prompt disaffirmance is ordinarily required, but Austin retained the ability to stop critical deliveries under the second subcontract. Given its earlier conduct, Loral reasonably feared that an immediate challenge would recreate the same emergency. Waiting until three days after the final delivery did not amount to acceptance of the coerced modification once free of the pressure. Id.

Finally, the majority addressed the limits of appellate review. It said that it accepted the facts found below and was correcting their legal significance. The dissent disagreed about that characterization. The case thus presents both a substantive test for business compulsion and a dispute about when an appellate court may conclude that the evidence satisfies that test as a matter of law. Id. at 131 n.5, 134–135.

## Separate Opinions

Judge Bergan dissented, joined by Judges Breitel and Jasen. He would have affirmed because whether Austin’s conduct actually created economic duress was a factual question resolved against Loral at trial and affirmed by the Appellate Division. In his view, the majority could not make that issue a legal one simply by describing the facts as undisputed. 29 N.Y.2d at 134–135.

The dissent identified two important factual disputes. First, Austin maintained that renegotiation arose because Loral had failed to honor an understanding concerning the earlier allocation of items, and that the stoppage involved a customary vacation closing rather than an extortionate threat. Second, a trade registry listed many suppliers beyond the vendors Loral contacted. Whether Loral’s narrower search left it without reasonable alternatives was, to the dissent, a question of fact. These are Austin’s account and the dissent’s reasons for deference, not facts the majority adopted. Id.

Chief Judge Fuld’s opinion was joined by Judges Burke, Scileppi, and Gibson. The vote was four to three. The division was not between acceptance and rejection of economic duress as a doctrine; it concerned its application and the court’s authority to revisit the record. Id. at 135.

## Notes

Why Article 2 does not end the case. The subject matter was a sale of goods. UCC § 2-209(1) removes the requirement of new consideration for a modification, so the lack-of-consideration objection familiar from Alaska Packers would not itself defeat the increased prices. Duress remains available through UCC § 1-103(b), and Official Comment 2 to § 2-209 independently requires good faith. The assigned note expressly asks students to connect those provisions. C&M p. 492.

Why the two subcontracts must remain distinct. The first already obligated Austin to deliver. The second was a new exchange involving additional business. Loral’s complaint attacked the increases paid under the first, not the validity of the second award. Removing the duress facts would not give Loral a right simply to renounce the second contract because the first modification lacked new consideration. Different promises, different obligations, and the requested remedy must be identified separately. C&M p. 492; 29 N.Y.2d at 129–130 & n.2.

Mapping to the Restatement. Under the later §§ 175–176 framework, the coerced assent and lack of a reasonable alternative correspond to § 175; using a threatened breach of an existing duty to extract the concessions implicates § 176(1)(d). The reasonable-alternative inquiry explains why proof of a threat cannot finish the analysis. The contemporaneous July 22 protest supports causation, but the rule is not that only a party using those particular words can claim duress. Restatement §§ 175, 176; Austin, 29 N.Y.2d at 130–133.

Analytical variations. A timely qualified substitute, a reliable extension eliminating the emergency, or justified renegotiation without wrongful pressure could weaken Loral’s claim. Delay in disaffirming after the pressure ended could raise ratification concerns. These are hypotheticals, not facts established in the opinion.
