# Cour de cassation, Third Civil Chamber, December 15, 1993 (Cruz)
Pourvoi no. 91-10.199; Bull. civ. 1993, III, no. 174, p. 115; D. 1994.507 | Contracts

## Decision and setting

The Third Civil Chamber rejected the beneficiaries’ appeal from a judgment refusing to compel a sale after the promisor withdrew a unilateral promise before they exercised the option. This is the historical 1993 decision commonly called Cruz. Its official text anonymizes the parties as Mme Y and the consorts X. President Beauvois presided; Cathala was the reporting judge. The published judgment gives no numerical vote or separate opinion. Its old formation rule must be distinguished from the later reversal discussed below. (Official judgment, heading, “Sur le moyen unique,” and disposition.)

## Facts and procedural history

On May 22, 1987, Mme Y gave the consorts X a promise to sell an immovable property, valid until September 1, 1987. On May 26, she notified the beneficiaries that she no longer wished to sell. They exercised the option on June 10 and sued to compel completion of the sale. Thus they acted within the stated option period, but after the seller’s withdrawal. The Court of Appeal of Paris rejected their demand on November 8, 1990. The beneficiaries brought a pourvoi challenging that refusal. The short published judgment supplies neither the price nor a detailed description of the property, and does not identify a third-party purchaser or a damages award. (First “Attendu”; heading identifying the attacked decision.)

## The beneficiaries’ argument and the issue

The beneficiaries argued that a promisor’s commitment under a promise of sale was an obligation to transfer, or obligation de donner. They said the Court of Appeal had violated former Civil Code articles 1134 and 1589 by instead treating it as an obligation to act, or obligation de faire, and refusing compelled completion. The question was whether their exercise of an option within its agreed period could produce an enforceable sale when the promisor had already withdrawn. Their argument directly challenged the classification that prevented that remedy. (Second “Attendu.”)

## Holding and reasoning

The court accepted the Court of Appeal’s classification: until the beneficiaries declared that they would purchase, the promisor’s obligation was only an obligation de faire. It then treated the withdrawal as preventing the later option exercise from bringing together reciprocal intentions to sell and to buy. On that account, timely exercise of the option was insufficient because the seller’s withdrawal had intervened first. The beneficiaries’ single ground of appeal therefore failed. The operative order rejected the pourvoi; it did not order a transfer, award compensation, or remand for a damages determination. (“Mais attendu”; “PAR CES MOTIFS.”)

The reasoning turns on the legal effect attributed to the promise before exercise of the option. Under the 1993 approach, the promise did not establish an irrevocable consent to the eventual sale sufficient to support compelled completion following withdrawal. The court’s brief reasoning does not separately discuss consideration, an option fee, impossibility of performance, or the adequacy of a monetary remedy. Those matters should not be supplied as additional grounds for its result.

## Assignment and later treatment

The assigned comparative account uses this decision when explaining the former limitation on specific enforcement of unilateral promises and the reforms removing it. Its illustration includes a promisor selling the property to a third party and a beneficiary confined to damages. Those are features of the assigned general explanation, not findings or relief stated in the 1993 judgment. The assignment then describes article 1124’s reform rule that withdrawal during the option period does not prevent formation of the promised contract. (FA26 physical pages 120–121, printed 205–206, paragraph spanning the page break and footnote 74; following reform paragraph.)

There is also a directly verified later judicial change. On June 23, 2021, the same chamber expressly identified the 1993 rule and adopted a different interpretation under the pre-2016 law. A unilateral promise already contains the seller’s consent and the essential terms of the final contract; unless the parties stipulate otherwise, the promisor commits definitively to sell when making that promise. Accordingly, withdrawal does not prevent a sale from forming upon timely option exercise. That later judgment rejected the seller’s appeal against recognition of a completed sale. This bounded treatment check establishes that Cruz’s formation rule is historical; it does not purport to survey every later decision. (Pourvoi no. 20-17.554, paragraphs 7–14 and disposition.)
