# Cleveland Board of Education v. Loudermill
470 U.S. 532 (1985), decided March 19, 1985 | Legislation and the Regulatory State | Public employment, property, and procedural due process

## Two employees and the opportunity they were denied

The Cleveland Board of Education hired James Loudermill as a security guard in 1979. His application stated that he had never been convicted of a felony. Eleven months later, a routine records examination disclosed a 1968 grand-larceny conviction. The Board's business manager dismissed him by a November 3, 1980 letter charging dishonesty; the Board formally approved the discharge on November 13. Loudermill received no opportunity to respond to the dishonesty charge before losing his job. 470 U.S. at 535.

He did not dispute that the application was inaccurate, but maintained that he had understood his conviction to be a misdemeanor. His six-month suspended sentence and fine made that explanation plausible, and his job performance had been satisfactory. The relevant question was therefore not simply whether a record showed a felony conviction: it was whether the inaccuracy reflected dishonesty warranting discharge. A pretermination response could inform that judgment without assuring him ultimate success. Id. at 544–545 & nn. 9–10.

Under Ohio law, Loudermill was a classified civil-service employee, entitled to retain his position during good behavior and efficient service and removable only for specified cause. He appealed to the Civil Service Commission. A referee heard his case on January 29, 1981 and recommended reinstatement. The full Commission heard argument on July 20 and upheld the dismissal; findings followed in August, and his lawyers received notice on August 21. Although state judicial review was available, he instead filed a federal suit challenging the absence of a pretermination response and the length of the administrative process. Id. at 535–536, 538–539, 546 n. 11.

The companion plaintiff, Richard Donnelly, was a bus mechanic employed by the Parma Board of Education. The Board fired him in August 1977 after he failed an eye examination; he did not accept an offer to retake it. After a dispute over the timeliness of his appeal, the Commission reinstated him without backpay. He brought an essentially identical federal challenge. The examination concerned driving buses rather than repairing them, and the Court found no apparent emergency requiring his immediate removal. It did not resolve why backpay was denied: the Commission's decision was absent from the record, and the parties and court below offered different accounts of its authority. Id. at 536–537 & n. 1, 545 n. 10.

## The pleading posture and the statutory-property argument

The District Court dismissed both suits for failure to state a claim. Initially, it reasoned that the statute creating continued-employment rights also defined the discharge procedures; compliance with those procedures supplied all the process due. In denying reconsideration, it instead relied on a balancing analysis, while reaching the same result. A divided Sixth Circuit reversed on the need for pretermination process but upheld dismissal of Loudermill's separate delay and liberty claims. Both employers sought Supreme Court review, and Loudermill cross-petitioned on the rulings adverse to him. The Supreme Court affirmed the Sixth Circuit and remanded. Id. at 536–538 & n. 2, 548.

The federal claim depended on a property interest in continued employment. The Constitution did not itself create that interest: Ohio's for-cause restrictions supplied the independent legal entitlement. The employers nevertheless argued that the entitlement was conditioned on the very discharge procedures the statute prescribed, and that judicially requiring more would enlarge the state-created right. Id. at 538–540.

The Court rejected that reasoning. Arnett v. Kennedy's plurality had described acceptance of a statutory employment benefit as taking its procedural limitations along with it—the “bitter with the sweet.” Six Justices in Arnett had rejected that account, and subsequent decisions confirmed that federal procedural requirements could not be reduced to whatever a state chose to provide. The Court now expressly separated substantive entitlement from the process for taking it away. A legislature could choose whether to confer protected employment; once it did, the Constitution governed the minimum procedures for deprivation. Otherwise, due process would merely repeat the state's own procedural commands and impose no independent constraint. Id. at 540–541.

The Cleveland Board also argued that alleged dishonesty in obtaining the job meant Loudermill never had a legitimate entitlement. The Court rejected that late-raised argument because it assumed disputed facts at the pleadings stage and contradicted the reality that he had been hired and held the position. An employer could not avoid its procedural duty by recasting its asserted ground for dismissal as proof that no employment right had ever existed. Id. at 539 n. 5.

## Why some process had to precede dismissal

Justice White's Court applied the competing considerations associated with Mathews v. Eldridge: the employee's interest, the government's interests in prompt removal and manageable administration, and the risk of error. Losing employment deprived the worker of a livelihood, and obtaining another job could take time and be complicated by the circumstances of dismissal. A response could expose mistakes about facts and could affect whether a permissible discharge was appropriate. The right to process did not depend on proving in advance that the worker would prevail. Id. at 542–544.

Donnelly's later reinstatement suggested that an earlier response might have prevented a mistaken decision. The full Commission's adverse ruling prevented the Court from declaring Loudermill's dismissal erroneous, but the referee's contrary recommendation showed the matter was arguable. The employer likewise had an interest in avoiding mistakes, preserving useful labor, and reducing unnecessary disruption. Allowing a response need not entail extensive delay; an employer concerned about a significant workplace hazard could suspend the employee with pay. Id. at 544–545.

Footnote 8 limited the role of discretionary argument. The Court did not create a due-process hearing right whenever government action was wholly discretionary or allow a person to demand leniency contrary to governing law. Rather, when an entitlement existed and the decisionmaker had a legally permissible choice, a response could make that discretion better informed. Id. at 543–544 n. 8.

## The required initial check and the importance of later review

The hearing before termination need not definitively resolve whether dismissal was correct. Ohio already provided a full administrative hearing afterward, with judicial review. The earlier process served as an initial check for reasonable grounds to believe the charges were true and supported the proposed action. Its required elements were oral or written notice of the charges, an explanation of the employer's evidence, and an opportunity for the employee to present his side, orally or in writing. The Court did not demand a full adversarial evidentiary trial before termination. Id. at 545–546.

That limited requirement depended partly on the availability of full post-termination proceedings. The Court distinguished Goldberg's welfare setting and recognized that the necessary formality varied with the interests and later procedures. It also acknowledged situations in which postdeprivation process alone could be adequate; the immediate dismissals here were not justified on that basis. Id. at 542 n. 7, 545–546.

Because the complaints alleged no opportunity to respond, dismissing the pretermination claims at the pleadings stage was erroneous. The Court did not order reinstatement or determine final damages. The case returned for further proceedings after recognition of the procedural claim. Id. at 548.

## The separate delay and liberty claims

Loudermill argued that the nine-month administrative process independently violated due process. The Court agreed that a hearing must occur at a meaningful time and that excessive delay could become unconstitutional. It held only that the allegations here recited the chronology and asserted that nine months was too long without explaining why the delay was unreasonable. Some time reflected thorough procedures, and nine months was not unconstitutional merely by its length. That pleading failed to state a separate delay claim; the Court did not approve nine months as a universal permissible period. Id. at 546–547 & nn. 11–12.

The liberty claim alleged that the dishonesty accusation remained over Loudermill during review. It failed because the complaint did not allege publication of the dismissal reasons. This ruling concerned a separate reputational-liberty theory, not the existence of his property interest or the need for a pretermination response. Id. at 547 n. 13.

## Marshall, Brennan, and Rehnquist

Justice Marshall joined Part II's rejection of the statutory-procedure theory and concurred in the judgment because the employees received recognition of all the process they had requested. He would have required more in cases involving substantial disputes in testimonial evidence: confrontation, cross-examination, and presentation of supporting witnesses before wages ended. He emphasized the economic and personal damage during prolonged review, the uncertain availability of unemployment benefits, and the fact that reinstatement might not include backpay. A small preliminary hearing risked subjecting an innocent worker to irreparable harm during months of later proceedings. Id. at 548–551 & Marshall's note.

Justice Brennan joined Parts I–IV. He stressed that the precise procedures for disputed testimony remained sensitive to context and that a meaningful initial check might sometimes require producing contrary evidence or confronting an accuser. He did not understand the Court to foreclose that possibility. He also agreed that nine months was not an automatically acceptable delay. Id. at 551–554.

Brennan dissented from rejection of the delay claim before factual development. The defendants had not answered, and the record did not explain the long gaps: backlog, the employee's conduct, bad faith, or other causes might matter. Ohio's hearing deadline and other states' shorter administrative schedules supported inquiry rather than dismissal. He questioned how much thoroughness explained a reversal of the referee's favorable recommendation without new evidentiary development. Applying the then-governing pleading standard and the Mathews considerations, he would remand the delay issue for further proceedings. These were objections to the majority's treatment of the pleaded claim, not a finding that the delay was already proven unconstitutional. Id. at 554–559 & Brennan nn. 1–5.

Justice Rehnquist alone dissented from the Court's decision. He retained Arnett's view that the state-created entitlement included the state's attached procedural limitations. Ohio had conferred limited tenure and specified the method for termination in a single statutory arrangement. In his view, selecting the substantive restriction while discarding the associated procedures departed from the principle that state law defined the property interest. Although he called the particular balancing result unobjectionable, he considered the method subjective and unpredictable across cases such as Goldberg and Mathews. Id. at 559–563 & Rehnquist's note.

## Judgment and alignment

The Court affirmed the Sixth Circuit in all respects and remanded. White wrote the Court's opinion, joined throughout by Burger, Blackmun, Powell, Stevens, and O'Connor. Brennan joined Parts I–IV and dissented on the delay disposition. Marshall joined Part II and concurred in the judgment. Rehnquist dissented. This alignment matters more than a single vote shorthand: eight Justices supported recognizing the pretermination claim, while the separate writings disagreed about how much process was necessary and whether the delay claim should survive. Id. at 534, 548–563.

The governing distinction is between the source of an entitlement and the source of its procedural protection. State law creates qualifying employment rights; federal due process constrains the method of deprivation. The initial opportunity to answer the employer's charges serves to reduce avoidable error before wages cease, with its adequacy assessed alongside the later review available.
