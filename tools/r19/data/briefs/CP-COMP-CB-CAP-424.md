# Colgrove v. Battin

**413 U.S. 149 (1973) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 21, 1973.** Brennan wrote for a five-Justice Court, joined by Burger, White, Blackmun, and Rehnquist. Douglas dissented with Powell; Marshall dissented with Stewart; Powell also wrote separately. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical 555–556, own account printed 1094, followed by all three editorial notes through printed 1095.

The Court upheld a Montana federal district court’s local rule requiring six-person juries in civil cases. Six satisfied the Seventh Amendment, the statutory jury protection was coextensive with that constitutional right, and the local rule did not conflict with the then-existing Federal Rule 48 provision for stipulated smaller juries. The Court affirmed denial of mandamus seeking a twelve-person jury. It expressly left juries smaller than six undecided. 413 U.S. at 160–164.

## Facts, procedure, and objections

Local Rule 13(d)(1) of the District of Montana required six jurors, with such alternates as were impaneled, for civil trials. When District Judge Battin set the petitioner’s diversity case for a six-person jury, the petitioner sought Ninth Circuit mandamus directing him to impanel twelve. The Supreme Court opinion does not describe the underlying diversity claim’s merits, and none needs to be invented to understand the rule challenge. Id. at 149–151 & n.1.

The petitioner advanced three grounds. First, the Seventh Amendment’s preservation of civil jury trial required the common-law jury of twelve. Second, 28 U.S.C. §2072 separately protected trial by jury as at common law and therefore might require twelve even if the Constitution did not. Third, the local rule conflicted with Federal Rule 48, which permitted parties to stipulate to fewer than twelve; Rule 83 allowed local rules only if consistent with the Federal Rules. The Ninth Circuit rejected these grounds and denied the writ. The Supreme Court affirmed, 5–4. Id. at 150–151 & nn.2–4, 164.

## Constitutional holding: substance rather than every historical incident

The Court had previously approved six-person juries in certain state criminal cases in *Williams v. Florida*. That decision expressly reserved whether the Seventh Amendment’s references to common law required a different civil result. *Colgrove* therefore did not treat the civil question as already decided; it analyzed the distinct Amendment and concluded that it did not demand twelve. Id. at 151–152.

The first clause’s reference to suits at common law identified the cases in which the jury right was preserved, rather than specifying every jury characteristic. Constitutional-history materials showed concern that the Constitution might otherwise permit civil juries to disappear. The original Convention’s proposed civil-jury protection failed amid difficulty accommodating different state practices, and the eventual Amendment used general language. The majority inferred preservation of the institution in common-law civil cases rather than fixation of all its historical incidents. Its long eighth and ninth notes reproduce historical discussions and Story’s analysis that support that interpretation. Id. at 152–156 & nn.6–10.

The Court’s prior cases preserved the substance of the common-law jury right while allowing procedural adaptation. It therefore asked whether twelve members belonged to that protected substance. Drawing on *Williams* and the purposes of collective factfinding, the majority rejected the contention that reliability necessarily depended on retaining twelve. Older decisions describing a jury as twelve had not actually litigated and resolved that number; the Court treated those references as dicta rather than controlling adjudications. Id. at 156–158 & nn.11–13.

The Court acknowledged extensive literature and debate about smaller juries. The fifteenth note discusses opposing accounts of representation and deliberation and identifies empirical studies the majority considered persuasive. The Court found no demonstrated difference requiring departure from *Williams*’ conclusion, especially because the criminal decision had relied heavily on civil-jury studies. On the material then available, six could facilitate group deliberation and a representative cross-section. The majority did not hold that any number would suffice or choose a constitutional rule for fewer than six. Id. at 158–160 & nn.14–17.

This is the Court’s historical and functional assessment in 1973. The brief does not independently certify its empirical conclusion or claim that every juror population or later study produces identical results.

## Statutory protection and local-rule authority

Section 2072 preserved trial by jury as at common law and as declared by the Seventh Amendment. Although its direct subject was the Court’s general rules, §2071 required local rules to remain consistent with Acts of Congress, making this statutory objection relevant to Montana’s rule. The petitioner read the common-law phrase as adding a protection of jury characteristics beyond the Constitution. Id. at 161 & n.18.

The majority rejected that reading. The relevant language derived from the 1934 Enabling Act’s authorization to merge law and equity procedure. Its purpose was to ensure that the merger neither expanded nor contracted the jury-trial right, rather than freezing every common-law procedural feature. The statutory right thus matched the constitutional right; it did not independently require twelve. Id. at 161–163 & nn.19–21.

The Court also rejected the claim that Rule 48 made twelve mandatory without consent. The drafters likely assumed the traditional number, but that assumption did not become an express command once the constitutional premise was rejected. The Rule governed party stipulations and did not purport to prohibit local reduced-size juries. The petitioner had not sought a jury of an intermediate smaller number, and the possibility of stipulating to such a jury did not invalidate this local requirement. Id. at 163–164.

The extended twenty-third note distinguishes *Miner v. Atlass*, which invalidated a local admiralty discovery rule inconsistent with the governing rules. The Court did not decide whether every basic procedural innovation lay beyond local authority: it considered a reduction from twelve to six not such an innovation, based on its conclusion about comparable outcomes. That reasoning is narrower than a general permission for district courts to disregard national rules. Id. at 163–164 & n.23.

## Three dissenting writings

Douglas, joined by Powell, would resolve the case under the Federal Rules without reaching the constitutional question. Rule 48 rested on the national rulemaking process and congressional review; its stipulated-smaller-jury provision assumed twelve without stipulation. The local rule collided with that arrangement. In his view, only the authority responsible for Rule 48 could make the change. His sole starred footnote explains the historical congressional-review framework. Id. at 165.

Marshall, joined by Stewart, challenged the majority on constitutional, statutory, and rule grounds. He distinguished state criminal-jury precedents from the directly applicable federal civil guarantee and emphasized the Seventh Amendment’s express preservation language. The fragmentary history did not justify discarding the historically settled twelve-person jury, and changing the institution’s composition was different from adapting surrounding practice. He also disputed the functional analysis: smaller juries could alter representation and variability, while the majority’s test supplied no principled way to choose six rather than another number. His first extended note presents statistical illustrations as part of his critique, not factual findings about this particular jury. Id. at 166–182 & nn.1–9.

Marshall separately read §2072’s protection of jury trial as at common law as doing work beyond the Amendment’s reference to suits at common law. The majority’s construction made the added language redundant. He also argued that a rule allowing stipulations to any number below twelve conflicted with a local requirement fixing six and excluding stipulated seven-to-eleven-person juries. Policy preference for efficiency could not replace constitutional interpretation or proper national rulemaking. Id. at 182–188.

Powell’s short additional dissent reaffirmed his agreement with Douglas about Rule 48 incompatibility. He expressly declined to decide the Seventh Amendment issue; he did not join Marshall’s constitutional conclusion merely because both opposed the judgment. Id. at 188.

## Disposition and assigned context

The Supreme Court **affirmed the Ninth Circuit’s denial of mandamus**. The decision authorized this six-person civil-jury arrangement; it neither ruled on the diversity dispute’s merits nor approved juries below six. Id. at 160, 164.
