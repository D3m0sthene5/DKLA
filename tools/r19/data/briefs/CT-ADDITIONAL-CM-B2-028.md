# Fletcher v. Marshall
260 Ill. App. 3d 673, 632 N.E.2d 1105 (1994) | Contracts

## Decision and posture
Illinois’s Second District Appellate Court held that Marshall ratified an apartment lease made during minority by moving in and paying rent after turning eighteen. Those acts prevented him from later disaffirming by moving out and refusing further payment. The court reversed judgment for him and remanded for a hearing on Fletcher’s damages; it did not itself award the $2,500 she demanded. Justice Bowman wrote the April 15, 1994 opinion, with Justices Doyle and Quetsch concurring and no separate writing. (673, 677.)

## Facts and disputed chronology
Kirsten Fletcher and John Marshall dated while in high school. After his parents expelled him from their home following graduation, he moved in with Fletcher and her parents. The couple agreed to rent an apartment and share expenses. A lease dated April 29, 1991 called for $525 monthly rent for July 1, 1991 through June 30, 1992. They paid an extra half month’s rent and moved in before the stated term. Fletcher’s mother sometimes supplied groceries and paid other expenses when the couple could not; she also held an account into which they deposited paychecks and from which she paid rent on their behalf. (673–674.)

Marshall said he signed April 29 while seventeen. Fletcher and her mother said the lease, although typed in April, remained unsigned until June 30, after his eighteenth birthday. His father established May 30 as his birthday. The trial court found that he signed while still a minor. Marshall acknowledged making rent payments and paying at least part of the security deposit, though the record did not establish the deposit’s date. He left because the relationship was not working and he had a college opportunity. The witnesses disputed whether departure occurred August 2 or August 15. Fletcher remained and paid the full rent for the remaining ten months. (674.)

## Contribution claim and review standard
Fletcher sued in small claims for $2,500 plus costs as contribution toward rent. The bench-trial judgment favored Marshall because the court regarded minority at signing as preventing joint liability under the lease. Fletcher unsuccessfully sought reconsideration, invoking both necessaries and ratification. Her sole appellate argument was ratification through post-majority possession and payment; the appellate decision therefore did not resolve whether this apartment was a necessary. Common-law contribution required compulsory payment by a joint obligor of more than that person’s share of a common obligation. Whether Marshall remained bound was the prerequisite for that claim. (673–675.)

A minor’s contract was voidable, not void from inception. After majority the former minor could disaffirm or ratify; failure to disaffirm within a reasonable time or a distinct, decisive act clearly showing affirmation could establish ratification. Once ratified, the obligation could no longer be avoided through minority. (675.)

The parties disagreed about appellate review. Fletcher proposed de novo review because the relevant conduct was undisputed. The court rejected that approach: intent could require factual inferences even from undisputed actions. Ratification, like reasonable timing of disaffirmance, therefore generally presented a factual question, and the trial judgment would be disturbed only if against the manifest weight of the evidence. That standard required the opposite conclusion to be clearly evident. (675, 677.)

## Precedent and application
The court compared three Illinois Supreme Court decisions. Rubin treated post-majority installment payments and recording of a real-estate contract as ratification. Schlig involved post-majority conduct acknowledging a buyer as landlord and collecting rent on that buyer’s behalf after a minor’s conveyance. Shepherd treated continued employment and residence recognizing a mother’s life estate, together with delay in disaffirmance, as ratifying a deed. These comparisons established that affirmative conduct recognizing an earlier transaction could make it binding without a newly signed promise. (675–676.)

Here Marshall moved into the apartment and paid rent about two weeks after turning eighteen. He remained for approximately a month and a half and did nothing before leaving that indicated disaffirmance. The appellate court found no record basis for inferring that those acts meant anything other than unequivocal ratification. It thus reversed even under deferential manifest-weight review. His subsequent departure and refusal to pay could not undo an obligation already ratified. He remained liable for the rest of the lease term and, on the contribution theory, for payments Fletcher had made on his behalf. The court’s conclusion rested on the combined conduct in this record, rather than a general rule that every isolated payment invariably ratifies any minor’s contract. (676–677.)
