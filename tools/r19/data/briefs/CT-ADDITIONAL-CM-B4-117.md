# Roach v. Atlas Life Insurance Co.
769 P.2d 158 (Okla. 1989) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
On February 21, 1989, the Oklahoma Supreme Court vacated the Court of Appeals' opinion and affirmed summary judgment for Atlas. Justice Kauger wrote the majority opinion, joined by Chief Justice Hargrave and Justices Hodges, Lavender, Doolin, Alma Wilson, and Summers. Vice Chief Justice Opala concurred in the judgment. Justice Simms agreed with affirmance but dissented from extending bad-faith liability to a life-policy beneficiary. Thus all listed justices agreed with the result, while the broader reasoning did not command all their assent. (158, 160, 164.)

## Facts and separate procedural stages
Atlas issued a $35,000 life benefit through a University of Oklahoma group policy in 1970, insuring Nadine Roach and naming her then-husband Robert as beneficiary. They later divorced without changing the named beneficiary. Nadine died December 3, 1980. The policy required university notice and a death certificate before payment. Before Atlas received either, it was served with a December 30 court order prohibiting payment. The documents eventually arrived March 10, 1981, with a comment questioning Robert's beneficiary status. (160.)

The estate administratrix sued Atlas in February 1982, claiming Robert was not the intended beneficiary. Atlas acknowledged the policy, expressed willingness to pay the proper claimant, and pointed to the outstanding prohibition. Robert appeared without counsel. In April 1982 Atlas deposited the proceeds in interpleader and sought dismissal. The court discharged Atlas in June and, in the same order, granted the administratrix summary judgment for the proceeds. Robert appealed the beneficiary determination but did not appeal Atlas's dismissal. While that appeal remained pending, the fund was paid to the estate and a requested stay was denied. (160.)

In February 1984 the Court of Appeals reversed the beneficiary summary judgment because the insured's intended beneficiary presented a factual issue. Robert sought redeposit of the proceeds in September, but the administratrix dismissed her action, his motion was dismissed for lack of jurisdiction, and no remand adjudication occurred. The reversal therefore did not itself produce a final merits determination that Robert should receive the fund. (160–161.)

Robert filed the present bad-faith action in July 1986. Atlas obtained summary judgment in November; the Court of Appeals again reversed, relying on beneficiary factual disputes and suggesting that Robert should be paid under the policy. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve the distinct questions of a life beneficiary's bad-faith rights, the effect of interpleader discharge, and the sufficiency of this record. (160–161.)

## Life beneficiary's right to claim bad faith
An insurer's good-faith duty applies across insurance types, but not to every person entitled to payment from proceeds. A claimant needs a contractual or statutory relationship supporting the duty. The majority held a life-policy beneficiary meets both requirements: the policy expressly provides a benefit, and Oklahoma's third-party contract statute allows enforcement before rescission. Recognizing such a cause of action serves the insured's purchase of security for the beneficiary following death. This is the particular distinction highlighted in the assigned account; it does not grant identical rights to every claimant under liability or medical-expense coverage. (161–162 & nn. 2–6.)

Note 1 identifies a later statutory rule revoking certain death benefits favoring a former spouse following divorce, but expressly says it does not apply to this case. That historical source qualification prevents using a later divorce statute to explain the Court's disposition here. (161 n. 1.)

## Interpleader discharge and preclusion
Robert conceded that Atlas's discharge was final and barred another contract action for the policy proceeds. Atlas argued that the discharge extinguished all liability on every theory. The majority applied the interpleader statute in force when discharge occurred, former § 238. Its limitation to liability respecting the subject of the action mattered: the original proceeding dealt with contractual responsibility for the disputed fund, not an adjudication of bad faith. The dismissal did not automatically immunize Atlas against a tort issue not determined there. Note 10 also distinguishes a different jurisdiction's order expressly discharging an insurer from any and all liability; this order merely granted dismissal following deposit. (162 & nn. 7–10.)

The former cross-claim statute reinforced that construction. It said a defendant may assert a claim against another party, making the procedure permissive. Robert's failure to assert bad faith as a cross-claim therefore did not necessarily bar a later suit arising from the same transaction. Treating interpleader discharge as absolute immunity would conflict with that permissive structure. Note 15 adds that Robert had not first sued on the contract and then split his own cause: the administratrix alone initiated the earlier action. Notes 8 and 11 identify replacement pleading provisions, while preserving the historical statutes controlling these events. (162–163 & nn. 8, 11–15.)

## Why Atlas nevertheless prevailed
Removing a categorical preclusion barrier did not establish bad faith. Even when underlying facts are undisputed, summary judgment is inappropriate if reasonable people could draw materially different inferences; otherwise the moving party may receive judgment as a matter of law. Here the outstanding order prevented Atlas from disbursing the policy proceeds. Under these facts, reasonable people would not disagree that Atlas acted in good faith in settling the claim. The trial court's summary judgment was therefore correct, regardless of the possibility of a beneficiary claim in a different record. (163–164 & n. 16.)

Justice Simms concurred in affirmance but characterized the extension of a Christian bad-faith cause of action to someone who was not a direct contracting party as dicta beyond the then-current boundaries. He believed the majority undermined the purpose of interpleader. His disagreement concerned the recognition and reach of the duty, not an alternative award to Robert. Opala supplied no separate written explanation for concurring only in the judgment. (164.)

## Exact disposition and source scope
The Court of Appeals' opinion was vacated and the trial judgment for Atlas affirmed. The Supreme Court neither awarded Robert $35,000 nor resolved the earlier beneficiary dispute in his favor.
