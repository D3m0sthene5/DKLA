# Trump v. Illinois
No. 25A443, 607 U.S. ___ (2025); assigned citation 146 S. Ct. 432 | Supreme Court of the United States | December 23, 2025 | LRS-SUP-0006B

## Facts and record posture

The federal government asserted that immigration-enforcement operations around Chicago encountered obstruction, threats, assaults, property damage, and violent protests, particularly at an Immigration and Customs Enforcement processing facility in Broadview. On October 4, 2025, President Trump called approximately 300 Illinois National Guard members into federal service to protect federal personnel and property. Texas Guard members were federalized and sent to Chicago the next day. The invoked statute was 10 U.S.C. §12406(3), permitting a call into federal service when the President is unable with the regular forces to execute federal law. Court opinion, slip pp.1-2.

The Court's introductory account expressly attributes the descriptions of resistance and violence to the Government. Justice Alito's dissent elaborates on the Government's declarations: blocked access to the Broadview facility, attacks on personnel and vehicles, injuries, threats against officers and families, and asserted failures of local police assistance. Those descriptions supported his evaluation of interim relief; the stay decision did not conclusively adjudicate every disputed incident. Alito dissent, slip pp.7-11. Nor did it decide whether any particular immigration stop or arrest violated the Constitution. Kavanaugh concurrence, slip pp.6-7 n.4, identifies that underlying dispute as outside this application.

## Procedure and parties' positions

Illinois and other respondents challenged federalization and deployment. The Northern District of Illinois entered a temporary restraining order barring both. The Seventh Circuit allowed Guard members to remain federalized within Illinois but maintained the restriction on deployment. The Government then requested a Supreme Court stay. Justice Barrett referred the application to the full Court. Court opinion, slip pp.1-2.

The Court requested supplemental letter briefs on the meaning of regular forces, an issue addressed by the district court but absent from the parties' initial Supreme Court briefs. In the supplemental briefing, the Government argued that the phrase referred to civilian federal law-enforcement officers, while respondents argued that it meant the regular military. The Government also relied on inherent presidential constitutional authority to protect federal personnel and property, while maintaining that such protection did not constitute execution of the laws under the Posse Comitatus Act. Id. at 2-3.

Alito disputed the propriety of reaching the regular-forces question at all. His account was that respondents had initially adopted the civilian-officer meaning, declined to defend the district court's different interpretation on appeal, and failed to contest the Government's waiver argument in their initial Supreme Court submission. He urged deciding the controversy on the parties' original premise. His waiver characterization is a dissenting analysis, not a holding that the Court was jurisdictionally forbidden to interpret the statute. Alito dissent, slip pp.2-4.

## Question, preliminary conclusions, and result

The immediate question was whether the Government had shown grounds to stay the order restricting Guard deployment under §12406(3). The Court denied the application. Its legal conclusions are expressly framed as likely conclusions at a preliminary stage, not a final merits judgment resolving the full statutory and constitutional landscape. Court opinion, slip pp.2-3.

The Court concluded that regular forces likely means the regular United States military. It reasoned that §12406(3) likely presupposes a situation in which the military could legally execute federal law but the President is unable to accomplish that function with those forces. The Government had not identified sufficient authority for that predicate in Illinois. Id. at 2.

The Court expressly left undecided the reviewability of presidential findings under §12406(3) or another statute. It also did not decide all alternative deployment authorities, the Insurrection Act, or the legality of particular immigration encounters. The denied stay left the operative deployment restriction in place; it was not a new order releasing Guard members from federal service. Id. at 1-3; Kavanaugh concurrence, slip pp.5-7 nn.2-4.

## Court's statutory reasoning

The first interpretive step identifies which forces the President must be unable to use. Treating regular forces as the regular military changes the predicate from the asserted inadequacy of civilian enforcement resources to inability to execute law with military forces. The Court did not explain this initial reading at length in its short order, but adopted it as the likely meaning after supplemental briefing. Court opinion, slip p.2.

The second step links factual or operational inability to legal authority. Because the statute calls for assessing military ability to execute law, the Court reasoned that it likely operates where the military could lawfully perform that task. The Posse Comitatus Act, 18 U.S.C. §1385, generally prohibits military execution of the laws absent express constitutional or congressional authorization. The President had not invoked another statute supplying such an exception here. Id.

The Government instead relied on a protective power while arguing that protection falls outside execution of the laws for Posse Comitatus purposes. That position created a statutory problem: if protection is not execution under one related statute, why is it execution under §12406(3)? The Court applied a presumption that Congress does not silently assign different meanings to the same term in related statutes, citing Azar v. Allina Health Services. On the Government's own account, the protective task therefore failed to show the statutory predicate necessary for this federalization. Court opinion, slip p.3.

The Court did not hold that every protective military mission is unlawful or definitively deny all inherent Article II protective authority. Its expressed conclusion was that the Government had not carried its burden, in this posture and on these arguments, to establish §12406(3) authority for the challenged Guard action. That limited phrasing matters alongside the separate opinions' warnings about the reasoning's possible consequences.

## Kavanaugh's concurrence in the judgment

Justice Kavanaugh voted to deny the stay but would have stopped at a narrower ground. He agreed that regular forces likely refers to the military. On the current record, however, the President apparently had not made the required finding that he was unable with the military, as distinct from civilian officers, to ensure execution of federal law. Kavanaugh recognized substantial presidential discretion to make that finding and would not have decided the further questions about legal authority and protective functions. His concurrence begins on Court slip p.3 and continues through p.7.

He considered the Court's further statutory analysis difficult and consequential. His hypothetical involved a mob threatening a federal courthouse when regular military forces could not arrive promptly and civilian security was overwhelmed. On his understanding of the Court's order, §12406(3) might still prohibit federalizing available Guard units for protection. The example is hypothetical, not an incident in this litigation. Kavanaugh concurrence, slip pp.4-5.

He observed that the order did not resolve Insurrection Act authority or purport to disturb the President's long-asserted Article II power to use the regular military, as distinct from federalizing the Guard, for protection. He worried that its interpretation could encourage more use of the regular military. He preferred further briefing and potentially argument before resolving such issues. Although the order was preliminary, he recognized that lower courts could treat its likely legal conclusions as guiding interim injunctions in comparable circumstances until the Court changed course. Id. at 5-6 nn.2-3.

## Alito's dissent, joined by Thomas

Justice Alito would grant the stay. His first objection concerned party presentation. He would assume the parties' original civilian-officer reading and decide whether the district court wrongly rejected the President's assessment of those resources. Raising an allegedly abandoned argument and expressing tentative answers to additional questions without substantial briefing or argument was, for him, an unjustified departure from the adversarial process. Alito dissent, slip pp.1-4.

On that assumed reading, he rejected the district court's stringent understanding of unable. The statute should not require deployment of every conceivable federal officer or the impossibility of enforcing even a small amount of law. Ordinary usage can describe inability when performance would impose unacceptable consequences. He favored substantial impairment rather than literal total incapacity. Id. at 4-6.

He also invoked Martin v. Mott and Luther v. Borden as supporting conclusiveness of, or at least deference to, presidential militia-call determinations. In his view the district court improperly discounted that precedent. He found the Government's declarations sufficient even without special deference. He criticized the district court's reliance on declarants' failure to mention certain grand-jury nonindictments and on a separate protest case: the record did not establish that the declarants knew of the grand-jury actions, and the cited separate order did not find those declarations false. He also distinguished local police capability from willingness to assist. Id. at 6-11.

Even if regular forces meant the military, Alito thought the presidential memorandum's express finding of insufficiency, together with a presumption of regularity, supported the call. He rejected the Court's apparent limitation that inability cannot arise from lack of lawful authority: ordinary usage includes legal inability, and the statute does not specify that the obstacle must be operational rather than legal. Id. at 11-12.

His further objections addressed interacting authorities. Section 12406(3) itself might supply congressional authorization under Posse Comitatus; inherent presidential protective power might also matter. In footnote 3, he questioned rather than definitively resolved whether Posse Comitatus applies to federalized Guard units. Most directly for interpretation, he argued that the same-meaning presumption is defeasible. The Executive's narrow reading of execution under Posse Comitatus rested on that Act's history, purpose, and constitutional concerns. Section 12406 could instead use execution in its ordinary broader sense, including protection. On the Court's approach, he argued, Guard members might be permitted more intrusive enforcement while being barred from protective duties, an implausible inversion of traditional practice. Id. at 12-16 & nn.1-3.

Alito did not purport to settle every issue he raised; he expressed doubts and criticized the breadth and process of the Court's interim reasoning. His assertions of judicial deference and constitutional authority are not majority holdings.

## Gorsuch's separate dissent

Justice Gorsuch also would grant a stay, proceeding on the parties' original civilian-enforcement premise. He relied substantially on Parts I-A, I-B-1, I-C, and I-D of Alito's dissent: party presentation, the meaning of inability, and the evidentiary record. He did not join Alito's full opinion or adopt its Part I-B-2 deference analysis. Gorsuch dissent, slip pp.1-2.

Gorsuch listed unresolved questions about regular forces, reviewability, independent statutory authority, interaction with Posse Comitatus and the Insurrection Act, inherent protective power, Congress's militia authority, and constitutional limits on domestic military use. He considered the compressed interlocutory record insufficient to answer those questions and expressly reserved them. His reasoning supports a narrow interim result rather than a comprehensive constitutional authorization.

## Alignment and practical disposition

The stay was denied, with Kavanaugh concurring in the judgment and Alito, Thomas, and Gorsuch dissenting. The resulting alignment is six votes against the stay and three for it. The Court's opinion is unsigned; the brief does not attribute its authorship to a particular Justice. Kavanaugh agreed with the regular-military reading but rejected the need for the Court's broader rationale. Gorsuch endorsed only specified portions of Alito's reasoning.

The operative distinction is between continued federalized status and actual deployment: the Seventh Circuit had permitted the former while preserving the restriction on the latter. The Supreme Court's denial left that interim arrangement in place. It did not finally dispose of the lawsuit, resolve all factual disputes, or issue a comprehensive rule governing every future emergency.
