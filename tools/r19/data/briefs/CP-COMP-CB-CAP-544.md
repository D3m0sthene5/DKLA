# Lucky Brand Dungarees, Inc. v. Marcel Fashions Group, Inc.

**590 U.S. ___, 140 S. Ct. 1589 (2020) · Supreme Court of the United States · May 14, 2020**

Justice Sotomayor wrote for a unanimous Court. The Court reversed the Second Circuit and remanded. The assigned account is Note 3 on CivPro PDF pages 647–648, printed 1282–1283. Pinpoints below use the Court opinion’s numbered pages 1–12 in the preserved revised official slip opinion; the preceding two syllabus pages are separate.

## The three rounds of litigation

Marcel Fashions and Lucky Brand sold jeans and other clothing. Marcel registered “Get Lucky” in 1986. Lucky Brand began selling apparel in 1990 using its registered “Lucky Brand” trademark and other marks containing “Lucky.” Those distinctions matter: litigation over the phrase “Get Lucky” was not automatically litigation over every mark containing the word “Lucky.” Opinion pp. 1–2.

Marcel sued in 2001, alleging that Lucky Brand’s use of “Get Lucky” in advertising infringed its mark. The parties settled in 2003. Lucky Brand agreed to stop using that phrase, and Marcel released claims concerning Lucky Brand’s use of its own trademarks. Id. at 2.

In 2005, Lucky Brand sued Marcel and its licensee, alleging that a new clothing line copied its designs and logos. Marcel counterclaimed. As relevant here, the counterclaims alleged that Lucky Brand continued to use “Get Lucky,” either in violation of the settlement or alongside Lucky Brand’s marks in a confusing combination. They did not allege that Lucky Brand’s own marks, standing alone and without its alleged use of “Get Lucky,” infringed Marcel’s mark. Lucky Brand raised the settlement release in a motion to dismiss. The district court denied that motion without prejudice; Lucky Brand pleaded the defense again in its answer, but did not pursue it further during the action. Id. at 2–3.

That litigation ended in two stages. As a discovery sanction, the court found that Lucky Brand violated the agreement by continuing to use “Get Lucky” and permanently enjoined copying or imitating Marcel’s mark. The injunction did not prohibit Lucky Brand’s other marks merely because they contained “Lucky.” A jury then found against Lucky Brand on the remaining counterclaims involving its continued use of the “Get Lucky” phrase alongside its own marks. Id. at 3.

Marcel sued again in April 2011. This time it challenged Lucky Brand’s post-2010 use of its own marks, including marks containing “Lucky.” It did not allege continued use of the “Get Lucky” phrase itself. Marcel claimed that this conduct infringed its mark in a manner previously found unlawful and sought an injunction against Lucky Brand’s marks containing “Lucky.” The Court’s first note reproduces the relevant allegations to document the distinction; it does not establish their merits. Id. at 3–4 & n.1.

## Lower-court decisions and question presented

The district court initially entered summary judgment for Lucky Brand because it considered Marcel’s new claims essentially the same as the earlier counterclaims. On the first appeal, the Second Circuit held that the new claims involved subsequent infringements and were not barred. It also rejected contempt: the earlier injunction governed “Get Lucky,” not Lucky Brand’s standalone marks. The earlier jury could have found infringement solely from use of the prohibited phrase, so its verdict did not establish that every separate Lucky Brand mark was unlawful. Id. at 4–5.

On remand, Lucky Brand again invoked the 2003 release. Marcel responded that Lucky Brand had lost that defense by failing to litigate it fully in 2005. The district court rejected that objection and dismissed the action based on the release. On the second appeal, the Second Circuit instead applied “defense preclusion.” Its test asked whether the prior action had produced a merits adjudication between the same parties, whether the defense was or could have been asserted there, and whether the district court considered preclusion appropriate. It vacated the dismissal. Id. at 5–6.

The Supreme Court considered whether that prior failure to pursue the release prevented Lucky Brand from using it against the new claims. Its answer was narrow: because these actions involved different claims, ordinary preclusion principles did not bar the defense. The Court did not establish that claim preclusion can never apply to defenses. Id. at 1, 7 n.2, 12.

## Governing distinctions and application

Issue preclusion concerns an issue actually decided and necessary to an earlier judgment. Claim preclusion concerns successive litigation of the same claim and can bar grounds or defenses that were available but never litigated. A common transaction or nucleus of operative facts identifies the latter category. When a plaintiff wins, the claim merges into the judgment: the plaintiff cannot independently seek more relief on that same claim, and the defendant cannot avoid the judgment by offering new defenses. But a genuinely new claim can permit assertions and defenses omitted from earlier litigation. Id. at 6–7.

The Court had never explicitly recognized defense preclusion as a free-standing category detached from claim and issue preclusion. Any bar had to satisfy at least one of those established doctrines. The parties agreed that issue preclusion did not apply here, making identity of claims a necessary predicate for the asserted bar. In its second note, the Court questioned whether applying claim preclusion to defenses might sometimes be inappropriate even when that predicate exists: defendants may omit defenses for reasons of expense, evidentiary difficulty, or the stakes of an earlier action. The Court expressly left that larger question unresolved. Id. at 7 & n.2.

The 2005 and 2011 actions lacked the necessary identity. The former depended on alleged use of “Get Lucky,” alone or together with Lucky Brand’s own marks. The latter depended on Lucky Brand’s own marks without use of that phrase. The new conduct also followed the end of the earlier action. Claim preclusion generally does not extinguish claims based on events occurring after the first complaint; such events can supply new material operative facts. Changing marketplace conditions make that distinction especially relevant in trademark disputes, where confusion and enforceability can change over time. Id. at 8–10.

The Court also rejected the idea that allowing the defense would impair the prior judgment. A defense against liability for new, different uses of other marks would not undo the earlier prohibition against using “Get Lucky.” The same distinction that permitted Marcel to bring the new claims also prevented the earlier litigation from barring Lucky Brand’s defenses to them. Id.

## Marcel’s contrary authorities and the judgment’s limits

Marcel relied on decisions involving enforcement of an existing judgment or a collateral attack seeking to undo it. Ordinary preclusion can prevent a later claim or defense from nullifying rights already established. But Lucky Brand’s release defense did not attack the earlier injunction or verdict; it addressed different conduct. The Court’s third note explains why enforcement in the original action and supplemental relief need not be treated as a separate lawsuit barred by claim preclusion. That explanation did not convert this new trademark action into enforcement of an adjudicated right covering all Lucky Brand marks. Id. at 10–11 & n.3.

The Court similarly distinguished Beloit’s successive suits concerning bonds of the same issue. A later defense attacking the securities’ original validity there threatened the first judgment’s established rights. Lucky Brand’s defense did not have that effect on the earlier trademark judgment. Id. at 11–12.

The Supreme Court reversed the Second Circuit and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion. It did not itself enter a new trademark-infringement judgment or definitively construe the release’s scope. Its holding permits the defense to be asserted in these circumstances; it does not guarantee the defense’s success on every possible subsequent question. Id. at 12.

The textbook preserves the central distinction and the expressly reserved question in note 2. Its discussion should be read as rejecting an independently expanded preclusion doctrine on these facts, not as a categorical abolition of all preclusion of defenses.
