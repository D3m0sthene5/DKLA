# Durfee v. Duke

**375 U.S. 106 (1963) — Supreme Court of the United States, December 2, 1963.** Justice Stewart delivered the Court's opinion. Justice Black concurred separately; no dissent is reported. The assigned account is CivPro, physical PDF pages 218–219, printed page 393.

## Facts and the first judgment

The parties disputed title to bottom land along the Missouri River, whose main channel formed the Nebraska–Missouri boundary. In 1956, the Durfees sued Duke in Nebraska to quiet title. Nebraska's subject-matter jurisdiction depended on whether the disputed land was in Nebraska. That location, in turn, depended on whether a shift in the river's course had occurred through avulsion or accretion. The parties did not dispute that this factual distinction controlled the jurisdictional issue. (107–108 & n.3.)

Duke appeared through counsel and expressly contested Nebraska's subject-matter jurisdiction. After a hearing, the trial court found for the Durfees and quieted title in them. On Duke's appeal, the Nebraska Supreme Court tried the matter de novo on the existing record and found that the avulsion rule applied, the land remained in Nebraska, Nebraska's courts had subject-matter jurisdiction, and the Durfees owned the land. Duke did not seek Supreme Court review of that judgment. Personal jurisdiction was uncontested: she had generally appeared and initiated the appeal. (108; 111 n.8.)

## The second lawsuit and procedural correction

Two months later, Duke sued the Durfees in Missouri to quiet title to the same land, alleging that it was in Missouri. Diversity allowed removal to federal district court. After hearing evidence, the district court expressed the view that the land was in Missouri, but held that the Nebraska judgment had already determined the issues and bound it under res judicata. The Eighth Circuit reversed, allowing renewed examination of Nebraska's subject-matter jurisdiction because the controversy involved land and territorial authority. (108–109.)

The assigned account attributes the allowance of collateral attack to the Missouri federal district court. The complete opinion establishes the opposite procedural sequence: the district court honored the Nebraska judgment; the **Court of Appeals** permitted renewed litigation. The Supreme Court reversed that appellate judgment and affirmed the district court's judgment. Keeping the courts straight matters because the Supreme Court approved the district court's decision to stop the second inquiry after identifying the prior jurisdictional litigation. (109, 116; assigned printed 393.)

## Issue, rule, and holding

Could Duke retry Nebraska's subject-matter jurisdiction in Missouri after the parties had fully litigated and obtained a final decision on that issue in Nebraska? No. A second court may inquire whether the first court possessed jurisdiction. But a judgment receives full faith and credit even on jurisdictional questions when that inquiry shows that those questions were **fully and fairly litigated and finally decided** in the original court. Here, the second court's permissible inquiry established those conditions, precluding another determination of where the land lay as between these parties. (110–111, 116.)

Article IV's Full Faith and Credit Clause and implementing §1738 generally require a judgment to receive at least the res judicata effect it would receive in the rendering State. There was no dispute that Nebraska would give this judgment full preclusive effect. The federal statute extends recognition obligations to federal courts as well as state courts. (107 nn.1–2; 109 & nn.5–6.)

## Why jurisdictional finality applied

Duke relied on precedents recognizing that a judgment without jurisdiction may be challenged elsewhere. The Court acknowledged that principle but explained that it did not answer the permissible scope of the second court's inquiry. Decisions concerning both personal and subject-matter jurisdiction had established the binding effect of a jurisdictional issue already contested and decided. The rule did not rest on pretending that jurisdiction can never be questioned; it prevented a second trial of a jurisdictional issue after a fair first trial. (110–113.)

Finality served the same institutional purpose here as on other issues. After parties present evidence and legal arguments and obtain a decision, a second court's reconsideration is not inherently more reliable than the first. There must be a point at which litigation ends. Footnote 4 specifically distinguishes this case from Chicot County's circumstance of an available jurisdictional objection not actually litigated; Durfee involved an express contest and final decision. The Court therefore did not need to decide every consequence of silence, default, or a mere opportunity to object. (108 n.4; 113–114.)

The land's importance did not justify a special exception. A court ordinarily cannot directly determine title to land in another State, just as a divorce court ordinarily requires the relevant domicile. But where the land lies, like where someone is domiciled, may itself require judicial determination. The prohibition on adjudicating out-of-state land did not permit the same litigants to relitigate that jurisdictional fact after Nebraska had fully and fairly decided it. (114–115.)

## Limits and Black's concurrence

The majority recognized possible overriding exceptions, including federal preemption and sovereign immunity, but found none here. Its discussion of Kalb and United States Fidelity noted that jurisdiction had not actually been litigated in those original forums. Footnote 12 also reproduced Restatement factors concerning the strength of the competing jurisdictional policy. The opinion therefore does not announce that every jurisdictional ruling is immune from collateral challenge in every setting. (114 & n.12.)

Equally important, the private Nebraska litigation settled title **between the parties**. It did not bind either State concerning its sovereign boundary or authority over the land. Either State could pursue an independent Supreme Court proceeding or a negotiated settlement of the boundary dispute. (115–116 & n.15.) Black concurred with the express understanding that the Court was not deciding whether Duke would remain bound if a later authoritative interstate proceeding or compact placed the tract in Missouri. That future consequence remained unresolved. (116–117.)

## Exact judgment and significance

The Supreme Court **reversed the Court of Appeals and affirmed the district court**. (116.) It did not independently decide the river-change evidence or redraw the States' boundary. Durfee makes the key distinction between checking whether a jurisdictional question was fairly and finally resolved and conducting that jurisdictional trial again. A competing view of the land's physical location did not undo the first private judgment.
