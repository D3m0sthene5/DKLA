# King Vision Pay Per View, Ltd. v. J.C. Dimitri’s Restaurant, Inc.

**Civil Procedure — 180 F.R.D. 332 (N.D. Ill. 1998). Decided June 17, 1998, No. 98 C 2787.** Shadur, Senior District Judge, issued a memorandum opinion and order on his own initiative. Assigned: CivPro physical page 357, printed page 681, Note 3. No separate opinion is reported.

The defendants responded to most of the complaint by neither admitting nor denying the allegations and demanding strict proof. The court held that this formula failed to comply with Rule 8(b). Under the historical Rule 8(d), the affected allegations were admitted, and the action would proceed on that basis. This was an order concerning the answer's effect, not a final judgment awarding King Vision damages or resolving every aspect of the underlying claim. (333–334.)

King Vision sued J.C. Dimitri’s Restaurant and James Chelios. Their response contained thirty-five paragraphs. According to the court, thirty paragraphs, including material incorporated by reference, used the offending nonresponse. The opinion identifies direct uses in paragraphs 6–12, 17, 25–26, and 33–34, with incorporation in paragraphs 19 and 28. It does not develop the underlying commercial events or independently establish liability facts. The relevant concrete conduct for this decision was the defendants' way of answering the complaint. (333.)

Rule 8(b), the court explained, gave defendants three ways to address an allegation. They could admit it, deny it, or state the rule's lack-of-knowledge-or-information disclaimer when that statement could be made in the objective and subjective good faith demanded by Rule 11. A proper disclaimer then operated as a denial. Declining both admission and denial while demanding proof was none of these responses. The demand itself did not excuse compliance or place an allegation in issue. (333.)

The court distinguished a genuine disclaimer from this evasive formula. The permitted disclaimer communicates that the responding party lacks knowledge or information sufficient to form a belief about the allegation's truth. The defendants' chosen language made no such representation. Its additional demand for strict proof had no recognized function in modern federal pleading, in the judge's account. A claimant's ordinary obligation to prove contested facts does not give an answering defendant a fourth option under Rule 8(b). (333 & n.3.)

The judge explained why he applied the admission consequence rather than merely directing another correction. He had encountered similar defective answers for years and had repeatedly addressed them in orders, published opinions, and educational programs. Footnote 2 points to his earlier published treatment in Gilbert; footnotes 1, 3, and 4 emphasize the simplicity of compliance, unsuccessful inquiries about the origin of the strict-proof formula, and the limits of educational efforts. These observations explain the court's response here, rather than adding an element that a defendant must previously receive a personal warning before the rule can operate. (333 & nn.1–4.)

Historical Rule 8(d) treated allegations requiring a responsive pleading as admitted when not denied, with an express exception for allegations concerning the amount of damages. The court invoked that provision and cited supporting decisions. Footnote 5 stresses that the clarity of the rule mattered more than the age or number of cases reiterating it. The opinion therefore applied the rule to the allegations addressed by the improper responses and directed that the case continue on that basis. Its concluding reference to future violators supplied notice of similar treatment; it did not impose a monetary sanction in this order. (334 & n.5.)

The assigned account accurately illustrates the difference between an effective denial and a nonresponse. It follows an editorial quotation about evasive denials and precedes a separate discussion of plausibility in defensive pleadings. This decision itself did not address Twombly or Iqbal, which postdate it, nor did it decide how a plausibility standard applies to an answer. Its useful distinction is between contesting an allegation through an authorized response and merely instructing the plaintiff to prove it. (Assigned Note 3 and surrounding text.)
