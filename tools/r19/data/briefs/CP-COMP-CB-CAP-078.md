# Covey v. Town of Somers

**Civil Procedure · CP-COMP-CB-CAP-078 · 351 U.S. 141 (1956) · May 7, 1956**

Covey held that statutory notice by mailing, posting, and publication did not satisfy due process in a tax foreclosure when the town knew the owner was mentally incompetent, unable to understand the proceeding, and unprotected by a guardian. The Court **reversed and remanded**. An important textbook correction concerns chronology: the owner had not yet been judicially declared incompetent or committed when notice was sent. Those events followed the foreclosure judgment and deed. The holding rested on the town's knowledge of her actual condition, not on a preexisting adjudication of incompetence. (351 U.S. at 142–147.)

New York's statutory procedure permitted judicial foreclosure of certain tax liens outstanding for more than four years. The filed delinquency list functioned as a complaint and notice of pendency. Notice announced a payment deadline at least seven weeks away and an additional twenty days to answer; default could produce a deed conveying a fee simple. The statutory procedure purported to apply to infants, incompetent persons, absentees, and nonresidents. Another provision made the deed presumptive evidence of regularity and, after two years, made that presumption conclusive while barring actions to set it aside. (Id. at 142–144.)

The Town of Somers began foreclosure against Nora Brainard's property on May 8, 1952. It mailed notice to her, posted notice at the post office, and published it in two local newspapers. She did not answer. Judgment was entered September 8, and a deed issued October 24. She was adjudged incompetent October 29 and committed to a hospital November 6. Covey filed bond pursuant to an order appointing him her committee on February 13, 1953. Counsel later offered to repay the town's outlays, but the town refused. The stated $480 total included taxes, interest, penalties, foreclosure expenses, attorney's fees, and maintenance. The town's proposed minimum sale prices—initially $6,500 and later $3,500—were not Supreme Court awards or findings of appraised value. (Id. at 144–145 & n.2.)

Covey moved in the original proceeding to open the default, vacate the judgment, set aside the deed, and permit an answer. Uncontroverted affidavits alleged that Brainard had been incompetent for upwards of fifteen years, and that town officials knew she could neither manage her affairs nor understand the notice. She allegedly owned four other improved properties and could have paid the taxes, but lived alone and lacked a relative in New York or someone protecting her interests. She had no guardian during the foreclosure. The Supreme Court assumed those uncontroverted allegations for purposes of the motion; it did not conduct independent factfinding about her condition. (Id. at 145–146.)

The County Court denied relief, including the constitutional challenge. The Appellate Division affirmed over one dissent, reasoning that relief was unavailable after the statutory deadlines. The Court of Appeals affirmed and amended its remittitur to state that a necessarily involved constitutional question had been presented and passed upon. That procedural history created a threshold disagreement about whether the Supreme Court should reach the notice issue at all. (Id. at 144–145.)

The town and New York argued that Covey had chosen the wrong procedural vehicle: an independent action rather than a motion was required. The Court's opinion found no statutory exclusion of a motion and noted state recognition of equitable power to open a foreclosure default. Footnote 1 addresses the state precedent on that power. The town also conceded that the statutory action concerned procedural irregularities, whereas the constitutional claim challenged the notice scheme as applied. The Court found the contrary position unsupported by a state decision, and treated the amended remittitur as confirmation that the state court had reached the federal question. (Id. at 143–144 & n.1.)

On that understanding of state procedure, the Court applied the requirement of notice reasonably calculated, in all the circumstances, to inform interested persons and afford an opportunity to object. Formal adherence to a mailing, posting, and publication statute was not enough when the authorities knew those measures would not meaningfully inform this owner. Brainard was allegedly unable to understand any of the notice and lacked a guardian to act for her. The notice was therefore constitutionally insufficient as applied. Her capacity to understand and the town's knowledge were essential; the opinion did not invalidate the tax foreclosure statute in every application or require actual receipt and understanding by every property owner. (Id. at 146–147.)

Chief Justice Warren wrote the Court's opinion. Although the motion also raised equal protection, the decision resolves the case on due process without a separate equal protection holding. Its final instruction is reversal and remand, not an order fixing damages, forgiving taxes, or immediately awarding property to Covey. (Id. at 142, 147.)

Justice Frankfurter supplied a separate statement at pages 147–148. The report does not label it a concurrence or dissent or state a numerical division. He agreed that reversal followed if the New York courts had treated the case as the Court understood them to have done, but doubted whether the state procedure had been sufficiently clarified. The remittitur's qualification that the constitutional issue had been decided “on this record” might concern the timeliness of this particular motion, while another statutory action remained available. Due process could permit reasonable procedural deadlines. On return, the state court could clarify the reach of the decision and the availability of other remedies. This procedural reservation should not be converted into an unqualified endorsement of every part of Warren's analysis.
