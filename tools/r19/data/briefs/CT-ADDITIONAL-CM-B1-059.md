# American Bronze Corporation v. Streamway Products
8 Ohio App. 3d 223, 456 N.E.2d 1295 (Dec. 16, 1982) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural scope

The Ohio Court of Appeals reversed and remanded the judgment against Streamway on its counterclaim. American Bronze had accepted three orders for castings and then unequivocally refused to perform them after receiving adequate assurances. That refusal was anticipatory repudiation, not a permissible unilateral rescission within a commercially reasonable time. Pryatel C.J. wrote the sole opinion, with Parrino and Markus JJ. concurring. The appeal concerned only the counterclaim; the separate stipulated judgment for American’s unpaid account was outside that limited disposition. (224, 226, 228–231.)

## Transactions and trial findings

American and Streamway had dealt for more than twenty years. Streamway regularly telephoned an order, followed it with a written purchase order, and American began production. Three accepted orders became the subject of this appeal. The November 21, 1979 order included castings of which American completed one type but failed to deliver 1,000 units of another. The December 27 order called for 13,000 castings; American delivered 5,087, leaving 7,913 outstanding. American accepted the January 3, 1980 order but delivered none of its three types. A separate January 14 order was returned and rejected January 18, and was not one of these three accepted contracts. (224, 227 & n.4.)

On January 17, American’s vice-president, Gerald Goldstein, told Streamway’s plant manager and president that American would accept no new orders but would produce the existing open orders. American objected that Streamway had ordered from other manufacturers goods American could have supplied. The castings involved in the existing contracts, however, were unique and custom made by American, with no duplicate tooling elsewhere. American requested that Streamway bring its account current, pay future invoices within ten days, agree on scrap prices, and hold American harmless for failure to produce. Streamway paid more than $66,000 on January 18, bringing the account current, and agreed to the ten-day future payment term. (224–225 & n.2, 230–231.)

On January 24, Goldstein announced that American would produce none of the outstanding castings and had melted down castings already produced for those orders. Only after that refusal did Streamway remove its active tooling. Streamway obtained substitute castings from Wolverine Brass Works. The trial court found $16,301.30 in additional cover prices, $2,374.11 in conversion costs, $2,970 in retooling, and $3,500 in miscellaneous costs, totaling $25,145.41. These were findings made after a bench trial, not merely damages allegations. (224–225, 228 & n.6.)

The trial court nevertheless concluded that American had unilaterally rescinded its acceptances within commercially reasonable periods and therefore had not breached. It expressly found the assurances adequate but thought American could still rescind. It entered judgment for American on the counterclaim and also entered a separate $9,489.64 stipulated account judgment, with eight-percent interest from December 3, 1981. Streamway appealed the counterclaim judgment through three assignments of error. Although the findings were journalized after transmission of the appellate record, the appellate court accepted them under Cobb; the appeal period ran from the judgment’s journalization rather than the later filing of findings. (225–226 & n.3.)

## Formation and the statute of frauds

Ohio’s sales provisions allowed a contract to arise through any conduct sufficient to show agreement, despite uncertainty about the exact moment of formation or some open terms. An offer ordinarily invited acceptance by a reasonable medium, and an order for prompt shipment could invite a promise to ship or shipment. Here, the established ordering procedure and American’s accepted orders established contracts. For two orders, production had already begun, providing additional conduct manifesting agreement. The trial findings themselves established acceptance of all three. This was a conclusion grounded in the parties’ course of conduct and actual acceptance, rather than a general rule that unanswered purchase orders bind every recipient. (226–228.)

The court rejected Streamway’s different argument that the merchant-confirmation provision itself proved formation. R.C. 1302.04(B) concerns enforceability under the statute of frauds: satisfying its requirements removes that defense, but does not relieve the proponent of proving that the preceding oral agreement existed. American’s acceptance supplied the formation evidence here. Footnote 5 reproduced the writing requirement and statutory exceptions; the court did not separately hold that each exception applied to these transactions. (227–228 & n.5.)

## Repudiation, rescission, and termination

Anticipatory repudiation required a definite and unequivocal refusal of future performance that substantially impaired the contract’s value. American’s January 24 announcement and melting of the castings met that test. Streamway had to find another producer at substantial additional cost. The seller’s repudiation breached the accepted contracts; it did not rescind them by mutual consent. The trial court could not convert a unilateral disavowal into consensual rescission merely by calling the elapsed time commercially reasonable. (228–229 & n.7.)

The court also rejected the characterization of this conduct as termination requiring reasonable notice. Termination under the sales statute meant ending a contract pursuant to a legal or agreed power otherwise than for breach. The provision governing successive performances of indefinite duration did not apply to these contracts for production of definite quantities. Ending the continuing relationship for future orders therefore did not discharge the already accepted orders. Streamway’s subsequent removal of tooling was a response to the seller’s repudiation, permitting buyer cancellation and resort to remedies, rather than a prior cancellation excusing American. (229 & n.8.)

The court discussed cover under R.C. 1302.85 and 1302.86: a buyer may reasonably purchase substitutes in good faith and without unreasonable delay, recovering the difference from the contract price together with applicable incidental and consequential damages, less expenses saved. Streamway had taken that course. Failure to cover would not itself bar other remedies. This discussion supported liability and the buyer’s remedial entitlement, but the appellate court did not itself render a $25,145.41 money judgment. (229–230 & nn.9–10, 231.)

## Adequate assurances and result

Reasonable insecurity permitted a request for adequate assurance; between merchants, commercial standards and good faith governed the grounds and adequacy. The reasonableness of American’s request was not contested in this appeal. The issue was the assurances given. Both the trial court and appellate court regarded bringing the account current and agreeing to pay future invoices within ten days as adequate assurances. Once American received them, it remained bound to perform; the assurance doctrine gave it no license to breach anyway. The court did not decide a disputed question about whether an oral request alone universally satisfies the statute’s written-demand language. (230–231 & nn.11–12.)

All three assignments of error were sustained. The findings supported accepted contracts, adequate assurances, repudiation, and the buyer’s remedies, rather than the trial court’s conclusion of no breach. The judgment was reversed and remanded on the counterclaim alone. (231.)
