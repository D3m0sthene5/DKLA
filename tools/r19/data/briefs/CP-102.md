# Cine Forty-Second Street Theatre Corp. v. Allied Artists Pictures Corp.
602 F.2d 1062 (2d Cir. 1979) | Civil Procedure | Gross negligence and discovery sanctions

## An antitrust claim stalled by discovery failures

Cine operated a Times Square movie theater beginning in July 1974. It alleged that neighboring exhibitors first tried to prevent its opening through city agency processes and then conspired with film distributors to deny it first-run, quality films. Its August 1975 antitrust action sought $3 million in treble damages and injunctive relief. The appellate court did not decide whether that conspiracy existed. Its concern was nearly four years of discovery failures that prevented litigation of the claim. 602 F.2d at 1064, 1068.

Eleven defendants served consolidated interrogatories in November 1975. Cine obtained an agreement to defer damages discovery while it retained an expert to examine competing theaters' box-office receipts. Its responses to the remaining questions nevertheless arrived four months after the agreed deadline and were seriously deficient. They used ambiguous cross-references, and drawings offered in response to specific questions about theater design failed to supply the requested dimensions. Supplemental responses remained inadequate. Cine characterized the requests as harassment, but had not sought an order striking or protecting it from them. Id. at 1064-1065.

Magistrate Gershon repeatedly ordered compliance. She assessed $500 in costs in October 1977 and warned that further failures would lead to dismissal. Cine still had not retained the damages expert. Even an order requiring a plan for answering produced no result. Two subsequent sets of damages answers were late or deficient: they omitted periods for which injury was claimed and failed to explain how a major portion of damages was calculated. On September 7, 1978, the magistrate orally ordered the deficiencies cured on pain of dismissal. Cine neither complied with, objected to, nor appealed that order. Id.

At the October 19 hearing, Cine's attorney asserted that an earlier understanding with defense counsel allowed a deposition of Cine's principal officer, Clark, to replace the disputed answers. There was no written or corroborating evidence of that understanding; in any event, the later order compelling answers superseded it. Finding willful noncompliance, the magistrate recommended precluding proof of damages. That would effectively eliminate **the damages claim**, while leaving the request for an injunction standing. It was not a recommendation to dismiss every aspect of the action. Id. at 1065.

## The district judge's legal doubt and interlocutory appeal

Judge Goettel thought drastic sanctions were warranted, but would not definitively find willfulness. Counsel's conduct was either willful or a complete dereliction of professional responsibility amounting to gross negligence. Because the magistrate's direction was oral, counsel might have misunderstood it without consciously deciding to disobey. The district judge read *Flaks v. Koegel* as withholding authority to impose such a severe sanction absent willfulness. He therefore declined the magistrate's recommendation and assessed $1,000 in costs instead. Defendants estimated their actual costs of compelling discovery at at least $50,000; that estimate in footnote 6 was not itself a costs award. Id. at 1065 & n.6.

The judge certified his own ruling for interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b), recognizing possible misunderstanding of circuit law. A panel accepted the appeal. On reargument below, Cine's eventual answers led the district court to adhere to its decision. The certified issue was whether fully avoidable noncompliance caused by gross professional negligence could support evidence preclusion tantamount to dismissal of a claim. Id. at 1064-1066.

## Fault extends beyond intentional disobedience

Chief Judge Kaufman explained that Rule 37 offered sanctions ranging from reimbursement of expenses through issue or evidence preclusion to dismissal and default. They serve three related purposes: preventing a party from benefiting from noncompliance, securing compliance in the case at hand, and deterring comparable misconduct in other litigation. Severe sanctions cannot be imposed as mere penalties without fault, but their permissible purpose includes general deterrence. Id. at 1066.

The decisive distinction came from *Société Internationale*: inability to comply without willfulness, bad faith, **or fault** does not justify dismissal. A party prevented from producing material by circumstances genuinely beyond its control cannot sensibly be induced to comply through a sanction. Punishing nonculpable inability would also offend due process. Cine's problem, however, was not inability. Its counsel was capable of responding but repeatedly failed to discharge professional duties. Id. at 1066-1067.

The court gave “fault” meaning independent of willfulness and bad faith. If it required deliberate disobedience, it would add nothing to those terms. At a minimum it encompassed the gross negligence found here. Authorities cautioning against dismissal for nonculpable failures or ordinary negligence did not decide this case. *Affanato*, by contrast, supported a severe sanction after a series of failures amounting to near-total professional dereliction without an express finding of willfulness. The 1970 Rule 37 amendment, discussed in footnote 8, also made willfulness relevant to selecting sanctions rather than an indispensable condition for every sanction. Id. at 1066-1067 & n.8.

The court did not hold that any careless answer warrants dismissal. Fairness may counsel against the harshest measures for an isolated oversight constituting simple negligence. Here counsel clearly should have understood the duty, received repeated opportunities and warnings, and allowed the litigation to remain frozen in discovery. Gross incompetence can produce the same delays and costs as intentional obstruction, and deterrence can address both. Id. at 1067-1068.

## The client's responsibility and belated answers

Under *Link v. Wabash*, counsel's acts and omissions ordinarily bind the client. The consequence can include loss of a claim. Footnote 10 distinguishes extraordinary circumstances such as counsel's disappearance or mental illness, which were not present. It also records that Clark knew about and was closely involved in discovery. The case therefore should not be described as a finding that an entirely unaware client could never obtain relief from a lawyer's extraordinary abandonment. Id. at 1068 & n.10.

Cine argued that its eventual damages responses should allow it to prove losses at trial. The court evaluated those responses against the whole record. Securing eventual compliance was only one purpose of sanctions. Giving decisive weight to answers supplied after repeated defaults would encourage parties to wait until a court had exhausted its patience and require constant judicial supervision. The late responses did not erase the fault or eliminate the reason for deterrence. Id. at 1068.

## Judgment and Judge Oakes's qualification

The court **reversed the district judge's refusal to adopt damages preclusion**. It found no need for extensive further proceedings: the district judge had made clear that he would have adopted the magistrate's recommendation but for his legal error. This is an appellate ruling allowing and directing the recommended severe discovery consequence, rather than an antitrust merits determination or dismissal of the remaining injunction claim. Id.

Judge Oakes concurred in the result. He agreed that if the interrogatories' complexity caused the difficulty, the remedy was a protective-order application under Rule 26(c); if the client caused it, preclusion reached the responsible party. He nevertheless emphasized great reluctance to impose counsel's wrongdoing on a client absent the client's knowledge, condonation, compliance, or causation. His qualification concerns attribution and fairness, not disagreement with the judgment. The three-judge panel unanimously agreed on the result, with this separate concurrence. Id. at 1068-1069.
