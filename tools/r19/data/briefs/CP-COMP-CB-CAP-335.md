# Trbovich v. United Mine Workers of America

**404 U.S. 528 (1972). Decided January 17, 1972. No. 71-119.** Justice Marshall wrote for Chief Justice Burger and Justices Brennan, Stewart, White, and Blackmun. Justice Douglas dissented in part; Justices Powell and Rehnquist did not participate. The disagreement was over the permitted breadth of intervention, not whether intervention was available at all. Assigned account: Civil Procedure casebook, physical PDF page 412, printed pages 792–793, Note 2 on inadequate representation.

## Election challenge and procedural history

The United Mine Workers held an officers’ election on December 9, 1969. A union member’s complaint to the Secretary of Labor initiated the enforcement process that eventually produced this suit. The Secretary sued under § 402(b) of the Labor-Management Reporting and Disclosure Act of 1959 (LMRDA), seeking to set aside the election and obtain a new election under his supervision. His complaint alleged numerous statutory violations, including lack of secret ballots, campaigning at the polls, denial of candidate observers, election-related reprisals, failure to hold elections in some locals, and use of union assets to favor incumbents. Those were allegations, not violations adjudicated in this intervention appeal. **404 U.S. at 529 & n.1.**

The complaining member, Trbovich, sought intervention under Rule 24(a). He wanted to support the Secretary with evidence and argument, add two grounds attacking the election, and propose safeguards for a replacement election. His additional grounds concerned pensioner locals allegedly improperly constituted under the union constitution and a pension increase allegedly arranged by the incumbent president to influence pensioners’ votes. His proposed safeguards included disbanding those locals, publishing a fiduciary-duty ruling against the president, and comprehensive rules for future elections. **Id. at 529–530 & nn.2–3.**

The district court denied intervention because the statute made the Secretary’s suit the exclusive post-election remedy. The D.C. Circuit affirmed on that reasoning. The Supreme Court expedited review because the underlying litigation remained pending and had not been stayed. **Id. at 530 & n.4.**

## Statutory issue and the Secretary’s position

Title IV established election rules and an enforcement sequence: the member first exhausts available internal remedies, complains to the Secretary, and obtains an investigation. If the Secretary finds probable cause to believe a violation occurred, he must bring the authorized civil action. Section 403 made this statutory remedy exclusive for an election already conducted, while preserving existing rights and remedies concerning elections not yet conducted. **Id. at 530–531.**

The statute prohibited a member from initiating a private suit to set aside a completed election. The question here was different: whether a member could join the Secretary’s already authorized suit. The Secretary argued that Congress’s rejection of proposals allowing member suits demonstrated an intention to exclude members from direct judicial participation altogether. The lower court had treated intervention as an impermissible route around exclusive public enforcement. **Id. at 531–532.**

## Holding on statutory exclusivity

The Court rejected a categorical prohibition. The statutory language barred a competing private action, but it did not by itself prohibit participation in an existing public enforcement suit. Legislative history showed two central objectives: protecting unions against frivolous litigation and unnecessary judicial intrusion, and concentrating warranted challenges to one election in one proceeding. The Secretary screened complaints and brought those considered meritorious in the chosen federal forum. **Id. at 531–533.**

The Court examined the Kennedy proposals, the competing Goldwater-Administration proposal, the Senate testimony, and the House alternative favoring private enforcement. Objections to private suits emphasized repeated defenses in multiple forums and the absence of screening. The House’s eventual adoption of public enforcement confirmed a need for the Secretary’s role; it did not show rejection of private assistance to that role. Footnote 6 distinguished the related debate over preexisting state remedies, including conflicts between state-defined and new federal rights, from the narrower question presented here. **Id. at 533–536 & n.6.**

Participation supporting the Secretary’s complaint did not defeat either statutory purpose. The union already had been summoned to defend the election in this one proceeding. Evidence and argument from the member did not generate a second forum or an independent, unscreened lawsuit. Title IV therefore did not bar that participation. **Id. at 536–537.**

## Why intervention was limited

The Court nevertheless prohibited Trbovich from adding new grounds to invalidate the old election. It described those grounds as ones the Secretary presumably had determined lacked merit; it did not itself adjudicate their truth. Requiring the union to defend them would bypass Congress’s screening choice. Although adding them to an existing case would be less burdensome than a new lawsuit, that practical difference did not eliminate the statutory screening function. **Id. at 537.**

This restriction applied to grounds for setting aside the completed election, **not** to proposed terms for a replacement election. If the court sustained the Secretary’s complaint, it would have to direct a new election complying with the statute and the union’s constitution and bylaws. The court was not restricted to remedies proposed by the Secretary, and an intervenor could help fashion an appropriate remedial order. This qualification in footnote 8 prevents the limited-intervention holding from being mistaken for a blanket exclusion of the member’s remedial suggestions. **Id. at 537 n.8.**

## Rule 24(a)(2) and inadequate representation

The Secretary separately argued that he adequately represented any legally cognizable interest Trbovich possessed. He did not contest the sufficiency of the member’s interest. The lower courts had not reached adequacy because they believed the statute categorically barred intervention. The Supreme Court found sufficient doubt to warrant intervention here. Footnote 9 reproduced the rule’s timely-application, interest, practical-impairment, and adequate-representation language; footnote 10 explained that showing representation may be inadequate requires only a minimal burden. **Id. at 538 & nn.9–10.**

The reason was the Secretary’s dual responsibility. He acted for the individual member’s statutory rights and also for the broader public interest in democratic union elections. Those related responsibilities could support different litigation choices. Consequently, the member did not have to prove that the Secretary had failed his statutory duty. Even competent performance of the public office could leave a legitimate concern about the representation of the member’s own interest. Here the complaint came from the member who had initiated the enforcement process. **Id. at 538–539.**

## Douglas’s partial dissent and judgment

Douglas agreed that Title IV allowed Rule 24 intervention, but he would also allow the additional grounds against the election. In his view the Secretary already had screened the overall election challenge as meritorious, the new allegations concerned that same election, and hearing them in the existing suit respected centralized litigation without imposing the burden of an independent action. He considered the majority’s restriction inconsistent with both Title IV’s purposes and Rule 24’s liberalizing 1966 amendments. His starred note described the alleged manipulation of pensioners underlying the two additional claims. **Id. at 539–540 & n.*.**

The Court **reversed and remanded to the district court with directions to allow limited intervention in accordance with its opinion**. Six Justices adopted the majority’s limit; Douglas sought broader intervention; two did not participate. The judgment did not itself invalidate the election, order the proposed safeguards, or find that the Secretary had acted improperly. **Id. at 539.**

## Assigned significance and source distinction

The assigned account highlights the minimal adequacy burden and the possibility that public and individual representation diverge. The complete opinion adds the statutory screening rationale, the prohibited new grounds, and the permitted participation in remedies. Its lesson depends on keeping those distinctions together rather than treating minimal inadequacy as permission to add any desired claim to a government suit.
