# Brower v. Gateway 2000, Inc.
246 A.D.2d 246, 676 N.Y.S.2d 569 (1st Dep’t 1998) | Contracts

## Court, claims, and posture
The New York Appellate Division, First Department, decided the appeal on August 13, 1998. Justice Presiding Milonas wrote; Justices Nardelli, Mazzarelli, and Saxe concurred. No dissent or separate concurrence appears in the complete report. Computer purchasers appealed the October 21, 1997 order of Supreme Court, New York County, Justice Beatrice Shainswit, dismissing their complaint based on an agreement to arbitrate. The appellate decision concerned formation, unconscionability, and scope of arbitration, not the truth of the purchasers’ advertised-service claims or entitlement to damages. (246 A.D.2d at 246, 248, 256.)

## Transaction and allegations
Gateway sold computers and software directly by telephone or mail. Beginning July 3, 1995, its shipments included a Standard Terms and Conditions Agreement and relevant warranties. A boxed customer notice, in slightly larger type, said that keeping the system beyond thirty days after delivery accepted the enclosed terms. The four-page agreement contained sixteen paragraphs. Paragraph ten required exclusive, final arbitration under International Chamber of Commerce rules before a sole arbitrator in Chicago, with a binding award enforceable in court. (Id. at 248, 253.)

The purchasers sought compensatory and punitive damages for themselves and similarly situated consumers, asserting seven causes of action including warranty, contract, fraud, and unfair-trade-practice claims. They alleged that Gateway promised free around-the-clock technical assistance, software support, and certain on-site services, while reaching a technician was virtually impossible and Gateway continued the advertising despite complaints. The appellants relevant to this decision bought after July 3, 1995. Gateway invoked the enclosed arbitration agreement. (Id. at 248–249.)

Purchasers objected that the clause was not part of their original bargain, was obscure and adhesive, and imposed an inaccessible, costly process. They pointed to ICC headquarters in France, difficulty locating the organization and rules, and correspondence routed to France even though hearings would occur in Chicago. For claims under $50,000, ICC advance fees were $4,000, including a $2,000 registration fee not refunded even to a successful claimant. Counsel estimated individual damages no greater than $1,000. Travel added expense, and ICC rules exposed an unsuccessful consumer to Gateway’s legal fees under a loser-pays approach. The court later accepted the prohibitive-cost objection, rather than treating every asserted inconvenience as dispositive. (Id. at 249, 254.)

## Formation holding
The court held that retaining the product beyond the stated thirty-day period manifested assent to the enclosed contract. On that analysis, neither the initial order nor receipt of the goods completed the contract. The arbitration clause was therefore a provision of the single agreement eventually accepted, rather than a material alteration of an already existing oral agreement under UCC § 2-207. (Id. at 250–251.)

The court adopted the reasoning of Hill and ProCD while acknowledging those Seventh Circuit decisions were not controlling New York authority. They explained the practical possibility of paying before receiving complete terms and distinguished a single enclosed form from a battle involving exchanged forms. Decisions concerning arbitration language added to an existing oral contract did not govern because Brower’s formation premise was different: the proposed agreement remained open for consumer rejection by return until the retention period ended. This is the historical court’s construction of this transaction; it should not be generalized into an assertion that any late-delivered term binds any buyer. (Id. at 250–251.)

## Adhesion and procedural unconscionability
Unequal bargaining power alone did not invalidate this agreement as adhesive. The court emphasized the option to buy a competitor’s product and the unqualified opportunity to return Gateway’s merchandise during thirty days, whether dissatisfied with the goods, the terms, or for no stated reason. Purchasers could inspect, obtain advice, and seek clarification. The court acknowledged that returning a shipment required affirmative action and might cost money, but viewed that burden as part of the trade-off for direct-order convenience and savings. Failing to read or understand the agreement did not alone undo assent. (Id. at 252.)

New York ordinarily required both procedural and substantive unconscionability. Procedural inquiry considered the setting, education and experience, fine print, high-pressure tactics, and bargaining inequality. The court found no supporting procedural defect here: the heading was conspicuous, the agreement was short, and its paragraphs used the same type size. Arbitration was not concealed in an inordinately complex document. Calling Gateway’s own conditions standard did not misrepresent them as industry-wide standards. (Id. at 253–254.)

## Substantive cost barrier and substitution
Chicago’s inconvenience by itself was insufficient. ICC costs were different: excluding consumers from court while demanding fees out of proportion to their claims effectively left them without an accessible forum. The court held that sufficiently severe substantive unconscionability alone could make this portion unenforceable despite the absence of a procedural defect. Hill had not addressed this particular cost objection. The decision invalidated the ICC designation, rather than the entire arbitration obligation. (Id. at 254–255.)

Gateway argued that replacement terms mooted the problem. In separate Filias litigation, a federal court had appointed the AAA after Gateway accepted substitution, although reconsideration remained pending. Gateway claimed a new agreement, circulated through a customer magazine, extended to all customers a choice between AAA and ICC and allowed a location chosen by mutual agreement not unreasonably withheld. These were Gateway’s claims about an offered amendment, not findings that the Brower purchasers had accepted it. They continued to object to AAA costs, and the court invoked UCC § 2-209 in rejecting any supposed acceptance. The parties agreed that AAA required a nonrefundable filing fee of at least $500; consumers claimed arbitration could exceed $1,000. The record lacked AAA rules and cost information sufficient to determine unconscionability. Consequently, the court neither approved AAA costs nor treated substitution as already agreed. (Id. at 254–255.)

The purchasers also argued, for the first time on appeal, that false advertising about technical support was outside arbitration because the separate service document lacked arbitration language. The court rejected that argument. Support was related to the computer agreement and was not a separate product they could keep while returning the computer and its accompanying terms. (Id. at 255–256.)

## Exact disposition and assigned scope
The court modified the order on the law and facts by vacating the requirement to arbitrate before the ICC, granted leave to seek appointment of an arbitrator under 9 U.S.C. § 5, and remanded for that purpose. It otherwise affirmed, without costs. It did not award consumer damages, select the AAA itself, invalidate arbitration generally, or adjudicate whether the proposed substitute’s costs were acceptable. (Id. at 256.)
