Watkins & Son v. Carrig
Citation
Robert G. Watkins & Son, Inc. v. James A. Carrig, 91 N.H. 459, 21 A.2d 591 (1941), decided July 15, 1941.
Supreme Court of New Hampshire; Chief Justice Allen wrote the opinion. Assigned excerpt: C&M pp.
477–480. This brief uses that excerpt and the full judicial opinion. The case enforces a voluntary adjustment
of an existing excavation contract even though the contractor was already obligated to remove the rock.
Parties
Watkins & Son was the excavation contractor and plaintiff seeking payment for work performed under the
increased rock-removal rate. Carrig was the owner and defendant who had promised that rate but contended
that the promise lacked consideration. His exceptions brought the legal challenge to the New Hampshire
Supreme Court. C&M p. 477.
Material Facts
The contractor agreed in writing to excavate a cellar for a stated total price. Its promise to excavate was
unqualified, and the agreement required removal of all material from the site. There was no contractual
reservation for unexpected subsurface conditions. The contractor’s manager did not investigate or inquire
into the character of the ground, the owner did not mislead him, and the parties did not share an
understanding that rock would be absent. C&M pp. 477–478; 91 N.H. at 460.
Soon after work began, solid rock was encountered. The manager notified Carrig, and the parties met. They
orally agreed on a separate unit price for rock removal approximately nine times the unit price used to
calculate the original gross excavation price. Rock ultimately occupied about two-thirds of the excavation.
The court described the special rock-removal price as fair and the value Carrig received as reasonable. A
large multiple of the ordinary excavation rate therefore was not, by itself, evidence that the renegotiated rate
was excessive for the different physical work involved. C&M pp. 477–478.
Prompt continuation mattered to Carrig. He either believed that rock removal was not covered by the original
rate or was willing to relinquish his right to that rate. The court did not determine which explanation
motivated him because either way he intentionally agreed that the original price would not control removal of
the rock. The contractor proceeded on the strength of that agreement. The opinion was willing to assume that
the contractor threatened to break the original contract, but characterized Carrig’s response as voluntary
relinquishment without protest, rather than a concession judicially found to have been coerced. C&M pp.
478–480; 91 N.H. at 461–465.
Procedural History
The contractor brought assumpsit, an action to recover on a contractual undertaking, for work done. A referee
found that the oral agreement superseded the written contract. The trial court accepted the report and ordered
judgment for the contractor. Carrig excepted to that action, and the legal questions were transferred to the
New Hampshire Supreme Court. The reviewing court overruled his exceptions, leaving the contractor’s
recovery intact. C&M p. 477.

Issue
May an owner’s voluntary promise to pay a fair additional price for rock excavation be enforced even though
the original contract already required the contractor to remove the rock? Must the parties carry out a
separately timed rescission before making a replacement agreement, or may a single transaction validly
relinquish the original right and establish the adjusted duty? C&M pp. 478–480.
Rule and Holding
The contractor was not relieved of its original obligation by mutual mistake: the unqualified excavation
contract allocated the relevant risk to it. Nevertheless, the owner’s intentional and voluntary surrender of his
right to excavation at the original rate was effective. The parties could rescind the old arrangement, in whole
or in relevant part, and establish the new one within a single transaction. The court enforced the fair new rate
without requiring a ceremonial interval between rescission and replacement. C&M pp. 477–480; 91 N.H. at
460–465.
The holding is not that encountering rock automatically creates consideration or excuses performance. Nor
did the court apply Restatement § 89, which was formulated later. Its explanation centers on voluntary
release of an existing contract right and recognition of the parties’ actual adjustment. The fairness of the price
and the absence of a finding of coercion are important limits on using the case to defend other modifications.
Judgment
The defendant’s exceptions were overruled. The judgment based on the referee’s finding of a superseding
agreement stood, and the contractor was entitled to the agreed special compensation for removing the rock.
C&M p. 480; 91 N.H. at 465.
Reasoning
The court first rejected the contractor’s possible excuse from the original duty. There had been no shared
assumption excluding rock, no misleading representation, and no contractual exception for it. The contractor
had undertaken to excavate a particular space regardless of the material encountered. Failing to investigate
did not convert an unfavorable risk into mutual mistake. That opening is essential: the court enforced the
modification while accepting that the contractor originally owed the rock excavation at the contract price.
C&M pp. 477–478; 91 N.H. at 460.
The referee’s finding of supersession established that the parties meant the new agreement to govern. The
owner’s precise reason for conceding the rate was secondary to the concession itself. Whether he doubted the
original duty or simply chose not to enforce it, he agreed to release the contractor from the lower-price
obligation with respect to the rock. The contractor then acted on the adjusted arrangement. C&M p. 478; 91
N.H. at 461.
Carrig argued that the transaction was merely a modification, not two separate agreements consisting of
rescission followed by replacement. On his view, the contractor’s same performance remained the only
proposed exchange, so the increased price lacked consideration. The court answered that a modification
incorporates a partial rescission. If the law recognizes the parties’ capacity voluntarily to discharge an
obligation, combining that discharge with the new promise should not make the discharge disappear. Legal
distinctness did not require temporal separation. C&M pp. 478–479; 91 N.H. at 461–463.
Williston’s contrary analysis treated the net effect as decisive: one party does what it already promised, while
the other pays more. The court recognized the force of that objection but rejected the proposition that

consideration must control the transaction at such a high level of abstraction. An adjustment of an existing
commercial relationship was different from a bare promise of a gift. Businesses reasonably expected to adapt
contracts by mutual action when performance became unexpectedly burdensome. The court preferred a
doctrine recognizing that practice where the adjustment was voluntary and reasonable. C&M p. 479.
Fairness did concrete work in the opinion. The court did not say that Carrig merely assumed the contractor’s
entire loss for nothing. It found a special price that reasonably compensated the materially more difficult
work. Although the original obligation included that work, its newly appreciated burden furnished a
commercially intelligible reason for the owner to concede a different rate. That supported the characterization
of a genuine adjustment rather than a demand exploiting an artificially created emergency. C&M p. 478.
The full opinion develops an additional release-and-gift analysis more extensively than the casebook excerpt.
The completed gift was not the owner’s executory promise to pay extra money. It was the release of the
contractor’s existing duty to work for less. The court distinguished relinquishing a claim against one’s debtor
from assigning an intangible claim to a third person, and regarded the former as capable of completion by a
present voluntary release. This discussion is part of the New Hampshire court’s explanation; it is not a
general statement that every jurisdiction treats oral releases or part-payment of debts the same way. 91 N.H.
at 463–464.
The court then returned to what Carrig had actually done. By agreeing that rock excavation would be paid at
the special rate, he intentionally surrendered the contrary right. Within the single transaction, the discharge
preceded the new duty in legal analysis even though there was no interval between the acts. The court
regarded it as unreasonable to permit the owner to accept the contractor’s performance on the new basis and
then revive a right he had voluntarily relinquished. C&M pp. 479–480; 91 N.H. at 464–465.
The opinion’s assumption that the contractor threatened breach does not mean threats were irrelevant. The
court characterized this owner as freely yielding, excusing the contractor, and making a reasonable new
arrangement. It did not find the absence of realistic alternatives that became central in Austin. The casebook
accordingly asks why the contractor’s insistence should count as less coercive than the fishermen’s demand.
That is a genuine question about the courts’ characterizations, not a distinction supplied by saying that one
party threatened and the other did not. C&M p. 480.
Separate Opinions
The full opinion states that Justice Burque did not sit and that the other justices concurred. There is no
reported separate concurrence or dissent. 91 N.H. at 465.
Notes
Relationship to Schwartzreich. In the casebook’s earlier discussion, Schwartzreich v. Bauman-Basch
enforced a higher salary after the parties signed a replacement agreement and tore their signatures from the
first. Watkins does not make enforceability depend on such a ceremony. It recognizes relinquishment and
replacement within one adjustment. Restatement § 89 comment b later expressly rejects simultaneous
rescission as a fictitious explanation and instead tests the fairness and justification of the modification
directly. C&M pp. 475–480; Restatement § 89 cmt. b.
Relationship to Restatement § 89(a). Illustration 1 uses the cellar, rock, and nine-times-unit-price facts and
treats the increased price as binding when the rock is unexpectedly encountered and the new rate is
reasonable. That later illustration supplies a direct modern doctrinal route to the result; it does not erase the

The risk-allocation tension. Carrig’s strongest argument is that the original fixed-price promise already
covered rock, so the contractor was trying to shift a risk it had assumed. The contractor’s stronger response is
that the law need not forbid an owner voluntarily to relieve an unforeseen burden at a fair rate merely because
the owner could have demanded strict performance. Under § 89, the inquiry would concern what the parties
anticipated and covered, and whether the adjustment was fair; the name of New Hampshire as the Granite
State is not itself dispositive evidence of their allocation. C&M pp. 477–480.
Do not turn lack of protest into a universal rule. The court relied on voluntary yielding without protest as
evidence here. Austin illustrates why even a deliberate commercial decision can be induced by duress. A
signature, willingness to keep the project moving, or failure to use a particular protest formula is not by itself
a complete answer to an improper threat and lack of a reasonable alternative. Restatement § 175; Austin, 29
N.Y.2d at 130–134.
