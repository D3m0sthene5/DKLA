# Howard v. Babcock
6 Cal. 4th 409, 863 P.2d 150 (1993) | Contracts

## Court, question, and result

On December 6, 1993, the California Supreme Court held that a reasonable financial consequence for a departing law partner's competition was not automatically void under the then-applicable professional-conduct rule. Justice Mosk wrote for six justices; Chief Justice Lucas and Justices Panelli, Arabian, Baxter, and George concurred. Justice Kennard dissented. The court did not conclusively approve this agreement's amount or restore every aspect of the trial judgment. It reversed the Court of Appeal only insofar as that court treated article X as unenforceable and ordered an accounting on that basis, otherwise affirmed, and required a remand to determine reasonableness and any resulting further accounting award. (6 Cal. 4th at 412, 425–426 & n. 9.)

## Agreement and departing lawyers

In 1982, partners of Parker, Stanbury, McGee, Babcock & Combs signed an agreement governing withdrawal benefits. Article V ordinarily gave a departing general partner the partner's capital interest plus the net-profit share the partner would have received during the ensuing twelve months. Article X applied when more than one partner, associate, or other individual withdrew before age 65 and, within a year, practiced together or in combination with others in liability-insurance defense work within the Los Angeles or Orange County court systems. At the remaining partners' discretion, that competition could forfeit withdrawal benefits other than capital. A separate provision for one departing partner specified 75-percent forfeiture for competition in those two counties and 25 percent in certain other counties. (Id. at 412–413 & n. 1.)

New partners joined in 1984–1986 without signing the existing agreement, and some participating partners were elevated to general partners. In December 1986, Howard, Moss, Loveder, and Strickroth announced that they would leave and begin competing in January. They maintained that article X was unenforceable and later asserted that the partnership agreement had ceased to govern; the remaining partners said they would withhold withdrawal benefits. The departing lawyers published a dissolution notice effective December 31 and opened their new firm on January 2, 1987. Clients substituted the new firm in approximately 200 cases. (Id. at 412–413.)

The dispute involved capital, accounts receivable for work already billed but unpaid, and unfinished cases requiring future work. The remaining partners tendered capital but disputed the departing lawyers' entitlement to receivables and unfinished-business proceeds. The departing lawyers sought an accounting, declaratory relief, and relief for alleged fiduciary breaches, including refusal to release lease guaranties. The remaining lawyers denied the asserted dissolution consequences and cross-claimed under numerous contract, fiduciary, conversion, fraud, and accounting theories. Those pleadings describe the broader litigation; the Supreme Court's review concerned whether article X was void. (Id. at 413–414, 426 n. 9.)

## Trial and intermediate appeal

The parties stipulated to try the agreement's validity and enforceability first, waiving a jury only for that issue. The trial court held article X valid. It also held that adding partners had dissolved the original partnership by operation of law in 1984, but that the signatories continued a partnership governed by the same agreement. It found that Strickroth was neither bound by nor benefited from that agreement. The interlocutory judgment required the departing partners to account for profits from former-firm matters and required the remaining partners to account for the departing partners' unpaid 1986 profit shares. (Id. at 414–415 & n. 2.)

An unsuccessful writ proceeding challenged the scope of that accounting ruling. The eventual final judgment required plaintiffs to pay defendants $382,686 and found that defendants owed plaintiffs nothing. Plaintiffs appealed; defendants conditionally cross-appealed as to other causes of action. The Court of Appeal declared article X void under professional-conduct rule 1-500, affirmed the award to defendants, but remanded for an accounting of amounts due plaintiffs under the partnership agreement and partnership statute and for trial of previously unaddressed claims. The Supreme Court's partial reversal addressed the premise of facial invalidity, leaving the rest of that appellate judgment in place. (Id. at 415–416, 426.)

## Statute and professional-conduct rule

The majority began with California's policy favoring competition, but emphasized the statutory exception permitting geographically limited noncompetition agreements among partners upon or in anticipation of dissolution. Business and Professions Code section 16602 referred to any partner, with no textual or demonstrated historical exception for lawyers. Earlier applications to physicians and accountants illustrated that compensation for competitive loss need not amount to withholding the privilege to practice. The court concluded that the statute included law partners; it expressly reserved agreements affecting employees rather than partners. (Id. at 416–418 & n. 4.)

That statutory conclusion did not eliminate the court's independent authority to impose higher ethical duties on lawyers. The question was whether rule 1-500 already barred this financial arrangement. That rule prohibited agreements restricting a lawyer's right to practice, subject to specified exceptions, including restrictions ending with the professional relationship and certain retirement arrangements. The majority distinguished an absolute restriction on practice from a reasonable economic consequence of a partner's choice to compete. It adopted Haight's view that such an arrangement could reconcile departure and client choice with the remaining firm's financial stability. The holding was an interpretation of California's then-existing rule, despite contrary decisions interpreting similar rules elsewhere. (Id. at 418–422 & nn. 5–7.)

## Economic reasoning and reasonableness limit

The majority described changes in law-firm economics: firms invested capital in developing clients, support services, and training; lateral movement and partner departures could remove income while leaving debts and overhead behind. It considered law practice commercially significant even while recognizing professional obligations. Clients' choice of lawyer and lawyers' freedom to accept representation already operated within practical limits involving affordability, conflicts, withdrawal rules, and the absence of a right to membership in any particular firm. The court therefore rejected the assertion that every financial consequence of competing necessarily violated the ethical rule. (Id. at 420–424.)

The majority also reasoned that protecting firm stability could protect clients from disruption and preserve incentives to finance shared resources. It drew comparisons to physicians and accountants and to the permitted sale of law-practice goodwill. These were the majority's policy judgments, disputed by Kennard; the court did not establish as an empirical fact that every departing partner unfairly takes clients or that every restriction benefits clients. (Id. at 423–425.)

Its limiting principle was important. An absolute ban on competition would be per se unreasonable. A geographically limited financial charge could instead function like liquidated damages, but only if, when agreed upon, it reflected a reasonable effort to estimate fair compensation for anticipated competitive losses. An amount disproportionate to that loss would be an ineffective penalty. The court allowed either payment or forfeiture of contractual benefits to be examined under this standard. It left the actual reasonableness of article X to the trial court, rather than holding that the particular forfeiture automatically passed. (Id. at 425–426.)

## Kennard's dissent

Kennard would affirm the Court of Appeal. She read rule 1-500 and its accompanying discussion as unambiguously permitting restrictions during a professional relationship but prohibiting contractual restrictions after its termination. A financial disincentive still restricted practice even if it did not prohibit practice altogether. She emphasized that the trial judgment required surrender of 82.5 percent of the departing firm's profits from the relevant work, which could discourage continued representation of clients who wanted those lawyers. In her view, the majority's distinction between a restriction and a reasonable cost failed to respect the rule's text. (Id. at 426–430.)

The disagreement also concerned the policy to be protected. For Kennard, the lawyer's fiduciary duty and the client's choice were paramount, not merely interests balanced against an established firm's economic stability. Limits arising from an attorney's unwillingness, conflicts, or lack of entitlement to partnership did not answer whether a willing attorney could represent a willing client. She rejected the majority's assumption that remaining partners deserved protection from departures: departures could reflect unfair income allocation, and client loyalty could be generated by a departing lawyer's labor and trustworthiness rather than firm capital. (Id. at 430–433.)

Finally, the commercial practices of other professions and lawyers' existing disregard of restrictive-covenant rules did not, in her view, justify lowering ethical standards. Increasing commercial pressure called for stronger protection of professional duties. Her sole proposed disposition was affirmance of the Court of Appeal. The report also records denial of rehearing on February 3, 1994, with Kennard favoring rehearing; it supplies no separate rehearing opinion. (Id. at 433–434.)

The complete judgment supplies the qualification: facial invalidity was rejected, but the provision's reasonableness remained to be decided on remand. The assigned account refers to three withdrawing partners; the actual narrative names four plaintiffs and footnote 2 separately explains why Strickroth was not governed by the agreement.
