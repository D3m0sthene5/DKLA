# Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons — Seventh Circuit discovery and preclusion stage

**726 F.2d 1150 (7th Cir. 1984) (en banc).** Decided January 3, 1984; corrected January 10, 1984. Posner wrote the principal opinion, with different alignments on discovery and claim preclusion. Assigned account: CivPro PDF page 469, printed pages 913–914, complete Note 5. This brief covers the 1984 circuit judgment; the assignment separately identifies the later Supreme Court reversal at 470 U.S. 373 (1985).

## Facts, litigation sequence, and the two appeals

In 1976, the Academy rejected the membership applications of orthopaedic surgeons R. Anthony Marrese and Michael Treister without a hearing or stated reasons. Membership offered alleged professional advantages, but it was not necessary to practice or obtain hospital staff privileges. Both doctors were certified and held privileges at several hospitals. They initially sued in Illinois state court, relying on common-law and state constitutional theories concerning reasonable admission standards and a hearing. They did not plead a state antitrust claim. **1151–1152.**

The Illinois Appellate Court directed dismissal of Treister's complaint for failure to state a claim, reasoning that Academy membership was not an economic necessity; the Illinois Supreme Court denied leave to appeal. Marrese's state suit had been stayed pending Treister's appeal and was subsequently dismissed. The timing requires care: although Posner described the federal action as following the state litigation's defeat, Flaum's note 1 and Cudahy's note 10 recognize that Marrese's own state dismissal occurred after the federal action was filed. Flaum viewed that stayed suit as effectively defunct; Cudahy objected to treating both doctors as having already lost before filing. **1151–1152; 1163 n.1; Cudahy n.10.**

The doctors' federal complaint sought damages and an injunction under Sherman Act § 1. It alleged that the Academy possessed substantial control over the market for orthopaedic surgical services and had excluded them because they competed too vigorously with existing members. Those were allegations, not adjudicated antitrust facts. The district court denied the Academy's claim-preclusion motion and initially refused interlocutory certification. **1151–1152.**

The doctors then demanded files concerning rejected membership applications from 1970 through 1980. The Academy refused to produce them even after an order requiring production under confidentiality protections. The district court entered criminal contempt and imposed a $10,000 fine. The Academy appealed that contempt judgment in No. 81-2671. Two successive panel decisions reversing contempt were each vacated in the course of rehearing proceedings. Before the second en banc hearing, the newly assigned district judge certified the refusal to dismiss the antitrust suit under § 1292(b). The court authorized that separate appeal, No. 83-2683, and consolidated it with the contempt appeal. The published report's main caption and mandate use that number; Eschbach's separate writing prints a different number when referring to the preclusion appeal. **1152; 1162–1163.**

## Appellate jurisdiction and the consequence of disobeying discovery

The en banc court upheld interlocutory certification despite the pending contempt appeal. The contempt proceeding was a collateral branch; the appeal did not transfer the entire underlying suit out of the district court. Criminal contempt itself was appealable as a final judgment. **1152.**

The principal opinion also held that review of criminal contempt permitted testing the underlying discovery order here. It distinguished situations in which an order could have been directly appealed or otherwise reviewed before disobedience. Discovery orders ordinarily were not immediately appealable, so accepting the risk of contempt furnished a route to appellate review. If the order was ultimately upheld, the disobedient party faced the sanction; the court did not grant a general immunity from court orders. **1157–1158.**

Posner explained that the Academy had not been punished for separate disruptive conduct, destruction of files, or the manner of its refusal. Its refusal was intended to obtain review of the allegedly invalid discovery order. The principal opinion treated the order as falling with the precluded suit, but it expressly did not use the contempt appeal itself as a device to review the whole suit's merits. Claim preclusion reached the court through the independently certified appeal. **1158–1159.**

## Why the court found the discovery order defective

The court also supplied a discovery ground independent of its claim-preclusion ruling. Rule 37's authority to order production had to be applied with the protective and sequencing powers of then-existing Rules 26(c) and 26(d). A district judge had broad discretion, but that discretion required evaluating the hardship to each side and considering a protective order capable of accommodating competing interests. Postponing particular discovery could be justified more readily than permanently denying it. **1159.**

The Academy claimed a First Amendment protection for its membership files. The court rejected complete immunity from discovery. It nevertheless recognized an associational interest in candid evaluations and exchanges of professional information. Disclosure that exposed evaluating members to depositions could discourage candid participation. Conversely, withholding every file could make the doctors unable to prove their antitrust case. The problem therefore called for tailored access, not treating the files as categorically unavailable. **1159–1161.**

The opinion identified several alternatives. The judge could first inspect the doctors' own application files privately, rather than immediately opening a decade of rejection records. Redaction could remove members' names and identifying material, with judicial comparison against originals to verify accuracy. Counsel could initially examine the redacted files and later seek identities if particular evidence or leads justified depositions. These were possible methods of reconciliation, not a holding that every discovery dispute requires the same sequence. **1160.**

The actual protective order allowed the doctors to see their own files, their lawyers to discuss the general contents of other files with them, and depositions of people identified in the files. The court found that arrangement insufficiently protective. It also considered sequencing nonsensitive discovery first where the case might fail on other essential elements. That approach depended on the plaintiffs needing such other evidence; if the files alone sufficed to establish the case, the court acknowledged that requiring unrelated discovery first would make no sense. **1160–1161.**

The opinion stressed the danger of sensitive discovery being used as settlement leverage and concluded that the district court had available methods to protect the Academy without denying essential evidence. It found the order erroneous in the circumstances. The opinion discussed the 1983 amendment to Rule 26 as reinforcing managerial responsibilities but recognized that the amendment took effect after the challenged order. It did not apply the textbook's later 2015 proportionality formulation as the governing 1984 rule. **1161–1162.**

## The separate claim-preclusion ruling

Posner's preclusion analysis reasoned that the doctors could have asserted an Illinois antitrust theory in their first litigation and that, for these allegations, state and federal liability standards were materially equivalent. The opinion accepted exclusive federal jurisdiction over Sherman Act suits, while questioning its rationale. It viewed the different treble-damages provisions as lacking practical significance here and concluded that the federal suit should not afford another opportunity to litigate the same conduct. Its discussion of § 1738 permitted greater preclusive effect than the rendering state might give and did not first resolve the case through an Illinois-law preclusion inquiry. **1152–1157.**

That analysis was disputed within the court. Cudahy characterized Posner's reasoning as a plurality on preclusion. Flaum agreed with dismissal through a different proposed rule requiring substantially contemporaneous litigation of related state and exclusively federal theories. Accordingly, the result directing dismissal should be distinguished from unanimous acceptance of one preclusion rationale. **1163–1167; Cudahy n.1.**

## All five separate writings and the divided results

Bauer concurred in the result. He regarded preclusion as sufficient and said he would approve the discovery reasoning if necessary. Eschbach agreed that the discovery order was an abuse of discretion and concurred in reversing contempt, but opposed barring the federal antitrust claims. **1162–1163.**

Flaum supported preclusion on his contemporaneous-litigation rationale but joined Wood concerning the survival of criminal contempt. His footnotes address Marrese's chronology, § 1738, differing claim-identity tests, and the limits and equitable administration of his proposed rule. **1163–1167, nn.1–9.**

Wood, joined by Cummings and Cudahy, opposed the discovery and contempt dispositions. He considered the district court's confidentiality compromise within its discretion, observed that the Academy had opposed proposed redaction, and rejected the conclusion that ordinary efforts to pursue evidentiary leads established abusive discovery. He would sustain contempt despite reversal of the underlying order; the court had jurisdiction, and claim preclusion was a defense rather than a jurisdictional defect. He also concluded that the Academy waived a jury objection to the $10,000 criminal fine after it had sought contempt as an appeal route and failed to object when notified of the proposed amount. He would affirm contempt while directing renewed consideration of private inspection and redaction before enforcing discovery. **1167–1173, nn.1–2.**

Cudahy, joined by Cummings and Wood, separately disputed the preclusion analysis and joined Wood's discovery dissent. He argued that § 1738 requires the rendering state's rules rather than an invented federal rule giving greater effect, stressed that state courts could not hear the Sherman Act claim, and rejected treating unpleaded state and federal antitrust theories as interchangeable. He disputed speculation about the doctors' litigation motives and damages objectives and emphasized the need for an opportunity to litigate an exclusively federal claim. His notes distinguish findings-of-fact estoppel, settlements, statutory remedies, and federal antitrust policy. **1173–1183, nn.1–18.**

The en banc mandate **reversed the contempt judgment and directed dismissal of the contempt proceeding** in No. 81-2671. In No. 83-2683 it **reversed the denial of the Academy's dismissal motion and directed dismissal of the complaint with prejudice**. The nine-member court divided on both results, with differing supporting coalitions; the individual writings above preserve those distinctions. **1162.**
