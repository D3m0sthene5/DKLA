# Kadic v. Karadzic

**70 F.3d 232 (2d Cir. 1995), decided October 13, 1995; rehearing denied, 74 F.3d 377 (2d Cir. 1996). Civil Procedure.** Chief Judge Newman wrote for a panel with Judges Feinberg and Walker. The assigned account is Note 2, CivPro physical pages 162–163, printed pages 276–277, ending before the next section on service and Wyman. It emphasizes that Karadzic’s United Nations visits did not immunize him from service outside the headquarters district. The complete opinion also decides important subject-matter-jurisdiction questions; those rulings explain why the service dispute mattered.

## The dispute and the dismissal

Two groups of plaintiffs sought civil remedies for atrocities allegedly committed against Croats and Muslims in Bosnia-Herzegovina. Kadic sued for herself and her infant sons; the plaintiffs also included other victims and women’s organizations. The complaints alleged rape, forced prostitution and impregnation, torture, and summary execution as parts of a systematic campaign to destroy ethnic and religious groups. They alleged that Radovan Karadzic, president of the self-proclaimed Bosnian-Serb republic known as Srpska, commanded the responsible forces. These were allegations accepted as true for jurisdictional review, rather than adjudicated findings of responsibility. 70 F.3d at 236–237.

The plaintiffs invoked the Alien Tort Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1350, the Torture Victim Protection Act (TVPA), general federal-question jurisdiction, and supplemental jurisdiction over state claims. They sought compensatory and punitive damages and other relief. Karadzic visited New York during early 1993 as an invitee of the United Nations. Those visits supplied opportunities for personal service that would otherwise have been difficult to obtain.

In the Doe action, process servers said they approached him in the Intercontinental Hotel lobby, identified their purpose, and attempted to hand over the papers from about two feet away. His security guards seized the papers, which fell to the floor. A State Department security officer corroborated the encounter but placed the server about six feet away. In the Kadic action, Judge Owen authorized delivery through a member of Karadzic’s security detail. The officer said he handed the complaint to Karadzic outside the Russian Embassy during a later visit, and Karadzic acknowledged receipt. The plaintiffs also alleged that he stayed outside the United Nations headquarters district and conducted non-United-Nations activities, including fundraising. The panel did not convert those allegations into established facts. Id. at 246–247.

District Judge Leisure dismissed for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The district court understood international human-rights liability to require state action, found the Bosnian-Serb entity insufficiently established as a state, and regarded the possibility of future recognition and head-of-state immunity as a reason against adjudication. It declined supplemental jurisdiction. The dismissal came without the further jurisdictional briefing or hearing the plaintiffs expected. The court of appeals reversed and remanded; it did not adjudge Karadzic liable for the alleged atrocities. Id. at 237–239, 251.

## Which international wrongs could support federal jurisdiction?

The Alien Tort Act requires an alien’s tort claim for a violation of the law of nations or a United States treaty. A merely colorable claim does not establish this jurisdiction: the asserted international norm must satisfy the statute. The panel used contemporary, universally recognized international norms rather than freezing the law of nations in 1789. It rejected the premise that every international-law violation necessarily requires official conduct. Piracy and the slave trade supplied historical examples of rules binding private actors; genocide and war crimes supplied the relevant modern examples. This reasoning did not make every private assault or other human-rights abuse an international tort. Id. at 238–243.

Genocide requires the specified intent to destroy a protected group, in whole or in part. The Genocide Convention expressly encompasses private individuals as well as officials. Congress’s criminal implementation, including its disclaimer of a new private cause of action, did not impliedly repeal the already existing Alien Tort Act. The plaintiffs alleged the necessary destructive purpose, although proof remained for later proceedings. Id. at 241–243 & n.6.

War crimes likewise can be committed by nonstate participants in armed conflict. Common article 3 of the Geneva Conventions binds parties to an internal conflict and protects persons not actively participating in hostilities against murder, torture, degrading treatment, hostage-taking, and executions without proper judgment. Organized insurgent forces do not acquire freedom to commit those acts merely because they lack recognition as a state. A commander’s responsibility to prevent offenses also informed the analysis. The allegations therefore supported jurisdiction over war-crime claims if proved. The panel expressly reserved whether Protocol II had become universally accepted customary law and whether the rules for international conflicts applied on the plaintiffs’ alternative theories. Id. at 242–243 & n.8.

The distinctions matter for torture and summary execution. When those acts form part of genocide or war crimes, the foregoing private-actor rules can apply. When they are alleged independently of those categories, the norms considered here require official conduct or action under color of law. At this threshold stage, the panel could not know whether the plaintiffs would prove genocidal intent or the armed-conflict connection. It held that the allegations were actionable without state action to the extent they involved genocide or war crimes, and otherwise could proceed if Karadzic was shown to be a state actor. Id. at 243–244.

The state-action alternatives also required further proceedings. International-law statehood involves territory, population, government, and capacity for foreign relations; formal recognition by other states is not an indispensable element. The allegations entitled the plaintiffs to try to prove that Srpska met the relevant requirements. Nonrecognition did not automatically make its governmental conduct a legal nullity. Alternatively, the plaintiffs alleged that Karadzic acted in concert with officials of the recognized former Yugoslavia or Serbia. Analogies to action under color of law in § 1983 litigation helped explain how joint conduct or substantial state assistance could satisfy the requirement. The panel did not conclusively determine Srpska’s statehood or Karadzic’s relationship to another government. Id. at 244–245.

The TVPA expressly creates a remedy for official torture and extrajudicial killing, subject to requirements such as exhaustion of adequate, available local remedies and its limitations period. It is not itself a jurisdictional statute; the Alien Tort Act and § 1331 supply jurisdiction for the authorized federal claim. Because the statutory remedies already supported the litigation, the panel left undecided whether § 1331 independently permits an implied cause of action for other international-law violations incorporated into federal common law. Id. at 245–246.

## Personal service and the United Nations visit

Personal service on an individual physically present in the district ordinarily satisfies the applicable service rule and personal-jurisdiction due process. Karadzic argued that his United Nations status nevertheless supplied immunity. The panel separately examined the Headquarters Agreement and the asserted federal common-law immunity. Id. at 246–248.

Section 9 of the Headquarters Agreement restricts service within the defined headquarters district without the Secretary-General’s consent. Karadzic was not served there. Section 15 gives diplomatic privileges to designated representatives of member states; his status as an invitee did not place him in that category. Section 11 prohibits impediments to an invitee’s transit to and from headquarters, but it does not expressly confer general immunity from lawsuits or service. Reading it as a blanket immunity would enlarge the treaty beyond its stated protections. Both State Department and United Nations legal views supported the conclusion that this invitee lacked immunity outside the district. Id. at 247.

The panel preserved a narrower question: immunity might conceivably protect someone in direct transit between an airport or other entry point and headquarters. It did not decide whether that protection existed because Karadzic was not served while traveling to or from headquarters. That reservation is in the original report at 247 n.9. It prevents the holding from becoming a categorical statement that every form of service during transit is permissible.

The common-law analogies also failed. Rules excluding United Nations activities from a doing-business jurisdictional calculation do not immunize an individual from otherwise sufficient personal service. Nor did governmental-contact or litigation-related immunity doctrines establish the claimed protection here. Future recognition as a head of state was speculative and did not justify declining present jurisdiction. The panel’s conclusion remained conditional on the plaintiffs establishing service and their associated factual allegations as they proposed to prove them. It rejected immunity rather than finally resolving the disputed hotel-service evidence. Id. at 247–248.

## Justiciability, judgment, and the distinct rehearing stage

Foreign-policy implications alone did not make the litigation a political question. Applying the Baker criteria, the panel found judicially manageable international norms and congressional authorization for adjudication. The Executive’s statement affirmatively disclaimed a political-question objection in these cases. The opinion also explained that a contrary Executive position would deserve respectful consideration without automatically determining justiciability. The act-of-state defense had not been raised below and was not before the panel; its skepticism that the alleged atrocities would qualify was not a dispositive act-of-state holding. No suitable alternative forum was then shown for a forum-non-conveniens dismissal, given conditions in Bosnia and the unavailability of Serbia as an adequate alternative on this record. Id. at 248–251.

The exact appellate judgment was **reversal of the subject-matter-jurisdiction dismissal and remand for further proceedings**. The opinion recognized cognizable federal claims and rejected the asserted service immunities; factual proof, remaining procedural questions, and liability were still ahead. Id. at 251. No separate judicial writing accompanies this panel opinion.

The subsequently reported rehearing denial is a separate procedural stage. Karadzic relied on a historical article arguing that the 1789 phrase “tort only” concerned particular prize-related torts. The same panel found that historical account interesting but insufficient to overcome the statute’s broad language, controlling Filártiga precedent, and Congress’s later endorsement of a broad reading through the TVPA. Even a particular historical occasion for legislation does not necessarily confine the enacted text to that occasion. The panel **denied rehearing**, without a separate writing or judicial footnote. 74 F.3d at 377–378. This brief states those historical rulings and does not certify the present scope of Alien Tort Statute liability.
