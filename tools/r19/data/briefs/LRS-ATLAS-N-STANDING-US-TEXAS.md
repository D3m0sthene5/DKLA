# United States v. Texas

**599 U.S. 670 (2023) · Supreme Court of the United States · June 23, 2023 · No. 22–58**

## Facts, statutory setting, and litigation

Federal immigration laws direct detention of specified noncitizens. Section 1226(c) provides for custody of certain noncitizens after release from criminal custody; §1231(a)(2) provides for detention during the removal period after a final removal order. In 2021, Secretary of Homeland Security Alejandro Mayorkas issued enforcement guidelines prioritizing noncitizens who presented national-security or public-safety threats or had recently entered unlawfully. The guidelines called for an assessment of individual circumstances rather than treating every statutory category as an automatic enforcement priority. Texas and Louisiana argued that this policy produced fewer arrests and detentions than the statutes required. They claimed resulting expenditures for incarceration, health care, and education. The question was whether those consequences allowed the states to obtain judicial review of the federal enforcement policy. (Court 1–3.)

After a trial, the district court found state costs, held that the states had standing, ruled the guidelines unlawful, and vacated them. The Fifth Circuit declined to stay that judgment. The Supreme Court granted certiorari before judgment in the court of appeals. The Supreme Court accepted that the district court had found costs, but held that the states lacked the kind of judicially cognizable interest needed for this suit. It **reversed the district court’s judgment**. The Supreme Court decided standing; it expressly did **not** decide whether the guidelines complied with the immigration statutes. (Court 2–3, 12–14.)

## The Court’s rule and reasoning

Justice Kavanaugh wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson. Standing requires injury, causation, and redressability, but an expense does not automatically create a judicially cognizable injury in every setting. Historical practice and the judicial role matter. These plaintiffs were asking a court to require the Executive to make more arrests of third parties. The Court found no tradition of suits of that kind and treated *Linda R. S. v. Richard D.* as establishing that a person ordinarily lacks a judicially cognizable interest in the prosecution of someone else. The majority did not say that the states’ financial losses were too small, or that financial injuries can never support state standing. Its conclusion concerned the object of this lawsuit: compelling a different allocation of arrests and enforcement resources. (Court 3–6 & nn.1–2.)

The Executive must select enforcement priorities when appropriations and personnel cannot support enforcement against every possible violator. That choice encompasses public safety, resource allocation, and, in immigration matters, foreign relations. Courts lack manageable standards for supervising an agency’s overall arrest priorities. A judicial order increasing enforcement would also place courts in tension with the Executive’s constitutional responsibility for enforcement. The Court observed that prioritization had occurred under multiple administrations; the structural problem did not depend on approval of this particular administration’s policy. (Court 6–9.)

The states’ downstream expenditures did not change that conclusion. Federal enforcement policies commonly affect state revenues and costs, sometimes beneficially and sometimes adversely. Allowing those indirect consequences to justify this kind of suit would draw courts into broad supervision of executive enforcement. That discussion supplemented the specific standing holding; it was not a general immunity from suit whenever a federal action affects state finances. (Court 8–9 & n.3.)

## The holding’s express limits

The Court carefully reserved several materially different situations. A person’s own selective-prosecution claim presents a different question. Congress might create a specifically authorized action permitting defined plaintiffs to seek particular enforcement orders; the Court did not decide whether or when that would overcome the constitutional difficulties. A complete abandonment of statutory responsibilities might also present a different analysis. The majority understood the states not to have advanced that complete-abdication theory in this case. (Court 9–11 & n.4.)

A policy combining nonenforcement with affirmative legal status or benefits can differ from simple enforcement priorities. The Court distinguished previous litigation over immigration programs of that kind. Continued detention of someone already arrested may likewise raise different questions from decisions about making arrests. The majority relied on the Government’s representation that these guidelines concerned apprehension and removal, rather than the duration of detention after custody had begun. Those distinctions are reservations, not rulings that plaintiffs necessarily would have standing in the hypothetical cases. (Court 11–12 & n.5.)

Finally, the Court separated legality from judicial reviewability. Its holding did not give the Executive a freestanding power to disregard federal statutes. Congress and the electorate have additional tools to address enforcement decisions, including oversight and appropriations. The Court’s narrower conclusion was that these states could not maintain this suit asking a federal court to require more arrests. (Court 12–14.)

The assigned account emphasizes *Massachusetts v. EPA*. The Court distinguished that case because it involved denial of a statutorily authorized rulemaking petition, rather than a challenge to an exercise of enforcement discretion. It did not overrule *Massachusetts* or announce that state standing generally had ended. The assigned pinpoint, 685 n.6, corresponds to this distinction. (Court 13–14 n.6.)

## Gorsuch’s concurrence in the judgment

Justice Gorsuch, joined by Justices Thomas and Barrett, agreed with the judgment but rejected the majority’s choice of standing rationale. He regarded the district court’s findings of financial injury as established and questioned the majority’s invocation of history and *Linda R. S.* to deny cognizable injury. He also questioned the continued significance of the “special solicitude” discussed in *Massachusetts*. The textbook’s quotation suggesting that lower courts leave that idea on the shelf comes from this **three-Justice concurrence**, not from the five-Justice Court opinion. (Gorsuch 1–4.)

For Gorsuch, the decisive defect was redressability. Section 1252(f)(1) restricted the lower court’s ability to enjoin operation of the covered immigration provisions. Vacating the guidelines did not itself command federal officers to arrest more people: even without the guidelines, officials retained enforcement discretion. The possibility that the officials might respond to a judicial opinion by changing priorities was not the same as relief likely to remedy the plaintiffs’ injury. Proposed routes around the injunction restriction depended on speculation about future relief, rather than a sufficient showing when this action began. (Gorsuch 4–8.)

Gorsuch separately raised serious questions about reading the Administrative Procedure Act’s direction to “set aside” unlawful agency action as authorizing universal vacatur. He examined the review provision’s language, its relationship to the APA’s remedial provisions, and older practice, and warned about effects on nonparties and forum selection. He also explained that even an available equitable remedy should be tailored to the plaintiffs’ injuries. These were questions and views in a concurrence; **the Court did not resolve the general legality of APA vacatur in this case**. (Gorsuch 8–19.)

## Barrett’s concurrence in the judgment

Justice Barrett, joined by Justice Gorsuch, agreed that redressability disposed of the suit but wrote separately about the majority’s injury theory. She read *Linda R. S.* as turning on the speculative connection between the requested prosecution and receipt of child support, rather than on a categorical prohibition on injuries related to another person’s prosecution. She also emphasized that the states disclaimed an interest in prosecution itself and sought detention during proceedings the Government had chosen to pursue. (Barrett 1–3.)

Barrett cautioned against merging distinct doctrines. *Castle Rock* concerned a property interest; *Heckler v. Chaney* concerned statutory reviewability under the APA. Neither necessarily answered whether the states had suffered Article III injury. Executive discretion and separation-of-powers concerns might bear on the merits or available relief without eliminating injury in fact. Her separate concurrence therefore reinforced the judgment while refusing to adopt the majority’s broader doctrinal route. (Barrett 4–6.)

## Alito’s dissent

Justice Alito alone dissented. He would have applied the traditional injury, causation, and redressability elements and found Texas’s standing established, making it unnecessary to decide Louisiana’s standing. He stressed the district court’s trial findings concerning withdrawn detainers, diminished detention, supervision costs, recidivism, and other state expenditures. Those factual details in his writing are his account of the trial record, not additional factual holdings by the Supreme Court majority. (Alito 1–10 & nn.1–4.)

Alito regarded the guidelines’ invalidation as likely to change agency conduct and reduce costs. He also challenged the concurrences’ remedial objections, distinguishing the statute’s restriction on lower courts from the Supreme Court’s authority and arguing that existing practice supported vacatur. He acknowledged the consequential APA question without treating it as a question the Court had granted and decided. (Alito 10–14 & nn.5–7.)

He found the majority’s treatment of state standing difficult to reconcile with *Massachusetts v. EPA*. More fundamentally, he argued that the majority improperly moved statutory reviewability and executive authority into Article III standing. His historical discussion of royal suspending and dispensing powers supported his view that the Take Care Clause did not permit the Executive simply to replace mandatory congressional commands with its preferred policy. He criticized the majority’s unresolved exceptions as uncertain boundaries for that new standing rule. These are dissenting criticisms, not the controlling holding. (Alito 15–29 & nn.8–18.)

## Result and significance

The judgment was **8–1**: five Justices adopted Kavanaugh’s injury-and-enforcement rationale; three concurred in the judgment on redressability grounds; Alito dissented. The district court’s judgment was reversed because these states lacked standing to challenge these enforcement guidelines. Statutory compliance, universal APA vacatur, and the reserved categories of enforcement suits remained undecided. For the course, the case shows why a concrete fiscal consequence does not dispense with identifying the judicial interest and remedy sought, and why the Court’s holding must be separated from concurring criticisms of special solicitude and agency remedies.
