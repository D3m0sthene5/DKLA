# Spivey v. Vertrue, Inc.
528 F.3d 982 (7th Cir. 2008) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts, removal, and the disputed application

Quinten Spivey sued Vertrue in Illinois state court on behalf of a proposed class. Vertrue marketed discount services, and Spivey alleged that it systematically imposed unauthorized credit-card charges through 22 programs. Vertrue removed under the Class Action Fairness Act. Spivey sought remand, arguing that the amount in controversy did not exceed the required $5 million. The district court agreed and remanded on April 8, 2008. (983.)

Vertrue sought permission to appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1453(c)(1). Its lawyer mailed the application on the seventh day after the remand order. The court received it on April 18, the tenth calendar day. Under Rule 25(a)(2), receipt rather than mailing constituted filing. Spivey argued that the application was too late, relying on other circuits’ interpretation of CAFA as establishing a maximum seven-day filing interval. Yet the enacted text permitted an application not less than seven days after the order. The central question was whether a court could reject as untimely an application satisfying that actual language by replacing less with more. (983–984.)

Chief Judge Frank H. Easterbrook wrote the June 11, 2008 opinion for a unanimous panel with Judges Ilana Diamond Rovner and Terence T. Evans. The opinion contains no judicial footnotes or separate writings. Unlike the Third Circuit’s Morgan timing opinion, Spivey also decided the amount-in-controversy question and reversed the remand order. (982–983, 986.)

## Text, apparent error, and protection of reliance

The panel recognized the likely drafting mistake. Appeals ordinarily have terminal deadlines; a provision requiring a minimum delay without stating an endpoint appears anomalous, particularly beside CAFA’s requirement of rapid appellate disposition. Other circuits had replaced the word less with more based on that context and legislative history. Congress had not yet enacted a technical correction when Spivey was decided. The Seventh Circuit nonetheless held that awkward or even perverse drafting did not justify rejecting a petition timely under the enacted words. (983–985.)

Easterbrook relied on Dodd v. United States, United States v. Locke, and Lamie v. United States Trustee to distinguish interpretation from revising a clear command. Legislative history could illuminate uncertain language in context, but could not turn a clear provision into its opposite. The opinion placed particular weight on litigants’ entitlement to rely on the signed statutory text. Even a judicial approach seeking to protect people from misleading drafting should not create a trap for a party that complied with Congress’s actual language. (984–985.)

The court narrowed its disagreement with the other circuits. Their applications had been submitted within seven days, and none of the cited decisions had actually dismissed a later application as untimely. The Seventh Circuit agreed that an early application need not be discarded and resubmitted after a week. It drew an analogy to Rule 4(a)(2), under which some premature notices of appeal remain on file and become effective when appeal is available. Accepting an early application was different from declaring a later, textually compliant application jurisdictionally and irreparably late. (984.)

Vertrue’s lawyers had tried to straddle the competing constructions by mailing on day seven and expecting receipt later. The court did not hold that mailing displaced the filing-by-receipt rule. Rather, it held that the actual filing was timely under the enacted statute, making the attempted mailing-date alternative unnecessary. The opinion also noted the effect of excluding weekends for seven-day periods under the then-current counting rule; that did not make this application premature. (983, 985.)

## The rules supplied an endpoint

Reading the statute literally did not permit indefinite delay. When a law allowing interlocutory review specifies no terminal deadline, Rule 5(a)(2) supplies the time established by Rule 4(a) for a notice of appeal. For this private-party dispute, that was 30 days. The statute’s minimum interval and the rule’s outer limit could therefore work together. This approach allowed more time than the probable drafters intended, but avoided both an unlimited window and the countertextual rejection of this application. Vertrue’s tenth-day filing was timely. (985.)

The rule-based endpoint is essential to the holding. A shorthand description that the court read less literally but allowed appeals forever would omit the mechanism the court used to reconcile the text with prompt forum determination. Conversely, treating Spivey as adopting the other circuits’ seven-day maximum would contradict its protection of this later filing. (985.)

## Amount in controversy and merits of the remand appeal

Vertrue supported federal jurisdiction with an affidavit showing almost $7 million in Illinois billings for only four of the 22 challenged programs. The district court thought that insufficient because Vertrue did not concede how much of that revenue consisted of unauthorized charges. The Seventh Circuit held that this demanded the wrong showing. A removing defendant did not have to admit liability to demonstrate the stakes; the amount in controversy depended on the relief the plaintiff sought, rather than what the plaintiff would certainly recover. (985–986.)

Spivey alleged that unauthorized charges were a standard practice common to the class and demanded refunds. Those allegations placed the propriety of Vertrue’s charges in controversy. Under Brill v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc., the removing party bore the burden of plausibly explaining how the actual demands exceeded $5 million. This was a pleading showing, not a requirement to establish ultimate liability through discovery or trial. Once that showing was made, the court required legal impossibility of a recovery exceeding the threshold before rejecting jurisdiction on amount-in-controversy grounds. The district court had identified uncertainty about recovery, not such impossibility. (985–986.)

## Exact judgment and assigned significance

The court granted Vertrue’s petition, accepted the appeal, reversed the remand order, and remanded for adjudication on the merits in federal court. Vertrue did not need another notice of appeal but did have to pay applicable fees under Rule 5(d). The appellate judgment determined the proper forum; it did not resolve whether any particular charge was unauthorized or establish a damages award. (986.)
