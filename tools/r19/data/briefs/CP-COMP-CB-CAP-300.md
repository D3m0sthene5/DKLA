# Business Guides, Inc. v. Chromatic Communications Enterprises, Inc.

**498 U.S. 533 (1991) · Supreme Court of the United States · February 26, 1991 · Civil Procedure**

**Assigned account:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 369, printed page 705: the complete Business Guides paragraph before the 705 marker. The surrounding page supplies Rule 11 history and later amendment context.

**Decision in one sentence.** Under the 1983 version of Rule 11, a represented party that signed a pleading or other court paper undertook an objective duty to make a reasonable inquiry; employing counsel did not excuse a sophisticated publisher's failure to check the supposed copying evidence on which its signed submissions depended. 498 U.S. at 541–551, 554.

## Facts and procedural path

Business Guides published directories covering eighteen specialized retail trades. To detect copying, it planted inaccurate entries, including small alterations to real listings and wholly fictitious businesses. It retained Finley, Kumble to sue competing directory publisher Chromatic for copyright infringement, conversion, and unfair competition. Its October 31, 1986 filings sought an immediate temporary restraining order. A lawyer and the company's president signed the initial application; supporting affidavits were submitted under seal. A declaration identified ten entries as copying evidence. Id. at 535–537.

The district judge's law clerk asked which information was false before the scheduled November 7 hearing. When counsel contacted research director Michael Lambe, Business Guides withdrew three alleged seeds. The clerk then spent approximately an hour checking the entries and found nine of the ten to be accurate. The court denied the TRO, stayed the proceedings, and referred possible Rule 11 violations to a magistrate. Lambe's supplemental affidavit identified seven alleged seeds; he crossed out a fourth inaccurate allegation before signing because that listing was also accurate. Id. at 536–538.

The explanation changed during three evidentiary hearings. At the first two, the publisher and its lawyers defended the accurate entries as coincidences. At the third, they explained that their master seed list had been compiled after publication by comparing initial customer questionnaires with the finished directory. They assumed the questionnaires were correct. In fact, proofreading had corrected input errors, so apparent discrepancies designated as seeds were accurate published entries. The magistrate accepted this explanation but found it did not excuse the deficient inquiry. Id. at 537–539.

The magistrate distinguished client and counsel. Given the urgency and the client's experience, the lawyers could initially rely on its factual representations. Business Guides itself was responsible for checking them before filing. After the withdrawal of three seeds, both client and lawyers should have investigated the remaining allegations. The magistrate also criticized their later oral coincidence arguments. The district court adopted the recommendations. By the sanctions-motion stage, Finley, Kumble had dissolved and entered bankruptcy; Chromatic withdrew its motion against the firm without prejudice because of the automatic stay. Id. at 538–540.

The remaining genuinely fictitious listing supplied no sound infringement inference either: Chromatic said a Business Guides employee had submitted a questionnaire planting it in Chromatic's directory, and the district court treated the publisher's failure to deny that explanation as a tacit admission. It dismissed the suit with prejudice and imposed $13,865.66 in legal expenses and out-of-pocket costs. The Ninth Circuit upheld the reasonable-inquiry requirement and the findings concerning signed submissions, but rejected oral testimony as an independent Rule 11 basis and vacated the sanctions for reconsideration. The district court reaffirmed dismissal and the monetary sanction. The Supreme Court reviewed whether the objective standard applied to represented parties. Id. at 539–541. The appellate correction matters: the Supreme Court's affirmance did not endorse every original sanctions rationale.

## Issues, arguments, and the Court's reasoning

**Did a represented party's signature carry a Rule 11 certification?** Business Guides emphasized the rule's opening sentences, requiring counsel to sign for represented parties and requiring unrepresented parties to sign personally. It argued that these requirements limited whose signature mattered. The Court instead read the certification sentence's reference to an attorney or party according to its ordinary breadth. The rule elsewhere expressly distinguished represented from unrepresented parties; it did not insert that qualification into the certification sentence. A party's signature could certify a document's factual and legal basis even when voluntarily supplied. Id. at 541–547.

The Court applied the personal responsibility principle of Pavelic & LeFlore: signing brought responsibility for the submission's validity home to the signer. A represented individual could not escape that responsibility by delegating factual verification to counsel. The majority also explained why a corporation's need to act through agents differed from an attorney's expressly individual signature. It nevertheless declined definitely to resolve the corporate-signature objection because Business Guides had not raised it below. That discussion is not an unrestricted holding attributing every employee's signature to the company. Id. at 547–548.

**Was subjective good faith enough?** No. The 1983 rule applied one reasonable-inquiry standard to attorneys and parties. The company urged a bad-faith threshold, invoking the distinct treatment of affidavits under then-Rule 56(g). The Court answered that rulemaking had replaced Rule 11's former subjective standard; interpreting the adopted language was different from choosing an ideal policy. Id. at 548–549.

The factual allocation of responsibility reinforced the text. A client may know the facts better than counsel. Here the publisher could check its own directory evidence while lawyers urgently sought relief. Exempting it merely because the lawyers reasonably relied on its representations would leave an avoidable factual failure unsanctioned. Reasonableness still depended on circumstances: a sophisticated publisher operating since 1948 was not measured as though it were a small, inexperienced litigant. The Court applied an objective standard, not a demand that every client possess an attorney's legal knowledge. Id. at 549–551.

**Did this interpretation impermissibly alter substantive rights?** Business Guides invoked the Rules Enabling Act and compared sanctions to fee shifting or a new tort of negligent prosecution. The majority treated Rule 11 as regulation of discrete litigation submissions, principally serving deterrence and the integrity of federal procedure. It did not create a prevailing-party compensation rule or substitute a general damages action for malicious prosecution. Incidental substantive effects did not invalidate a procedural rule reasonably necessary to maintain that system. The Court acknowledged possible misapplications but treated appellate correction, rather than invalidation of the rule, as the response. Id. at 551–554.

The holding concerned **signing represented parties**. The Court expressly reserved when a **nonsigning** party could be sanctioned. Id. at 554. It also reserved the copyrightability of the directories; this sanctions judgment did not resolve that underlying merits issue. Id. at 535 n.†.

## Judgment and separate opinion

The Court **affirmed the Ninth Circuit's judgment**, sustaining the challenged objective standard and the resulting sanctions disposition. Id. at 554. Justice O'Connor wrote for Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices White, Blackmun, and Souter: five Justices. Justice Kennedy dissented, joined throughout by Justices Marshall and Stevens; Justice Scalia joined only Parts I, III, and IV, not Part II.

Kennedy read the rule's certification provision in light of its specified attorney and unrepresented-party signatures, its history as regulation of those practicing before courts, and the Advisory Committee's lawyer-centered explanations. He also argued that evidentiary affidavits differed from papers invoking judicial action and that the majority's corporate attribution conflicted with Pavelic & LeFlore. Id. at 555–564. Part II, without Scalia, developed federalism and rulemaking concerns: imposing objective prefiling duties on ordinary represented citizens threatened substantive policy choices, good-faith access to courts, and the allocation of litigation costs. Id. at 564–569.

Part III supplied a case-specific reason to reverse: no attorney violation had been established in the initial filing; Lambe's later declaration was neither signed by an attorney nor necessarily signed on the corporation's behalf; accompanying sealing papers did not depend on the factual truth of his declaration. Kennedy would also require a subjective bad-faith threshold for sanctioning a represented party. His final footnote declined to adopt an unbriefed continuing-duty theory. Id. at 569–570 & n.5. These are dissenting positions, not exceptions adopted by the Court.

## Assignment and later-rule distinction

The textbook accurately identifies the objective standard and Kennedy's objection, but its compressed statement that the Court equated client and attorney standards should be read with the majority's circumstances-sensitive explanation. Likewise, the firm's insolvency explains the withdrawal of the motion against it, rather than showing that the Supreme Court sustained a sanction against the firm.

This brief's holding describes **the 1983 rule**. The assignment separately describes the 1993 revision. The subsequently amended [Rule 11 text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_11) provides discretionary sanctions, a 21-day motion safe harbor, law-firm responsibility subject to exceptional circumstances, and a prohibition on monetary sanctions against represented parties for legal-contention violations under subdivision (b)(2). Those provisions must be considered separately from the historical holding; this is not a current-law citator certification.
