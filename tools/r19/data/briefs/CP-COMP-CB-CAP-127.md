# Sadat v. Mertes

**615 F.2d 1176 (7th Cir. 1980) — United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, February 19, 1980.** The court issued a per curiam opinion on a panel of Judges Cummings, Pell, and Cudahy. No separate opinion is reported. The assigned account is CivPro, physical PDF page 182, printed page 317.

## Facts and the changing domicile

Moheb Sadat, born in Egypt, came to the United States in 1963 and apparently was domiciled in Pittsburgh by 1973. He owned a home there, his wife worked at the university, and his children attended local schools. In 1973, with Egypt's permission, he became a naturalized United States citizen. Egypt's permission expressly preserved his Egyptian citizenship. The appellate court accepted that the record established Egyptian nationality under that country's law; the jurisdictional problem was how to treat his simultaneous American nationality. (1178–1179, 1183–1184.)

Also in 1973, Sadat accepted a position with Kohler International requiring relocation to Beirut after training in Wisconsin. He sold the Pittsburgh house and moved his family and property. While traveling from Kohler's Wisconsin offices to O'Hare Airport, he was involved in the automobile accident underlying this suit. He nevertheless completed the move to Beirut and identified it to the United States Embassy as his permanent overseas residence. (1179.)

His employment ended in 1975. A release required Kohler to pay relocation expenses to a destination Sadat selected, subject to a cap based on the cost of travel to Pittsburgh. Fighting in Lebanon led his family to leave hurriedly for Egypt. Sadat said Kohler did not pay the promised transportation costs and that financial difficulty prevented returning to America. He remained in Cairo through the filing of this action, living in a house his mother purchased, registering with the American Embassy, holding Egyptian driver's licenses, paying property taxes, and maintaining a bank account. He returned to the United States in 1978 and lived in Milwaukee. (1179–1180 & nn.1–2.)

## Procedure and the three arguments

Sadat filed on June 7, 1976, seeking $1 million for alleged negligence by drivers Mertes and Galganites and joining their insurers. Additional defendants entered through third-party proceedings. The complaint called Sadat an American citizen residing in Egypt and the named defendants Wisconsin or Connecticut citizens. A jurisdictional challenge was not raised until January 1979, when third-party defendants moved to dismiss and the other defendants joined. (1177–1178.)

Sadat answered that he remained domiciled in Pennsylvania; alternatively, his Egyptian citizenship permitted alienage jurisdiction. He also argued that the defendants should be estopped because their delay allowed the limitations period to expire. The district court found him domiciled in Egypt and dismissed. The Seventh Circuit rejected all three appellate grounds and affirmed. A separate employment suit mentioned in footnote 1 was affirmed in a separate unpublished order, not decided on its merits in this reported opinion. (1178–1179 n.1, 1189.)

## State diversity: domicile at filing

For §1332(a)(1), an individual must be both an American citizen and a citizen of a particular State. State citizenship is determined by domicile, ordinarily requiring physical presence and intent to make the place home at least for the time being. Citizenship is assessed when the complaint is filed; the later move to Wisconsin did not supply a missing basis. (1180 & n.3.)

The court found ample evidence that Sadat had abandoned Pennsylvania for Lebanon and then established an Egyptian domicile. His family, home, possessions, schooling, and licenses outweighed a contrary assertion of intent. Voting by absentee ballot from Pennsylvania was one factor, not conclusive proof. His contract did not require relocation to Pittsburgh; it merely used Pittsburgh's transportation cost as a ceiling, and his deposition also identified California as a possible destination. (1180–1182 & nn.5–6.)

The court's alternative reasoning mattered: even if financial compulsion prevented an intent to make Egypt his new home, his established Lebanese domicile continued until replaced. He had not physically returned to Pennsylvania to reestablish domicile. Either way, he was an American domiciled abroad when he filed and could not invoke diversity between citizens of different States. The court did not equate every involuntary residence with a new domicile. (1181–1182.)

## Alienage: foreign nationality was real but not controlling

The court explained alienage jurisdiction's concern with national responsibility for treatment of foreign nationals. It recognized that a country ordinarily determines its own citizens and found the Egyptian documents sufficient to establish that Egypt still regarded Sadat as Egyptian. Thus rejection of jurisdiction did not rest on erasing his foreign citizenship as a matter of Egyptian law. (1182–1184 & n.8.)

Instead, the court ordinarily treated only the American nationality of a dual American–foreign citizen as relevant to §1332. The United States' treatment of its own citizen was less likely to create the foreign-sovereign friction that justified alienage jurisdiction. Allowing a dual national to elect foreign citizenship could also give access to federal court that a similarly situated American without dual nationality lacked. The court limited its analysis to a person with American and foreign nationalities; footnote 9 left the considerations for two exclusively foreign nationalities distinct. (1184–1187 & nn.9–13.)

The court did not claim that domicile and nationality were interchangeable. Nor did it treat corporate dual-citizenship rules as controlling individual nationality. It considered whether an effective or dominant foreign nationality might justify an exception, but **assumed that possibility only for argument's sake**. Sadat would not satisfy it: he had voluntarily naturalized, sworn American allegiance, registered with American embassies, voted in an American election, and maintained that he wished to retain American citizenship. He had not taken reasonably practicable steps to end that status. Egyptian residence alone was not enough to establish dominant Egyptian nationality. (1186–1188 & nn.14–16.)

## Delay could not cure this jurisdictional defect

Subject-matter jurisdiction ordinarily cannot be created by consent, waiver, laches, or estoppel, and Rule 12(h)(3) requires dismissal when its absence appears. The court acknowledged the hardship alleged and considered Di Frischia's unusual estoppel decision. But those defendants had raised an objection, abandoned it, stipulated to jurisdictional facts, and then revived the objection after limitations expired. Here, there was no comparable sequence or factual stipulation, and Sadat's own jurisdictional allegation was facially insufficient. Whatever exceptional case might justify estoppel, this was not it. (1188–1189.)

## Judgment and limits

The Seventh Circuit **affirmed the district court's dismissal for want of subject-matter jurisdiction**. (1189.) It did not decide negligence, damages, or whether some other forum would ultimately accept a timely action. The conclusion concerned the scope of §1332 as construed, not a holding that Article III necessarily prohibited Congress from providing any broader jurisdiction. The assigned summary is useful but should retain the court's alternative domicile reasoning and its expressly assumed, rather than adopted, dominant-nationality exception.
