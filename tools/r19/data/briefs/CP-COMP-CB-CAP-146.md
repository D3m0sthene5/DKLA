# Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals Inc. v. Thompson

**478 U.S. 804 (1986) · Supreme Court of the United States · July 7, 1986**  
**Civil Procedure — federal questions embedded in state-law claims**

The Court held that the asserted violation of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act did not make these state tort claims removable under federal-question jurisdiction. Its reasoning depended on the assumed congressional decision to withhold a private federal remedy and the nature of the federal interest implicated by incorporating the federal standard into state negligence law. Justice Stevens wrote for five Justices; Justice Brennan dissented for four. The assignment appears in CivPro PDF pages 193–194, printed pages 342–343, including extracts from majority footnote 12 and dissent footnote 1.

## Allegations and the path to the Court

The Thompson respondents lived in Canada, and the MacTavish respondents lived in Scotland. They filed virtually identical complaints in an Ohio state court against the manufacturer and distributor of Bendectin. Each alleged that a child’s birth deformities resulted from the mother’s ingestion of the drug during pregnancy. Five of six counts sought damages under familiar common-law theories: negligence, breach of warranty, strict liability, fraud, and gross negligence. Count IV alleged that the drug was misbranded under the FDCA because its label inadequately warned of potential danger. The complaints treated the alleged federal violation as a rebuttable presumption of negligence and alleged that the violation proximately caused the injuries. These were allegations, not findings that Bendectin caused the deformities or that the company violated the statute. **805–806.**

Merrell Dow removed the actions, and they were consolidated in federal district court. The company relied on the federal component of Count IV. It also asserted alienage, but its principal place of business was Ohio; the historical forum-defendant restriction in §1441(b) made removal improper without a federal-question basis. The district court refused the families’ request to remand, reasoning that *Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.* supported jurisdiction, and then dismissed on forum non conveniens grounds. The Sixth Circuit reversed: the FDCA provided no private action, and the causes of action did not arise under federal law. **806–807 & nn.1–2.**

The Supreme Court reviewed the jurisdictional question, not the drug’s safety, the truth of the tort allegations, or the appropriateness of a foreign forum if jurisdiction existed.

## The majority’s jurisdictional reasoning

Section 1331 does not reach every dispute that Article III could constitutionally permit Congress to place in federal court. Jurisdiction normally follows the plaintiff’s well-pleaded complaint, not an anticipated federal defense. Holmes’s proposition that a suit arises under the law creating its cause of action describes the great majority of cases, but it is not an exclusive rule: a state-created claim can sometimes turn on a sufficiently substantial federal issue. The difficult question was whether this particular incorporated federal standard supplied such an issue. **807–810 & nn.3–6.**

Both sides agreed, for purposes of the case, that the FDCA did not create a private federal cause of action. The majority expressly **assumed that proposition without deciding it**. It examined why the modern implied-remedy inquiry demanded attention to legislative intent, the protected class, statutory purposes, and the traditionally state character of the proposed remedy. Permitting a state claim’s incorporation of the same federal standard to establish federal jurisdiction would, in the majority’s view, undermine the significance of Congress’s assumed decision to withhold a federal private remedy. **810–813 & nn.7–10.**

The company argued that federal jurisdiction nevertheless followed because federal statutory interpretation was necessary to Count IV. The Court rejected an automatic necessity test. A necessary federal ingredient does not dispense with the inquiry into substantiality and congressional intent. In these circumstances, the assumed congressional determination against a private federal remedy meant that alleging a statutory violation as one element of a state cause of action did not supply a sufficiently substantial federal question. **813–814 & n.11.**

Footnote 12 explained the distinction between *Smith* and *Moore v. Chesapeake & Ohio Railway Co.* In *Smith*, the state claim challenged the constitutionality of an important federal statute. In *Moore*, incorporating a federal safety standard did not fundamentally transform the state tort claim. The Court also noted that even a formally federal cause of action might predominantly involve state questions, as in *Shoshone Mining*. Thus, formal origin or the presence of federal language alone did not settle the jurisdictional inquiry. **814–815 n.12.**

The company’s remaining arguments did not change the result. A general interest in uniform federal interpretation did not justify original jurisdiction here; Supreme Court review of state judgments remained available for federal issues. Preemption was a separate question from original jurisdiction. Nor did the novelty of whether the FDCA applied to sales in Canada and Scotland itself create jurisdiction. **815–817 & nn.13–14.**

The Court also corrected a possible flaw in the Sixth Circuit’s explanation. The mere availability of recovery on other state-law counts would not defeat jurisdiction if Count IV independently supported it; the other counts could then fall within pendent jurisdiction. That correction did not alter the outcome because Count IV itself lacked the required federal jurisdictional basis. **817 n.15.**

## Brennan’s dissent

Justice Brennan, joined by Justices White, Marshall, and Blackmun, would have recognized jurisdiction. For him, *Smith* established that a state cause of action arises under federal law when resolving a colorable federal question is necessary to recovery. Count IV independently depended on the FDCA, regardless of the availability of other counts. The question of the statute’s application abroad was not frivolous. He criticized the majority’s comparison of the importance of different federal interests as malleable and thought *Moore*, if inconsistent with *Smith*, should be overruled rather than used to weaken the latter’s rule. **818–824 & nn.1–3.**

Brennan accepted the absence of a private FDCA action only as the same assumption the majority had made. He distinguished creating a remedy from providing a federal forum for a remedy supplied by state law. Congress’s decision against a federal private action could reflect concerns about private enforcement, but it did not logically show hostility to federal adjudication of an otherwise permissible state action. Federal-question jurisdiction promoted expertise and uniformity; the FDCA’s own public-enforcement structure relied on federal courts. If Congress meant to prohibit the state remedy itself, preemption could accomplish that. If the state remedy remained permissible, Brennan saw no reason to insist that only state courts adjudicate its federal component. **824–832 & nn.4–8.**

## Judgment and limits

The Court **affirmed the Sixth Circuit**, **817**. Stevens’s majority comprised Chief Justice Burger and Justices Powell, Rehnquist, and O’Connor; Brennan’s dissent comprised White, Marshall, and Blackmun. **805.** The decision denied removal on the asserted federal-question basis, without resolving tort liability or announcing a newly adjudicated absence of an FDCA private remedy.

The assignment summarizes the plaintiffs’ theory as per se negligence; the actual complaints, as quoted by the Court, alleged a rebuttable negligence presumption. The assignment’s subsequent discussion of circuit disagreement is editorial context. This brief describes the 1986 decision and does not certify the later or current boundaries of embedded-federal-question jurisdiction.
