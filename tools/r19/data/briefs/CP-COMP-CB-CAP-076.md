# Dusenbery v. United States

**534 U.S. 161 (2002) · Supreme Court of the United States · January 8, 2002 · Civil Procedure**

Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote for five Justices, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. Justice Ginsburg dissented, joined by Stevens, Souter, and Breyer. The Court affirmed the Sixth Circuit’s judgment that the notice of the **cash forfeiture** satisfied due process. The assigned account appears in *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 149, printed pages 247–248.

## Facts and procedural history

Federal officers arrested Larry Dean Dusenbery at a house trailer in Atwater, Ohio, in April 1986. A subsequent warranted search produced drugs, weapons, an automobile registered to his stepmother, and other property. Officers seized $21,939 in cash: $394 from Dusenbery, $7,500 from a coat, and $14,045 from a briefcase. Dusenbery pleaded guilty to possession with intent to distribute cocaine and received 12 years’ imprisonment followed by six years’ special parole. (163.)

In 1988, the FBI began administrative forfeiture of the cash and automobile. The then-applicable statutes permitted an agency to forfeit covered property worth no more than $100,000 without a judicial proceeding if no interested person filed a claim within the specified period after publication. The agency had to send written notice of the seizure and forfeiture procedures to each apparent interested person and publish for three successive weeks. (163–164.)

The FBI mailed certified cash-forfeiture notices to Dusenbery at the Federal Correctional Institution in Milan, Michigan, where he was imprisoned; to the residence where he had been arrested; and to an address in the town where his mother lived. It published in three consecutive Sunday editions of the *Cleveland Plain Dealer*. With no response in the allotted time, it declared the property forfeited and transferred the cash to the Marshals Service on December 13, 1988. Similar procedures were used for the automobile, but the cash notice was the issue ultimately reviewed here. (164, 167.)

Nearly five years later, Dusenbery sought return of the seized property under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 41(e). The district court denied the motion because a civil action, rather than an ancillary motion in the closed prosecution, was the appropriate vehicle. The Sixth Circuit vacated and remanded: the court should have construed the filing as a civil complaint seeking equitable relief on the notice challenge. On remand, discovery included testimony from prison mail officer James Lawson, who had signed the receipt for the FBI’s certified letter and described prison delivery practices. The district court granted the government summary judgment on the cash notice; the Sixth Circuit affirmed despite the lack of proof that the letter had actually reached Dusenbery. (165–166.)

## Question and holding

Was certified mail addressed to a federal prisoner at his institution, transmitted through the described prison delivery system, constitutionally adequate without proof of actual receipt?

The Court held that it was. *Mullane*’s reasonably-calculated-under-the-circumstances standard governed the adequacy of the method. Due process required a reasonable effort to inform Dusenbery, rather than a guarantee that he received the letter. The majority considered the postal and prison system shown in the record sufficient. The holding did not substitute publication alone for direct notice or decide whether the cash was substantively traceable to a drug transaction. (167–173.)

## Majority’s analysis

Dusenbery urged the three-factor *Mathews v. Eldridge* framework: private interest, risk of error and value of safeguards, and government burdens. The government instead urged *Mullane*. Rehnquist chose the latter, explaining that *Mathews* was not an all-purpose formula for every procedural due-process question and that the Court regularly used *Mullane* to assess how notice was given. The issue involved the Fifth Amendment because the federal government acted, although the notice principles had also developed in Fourteenth Amendment cases concerning states. (167–168.)

The government’s proof described a chain of delivery. Prison staff collected institutional mail from the city post office daily and signed for certified items. The mailroom entered those items in a logbook. A member of the inmate’s Unit Team signed to take the item from the mailroom, and a Unit Team member or other staff distributed it at mail call. Lawson testified that this was the procedure that would have been followed. The old logs were no longer available because they had then been retained only one year after closing; footnote 2 reported that the Bureau of Prisons subsequently adopted longer retention. The record did not prove Dusenbery’s personal receipt. (165–166 and n.2, 168–169.)

The majority distinguished direct notice **as a method** from actual notice **as receipt**. Earlier decisions accepting mail did not make proof of receipt indispensable. Its discussion of *Mennonite* read that decision as requiring efforts to provide actual notice, not a guaranteed outcome. The Court expressly clarified its own use of “actual notice” to mean receipt for purposes of answering Dusenbery’s argument. (169–170 and nn.4–5.)

Nor did federal custody require extraordinary measures to guarantee delivery. Dusenbery proposed having an employee observe him open the notice, obtain a signed receipt, and return it to the agency. Rehnquist regarded that principally as a means of proving receipt—a result the Constitution did not require. He also emphasized the difficulty of reconstructing actual delivery years later and the resulting uncertainty in property titles. The majority separately rejected the dissent’s argument that a signature system materially improved the reliability of delivery itself: it found no persuasive explanation or probative record evidence demonstrating that claimed improvement. (170–172 and n.6.)

The government had meanwhile improved its procedures. The majority did not consider later reforms proof that the prior system was unconstitutional. It analogized to the policy against treating subsequent remedial measures as admissions of earlier fault, without suggesting that every prior method would therefore be immune from review. On the system actually described, the FBI’s effort was reasonably calculated to inform Dusenbery. (172–173.)

## Ginsburg’s dissent

Ginsburg agreed that *Mullane* governed and that the dispute was not about ordinary postal delivery from the FBI to the prison. It concerned the next link: prison staff’s delivery to a prisoner whose location and movements the government controlled. Lawson could describe mailroom practices but knew little about what happened afterward, and no Unit Team employee supplied that missing evidence. The dissent therefore regarded the showing of reliable internal delivery as inadequate. (173–175.)

She also distinguished *Mullane*’s common-trust setting. A beneficiary who missed notice could benefit from another beneficiary’s objection on a shared issue, and a court reviewed the trustee’s accounting. Dusenbery’s claimed ownership stood alone; without notice he could not present his claim that the money was unconnected to drugs. Mail had to traverse two systems that he could neither supervise nor control. A trustworthy postal service did not itself establish trustworthy prison delivery. (175–180 and dissent nn.1–2.)

The dissent proposed a feasible safeguard, rather than an unconditional actual-receipt requirement. The later prison procedures required an inmate’s signature or documentation of refusal and treated forfeiture notices as special mail opened in the inmate’s presence. They demonstrated, in her view, that reliable proof and delivery incentives could be obtained with little added effort. Signatures would let the sender recognize failed delivery and follow up; making forfeiture depend on delivery documentation would encourage careful internal handling. That argument was about the relative reliability and practicability of methods, not automatic liability whenever a letter went astray. (180–183 and dissent nn.3–5.)

## Judgment and assigned-source discrepancies

The Court **affirmed** the Sixth Circuit. The five-to-four division concerned reasonable notice procedures; Ginsburg did not adopt Dusenbery’s argument that every proceeding required proved receipt. The judgment upheld the cash-notice method shown in this record, rather than declaring any letter addressed to any prison necessarily adequate. (171–173.)

The textbook describes approximately $30,000 and refers to a letter addressed where the stepmother lived. The report specifies **$21,939** and calls the third mailing destination the town where his **mother** lived; it separately identifies the car as registered to his stepmother. The brief retains those distinctions and the Court’s cash-only review scope. The reported 1988 practice and later reforms are historical facts described in this decision, not assertions about today’s Bureau of Prisons policies. (163–167; assigned physical 149.)
