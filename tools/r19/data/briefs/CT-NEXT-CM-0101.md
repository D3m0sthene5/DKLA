# Graham v. Scissor-Tail, Inc.
28 Cal. 3d 807, 171 Cal. Rptr. 604, 623 P.2d 165 (1981) | Contracts

## Court and the two appeals

The California Supreme Court issued an opinion by the court on February 5, 1981. Justice Mosk did not participate; no separate opinion appears. Graham appealed confirmation of an arbitration award, and Scissor-Tail appealed a later order striking its claimed attorney fees from costs. The court reversed the confirmation judgment, directed vacation of the order compelling arbitration before the musicians’ union, and prescribed further proceedings allowing a suitable substitute arbitrator. It dismissed the fee-order appeal as moot. Footnote 1 explains that the order compelling arbitration was not independently appealable, but could be reviewed through the confirmation appeal. 28 Cal. 3d at 811–812 & n.1, 831.

## The concert contracts and loss-allocation dispute

Bill Graham was an experienced concert promoter. Performer Leon Russell, legally C. Russell Bridges, belonged to the American Federation of Musicians and wholly owned Scissor-Tail, which marketed his group’s services. Forest acted as booking agent. In 1973, Russell’s manager Dennis Cordell arranged four concerts with Graham as part of a larger tour. All four used the union’s Form B. Graham signed them all; Cordell signed for Scissor-Tail only for Ontario and Oakland, scheduled July 29 and August 5, 1973. Id. at 812–813; casebook printed 662.

The form characterized Graham as employer or purchaser of music and listed the seven performers as musicians. Compensation was the greater of applicable union scale or a stated percentage of receipts after specified expenses and taxes: 85% for three venues and 90% for Long Island. Dates, performance hours, and guest-artist matters appeared in blanks. The contracts did not expressly allocate net losses. They required disputes connected with the engagement to be decided finally by the union’s executive board or an appropriate local board. Footnote 2 supplies additional California engagement provisions distinguishing union and local competence; the parties agreed the quoted Labor Commissioner notice provision did not apply here. Id. at 812–813 & n.2.

Ontario produced about $173,000 in receipts against $236,000 in expenses, a loss of roughly $63,000; Oakland generated about $98,000 net profit. Scissor-Tail and Forest claimed Graham bore losses without offset. Graham invoked an industry understanding that a promoter taking only a 10% or 15% profit share bore no loss risk and contended that the Russell interests should bear it. The dispute remained unresolved, Scissor-Tail refused to execute the other two contracts, and another promoter apparently serviced those concerts. Footnote 3 records Graham’s own threat to file union charges during negotiations over the dispute, relevant to his awareness of the arbitration term. Id. at 813–814 & n.3.

## Arbitration proceedings and the judgments below

Graham sued in October 1973 for breach, declaratory relief, and rescission. Following an initial arbitration order and reconsideration allowing discovery on agreement and revocation grounds, the trial court finally compelled arbitration in March 1976. Its findings rejected waiver and grounds for rescission and treated the pleaded disputes as arbitrable. Notes 4 and 5 reproduce the governing petition provision and findings. Id. at 814 & nn.4–5.

The union initially awarded the full claim of about $53,000 without the requested hearing. Graham protested; with opposing counsel’s consent, the matter reopened for hearing. Meanwhile he was put on a defaulter list apparently preventing union members from dealing with him. Scissor-Tail sought an additional $20,000 by challenging expenses, as well as interest and $15,000 in fees. The October hearing before a former union executive denied Graham’s request to have his reporter transcribe it. He nevertheless presented testimony, stipulated testimony, and sworn statements supporting the industry loss-allocation custom; Scissor-Tail offered no contrary custom evidence. Id. at 814–816.

The referee recommended the original approximately $53,000 and denied the added claim, noting the absence of union directions regarding it. The board issued that award in February 1977. Footnote 6 explains that the amount represented 85% of Oakland net receipts less an advance, after Graham apparently retained the net proceeds as an offset. Footnote 7 clarifies that Forest was the formal union claimant, while the opinion uses Scissor-Tail as shorthand for the Russell interests. The superior court confirmed the award and later struck an approximately $16,000 attorney-fee cost item. Footnote 8 reproduces findings that there had been no corruption, misconduct, actual bias, prejudice, or excess of arbitral power, while noting Graham knew the designated forum. The supreme court’s structural objection did not depend on overturning those findings with a new determination of actual corruption. Id. at 815–817 & nn.6–8.

## Adhesion despite a commercially experienced adherent

The court separated two inquiries: whether the contract was adhesive and whether its terms were unenforceable. Adhesion describes a standardized agreement imposed by superior bargaining strength with an opportunity to accept or reject rather than negotiate the relevant terms. It can arise in business dealings as well as consumer purchases. Footnote 13 expressly makes the commercial setting compatible with adhesion when the required characteristics are present; footnote 15 recognizes standardized contracts’ efficiency and reduced transaction costs. Their utility did not prevent examining the imposed provisions. Id. at 817–819 & nn.9–15.

Graham’s prominence did not give him practical freedom to remove this arbitration clause. Significant artists belonged to the union, its rules required its forms, and Scissor-Tail insisted on the percentage arrangements. Promoting these concerts required adherence to those forms and that forum. Negotiability of performance timing or guest-artist terms did not change the result because those terms were minor beside compensation and dispute resolution. The court allowed that bargaining over sufficiently significant other terms might overcome adhesive characteristics in another case; this record did not establish such accommodation. Id. at 818–819.

## Reasonable expectations did not invalidate the term

Adhesion is a starting classification, not a categorical invalidity rule. The opinion identifies two possible judicial limits: provisions outside the adherent’s reasonable expectations and provisions so oppressive or unconscionable in context that enforcement is improper even when expected. Note 16 distinguishes ambiguity construed against the drafter from enforceability. Note 18 treats notice as a significant expectations factor and explains that the court did not find this particular contract to have a public-service character. Notes 19 and 20 connect statutory unconscionability and broader public-policy limits without restricting the judicial concept to the single dispute presented. Id. at 819–821 & nn.16–20.

The union forum was within Graham’s reasonable expectations. He had signed thousands of similar contracts, promoted at least fifteen Russell concerts under similar provisions in the preceding three years, participated in prior union proceedings, and threatened to initiate union charges in this very dispute. Familiarity with an imposed term therefore defeated the unexpected-term objection. It did not answer whether the term itself imposed an unacceptable adjudicative structure. Id. at 821.

## Structural integrity rather than a universal neutrality rule

The Arbitration Act allowed considerable autonomy in selecting arbitrators, including arrangements with nonneutral participants. The court did not prohibit every known relationship or interest. It distinguished permissible variations from neutrality from naming a party, or a body whose interests were effectively identical to that party’s, as sole adjudicator. Drawing on Cross & Brown, the court reasoned that the latter yields the power of decision to the adverse interest and becomes an illusory substitute for genuine adjudication. Adhesive circumstances required especially careful scrutiny of whether the weaker party retained a realistic, fair opportunity to prevail. Id. at 821–825.

The opinion examined Federico’s contrary approval of a nonneutral arbitrator, intraorganizational realtor arbitration, and stock-exchange cases whose procedural safeguards and other grounds differed. It ultimately disapproved Federico to the extent inconsistent with its holding. Notes 21 and 22 qualify the treatise’s government-contract analogy and report changes in exchange arbitration rules; the court did not base this private arrangement’s validity on treating all those institutional contexts alike. Id. at 822–824, 828 & nn.21–22.

A distinct possible ground was arbitral procedure that essentially foreclosed a fair opportunity to present a position. Footnote 23 reserves prearbitration refusal on that basis for the clearest cases; ordinarily arbitration could proceed and actual procedural deprivation could be challenged on a later vacation petition. Crucially, the court found nothing establishing that the union’s rules denied a fair presentation before decision. Footnote 24 notes both parties’ consent to written evidence and waiver of oral testimony and argument; it found no unequal right to a hearing for members alone. The initial award without hearing and later reporter refusal did not become a judicial holding that these procedures themselves established the decisive ground. Id. at 825–827 & nn.23–24.

The decisive problem was the union’s identity and institutional function. A labor union seeks favorable wages and employment conditions for members and naturally shares their interest in a compensation dispute against a third party. Designating that union as sole adjudicator therefore failed the minimum integrity demanded of contractual substitutes for courts. The court regarded this defect as grounds for denial even apart from adhesion, with adhesion making scrutiny particularly necessary here. Id. at 827–828.

## The federal labor-law argument and the remedy

Scissor-Tail argued that the form was effectively a collective bargaining agreement within Labor Management Relations Act §301 and that federal labor law required union arbitration. The court doubted the premise: the employer label might not reflect the relationship, the union did not sign or negotiate, and the booking-agency caption and nonmember corporate officer’s signature differed from the district-court cases invoked. It did not rest entirely on those distinctions. Even assuming §301 applied, it found no persuasive controlling federal rule requiring this illusory forum and declined to treat unpersuasive lower federal decisions as binding on the state court. It assumed applicable federal law did not require a different result pending further development. Id. at 828–831.

The parties had still agreed to arbitrate. On remand they were to receive a reasonable opportunity to agree on a suitable arbitrator; if they failed, either could petition the court to appoint one. Only in the absence of agreement or a petition for appointment would the court proceed to judicial determination. Thus the remedy removed the disabled sole forum while preserving an opportunity for arbitration; it neither imposed immediate merits trial nor canceled every contractual obligation. The confirmation judgment was reversed, the compulsion order was to be vacated, and the fee appeal was dismissed as moot. Id. at 831.
