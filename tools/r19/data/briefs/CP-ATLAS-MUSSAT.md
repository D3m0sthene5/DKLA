# Mussat v. IQVIA, Inc.

**953 F.3d 441 (7th Cir. 2020) — decided March 11, 2020.** Chief Judge Wood wrote for the panel of Wood, Kanne, and Barrett. There is no separate opinion. Assigned Civil Procedure source: physical PDF pages 454–455, printed pages 884–886. Pinpoints below refer to the court-issued opinion's twelve slip pages, rather than an independently verified mapping to the Federal Reporter.

**Central holding.** A nationwide Rule 23 action under a federal statute does not require a separate showing of specific personal jurisdiction over the defendant for each absent member's claim. The named representative must establish personal jurisdiction, but *Bristol-Myers Squibb*'s treatment of individually joined plaintiffs does not convert every unnamed class member into a named litigant. The Seventh Circuit also held that striking the nationwide portion of the proposed class was functionally a partial denial of certification, reviewable through Rule 23(f). It reversed and remanded; it did not certify the class or decide TCPA liability. (Slip 2–5, 9–12.)

## Facts and procedural sequence

Florence Mussat was an Illinois physician operating through a professional services corporation. She received two unsolicited faxes from IQVIA, a Delaware corporation headquartered in Pennsylvania, that lacked the opt-out notice required by federal statute. The corporate plaintiff sued in the Northern District of Illinois under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, 47 U.S.C. § 227. It proposed to represent itself and people throughout the country who had received similar IQVIA junk faxes during the preceding four years. The opinion uses “Mussat” as shorthand for the corporation; the physician's name in the caption should not obscure who brought the action. (Slip 1–2.)

IQVIA moved to strike the nationwide class definition. It was not subject to general jurisdiction in Illinois. Invoking *Bristol-Myers Squibb*, it argued that the court also lacked specific jurisdiction for unnamed members injured outside Illinois. The district court agreed that each class member's claim needed minimum contacts between IQVIA and Illinois and struck the non-Illinois portion of the proposed class. An Illinois-only class remained possible. Mussat obtained permission to appeal under Rule 23(f), and IQVIA challenged the appellate court's authority to review the order as well as its substantive correctness. (Slip 2–3.)

## Why an interlocutory appeal was available

Rule 23(f) permits a discretionary appeal from an order granting or denying class certification. IQVIA emphasized that the district judge had granted a Rule 12 motion to strike, rather than decided a motion labeled “class certification.” It also argued that no complete denial had occurred because Mussat could still seek an Illinois class. Ordinarily an interlocutory ruling must await a final judgment, so this jurisdictional objection had to be addressed first. Review of the jurisdictional question was de novo. (Slip 2–3.)

The panel looked at the order's effect. Its earlier *Bemis* decision treated a ruling that resolved a dispute about representative litigation as the functional equivalent of a certification ruling. The Supreme Court's *Microsoft v. Baker* had endorsed that approach to an order striking class allegations. Here, the district court eliminated any possibility of certifying the nationwide class Mussat actually sought. Prior circuit decisions likewise recognized appeals from orders denying certification for part of a proposed class or materially changing its definition. Leaving a smaller class available did not eliminate the denial concerning everyone excluded. The panel therefore reaffirmed its Rule 23(f) permission rather than requiring a final judgment. This reasoning concerns an order functionally resolving certification, not every preliminary ruling bearing on a class action. (Slip 3–5.)

## The competing personal-jurisdiction positions

IQVIA advanced two related theories. First, *Bristol-Myers* required a connection between the forum and each plaintiff's claim, making a nationwide class impossible in a state where IQVIA lacked general jurisdiction. Second, Rule 4(k), which ordinarily links a federal district court's personal jurisdiction to that of the forum state's courts, imposed the same restriction independently. Mussat's position rested on the difference between a Rule 23 representative action and consolidated suits in which each claimant is a named party. The panel treated that difference as legally important, rather than merely an administrative way of combining claims. (Slip 5–9.)

In *Bristol-Myers*, individual plaintiffs sued over injuries allegedly caused by Plavix. Nonresidents had not acquired, used, or suffered injury from that drug in California. The Supreme Court rejected specific jurisdiction over their claims in the California mass action. But those individuals were named parties asserting their own claims. They were not unnamed members represented through a certified Rule 23 class. Accordingly, *Bristol-Myers* did not itself decide whether every absentee in a federal class action needed a separate forum connection. (Slip 6–9.)

The panel observed that nationwide classes had long been entertained without examining each absent member's contacts with the forum. Its references to *Wal-Mart*, *Shutts*, and *Yamasaki* illustrated that established practice; they were not portrayed as Supreme Court decisions resolving this precise post-*Bristol-Myers* question. The proposed change would sharply reduce nationwide classes whenever a defendant lacked general jurisdiction in the chosen forum. That consequence required attention to the procedural difference the district court had overlooked. (Slip 5–8.)

## Why representative litigation changes the analysis

Rule 23 requires representatives to satisfy all four requirements of subdivision (a) and one branch of subdivision (b). These safeguards are prerequisites to representing and binding absent members. The panel used *Taylor v. Sturgell*'s rejection of free-form “virtual representation” to show why a certified class cannot simply be equated with informal aggregation or ordinary joinder. Representative litigation carries its own procedural protections. (Slip 8–9.)

Nor does the word “party” have one invariant meaning. Under *Devlin*, unnamed members can be parties for some procedural purposes and not others. Their citizenship ordinarily does not defeat complete diversity; supplemental jurisdiction can encompass their claims when the named representative satisfies the amount requirement; and venue need not be independently established for every absentee. The Seventh Circuit reasoned that personal jurisdiction should follow the same representative focus: the named plaintiffs must establish general or specific jurisdiction over the defendant, while each unnamed member need not independently do so. This is a ruling about the defendant's amenability to suit on class claims, not an assertion that absent members have no legally protected interests or no obligations under other procedural doctrines. (Slip 9–10.)

## Why Rule 4(k) did not require a different outcome

IQVIA read Rule 4(k) as an independent obstacle whenever Illinois courts could not hear an absent member's individually filed claim. The panel answered that this conflated service and jurisdiction with the identity of those whose claims the representative may assert. Rule 4(k) addresses how and where process establishes personal jurisdiction; it does not decide whom a representative action must treat as a separately appearing litigant. Once the defendant is subject to personal jurisdiction with respect to the class representative's claim, the representative action may proceed without separately establishing jurisdiction for each absentee. Rule 17's provision for executors, administrators, guardians, and trustees illustrated the broader availability of litigation through representatives. The panel also noted the tension with Rule 82's instruction that the Rules do not extend or limit district-court jurisdiction or venue. (Slip 10–11.)

Rule 23(b)(3)'s forum-concentration factor reinforced this approach. Its Committee Note expressly contemplates concentrating claims instead of leaving them to separate suits in their ordinary forums. The structure permits a class to cross state boundaries even where the defendant is not subject to general jurisdiction. Certification requirements still matter; this jurisdictional ruling did not dispense with them. (Slip 11–12.)

## Judgment, reservations, and assigned-source cautions

The unanimous panel reversed the district court's judgment and remanded for further proceedings. It rejected both IQVIA's *Bristol-Myers* theory and its alternative Rule 4(k) argument. Neither class certification nor liability was finally adjudicated. (Slip 12.)

The court also carefully identified *Bristol-Myers*'s unresolved boundaries. That Supreme Court decision reserved whether the Fifth Amendment imposes the same personal-jurisdiction restrictions in federal court as the Fourteenth Amendment does in state court. Sotomayor's dissent separately noted that the Court had not addressed class actions. Mussat relied on those limits and the representative structure of Rule 23; it should not be rewritten as a Supreme Court resolution of either reserved question. The panel also noted that the district court had invoked the Fourteenth Amendment even though the federal court's relevant due-process provision was the Fifth, without making that labeling error dispositive. (Slip 8, 12.)
