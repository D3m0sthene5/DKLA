# Krell v. Henry
[1903] 2 K.B. 740 (Court of Appeal, August 11, 1903) | Contracts

## Material facts and chronology
Krell owned rooms at 56A Pall Mall, London, overlooking the announced route of the royal coronation processions. An advertisement at the premises offered windows from which to view the processions. Henry approached the housekeeper because of that advertisement and was told that the rooms were available for viewing the processions during the daytime of June 26 and 27, 1902, without overnight accommodation. Two letters dated June 20 recorded an agreement for those days at £75: £25 paid immediately and £50 payable on June 24. The letters did not expressly mention the coronation. The agreed affidavits nevertheless established that both parties understood the viewing occasion as the basis of the transaction. The King's serious illness caused postponement of the coronation and cancellation of the processions on the specified days. Cancellation was publicly announced early on June 24. The rooms still existed and could physically be occupied. Henry refused to pay the balance. (740–743, 750–752, 754–755.)

## Proceedings and competing positions
Krell sued for £50; Henry counterclaimed for repayment of his £25 deposit. Darling J. decided against Krell and allowed the counterclaim. Krell appealed. During the appeal, Henry withdrew his counterclaim, leaving only the unpaid balance in issue. The appellate judgment therefore does not decide whether Henry could recover his deposit. (740–745.)

Krell maintained that this was an unconditional agreement to provide rooms: the rooms remained available, the letters contained no coronation condition, and Henry's disappointed purpose could not alter his promise to pay. He argued that payment became due on June 24, before the days of occupation, and invoked decisions preserving rights accrued before a frustrating event. Henry answered that the rooms' peculiar position and their advertisement for viewing the processions made the occurrence of the processions a common foundation, although the written terms did not say so expressly. Their disappearance excused the remaining obligation under the principle of Taylor v. Caldwell. (741–747, 754–755.)

## Issue, rule, and holding
The question was whether cancellation of the processions discharged Henry's obligation to pay the remaining £50, despite the continued existence of the rooms and the absence of an express condition. Vaughan Williams L.J. held that the principle associated with Taylor v. Caldwell extends beyond physical destruction of an identified thing. The court must ascertain the substance of the transaction from its terms and, where necessary, surrounding circumstances recognized by both parties. An assumed state of things may form its foundation and qualify otherwise general contractual language. (747–751.)

Vaughan Williams framed three inquiries: what was the foundation of the contract; whether performance of the substantial contract was prevented; and whether the event preventing it was of a kind that could not reasonably have been contemplated when the parties contracted. On this agreement and its established circumstances, all three favored discharge. The processions on the announced days and route were the foundation of the undertaking. Their cancellation meant that the contemplated transaction could not occur, even though providing ordinary access to the rooms remained physically possible. Henry was not liable for the balance. (750–753.)

## Reasoning and limits of the analogy
The daytime restriction, the windows' location, the advertisement, and the parties' agreed account of the negotiations showed a common basis beyond Henry's private motive. Using surrounding evidence to identify that basis did not simply replace the agreement with a unilateral purpose. The rooms were selected because they had the particular suitability required for this occasion. General words requiring payment were understood against that shared premise and did not allocate the risk of the unexpected cancellation to Henry. Vaughan Williams distinguished the inquiry from an implied warranty such as that discussed in The Moorcock: this case concerned a condition limiting the undertaking when its foundation failed. (747–753.)

The court's cab-to-Epsom hypothetical illustrates the limit. A person might hire a cab at an enhanced price to attend the Derby, yet cancellation of the race would not ordinarily excuse the cab contract. Any suitable cab could perform the promised transport; the race would generally be the passenger's purpose rather than the common foundation of that transport bargain. Here the particular rooms' position and the understood viewing arrangement made the processions essential to the substantial transaction. Mere loss of the expected usefulness of an ordinary service does not automatically establish the same condition. (751–753.)

The timing argument also failed. Cancellation had been announced early on June 24, while the contract allowed payment during that day. No cause of action for nonpayment had accrued before the frustrating event merely because June 24 was the payment date. The court could therefore resolve the outstanding £50 without deciding a general right to recover money already paid. (754–755.)

## Judgment and separate opinions
The Court of Appeal dismissed Krell's appeal. Vaughan Williams L.J. supplied the principal reasons. Romer L.J. concurred but expressly acknowledged difficulty, particularly with the conclusion that the unexpected event was outside the reasonable contemplation of the parties and did not fall within a risk Henry had undertaken. He accepted the common-foundation analysis and the agreed facts rather than treating the outcome as obvious. Stirling L.J. agreed with Vaughan Williams while also describing the case as difficult. The three judges agreed in the disposition; no dissent is reported. (754–755.)
