# Hoffman v. Red Owl Stores, Inc.
26 Wis. 2d 683, 133 N.W.2d 267 (1965) | Contracts

## Decision and doctrinal setting
The Wisconsin Supreme Court recognized promissory estoppel under Restatement (First) of Contracts § 90 and sustained liability for promises that induced substantial detrimental reliance during franchise negotiations. The promises did not need to contain every term necessary for an enforceable franchise agreement. Relief was shaped to prevent injustice, rather than automatically giving the plaintiffs the value of a completed franchise bargain. Chief Justice Currie wrote the court's opinion; no concurrence or dissent is reproduced. The opinion does not supply a numerical vote alignment. 26 Wis. 2d at 693–699.

The court affirmed the trial court's order, including a new trial confined to damages arising from the sale of the plaintiffs' grocery-store inventory and fixtures. It did not affirm the jury's entire $16,735 award for that sale. ## Negotiations, assurances, and reliance
Joseph Hoffman and his wife operated a bakery in Wautoma, Wisconsin. Beginning in 1959, Hoffman discussed obtaining a Red Owl grocery franchise and explained that he had only $18,000 to invest. Red Owl's representative, Lukowitz, repeatedly assured him that this would suffice. The assigned editorial introduction supplies the detailed sequence omitted from the published opinion's short factual discussion: in February 1961 Hoffman bought a small grocery store to gain experience; in June, on Lukowitz's advice, he sold it despite a profitable start and the approaching summer tourist season. He was told that Red Owl would establish him in a larger store. C&M at 328; 26 Wis. 2d at 696–697.

In September, on Red Owl's advice, Hoffman paid $1,000 toward a Chilton lot chosen for the new venture. Lukowitz said preparations were ready and told him to gather the money. Red Owl then urged sale of the bakery building, telling him this was the last necessary step. The building sold in November at a $2,000 loss. Hoffman rented a home in Chilton for one month at $125, but the family ultimately moved to Neenah after Red Owl suggested he gain further experience in a store in the Fox River Valley. That anticipated job did not materialize; he worked nights at a bakery in Appleton and remained available for Red Owl work. Moving cost $140. C&M at 328–329; 26 Wis. 2d at 697, 699–700.

Red Owl's financing requirements kept increasing. The original $18,000 figure became $24,100; Hoffman was later told another $2,000 would permit the deal to proceed. The parties considered financing from his father-in-law, including a partnership, but Red Owl insisted on an outright gift or subordinated funds. The assigned introduction describes a final proposed statement calling for $34,000, including $13,000 from the father-in-law as a gift, which Hoffman refused. Talks collapsed without agreement on the building's size, cost, design, and layout or important lease provisions concerning rent, maintenance, renewal, and purchase. The brief distinguishes the casebook's extended narrative from the court's actual findings and legal analysis; neither establishes an executed franchise contract. C&M at 328–329; 26 Wis. 2d at 697–698.

## Trial and appellate issues
The Hoffmans sued Red Owl and its representative. A special verdict found the necessary representations, reliance in the exercise of ordinary care, and fulfillment of the conditions demanded of Hoffman during negotiations. The jury assessed $16,735 for the grocery sale, $2,000 for the bakery loss, $1,000 for the lot payment, $140 moving expenses, and $125 rent. The trial court upheld the latter four items but ordered a new trial on the grocery-sale damages. Red Owl appealed liability and all damage items; the Hoffmans cross-appealed the partial new-trial order. C&M at 328–329; 26 Wis. 2d at 693, 697, 699–703.

The appellate questions were whether Wisconsin should recognize § 90 as an affirmative basis for relief, whether it could apply despite incomplete negotiations, and whether the damage awards were supported. Red Owl's central legal defense treated an insufficiently definite prospective bargain as insufficient for promissory estoppel as well. Its damage defenses also challenged the wife's ability to recover, the continuing value or avoidability of the lot expenditure, and the other claimed losses.

## Why liability could exist without a completed bargain
Wisconsin had approached § 90 in earlier cases without squarely adopting it. The court now endorsed it as a needed means of preventing injustice from induced reliance. Fraud was not an adequate alternative theory here. A promise of future performance supports fraud only if made with a present intention not to perform; the record did not support a finding that Lukowitz made the promises in bad faith with such an intention. A broken promise can produce actionable reliance without satisfying fraud's mental-state requirement. 26 Wis. 2d at 694–696.

Under the version of § 90 adopted, the promise must foreseeably induce action or forbearance of a definite and substantial character, actually induce it, and require enforcement to prevent injustice. The first two questions ordinarily belong to the jury. The third calls for a judicial policy judgment, including discretion about the remedy. The evidence supported the jury's findings that Red Owl made actionable assurances, that Hoffman reasonably relied, and that he fulfilled the conditions imposed during negotiations. Id. at 696–699.

The court rejected the assertion that the promise must be as comprehensive as an offer that would form a bargain upon acceptance. Earlier uses of promissory estoppel often supplied consideration for an otherwise complete gratuitous promise. But § 90 did not limit the doctrine to that role. Treating it as identical to breach of contract would deny relief precisely because details of the proposed franchise remained unresolved. Here the operative assurances induced specific sales and expenditures before a final agreement, and denying all relief would leave that induced detriment uncompensated. Id. at 697–699.

This reasoning does not make all disappointing negotiations actionable. The court relied on proved promises, foreseeable and actual reliance, and its judgment that injustice required some relief. Its repeated insistence on remedial discretion prevents a simple rule that any unconsummated negotiation generates full expectation damages.

## Why particular damages survived and the largest item did not
The $2,000 bakery loss was supported. Red Owl argued that half belonged to Mrs. Hoffman, who had not personally negotiated with it. Although promissory estoppel ordinarily protects the promisee, Red Owl had actually foreseen and requested sale of her jointly owned interest. Excluding her loss would disregard the reliance it sought. Id. at 699.

The $1,000 lot expenditure was not merely an option fee: the court explained that Hoffman paid it toward the price after exercising an option and acquired an equity in the lot. Red Owl argued the payment had already been lost because the remaining $5,000 was overdue, or that Hoffman should have paid the balance because the lot might have been worth $6,000. The record showed no foreclosure of his equity, and preventing the $1,000 loss did not reasonably require committing another $5,000 after the franchise plan failed. Id. at 699–700.

The $125 Chilton rent followed assurances that selling the bakery would complete preparations, and the $140 move would not have occurred but for the induced bakery sale and relocation plan. These were proper reliance losses. The court did not treat the rent as proof that the family actually moved to Chilton; it expressly explained the different move to Neenah. Id. at 700.

The grocery-sale award lacked adequate proof. The trial court allowed the difference between fair market value and sale proceeds, including goodwill attributable to a going business, but there was no direct evidence of that market value. Hoffman bought $9,000 of inventory, added $1,500, and sold it for $10,000; equipment purchased for $7,000 sold for $7,955.96 according to his tax return. The buyer's evidence of $44,000 in sales and $6,000 gross profit during eleven weeks was not reliable proof of a $16,735 compensable loss. Anticipated summer profits were not recoverable as though a franchise contract had been breached. Profits could inform an expert valuation of goodwill, but that required a valuation foundation. Id. at 700–701.

The grocery was purchased as a temporary experiment to gain experience, with a larger location already contemplated. Justice did not require compensating every profit Hoffman might have earned by keeping it. The appropriate loss was the proven fair-market-value shortfall resulting from the induced sale. Promissory estoppel remedies must fit the injustice rather than mechanically import all contract damages. Id. at 701–703.

## Judgment and source limitations
The order was affirmed, leaving the four sustained amounts intact and the grocery-sale damages for a new trial. Because of the cross-appeal, the plaintiffs could tax only two-thirds of their costs. The reproduced notation states that rehearing was denied with costs on April 27, 1965; this is not a later substantive holding. Id. at 703.
