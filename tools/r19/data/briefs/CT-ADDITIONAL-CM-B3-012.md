# Green v. Continental Rentals
292 N.J. Super. 241, 678 A.2d 759 (Law Div. 1994) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and judgment
Judge Alterman of the New Jersey Superior Court, Law Division, Passaic County, decided Green on March 25, 1994. These consolidated damages actions presented cross-motions for partial summary judgment, with the state Attorney General intervening. The court granted the consumers' and intervenor's motions, holding that the challenged agreements were security transactions subject to TILA, RISA, and the UCC, and violated the Consumer Fraud Act. It denied Continental's motion directed to common-law fraud because fraudulent intent remained genuinely disputed. No final damages amount, appellate affirmance, jury finding of intent, footnote, or separate opinion appears. Some online labels call this appellate, but the original caption confirms Law Division. (292 N.J. Super. at 241, 245, 257.)

## Transactions and repossession
Continental supplied furniture and appliances through agreements styled as short-term leases. Each renewed when the customer paid the next periodic charge. Completion of a specified series of payments transferred ownership without further consideration. A customer could end the arrangement by returning the goods and paying rental charges then due; Continental could terminate upon nonrenewal. The customer bore loss, theft, and destruction risks. Early purchase credited forty percent of prior payments toward the price. Although the form allowed departure after a short term, a consumer who departed before completion lost the equity accumulated toward ownership. (Id. at 245–246.)

Green entered five agreements in 1988–1989 for living-room furniture, end tables, a stereo and freezer, a washing machine, and a dinette and hutch. Payment schedules varied among monthly, biweekly, and weekly installments. She paid from April 1988 through August 1989. Continental sometimes accepted late payments at a dollar-per-day charge and spread partial payments among the agreements. After default, it repossessed all the items on August 28, 1989. Levine separately contracted for a washer and dryer through seventy-eight weekly payments of $23.95. She defaulted after forty-three payments, and Continental repossessed those goods. (Id. at 246–247.)

## Credit-sale classification and competing interpretations
The TILA definition included certain leases in which a lessee contracts to pay an amount substantially equal to or exceeding the property's aggregate value and becomes its owner for no or nominal further consideration. Courts differed over the requirement to “contract to pay.” One line treated a consumer's ability to terminate after a week as decisive: the only binding payment commitment was the initial rent, so there was no credit sale. Another focused on the transaction's substance, continued ownership incentive, and forfeiture of accumulated equity. Green examined both lines rather than treating the issue as settled nationally. (Id. at 247–250.)

An amendment to Regulation Z excluding leases terminable without penalty did not end that disagreement. Some courts understood penalty as an additional amount charged to terminate; others included forfeiture of accrued equity. This court adopted the latter understanding, identifying the forty-percent early-purchase credit as the consumers' equity. It considered Federal Reserve interpretations entitled to substantial deference but not binding in the manner asserted by the seller. That reasoning belongs to this 1994 trial-court stage; it is not a certification of present federal regulatory law. (Id. at 248–250.)

The New Jersey Retail Installment Sales Act presented a parallel “agrees to pay” question. Other states' outcomes differed, partly with statutory wording. New Jersey provisions coordinating TILA and RISA supported similar interpretation where feasible. The court relied on their remedial purposes: meaningful credit-cost disclosure, avoidance of uninformed or excessive credit use, and protection against overreaching. Looking through the lease label, it found an economic sale: the customer's object and incentive were eventual ownership. Both statutes therefore applied. (Id. at 250–253.)

## The UCC's separate classification rule
The UCC analysis did not depend on resolving every TILA ambiguity. Under the then-quoted New Jersey § 1-201(37), a lease option alone did not necessarily create security, but a provision granting ownership on compliance for no additional or nominal consideration did. These agreements expressly supplied that ownership transition. Their language therefore established security transactions as a matter of law. The court distinguished cases requiring a broader factual inquiry where this specific statutory condition was absent. It treated contract language rather than unexpressed subjective intention as decisive here. (Id. at 253–254.)

## Unconscionable practices, price, and equity
The Consumer Fraud Act included rental transactions and prohibited unconscionable commercial practices as well as intentional deception. Actual deceit or fraudulent intent was not essential to the unconscionability ground. The court emphasized these financially constrained consumers' minimal bargaining power and the unfairness of the arrangement, rather than making a finding that they had signed unknowingly. (Id. at 255–256.)

Green paid $3,335.86 against combined cash prices of $2,785.12 and would have paid $6,663.85 if she completed every schedule. Yet Continental repossessed all the property. The court explained that statutory first-in, first-out allocation, rather than dispersing payments across accounts, would have prevented her losing everything. Continental derived total installments by multiplying wholesale cost by 3.5, and cash price by taking forty percent of that product. The difference was called the cost of leased services, although no such services existed. The court characterized that difference as interest. (Id. at 256.)

The seller's right to require payment as due or return of the goods constituted forbearance in the transaction's substance, notwithstanding the short renewal form. The effective rates substantially exceeded the thirty-percent annual statutory limit identified by the court and constituted an unconscionable practice. This was statutory consumer-fraud liability; it did not itself resolve the separate allegation that particular statements had been made with an intent to deceive. (Id. at 256–257.)

## Consequences and assigned treatment
The resulting partial judgment established statutory coverage and Consumer Fraud Act violation, while leaving common-law intent for further litigation. Plaintiffs were directed to submit an order on five days' notice. The report does not assess a particular recovery, return the property by express order, or describe subsequent proceedings. (Id. at 257.)
