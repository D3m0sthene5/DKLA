# McCormick v. Union Pacific Railroad Co.
983 P.2d 84 (Colo. App. 1998) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result

The Colorado Court of Appeals, Division V, decided this quiet-title appeal December 10, 1998. Judge Taubman wrote, and Judges Marquez and Briggs concurred without separate writings. The court affirmed summary judgment for the railroad-related and other defendants, holding that the mineral reservations in these deeds included oil and gas. The report records rehearing denied February 25, 1999, and certiorari granted September 13, 1999. This brief addresses the 1998 appellate opinion; it does not convert that later grant into either an affirmance or reversal by the Colorado Supreme Court. (84–85, 88.)

## The reservations and the landowners' claim

Between 1906 and 1909 Union Pacific Railroad conveyed land to predecessors of the plaintiff farming-property owners. Most deeds reserved all coal and other minerals within or underlying the land; deeds covering the Kunzman and Ritchey properties instead expressly listed oil, coal, and other minerals. Union Pacific Resources succeeded to the railroad's interest, while the plaintiffs succeeded to the conveyed properties. The plaintiffs sued to quiet title to the various estates. The trial court concluded they owned the surface and that the reservations retained valuable nonsurface substances, including oil and gas. (85–86.)

The landowners argued that the term minerals was ambiguous as a matter of law and that a trial should permit extrinsic evidence of the original parties' intent. They conceded no material factual dispute. The Court of Appeals treated ambiguity as a legal question and explained that disagreement over meaning alone did not create it. Therefore summary judgment was procedurally proper if the reservations were unambiguous. The question whether a minerals reservation included oil and gas was one of first impression in Colorado, although existing decisions addressed related questions. (86.)

## Mineral-estate severance and the surface distinction

A deed reservation could sever a fee-simple mineral estate from the surface; the surface grantee would then have no title to minerals retained in the reserved estate. The landowners correctly observed that the cited severance cases did not directly define minerals, but the court considered them useful background rather than mischaracterizing them as identical holdings. Other Colorado cases had assumed that oil and gas were minerals. (86–87.)

The court distinguished decisions finding ambiguity concerning surface substances. Where sand and gravel constituted the entire conveyed surface, treating the reservation as retaining them could nullify the surface grant: the deed would both grant and reserve the disputed property. Similar concerns attended other surface-material disputes. The reservations here did not both convey and retain the property's entirety, and the question concerned subsurface oil and gas. Thus a contextual difficulty involving surface substances did not make minerals inherently ambiguous for every reservation. (86–87.)

## Comparative decisions and historical meaning

The Tenth Circuit had construed the same railroad reservation language under Wyoming and Utah law as unambiguously including oil and gas. Its Wyoming analysis emphasized the historical treatment of those substances as minerals in statutes, constitutional provisions, and decisions. Its Utah analysis adopted the majority rule where the instrument lacked additional ambiguity-producing language. The Colorado court considered these decisions and similar holdings from other states, while recognizing a minority approach treating mineral reservations as ambiguous. It chose the majority approach for the deeds before it rather than importing a Wyoming or Utah judgment as binding Colorado authority. (87.)

Early Colorado enactments supplied local historical support. An 1887 law authorized state-land leases for coal, oil, gas, or other minerals; an 1895 law funded prospecting for mineral oils. A 1903 enactment gave the Mineral Department responsibility for mines producing oil or other mineral products, and the 1907 Geological Survey enactment included oil and gas among economic mineral resources. These usages supported inclusion at the period of the railroad conveyances. Treatise material likewise identified a substantial majority treating an unqualified mineral reservation as including oil and gas, subject to limiting language in the instrument. The court acknowledged other interpretive approaches rather than claiming unanimous national agreement. (87–88.)

## Holding and limits

The court held both the coal-and-other-minerals reservation and the oil-coal-and-other-minerals reservation unambiguously included oil and gas. It expressly qualified its conclusion as applicable at least absent an allegation that exercise of the mineral-estate rights unduly affected the surface estate. Because defendants disclaimed rights to surface minerals and water, the court did not decide the plaintiffs' contentions concerning those substances. Summary judgment was affirmed; there was no trial remand, damages award, or general holding transferring every substance on the land to the mineral owners. (88.)
