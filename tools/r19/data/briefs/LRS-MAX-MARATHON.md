# Marathon Oil Co. v. Environmental Protection Agency
564 F.2d 1253 (9th Cir. 1977), decided November 21, 1977 | Legislation and the Regulatory State | Formal adjudication, the agency record, and pollution-permit limits

## The platforms, waste streams, and statutory standard

Marathon, Union, Atlantic Richfield, Mobil, and Shell challenged EPA permits governing oil and gas operations in Alaska's Cook Inlet. Two consolidated cases concerned offshore platforms, whose drilling and related operations produced deck drainage, water accompanying recovered oil and gas, and sanitary waste. Another concerned three onshore facilities separating crude oil and gas from the produced water pumped ashore. At the time, all platforms sent produced water to shore, although offshore discharge was expected eventually; some discharged deck drainage offshore. The different waste streams and operations mattered because attainable pollution control depended on what was being treated and under what conditions. OpenJurist judicial reproduction, ¶¶ 1, 4–7 & nn. 3–6.

The Federal Water Pollution Control Act prohibited pollutant discharge without a permit. Section 402 authorized permits incorporating section 301's pollution-control requirements. The relevant technological standard was the best practicable control technology currently available, abbreviated BPCTCA in the opinion. The agency had to identify performance attainable by exemplary use of that technology, rather than impose a level beyond the statutory standard. State certification also formed part of the permitting process; Alaska ultimately waived its certification right. Id. ¶¶ 8–10, 19, 25.

## The administrative proceedings and the disputed choices

The companies originally sought permits from the Army Corps of Engineers. Before those applications were decided, the 1972 Act transferred the governing system to EPA. They applied under section 402 in 1973. The Seattle Regional Administrator circulated draft permits, conducted informal hearings, and tentatively issued limits. The applicants then obtained adjudicatory hearings before an administrative law judge, including cross-examination; Shell received a separate offshore hearing. After the judge certified the records, the Regional Administrator made initial decisions, and the companies obtained further review by the Administrator. Id. ¶¶ 13–24.

The Administrator's September 1975 decisions relaxed some numerical limits but rejected formal exceptions for unavoidable upsets and requests for broader bypass provisions during equipment maintenance. The final offshore deck-drainage concentration limits were 65/90 milligrams per liter, with an alternative daily-poundage limit. Produced-water limits were 48/72 milligrams per liter for offshore and onshore facilities. The applicants sought judicial review, arguing that the standards and procedures did not comply with the Act and the APA. Id. ¶¶ 18–25.

They challenged more than stringent numbers. They disputed which facilities and operating conditions EPA used as its statistical baseline, the use of undisclosed data and an internal statistical memorandum, and permits that appeared to demand perfect compliance although the agency's own model predicted unavoidable excursions. They also objected to the Regional Administrator's deciding a matter on which he had already issued draft permits. Id. ¶¶ 28–29, 44–48, 53–54.

## Why the formal APA provisions applied

Judge Sneed's opinion held that sections 554, 556, and 557 governed these adjudications, even though section 402 required an opportunity for a public hearing without expressly saying that decisions must be made on the record. EPA admitted permitting was adjudication under the APA but argued that the missing words defeated the formal-hearing requirement. The court rejected that reading. Id. ¶¶ 31–38.

Individual permits involved disputed facts about practicable control for particular dischargers. That was different from formulating prospective, industry-wide guidelines. Adversarial procedures could improve the agency's factual determination and permit meaningful review. The provision for judicial review in the courts of appeals supported an implied requirement of decision on a record. More broadly, the court read the APA's terminology and legislative history as distinguishing proceedings warranting formal safeguards from business-like administration, rather than demanding a special verbal formula from each later Congress. Id. ¶¶ 31–38 & nn. 26–33.

This was a statutory interpretation grounded in the hearing requirement, statutory context, and character of this permitting process. The court acknowledged that no hearing requirement, or evidence of contrary congressional intent, could support a different result. It distinguished the Supreme Court's treatment of hearing language in legislative rulemaking. The court also rejected the Seventh Circuit's alternative view that section 558(c) independently required formal hearings for every legally required license: that provision required timely completion of proceedings required elsewhere, rather than creating the hearing duty by itself. Id. ¶¶ 35–38 & nn. 25, 30–31.

## Formal procedure did not give the ALJ exclusive initial authority

The applicants contended that sections 554(d) and 557(b) required the hearing judge to make or recommend the initial decision. The court held that these were applications for initial licenses: no earlier discharge permits had been issued. The APA's pertinent exceptions therefore allowed the agency's decisional arrangement. This was distinct from the general conclusion that formal-adjudication safeguards applied. Id. ¶¶ 41–42.

The constitutional bias challenge also failed under the majority's analysis. The applicants had not shown an unacceptable risk of bias. The Regional Administrator's involvement resembled permissible combination of investigation and adjudication, and the majority would not presume inability to reconsider an earlier position after further evidence. It relied on the presumption of administrative objectivity rather than establish that all self-review was constitutionally permissible. Id. ¶¶ 46–48.

## The technical findings that survived and those that required correction

The court applied substantial-evidence review, expressly describing it as more demanding than arbitrary-and-capricious review in this 1977 opinion. It accepted EPA's general method of studying exemplary technology and using statistical intervals to account for variation. It nevertheless tested the particular data selections and resulting limits. The Brown and Root study of produced-water treatment did not by itself establish the best technology for deck drainage. Other testimony and comparative performance data supported EPA's identification of flotation systems as BPCTCA for that waste stream. Choosing among competing reasonable conclusions did not make the determination unsupported. Id. ¶¶ 50–59 & nn. 38–43.

EPA could use data from exemplary flotation-system platforms, but could not exclude normal drilling, rig-moving, and cleanup periods and then assume the resulting limits were attainable during those periods. The excluded operations generated greater oil and grease concentrations. On remand, EPA had to adopt supported limits accounting for those activities or reopen proceedings to demonstrate that the existing limits could be met. The court separately rejected a seasonal-data objection because winter and early-spring Mobil data received adequate consideration; Shell winter data affected by correctable contamination had properly been excluded. Id. ¶¶ 61–64 & nn. 45–46.

The uniform pounds-per-day alternative also lacked sufficient support. Platform flows varied greatly, so a fixed daily amount attainable at one platform did not automatically represent attainable performance at another. Allowing applicants to choose concentration limits instead did not excuse an unsupported alternative standard. The remand eliminated the existing poundage limits, while permitting new ones supported by evidence introduced in an adjudicatory hearing. Id. ¶¶ 66–69, 95 & nn. 47–51.

## The undisclosed data and the Liberson memorandum

For produced water, EPA relied on Gulf of Mexico data because no Cook Inlet platform then discharged that water offshore. The companies accepted flotation technology and use of Gulf data but contested EPA's statistical adjustments and assumptions. The court found no substantive error apparent on the record in the adjustments or the decision to treat the data as lognormally distributed. It accepted exclusions of small samples, statistical outliers, startup-period observations covered by a grace period, and preventable malfunction data. Id. ¶¶ 72–74 & nn. 52–53.

The difficulty was procedural: EPA had relied on an additional year's data it had never disclosed and on statistician Gary Liberson's internal memorandum without allowing the applicants to challenge either. The court could not know the new data's source or integrity, and comments might change the agency's assessment. Section 556(e)'s record restriction was therefore consequential to both fair participation and reliable review. On remand EPA had to reveal the data and reopen hearings for challenge to the data and memorandum. It remained free to reaffirm or revise the limits; the court did not itself choose new limits or declare every statistical choice invalid. The same direction applied to the onshore permits. Id. ¶¶ 44, 73–80, 96 & nn. 34, 54.

## Upsets, bypass, and the precise remand

EPA's method anticipated that even exemplary technology would meet the produced-water limits only 99 percent of the time and the deck-drainage limits 97.5 percent of the time. Yet permits demanded compliance at all times. The court required formal upset provisions addressing excursions beyond the permit holder's control. An assurance of discretionary nonprosecution was inadequate: EPA could change its practice, and private citizens could enforce the permit regardless of EPA's preference. EPA could place a reasonable evidentiary burden on the holder to establish the unavoidable upset. This did not authorize a fixed number of pollution days irrespective of cause. Id. ¶¶ 82–88 & nn. 55–57.

The bypass ruling was narrower. Ordinary maintenance often could occur without bypass or with storage or diversion ashore, so the record did not require a general maintenance exception. But shutting in a producing well could damage its reservoir and permanently reduce recoverable resources. EPA had suggested that the existing severe-property-damage exception might cover that situation. The court required clarification that, under appropriate circumstances, shutting in a well could constitute severe property damage permitting bypass when it was the only alternative. Id. ¶¶ 89–91.

The judgment was a limited remand: reconsider operational-period deck-drainage limits; eliminate unsupported poundage limits unless new evidence supported replacements; disclose the produced-water data and reopen proceedings for challenge; add upset provisions; and clarify bypass language. The court did not simply vacate the entire regulatory program or award the applicants their requested numerical limits. Id. ¶¶ 93–99.

## Wallace's constitutional disagreement

Judge Wallace agreed with the majority's interpretations of the APA and pollution statute but dissented from rejection of the due-process challenge. In his view, the applicants' ongoing businesses were protected property, and restrictive permits could impair that property just as denial could stop operations. He also suggested that the statutory program might create a qualifying entitlement to the permit. Id. ¶¶ 101–105 & dissent nn. 1–2.

Wallace treated the Regional Administrator's draft permit and subsequent review as two adjudications, rather than investigation followed by a first adjudication. The draft became final unless the applicant sought review, and the regulations described later hearings as reconsidering the permit determination. He read Withrow as distinguishing permissible investigation plus adjudication from impermissible review of one's own prior adjudicatory decision. Requiring a different reviewer would impose little burden compared with the companies' potential loss. This was a constitutional rationale beyond the statutory corrections on which the panel agreed. Id. ¶¶ 106–117 & dissent n. 3.

Sneed and District Judge Zirpoli constituted the majority on that constitutional question; Wallace agreed with the statutory analysis and remand directions but would also find a Fifth Amendment violation.
