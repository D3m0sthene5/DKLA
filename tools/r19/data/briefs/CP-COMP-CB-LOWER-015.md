# Avista Management, Inc. v. Wausau Underwriters Insurance Co.

**2006 WL 1562246 (M.D. Fla. June 6, 2006).** Case No. 6:05-cv-1430-Orl-31JGG (Consolidated), Orlando Division. District Judge Gregory A. Presnell signed the two-page order. Assigned source: CivPro physical page 34, printed page 6, Note 4.

## The narrow dispute

Avista Management, doing business as Avista Plex, was the plaintiff; Wausau Underwriters Insurance Company was the defendant. Avista filed document 105, asking the court to designate the location of a Rule 30(b)(6) deposition. The order describes this as another dispute the parties had been unable to untangle without federal-court assistance. It supplies no account of the underlying insurance claims, competing preferred locations, or the parties’ substantive arguments. Those details cannot be inferred merely from the companies’ names. (Order 1.)

## Ruling and the procedure imposed

The court **denied the motion to designate a location**. Instead of itself choosing a deposition site, it directed counsel to resolve that limited question through a single game of rock, paper, scissors. The directions were specific:

- Counsel were to meet at 4 p.m. on June 30, 2006, at a mutually acceptable neutral site.
- If they could not agree on that site, the fallback was the front steps of the Sam M. Gibbons U.S. Courthouse in Tampa.
- Each lawyer could bring one paralegal as attendant and witness.
- The winner could choose the deposition site, but only somewhere in Hillsborough County; the deposition was to occur during July 11–12, 2006.
- Either party could dispute the game’s outcome. The order called this an “appeal” and scheduled a July 7, 2006, 8:30 a.m. hearing before Presnell in Orlando. This was review by the issuing district judge, not a described appeal to the circuit court. (Order 1–2.)

The practical reasoning is apparent from the court’s description of repeated unnecessary intervention: it sought a simple mechanism for a logistical disagreement while preserving geographic, timing, and judicial-review constraints. That is an explanation of the order’s structure. The judge supplied no broader legal test validating games or chance procedures, cited no authority, and decided no underlying merits question.

## What the order establishes—and what the assignment asks

The judicial act is a case-management direction, not an adjudication of insurance liability through a game. Nor does the order show that counsel actually played, who won, or whether anyone sought the scheduled review. There are no footnotes, separate writings, or appendices. The complete source includes the judge’s signature and June 6 date on page 2.

The textbook summarizes the courthouse meeting as an order to meet on the steps. The actual order makes that site **conditional on failure to agree on a neutral location**. The textbook also asks whether using a children’s game was appropriate and whether drawing lots would differ. Those are editorial questions about procedural values; the order does not resolve them or announce that random selection is generally permissible. Its historical, limited direction should therefore be studied alongside those questions without converting them into judicial holdings.
