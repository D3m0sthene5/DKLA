# TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez
594 U.S. 413 (2021), No. 20–297, decided June 25, 2021 | Civil Procedure; Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, posture, and judgment
The United States Supreme Court reversed the Ninth Circuit’s judgment sustaining damages for an 8,185-member Fair Credit Reporting Act class and remanded. It held that 1,853 class members whose misleading credit reports reached third-party businesses had Article III standing on the reasonable-procedures claim, while 6,332 members whose reports were not shown to have been disseminated lacked standing on that claim. Only named plaintiff Sergio Ramirez retained standing on the separate disclosure and summary-of-rights claims. Justice Kavanaugh’s majority was joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Alito, Gorsuch, and Barrett. Justice Thomas dissented, joined by Justices Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan; Kagan also wrote a dissent joined by Breyer and Sotomayor. The vote was 5–4. The Court did not decide Rule 23 typicality, leaving the Ninth Circuit to reconsider certification in light of the standing ruling. Majority slip op. 1–2, 27; Thomas 1; Kagan 1.

## Facts and statutory setting
TransUnion compiled consumer credit information and sold reports to businesses. Its OFAC Name Screen Alert compared consumers’ first and last names with the Treasury Department’s list of specially designated nationals, including terrorists, drug traffickers, and other serious criminals. It did not compare other identifying data. A name match caused an alert identifying a potential OFAC match, generating false positives among consumers who shared names with listed individuals. The dissent emphasized that earlier litigation involving Sandra Cortez had already exposed the shortcomings of the system, after which TransUnion changed its labeling and required exact first-and-last-name matches but did not add other identifying comparisons. Majority 2–4; Thomas 1–3.

In February 2011, Ramirez went with his wife and father-in-law to buy a car. A dealership credit check returned an OFAC alert, and the salesman said that Nissan would not sell the car to him because he appeared on a terrorist list. His wife bought it in her own name. Ramirez requested his credit file. TransUnion first mailed a file disclosure and the required summary of rights without the OFAC information, then separately mailed an OFAC letter without another summary of rights. Ramirez consulted counsel and canceled a planned Mexico trip; TransUnion eventually removed the alert. Majority 4–5.

The FCRA required reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy, disclosure on request of all information in the consumer’s file, and a summary of rights with each written disclosure. For willful violations, it authorized actual damages or statutory damages of $100 to $1,000, plus punitive damages and fees. Ramirez alleged violations of those three duties and sought statutory and punitive damages for himself and consumers receiving comparable separate OFAC mailings between January 1 and July 26, 2011. Majority 2–5.

## Proceedings and positions
The district court certified the class over TransUnion’s objection. The parties stipulated that the class had 8,185 members and that only 1,853, including Ramirez, had reports provided to potential creditors during the seven-month class period. The district court held that every member had standing. At trial Ramirez testified about his experiences, but the plaintiffs presented no evidence of other members’ experiences. The jury awarded each member $984.22 in statutory damages and $6,353.08 in punitive damages, totaling more than $60 million. The Ninth Circuit sustained standing and typicality but reduced punitive damages to $3,936.88 per member, producing a total award of about $40 million. Judge McKeown dissented on standing for the members without demonstrated publication and for the mailing claims other than Ramirez’s. Majority 5–6.

The plaintiffs relied on the defamatory character of the OFAC designation, the risk that undisclosed alerts would later reach creditors, and infringement of statutory disclosure rights. They also argued that dissemination likely occurred outside the stipulated seven-month period because their damages claims reached a longer, 46-month limitations period. TransUnion contended that a statutory violation alone was insufficient; a misleading alert was not literally false; undistributed information caused no reputational harm; future risk could not supply retrospective damages standing; and the mailings had not caused proven harm. The United States separately advanced an informational-injury theory for the mailings. Majority 16–27.

## Standing principles and holding
Article III standing requires a concrete, particularized, actual or imminent injury, likely causation by the defendant, and likely redress through judicial relief. Concreteness is distinct from particularization. Tangible physical or monetary harm readily qualifies; intangible harm can qualify through a close relationship to harm traditionally recognized as a basis for suit, including reputational injury, disclosure of private information, or intrusion upon seclusion. An exact historical duplicate is unnecessary. Congress’s identification of harms deserves respect and can make previously inadequate concrete injuries legally cognizable, but a statutory cause of action does not itself eliminate the requirement of concrete injury. The majority grounded that constraint in separation of powers and the limits on courts’ role in enforcing general regulatory compliance. Majority 7–14 & nn.1–3.

Every member must have standing to recover individual damages. Standing must exist for each claim and form of relief, supported by the degree of proof appropriate to the litigation stage; after trial, allegations alone do not suffice. Footnote 4 expressly reserved whether every member must demonstrate standing before class certification. For purposes of the decision, the Court assumed the statutory violation extended even to undistributed alerts and took no position on that merits question. Majority 15–16 & nn.4–5.

## Reasoning and application
Publication of misleading OFAC alerts to third-party businesses harmed the 1,853 members in a way closely related to defamation. Labeling someone a potential terrorist was sufficiently analogous to falsely labeling the person a terrorist, despite TransUnion’s argument that the name-match statement was technically accurate. Those members did not have to establish a denied loan or a quantified monetary loss to establish the recognized reputational injury. Majority 16–17.

The remaining members lacked demonstrated publication or another concrete harm. The mere presence of inaccurate information in an internal file resembled a defamatory letter stored in a drawer. A newly raised theory that transmission to employees and printing vendors constituted publication was forfeited; the majority also found that it lacked the traditional publication relationship and evidence that anyone had read the material rather than merely processed it. Majority 18–19 & n.6.

The Court distinguished standing for prospective relief from standing for damages. A sufficiently imminent and substantial risk can support an injunction, but an unmaterialized risk alone does not support retrospective damages absent a separate concrete injury from exposure to that risk. Here there was no proof that the undistributed alerts were later disseminated or that the members suffered independent injury from the risk. The Court reserved whether emotional or psychological harm from knowledge of a risk could otherwise suffice; these plaintiffs had not invoked or established that theory. It also found the asserted likelihood of disclosure too speculative and the inference of dissemination outside the seven-month stipulation too weak, particularly when more direct proof could have been sought. Majority 20–24 & n.7.

For the two mailing claims, the majority found no evidence that any member besides Ramirez opened the letters, became confused or distressed, relied on them, or would have corrected a file if the information had arrived in the proper format. The claimed procedural defects therefore lacked demonstrated concrete consequences. The plaintiffs had received the information, albeit in the challenged two-mailing format, so the public-information precedents cited by the United States did not control. Nor was there evidence of downstream harm from the asserted information deficit. TransUnion had not meaningfully contested Ramirez’s individual standing on these claims, and the Court left that determination undisturbed rather than announcing a general rule that every recipient of such mailings has standing. Majority 24–27 & n.8.

## Dissents
Thomas distinguished private individual rights from duties owed to the community. In his historical account, violation of a private right supported suit without proof of additional loss, including where statutes created new rights and minimum recoveries. Each FCRA duty here ran to a specific consumer, the statute authorized that consumer to sue, and the jury found willful violation. He would treat those violations as sufficient for standing and would sustain certification. He viewed the majority’s approach as displacing Congress’s authority to define enforceable rights, rather than preserving separation of powers. Thomas 4–13 & nn.1–5.

Thomas also argued that the actual harms satisfied the majority’s test: withholding requested information and a rights summary was more than defective formatting; the reports had commercial value; publication to creditors for nearly a quarter of the class over seven months showed a material risk; and disclosure to vendors could be publication under historical libel principles. He disputed the majority’s interpretation of Spokeo’s risk analysis and stressed the practical harm of receiving a potential-terrorist designation. His note 9 observed that federal standing limits did not prevent Congress from creating the underlying rights and could leave state courts, which are not governed by Article III, as a forum for claims excluded from federal court. That observation was a dissenting discussion of possible consequences, not the Court’s disposition of a state-court case. Thomas 13–19 & nn.6–9.

Kagan, joined by Breyer and Sotomayor, agreed with Thomas’s result and generally joined his dissent, but retained Spokeo’s requirement of concrete injury even for statutory violations. She would defer to Congress’s judgment about real harms and risks except in highly unusual cases where Congress could not reasonably believe suit would compensate or prevent the relevant harm. She considered the risk of a credit-report seller disseminating its reports substantial and criticized the majority’s treatment of requested mailings and consumer confusion. Kagan 1–3.
