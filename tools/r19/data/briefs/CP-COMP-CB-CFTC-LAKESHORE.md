# Commodity Futures Trading Commission v. Lake Shore Asset Management Ltd.

**496 F.3d 769 (7th Cir. 2007) · United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit · August 2, 2007 · Civil Procedure**

The court vacated an indefinite ex parte order requiring production of records and freezing more than $200 million in customer property. Statutory authorization for certain emergency restraints did not displace Rule 65(b)'s procedural protections or make an asset freeze appropriate for every asserted records violation. The mandate issued immediately. The assigned account appears at CivPro physical PDF page 603, printed pages 1190–1191, immediately before Vuitton.

## Facts and procedure

The CFTC alleged that Lake Shore, a commodity-pool operator and adviser in the derivatives business, failed to produce required records under the Commodity Exchange Act and its regulations. It filed its complaint on June 26, 2007. The following day, District Judge Blanche M. Manning entered an order without notice directing compliance with the agency's view of Lake Shore's disclosure obligations and freezing assets of Lake Shore and firms under common control. Four related foreign businesses were covered, and the freeze reached more than $200 million belonging to customers. **496 F.3d at 771; original typescript at 1–2.**

Lake Shore and its customers, principally substantial businesses including the Royal Bank of Canada, sought relief. The restraint denied customers liquidity: they could neither trade nor cash out their positions. On July 13, the judge extended the order indefinitely, with only modest wording changes. She set briefing through August 23 and promised a decision by mail, but held or scheduled no evidentiary hearing. The court had supplied no explanation supporting the original order or the asset freeze specifically. **Id. at 771; typescript at 2.**

## Appealability and the continuing need for a hearing

Chief Judge Easterbrook, writing for a panel with Judges Bauer and Manion, treated the long-running temporary order as a preliminary injunction. Under the historical Rule 65(b) framework applied in the case, a temporary restraining order lasting more than twenty days was treated as an injunction appealable under §1292(a)(1). The agency's reliance on Carson failed: Carson concerned whether particular conduct amounted to denial of an injunction, while this district court plainly had issued an injunction. **Id. at 771.** The twenty-day period is the decision's historical benchmark, not a statement of the current rule's deadline.

The CFTC argued, and the district court had held, that §6c of the Commodity Exchange Act authorized the order independently of Rule 65(b). Because the statute specified no time limit, they reasoned that the order could continue indefinitely without a hearing. The panel rejected that reading. It threatened to destroy a business while denying the affected party any opportunity to present evidence. Emergency action may initially precede an adversarial hearing; once time permits, the court must consider the defendant's evidence and arguments. The emergency no longer justified withholding that opportunity here. **Id. at 771–772.**

The statute's silence about procedure did not abolish procedural protections. It authorized equitable relief and could change substantive requirements, including the ordinary requirement to show irreparable injury, but it did not eliminate answers, testimony, hearings, or applicable Federal Rules. Rules 1 and 81 supplied the usual procedural framework. The panel also relied on the Rules Enabling Act's supersession provision, while acknowledging Congress's ability to supersede the rules itself. The Commodity Exchange Act had not done so. **Id. at 772.**

## Statutory permission is not proof that this freeze was justified

The statute authorized injunctions against violating acts or practices and generally forbade ex parte injunctions, subject to specified exceptions for records orders, asset restraints, and temporary receivers. The agency and district court treated the exceptions as affirmative grounds for imposing an asset freeze whenever the CFTC brought a case. The panel instead distinguished the kinds of relief permitted without notice from the facts necessary to justify a particular remedy. The remedy still had to fit the alleged violation. **Id. at 772–773.**

The asserted violation here was failure to disclose required records. A freeze would require evidence that customer financial interests were otherwise endangered. The district court had made no such finding, and the agency's appellate response supplied no apparent evidence of danger to customer assets. The panel qualified its account of customer complaints and the underlying dispute: as far as it could tell, no customer had complained about investment handling, and the principal question concerned the disclosure of foreign-investor transactions on London exchanges. These were descriptions of the available record, not final findings that later misconduct could never be proved. The freeze apparently injured customers by denying control of their investments. **Id. at 773.**

## Exact disposition and scope

The court **vacated the ex parte order and directed immediate issuance of the mandate**. It instructed the district court to conduct a prompt hearing on whether a preliminary injunction was appropriate and what its terms should be. It did not decide the final records-disclosure merits, grant blanket immunity from CFTC regulation, or prohibit an appropriately supported asset freeze after proper consideration. **Id. at 772–773; typescript at 3–5.**

The assigned quotation about the constitutional danger of destroying a business without an opportunity to present evidence accurately captures the opinion's procedural concern. The full decision adds an independent tailoring problem: the statutory exception allowing some no-notice asset orders did not justify this freeze on the existing record. The mandate is vacatur, rather than an adjudication of the agency's ultimate claim. **Assigned physical 603, printed 1190–1191.**
