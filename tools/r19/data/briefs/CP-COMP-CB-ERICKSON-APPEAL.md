# Erickson v. Pardus — the 2006 Tenth Circuit judgment

**198 F. App'x 694 (10th Cir. 2006) · September 14, 2006 · No. 06-1114 · Civil Procedure**

The Tenth Circuit affirmed dismissal of all three claims in a pro se prisoner's complaint. Its medical-treatment ruling was later vacated by the Supreme Court in *Erickson v. Pardus*, 551 U.S. 89 (2007). This brief preserves the earlier appellate decision as a distinct stage because the assigned account quotes its case-specific harm analysis. The assignment is CivPro physical page 340, printed page 646, with attached editorial questions continuing onto printed page 647; the linked complaint appears in the Supplement at physical pages 303–312, printed pages 443–451.

William Erickson sued Colorado prison officials Barry Pardus and Dr. Anita Bloor under § 1983. According to his complaint, he completed required drug and alcohol classes, underwent a liver biopsy, and began a yearlong hepatitis C treatment using weekly interferon injections and twice-daily ribavirin. On March 19, 2004, a syringe went missing from the medical department. Officials searched his 52-person living pod and found a modified syringe in a communal trash can. Erickson said it was not the missing syringe and that there was insufficient time to alter that syringe. Officials nevertheless placed him in segregation, found him guilty of possessing drug paraphernalia, and stopped his treatment. His account of innocence and medical necessity was an allegation rather than an appellate factual finding. **Part I.**

His first claim alleged deliberate indifference to serious medical needs. His second asserted that the Department's treatment protocol entitled him to continue treatment, creating procedural due process protections against removal for this disciplinary infraction. His third challenged the copayment policy allegedly enacted by Pardus. He claimed that medical charges were deducted ahead of purchases of hygiene supplies, leaving his limited prison earnings insufficient to obtain both medical attention and ordinary hygiene items. He requested declaratory, injunctive, and monetary relief. The supplied form confirms these separate theories and includes a January 22, 2005 signature; referenced protocol exhibits and grievances are not included in its reproduced fragments. **Part I; supplied complaint numbered pages 1–8.**

The defendants moved to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6). A magistrate judge recommended dismissal, and the district judge adopted the recommendation after de novo review. The appellate panel consisted of Judges O'Brien, Porfilio, and Stephen H. Anderson; Anderson signed the order for the court. It exercised appellate jurisdiction under § 1291, reviewed legal sufficiency de novo, and unanimously determined that oral argument would not materially assist. No separate opinion is reported. The order's sole starred note states its contemporary nonprecedential status, subject to law of the case, res judicata, and collateral estoppel, and the citation conditions then in effect. **Opening; Parts I–II; starred note.**

The panel stated the pre-*Twombly* rule that dismissal was improper unless it appeared beyond doubt that the plaintiff could prove no set of facts entitling him to relief. Allegations were presumed true and construed favorably. Pro se pleadings were liberally construed, but the court would not become the plaintiff's advocate; conclusory allegations unsupported by factual averments did not state a claim. Its application of that final qualification proved decisive for the medical claim. **Part II.**

The panel divided deliberate indifference into objective seriousness and subjective culpability. It recognized hepatitis C as a serious medical problem but framed the immediate issue as whether stopping treatment shortly after it began, or delaying it for approximately eighteen months, caused substantial harm. It quoted authorities distinguishing the underlying condition from the injury attributable to the interruption, and describing lifelong handicap, permanent loss, or considerable pain as possible substantial harms. The panel adopted the magistrate's conclusion that Erickson alleged no sufficiently distinct harm from the interruption itself. **Part III.A.**

The opinion did not overlook his assertions of danger. It collected allegations from his complaint, expedited-review motion, response, objections, and appellate briefs: life endangered, continuing need for treatment, irreversible liver damage, possible death, and likelihood of worsening without treatment. It nevertheless called those assertions conclusory allegations of cognizable independent harm. Because it found the objective component insufficient, it expressly declined to decide the subjective component. That characterization, rather than a merits finding that treatment was unnecessary, was the error later identified by the Supreme Court. **Part III.A.**

The procedural due process theory failed for different reasons. The panel found no applicable liberty interest arising directly from the Due Process Clause and applied *Sandin* to the asserted state-created interest. It held that Erickson alleged insufficient facts showing atypical and significant hardship relative to ordinary prison life. As an additional consideration, it noted that he used the entire three-step prison grievance procedure and had an opportunity to be heard and to hear the evidence at his disciplinary hearing. The court did not hold that every breach of a treatment protocol is constitutionally immaterial; it rejected this pleaded liberty-interest theory and identified the process afforded in this case. **Part III.B.**

The hygiene claim alleged deprivation of such items as soap, toothpaste, toothbrushes, and toilet paper, with deteriorating health or future risks. The panel recognized that serious future harm could support a conditions claim. But it found no alleged present physical injury comparable to the bleeding gums, receding gums, and tooth decay in *Penrod*, and no sufficient factual allegation of objectively serious future harm comparable to the prolonged toothpaste deprivation described in *Board*. It therefore rejected the claim as pleaded, rather than approving every copayment policy or establishing that only an existing injury could ever suffice. **Part III.C.**

Erickson argued in his appellate reply that the district court should have allowed amendment of the copayment claim. The panel deemed the issue waived because he omitted it from his opening brief. Independently, he had filed no formal amendment motion in the district court, and his bare request in objections did not adequately identify the proposed amendment's basis. Under the cited *Calderon* rule, that request did not require the district judge to treat an amendment motion as pending. The failure to address it was thus not an abuse of discretion. **Part III.D.**

The September 2006 mandate **affirmed the district court's dismissal of the action**, granted leave to appeal without prepayment of fees or costs, and reminded Erickson that he remained obligated to make partial payments until the assessed balance was paid. Those financial directions did not award damages or relieve all fee obligations. **Final paragraphs.**

The later history must qualify this earlier result. The Supreme Court vacated the appellate judgment on June 4, 2007 because the treatment-withdrawal allegations were sufficient under Rule 8(a)(2). On June 7, the Tenth Circuit reinstated the earlier due process and hygiene dispositions, held the medical claim sufficiently pleaded under both deliberate-indifference components, and vacated the medical portions of the district court's order for further proceedings. The 2006 medical pleading holding therefore cannot be presented as a surviving approval of the dismissal or as the Supreme Court's rule. **551 U.S. 93–95; June 7, 2007 order, pages 1–3.**
