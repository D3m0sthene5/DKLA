# Pincay v. Andrews — 2003 panel decision

**Citation:** 351 F.3d 947 (9th Cir. 2003), No. 02-56577. **Court and date:** Ninth Circuit, December 10, 2003; argued July 16. **Opinions:** Noonan for the majority, joined by Wardlaw; Kleinfeld dissented. **Course:** Civil Procedure — excusable neglect and extension of appeal time. **Assigned:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 597, printed page 1178, Pincay paragraph before the newly-discovered-evidence heading. **Stage qualification:** This is the assigned 2003 panel opinion. The later en banc decision, 389 F.3d 853 (2004), reached the opposite result on the extension; its holding must not be substituted into this historical brief.

## Facts and procedural posture

Pincay and McCarron sued Andrews and related defendants in 1989 over financial injuries under RICO and California law. A 1992 jury found for plaintiffs on both theories. Required to elect a remedy, plaintiffs chose RICO; that judgment was reversed in a prior appeal as time-barred. On remand they elected the state remedy, and judgment was entered July 3, 2002. The present appeal challenged an extension of time to appeal that judgment, rather than independently reviewing the underlying financial-injury merits. (948.)

On July 10, a nonlawyer calendaring clerk sent the judgment to defendants' supervising lawyer, who was out of the office. Asked when the appeal period ran, the clerk mistakenly used sixty days and ultimately proposed September 3 after adjusting for Sunday and Labor Day. The lawyer requested an earlier August 29 calendar date and later confirmed that date had been entered. The ordinary thirty-day deadline was actually August 2. Moving the mistaken date earlier did not make it timely. (948–949.)

On August 22, counsel handling Andrews's Connecticut bankruptcy told the lawyer that Pincay had represented the judgment as final because appeal time had expired. Andrews noticed an extension motion August 25 and filed the notice of appeal August 27, twenty-five days late. The majority's chronology states that the district court granted an extension August 30, making the August 27 notice timely retroactively. Pincay appealed the extension order. (949.)

## Issue, rule, and panel holding

**Was counsel's reliance on the calendar clerk's erroneous reading excusable neglect permitting the extension? The panel majority held that it was not and reversed the extension order.** It treated the district court's acceptance of counsel's excuse as an error of law. The panel did not grant a new damages award or retry the substantive claims. (948, 949–952.)

As quoted in the opinion, Appellate Rule 4(a)(1)(A) generally required filing within thirty days. Rule 4(a)(5) allowed a district court to extend time on a motion filed within thirty days after expiration of the ordinary deadline if the movant showed excusable neglect or good cause. The majority found no good cause and rejected the asserted neglect as excusable. These were the historical provisions applied to the identified appeal. (949.)

The majority recognized Pioneer's flexible conception of neglect, including carelessness, but emphasized its statement that ignorance or misinterpretation of rules ordinarily was not excusable. It relied on Kyle's treatment of an unambiguous-rule mistake and distinguished Marx's uncertain cause of a one-day delay, Briones's pro se circumstances, and Bateman's different procedural and equitable circumstances. Those comparisons are the majority's analysis, rather than additional dispositions in this case. (950–952.)

The decisive distinction was between delegating work and delegating professional responsibility. Nonlawyer assistance could be efficient, but the lawyer retained responsibility for knowing the governing rule. Counsel offered reliance on the clerk without stating that he had read or misremembered the appeal rules. The majority regarded that ignorance, compounded by delegation of knowledge, as inexcusable here. It expressly called the ordinary ignorance-of-law principle something other than a per se rule; the dissent nevertheless understood the operative reasoning as a categorical exclusion incompatible with Pioneer. Both positions should be preserved. (951–952.)

## Kleinfeld's dissent

Kleinfeld would affirm. He agreed that careless delegation and inadequate supervision could be negligent, but argued that negligence—including ignorance of a rule and negligent delegation—could still qualify as excusable neglect after an equitable assessment. Ordinary professional delegation was unavoidable; the relevant question was supervision and the full circumstances, rather than automatic exclusion. (952–953.)

He identified Pioneer's factors as prejudice, length of delay and procedural effect, reason for delay including reasonable control, and good faith. According to his account, the district court carefully considered each: plaintiffs suffered no significant prejudice beyond losing a missed-deadline advantage; the delay was only a few weeks; no bad faith was alleged; and the reason was addressed in detailed analysis. He regarded Marx and Bateman as requiring this approach, and Kyle as both too narrowly construed and distinguishable because it involved an ancillary fee motion rather than appeal of the merits. (953–955.)

The second objection concerned review. Extension decisions were committed to district-court discretion; the appellate court should not substitute its judgment when the trial court permissibly balanced the factors. Kleinfeld thought the majority avoided that deference by labeling the equitable assessment a legal error. He also emphasized access to merits review, professional civility, and the disproportionate consequences for lawyers and clients. Those were dissenting reasons, not the panel's holding. (955–956.)

All forty dissent footnotes were read. They include the distinction between fault-based excusable neglect and no-fault good cause, a fuller Pioneer quotation, the district court's reasoning, comparative authorities, Rule 1's competing goals, and the review standard. Footnotes 15–17, 19, and 39 identify a district order dated September 3, 2002, whereas the majority's narrative states August 30; this source difference is retained rather than silently converted into a single date. Note 32 retains a slip-opinion pinpoint rather than a reporter page. (952–956, especially 953 nn.6–7, 954 nn.15–19, 956 nn.32–40.)

## Disposition, subsequent stage, and textbook comparison

**Panel disposition: reversed the district court's retroactive extension.** The court's opening and concluding directions concern that order. It did not adjudicate the underlying state-law damages anew. (948, 952.)

The textbook accurately attributes its quoted reasoning and rejection of excusable neglect to this 2003 report. It omits Kleinfeld's dissent and does not explain the contrary later en banc outcome. The separate 2004 decision upheld the district court's exercise of discretion and rejected categorical treatment of delegation. Accordingly, the assigned panel account is historically accurate but needs this stage qualification when used to understand later doctrine.
