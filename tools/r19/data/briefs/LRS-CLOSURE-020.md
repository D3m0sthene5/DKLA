# Core Communications, Inc. v. FCC
592 F.3d 139 (D.C. Cir. 2010) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, order reviewed, and result

On January 12, 2010, Senior Judge Stephen F. Williams wrote for a unanimous D.C. Circuit panel with Chief Judge Sentelle and Senior Judge Randolph in consolidated Nos. 08-1365, 09-1046, 08-1393, and 09-1044. Core and state regulators challenged FCC's November 5, 2008 order supplying the statutory basis for its ISP-bound intercarrier-compensation rate caps. That order arrived on the last day allowed by the earlier mandamus judgment. The court found no legal error in FCC's analysis and denied the petitions. There were no separate writings. This was review of the completed explanation, not the 2008 order compelling one. (140–143, 146.)

## Compensation problem and procedural setting

Dial-up internet access involved a call carried by an originating local exchange carrier to another local carrier serving an ISP, which connected the caller to the internet. Section 251(b)(5)'s reciprocal-compensation regime ordinarily required originating carriers to pay terminating carriers for their facilities. Sections 251(c) and 252 imposed additional incumbent duties and provided negotiation, mediation, and state arbitration procedures. (141–142.)

FCC believed the one-way pattern of ISP calls created excessive reciprocal payments, attracted competitive carriers primarily serving ISPs rather than providing broader local competition, and enabled them to pay ISPs for becoming customers. It adopted rate caps below previous compensation levels. Its initial jurisdictional explanation failed judicial review in 2000; its second reliance on § 251(g) failed in WorldCom in 2002 because that provision was transitional. The latter decision remanded without vacating, expecting another valid basis might exist. Separate forbearance litigation followed, and FCC's continuing silence eventually led to the 2008 mandamus deadline. Its new order relied principally on § 201 rather than the rejected transitional provision. (142–143.)

## Overlapping statutes did not withdraw FCC's authority

Section 201 required just and reasonable charges for interstate communications and authorized implementing rules. Section 251(i) expressly preserved that authority. The parties agreed that Chevron's then-governing framework applied. Petitioners nevertheless argued that the supposedly specific intercarrier provisions in §§ 251–252 displaced general § 201 authority. (143.)

The court rejected that characterization because the two regimes covered intersecting sets rather than one narrow subset of the other. Interstate communications need not involve two directly connecting local carriers, while local intercarrier calls need not be interstate. Dial-up traffic fell in the intersection because it used local calls to deliver interstate communication. With § 251(i)'s express saving clause, FCC's construction was at least reasonable. The court did not hold that every local call was interstate or every § 251 obligation disappeared. (143–144.)

State petitioners also invoked the regulatory definition of termination as switching and delivery to the called party's premises, arguing a call ended locally at the ISP. That implicitly rejected end-to-end analysis even though they had not challenged that analysis. FCC consistently used it to classify communications under § 201, and petitioners did not dispute that the communication, traced through the ISP to the internet, was interstate. A different endpoint used for § 251(b)(5) did not defeat § 201 jurisdiction. The court separately declined to decide an Eighth Circuit question about FCC's actual-price-setting authority under a different statutory setting; that decision had not addressed this interstate-communications power. (144–145.)

## Differentiated rate treatment was adequately explained

Core challenged the distinction between rate-capped ISP traffic and ordinary traffic compensated under state application of FCC's costing methodology. The court assumed without deciding that discrimination concepts applied across these distinct statutory regimes. On narrow arbitrary-and-capricious review, FCC supplied a solid basis for different treatment. Ordinary reciprocal traffic was relatively balanced; ISP traffic was overwhelmingly one-way. Distorted payments encouraged arbitrage, inaccurate competitive entry, and cross-subsidies from incumbent customers not making ISP calls. Continued application of the old framework could distort internet-access prices and undermine competitive markets. The court therefore rejected the asserted irrational differentiation; it did not calculate a new rate. (145.)

The sole note supplies record estimates of dial-up's declining but nontrivial share of internet access, including an expressly predictive figure for 2014. Those figures describe the historical record, not current market shares. (140 n. 1.)

## The mandate required an explanation, not a predetermined route

Core argued FCC violated the 2008 mandate because its explanation acknowledged ISP traffic was subject to the reciprocal-compensation provisions even while applying § 201 rate caps. The court read its earlier order in context: it required an explanation of authority for the existing rules, not a preselected analytical path excluding all application of § 251(b)(5). FCC's treatment of overlapping authority therefore did not evade the mandate. The court's acceptance of this new explanation did not revive the rejected § 251(g) theory. (145.)

## Late arguments and exact judgment

Core used its status as an intervenor in the state petitions to present issues omitted from its own opening petitioner's brief. Intervenors generally could join only issues properly placed before the court by another party. The prudential exception for an otherwise unincentivized intervenor raising an essential predicate did not fit: Core had incentive to petition, had actually petitioned, and raised intervenor issues with no substantive connection to the initial challenges. The court declined those new issues, as well as arguments first raised in reply, which would deprive respondents of a fair opportunity to answer. These were preservation rulings, not decisions on the omitted arguments' merits. (145–146.)

The final judgment denied all petitions. The appendix reproduced relevant § 201(a) and (b) provisions concerning carrier service, connections, just-and-reasonable charges, and implementing rules. Both the full appendix and sole judicial note are covered. (146 & appendix.)
