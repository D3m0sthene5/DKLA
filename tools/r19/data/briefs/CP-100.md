# Hickman v. Taylor

**Course:** Civil Procedure
**ID:** CP-100
**Review:** Complete source comparison; requesting lawyer’s stated purpose corrected. Independent source review passed.

## Citation

Hickman v. Taylor, 329 U.S. 495 (1947). Supreme Court of the United States; argued November 13, 1946; decided January 13, 1947. Justice Murphy delivered the Court’s opinion; Justice Jackson concurred, joined by Justice Frankfurter. The judgment was unanimous. Assigned casebook pp. 944–953. Full opinion: [complete official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-329/pdf/USREPORTS-329-495.pdf). Both the assigned excerpt and full opinion were used. The account of the Rules is historical: the decision interpreted the discovery provisions then in effect, before the later express work-product provision discussed in Upjohn.

## Parties

Hickman was the representative of one of the five seamen who died when a tug sank, and the only one of the five claimants who ultimately pursued litigation rather than settlement. He sued the two tug owners, individually and as partners, and the Baltimore & Ohio Railroad under the Jones Act. Fortenbaugh represented the tug owners and their underwriters; he was not a defendant in the underlying death action but became subject to the production and contempt orders. The discovery dispute concerned his preparation for anticipated litigation arising from the sinking. 329 U.S. at 498–500.

## Material Facts

On February 7, 1943, the J. M. Taylor sank while assisting in towing a railroad car float across the Delaware River at Philadelphia. Five of its nine crew members drowned. The cause remained unknown in the account before the Court. Three days later, the owners and underwriters retained Fortenbaugh’s firm both to defend expected death claims and to pursue a claim against the railroad for damage to the tug. This was preparation concerning a specific accident and anticipated claims, rather than routine recordkeeping unrelated to litigation. 329 U.S. at 498.

The four survivors testified at a public hearing before the United States Steamboat Inspectors on March 4. Their testimony was recorded and available to interested persons. Fortenbaugh then interviewed them privately and obtained statements signed on March 29. He also interviewed other potential witnesses and sometimes made memoranda of their accounts. Those signed statements, interview memoranda, and his recollections were distinct forms of material, a distinction important to the Court’s analysis. 329 U.S. at 498–499.

After filing suit, Hickman served thirty-nine interrogatories on the tug owners. Number 38 sought copies of written witness statements and detailed accounts of any oral statements. Supplemental interrogatories sought related reports and memoranda. The owners answered the other interrogatories, giving facts about the accident and identifying witnesses, but refused to provide counsel’s collected statements or reconstruct his interviews. Hickman did not claim that the other answers were dishonest or incomplete, identify unavailable witnesses, or show that denial of these materials would cause hardship in preparing his case. 329 U.S. at 499, 508–509.

## Procedural History

Hickman commenced the federal action on November 26, 1943, and served the interrogatories approximately a year later. Fortenbaugh explained how he obtained the statements in connection with the hearing on objections, but no express demand to produce them was made during his informal deposition. The Eastern District of Pennsylvania, sitting en banc, found no privilege and ordered production of written statements, disclosure of facts learned in oral interviews, and production or judicial submission of counsel’s memoranda for separation of factual portions. 329 U.S. at 498–500.

The owners and Fortenbaugh refused. The district court held them in contempt and ordered imprisonment until compliance. The Third Circuit, also sitting en banc, reversed, treating the materials as protected lawyer work product. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and affirmed that reversal. Its ruling addressed production and contempt; it did not decide the Jones Act claim or responsibility for the sinking. 329 U.S. at 500, 514.

## Issue

Do the Federal Rules give a litigant an unqualified right to obtain witness statements, an opposing attorney’s interview memoranda, and the attorney’s account of oral interviews merely because they concern relevant facts and fall outside attorney-client privilege? What showing is needed to obtain litigation-preparation material, and how does access to facts differ from compelled disclosure of counsel’s own preparation? 329 U.S. at 506–513.

## Rule and Holding

The materials were not attorney-client communications: the relevant information came from third-party witnesses, and counsel’s own working papers were not protected on that basis. Nevertheless, discovery did not authorize their production as of right. The orderly preparation of litigation requires protection for counsel’s work, including written materials and mental impressions, unless the requesting party establishes adequate reasons for the intrusion. 329 U.S. at 508–512.

Written statements obtained in anticipation of litigation are not absolutely immune. Necessity, difficulty obtaining information from witnesses, or other case-specific circumstances may justify production. Here, witnesses were identified and apparently available, public testimony existed, and the plaintiff supplied no showing of need beyond a general demand. Compelling counsel to reconstruct oral interviews raised additional concerns about accuracy and turning the lawyer into a witness; the stated purpose of checking that the requesting lawyer had overlooked nothing in preparing his own examination was insufficient. 329 U.S. at 511–513.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court affirmed the Third Circuit’s judgment reversing the district court’s contempt and production ruling. The tug owners and Fortenbaugh were entitled to resist the demands made on this record. The Court did not hold that all later requests for factual materials in counsel’s possession must fail or grant judgment on the underlying wrongful-death claim. 329 U.S. at 511–514.

## Reasoning

The Court first clarified the procedural vehicle. Hickman primarily used Rule 33 interrogatories addressed to the owners. Under the Rules then in force, those interrogatories did not authorize compelling their nonparty lawyer to produce his papers, and no appropriate Rule 34 production motion appeared in the record. A deposition and subpoena directed to counsel would have raised the issue through a different procedure. The Court nevertheless declined to rest solely on that defect because the same substantive question would recur under the proper discovery mechanism. 329 U.S. at 501–506.

The Court’s historical discussion does not mean a party can refuse to answer factual interrogatories simply because the information is known only to its lawyer. It expressly rejected that proposition. The demand here went further: it requested production of counsel’s collected documents and his account of conversations. Confusing the obligation to disclose responsive facts with an automatic obligation to surrender counsel’s preparation would eliminate the distinction on which the decision rests. 329 U.S. at 504, 508–509.

The Rules had assigned discovery the functions of revealing facts and narrowing issues that earlier pleading practice handled poorly. The Court endorsed broad inquiry and rejected a generalized objection that discovery was an improper fishing expedition. But scope had limits, including protection against oppression and respect for privileged information. Finding the particular materials relevant and outside attorney-client privilege did not establish that every method of obtaining them was proper. 329 U.S. at 500–501, 507–510.

Attorney-client privilege was an inadequate explanation for protecting these materials. Fortenbaugh had obtained the accounts from witnesses while representing his clients; the accounts were not confidential client communications. His legal theories and working notes likewise required a different justification. The Court based protection on the functioning of adversarial preparation rather than stretching the attorney-client relationship to encompass everything a lawyer learns. 329 U.S. at 508–511.

That preparation involves gathering facts, selecting what matters, evaluating theories, and planning strategy. A routine obligation to hand this process to an opponent would discourage recording thoughts, interfere with professional judgment, and invite reliance on the other side’s work. The Court’s rationale protected clients and the administration of litigation, not a professional privilege to conceal the underlying events. It applied to both sides, rejecting the claim that the rule should be framed solely as an advantage for corporate defendants against individual plaintiffs. 329 U.S. at 506–507, 510–511.

The factual record made the lack of need decisive. The owners had answered broad questions about events surrounding the accident, and those answers necessarily drew on information counsel gathered. The identities of witnesses were known, no impaired access was shown, and the survivors’ public testimony could be consulted. The Court would not presume evasive answers without a claim or evidence of evasion. Hickman therefore sought a second lawyer’s collection and organization of accessible information without identifying an obstacle to his own preparation. 329 U.S. at 508–509.

The Court preserved a qualified route to written materials when a real need exists. A statement may contain otherwise inaccessible facts, identify leads, or serve evidentiary purposes; the original witness might be unavailable or difficult to reach. In such circumstances, the interests served by discovery may support production. The requesting party bears the burden of establishing a reason adequate to overcome the policy protecting preparation. The district court here found only materiality and the absence of attorney-client privilege, leaving that required showing entirely unmade. 329 U.S. at 511–512.

Oral interviews presented a further problem. An order requiring counsel to recall or write out everything a witness said would produce a lawyer’s reconstruction, with risks of incompleteness and inaccuracy, and could force the lawyer to defend it as a witness. The Court found no possible showing on this record that justified that demand. It nevertheless acknowledged the possibility of a rare situation involving such material and did not need to define every conceivable exception. The safe statement of the holding is strong protection on these facts, rather than an unlimited rule that counsel’s recollections can never be reached under any circumstances. 329 U.S. at 512–513.

## Separate Opinions

Justice Jackson, joined by Frankfurter, agreed with affirmance and developed the practical consequences of compelled interview accounts. The demand sought creation of a new written narrative where no transcript necessarily existed. Even a conscientious lawyer’s narrative would use the lawyer’s language and reflect inferences about emphasis and significance. Differences between that account and later testimony could invite attacks on either the witness’s or the attorney’s honesty. The attorney might then have to take the stand to defend professional credibility, potentially against the attorney’s own witness or client. 329 U.S. at 514–517 (Jackson, J., concurring).

Jackson also emphasized difficult witnesses. Counsel might interview an unreliable or hostile person to prepare for adverse testimony without intending to sponsor that person’s account. A rule requiring delivery of every reconstruction could misleadingly associate counsel with the witness’s credibility or produce accusations of suppression. Direct questioning or deposition of the identified witness would obtain the witness’s own account and permit meaningful impeachment without making the opposing lawyer a substitute witness. 329 U.S. at 517–518.

For signed or witness-written statements, Jackson accepted that genuine difficulty obtaining the witness’s information might justify production. He distinguished that showing from the claim of entitlement made here. He also read procedural text against the background of legal practice and declined to infer a major alteration of the lawyer’s role simply from broad discovery language. His concurrence supports qualified access to witness statements while explaining the heightened concerns over compelled attorney recollections. 329 U.S. at 518–519.

## Notes

Hickman separates three categories that should not be collapsed: discoverable facts, confidential attorney-client communications, and material reflecting litigation preparation. A fact does not become undiscoverable because counsel learned it. Conversely, counsel’s acquisition of relevant facts does not give an opponent an unqualified right to the particular papers, selection, impressions, or reconstruction generated in preparing the case. 329 U.S. at 504, 508–513.

The opinion predates express Rule 26(b)(3). Its discussion of production motions, good cause, and numbered discovery provisions concerns the historical Rules. Upjohn later discusses the relationship between Hickman and that express protection. This brief does not attribute the later Rule’s exact wording to the 1947 Court or turn Hickman’s qualified reasoning into a blanket immunity for every document handled by a lawyer. 329 U.S. at 511–514; see Upjohn brief, 449 U.S. at 397–402.
