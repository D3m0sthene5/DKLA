# Sundance Cruises Corp. v. American Bureau of Shipping
7 F.3d 1077 (2d Cir. 1993) | Contracts

## Court, ship conversion, and sinking

Judge Pratt wrote the Second Circuit’s October 15, 1993 opinion for a panel with Judges Timbers and Kearse. The court affirmed dismissal with prejudice of the shipowners’ contract and tort claims on two distinct grounds: Bahamian immunity for governmental safety certifications and absence of demonstrated damage from a private classification certificate. It reviewed summary judgment de novo, construing facts favorably to Sundance. No separate opinion appears. (7 F.3d 1077–1080, 1085.)

In early 1984, Sundance bought an overnight car ferry and converted it at a Swedish shipyard into a luxury cruise ship for more than seven hundred passengers. It chose Bahamian registration and needed international safety certificates, as well as a private classification certificate for insurance. A March 5 agreement engaged ABS to inspect compliance with its own class rules and conduct regulatory checks for the Bahamian government. ABS attended portions of the conversion and received many plans, but no plans for the grey-water piping system were supplied. On June 14 it issued provisional Load Line and SOLAS safety certificates for the government, followed shortly by a provisional private classification certificate. (1079.)

On June 29 the ship hit an underwater rock off British Columbia. It was designed to survive flooding of any two of thirteen compartments, and the initial hull breach flooded two. Water then passed through two bulkhead holes and an unvalved grey-water system into other compartments, causing the vessel to list and sink at a nearby pier. The missing valves violated SOLAS, while the bulkhead holes violated both SOLAS and ABS rules; ABS had not reported them. The ship was a constructive total loss, and passengers and crew were injured, but no one died. Sundance alleged negligent and grossly negligent certification, misrepresentation, breach of contract, and breach of a Ryan implied warranty of workmanlike performance. It sought $264 million actual and punitive damages after paying ABS $85,000. The allegation that proper certification would have prevented the sinking must be distinguished from an appellate determination of ABS’s negligence. (1078–1080.)

## District-court rulings and scope of appeal

The district court concluded that Bahamian law immunized ABS’s governmental certifications. It also identified insufficient gross-negligence and misrepresentation evidence and rejected a Ryan warranty in this setting. Its initial analysis distinguished ship-loss negligence damages from possible personal-injury indemnity and recognized potential fact issues, absent immunity. On reargument it clarified that immunity did not extend to the private classification certificate, but Sundance had supplied no evidence of damage caused by errors in that particular certificate. Final July and September 1992 judgments dismissed the complaint. The appellate court addressed governing law, statutory immunity, and private-certificate damage, rather than affirmatively adopting every alternative district-court ground. (1079–1080, 1084–1085.)

## Maritime law and the Bahamian flag

Inspection and classification contracts were maritime because their performance concerned the ship’s fitness for navigation and certificates needed before operating. Federal maritime choice-of-law principles therefore governed. Sundance invoked New York clauses printed on later invoices, while ABS resisted applying its own fine print. Approval stamps had obscured those clauses. The court considered contractual assent a closer question than the district court did, but held it immaterial to the result: direct maritime rules and New York law’s incorporation of maritime law led to the same governing framework. It did not rest its decision on a universal rule that postcontract invoice terms can never modify an agreement. (1080–1081.)

Applying Lauritzen’s relevant contacts, the court emphasized the ship’s Bahamian flag and that government’s interest in the safety certifications. Other contacts spread across Sweden, Canada, New York, Washington, and several other places, without displacing that connection. Sundance voluntarily chose the flag for convenience. Concerns about owners invoking a sham flag to escape stricter law did not favor Sundance here: the owner was trying to avoid its own selected flag’s law, and the immunity benefited the certifying agents. (1081–1082.)

## Direct statutory immunity, with a private-certificate exception

Bahamian Merchant Shipping Act § 279 protected authorized governmental agents for good-faith performance of statutory duties. ABS was nominated as an approved classification society and specifically confirmed as acting for the government on these SOLAS and Load Line surveys. The Bahamian interpretive statute included corporations within persons unless context required otherwise. (1082–1083.)

The appellate court rejected the district court’s restrictive reading of the different appointment verbs in § 66. Individual surveyors and nominated societies performed the same governmental role; context did not require immunizing surveyors while excluding their corporate principal. ABS therefore had direct statutory immunity, rather than only derivative protection through employees. The protection was tied to good-faith governmental work and did not immunize every service ABS performed. The private classification certificate was needed for private insurance purposes and fell outside § 279. (1083, 1085.)

## Why classification was not an owner’s construction guarantee

For that nonimmune certificate, the court accepted the absence-of-damage rationale. Sundance could not treat classification as ABS’s guarantee to the owner that its own vessel had been soundly constructed. The enormous disparity between the $85,000 service fee and $264 million claim supported an inference that the parties had not allocated that risk to ABS; the opinion cited Restatement § 351 comment f through Vitol. More importantly, Sundance retained control and responsibility for conversion, repair, maintenance, and its nondelegable seaworthiness duty. Inspection and certification did not transfer those obligations to ABS. (1084.)

The court distinguished claims by an injured third party relying on a certificate. Sundance was the owner responsible for the defects, not a purchaser shortchanged by someone else’s certified product. The certificate’s purpose for this owner was access to insurance rates for a classed ship, not a guarantee against safety defects. With no evidence of damage flowing from the particular private certificate, both related contract and tort theories failed. The appellate court consequently did not decide the district court’s alternative grounds. The decision does not establish unlimited immunity for classification societies or dispose of every possible third-party reliance claim. (1084–1085.)

## Exact judgment and reading scope

Dismissal of all Sundance claims against ABS was affirmed, without a damages award or further merits trial. The two bases remain distinct: direct good-faith governmental immunity for statutory certificates, and the private classification claim’s lack of demonstrated certificate-caused damage. (1085.)
