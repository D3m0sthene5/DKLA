# Boulton v. Jones and Another
(1857) 2 H. & N. 564; 157 E.R. 232 | Contracts

## Tribunal, posture, and precise ruling

On November 25, 1857, the Court of Exchequer made absolute a rule to enter a verdict for the defendants in an action for the price of goods. Pollock C.B., Martin B., Bramwell B., and Channell B. each gave a short agreeing explanation. The initial jury verdict for the supplier, entered under the Assessor’s direction in Liverpool’s Court of Passage, consequently did not stand. The reported decision was not a new trial, an award of restitution, or a finding that an undisclosed principal could enforce this particular order. The court held that the plaintiff had not shown a contract with himself. (Complete reproduced Hurlstone and Norman report, 2 H. & N. at 564–567.)

## Purchase of the business and fulfillment of the order

Boulton had served as foreman and manager for Brocklehurst, a manufacturer of leather pipe hose. The defendants regularly dealt with Brocklehurst and had a running account with him. On the morning of January 13, 1857, Boulton bought and paid for the stock, fixtures, and business. That afternoon the defendants’ servant delivered a written order addressed to Brocklehurst for three fifty-foot lengths of leather hose of the specified diameter. Boulton supplied them without notifying the defendants of the change in supplier. His bookkeeper struck Brocklehurst’s name from the order and substituted Boulton’s. When an invoice in Boulton’s name arrived, the defendants replied that they knew nothing of him. (Id. at 564–565.)

The defendants pleaded that they were never indebted. At trial the jury found for Boulton under direction, with leave reserved for the defendants to seek entry of a verdict in their favor. Mellish obtained a rule nisi, and counsel then argued whether the defendants’ receipt and retention of Boulton’s goods could support the price action. (Id. at 564–565.)

## Arguments and the importance of the counterparty

Boulton’s counsel emphasized that the goods belonged to him and that retention after notice of his ownership amounted to adoption of a contract with him. He invoked cases concerning persons who contracted as agents but were actually principals, and suggested that the defendants might have known of the altered name on the order. These were advocacy positions, not findings that notice or adoption had occurred. The defendants responded that this was not an agency transaction, that goods could have been used before the invoice, and that a debt owed by Brocklehurst could not be set off against Boulton’s claim. The pertinent question was who had contracted, rather than who owned the delivered goods. (Id. at 564–565.)

Pollock held that the written order disclosed an intention to deal with Brocklehurst. A successor could not acquire a contractual right simply by performing an order directed to someone else without disclosing the substitution. He suggested that Brocklehurst might have adopted the supply and sued, but that hypothetical route was not this plaintiff’s action. Martin likewise found no principal-and-agent arrangement and no contract with Boulton. (Id. at 565–566.)

Bramwell stressed the practical prejudice: allowing Boulton to sue would deprive the defendants of their set-off against the party they intended to deal with. A counterparty’s identity could matter because of personal skill, as in writing a book, or because of a financial adjustment available against that person. Another supplier could not unilaterally interpose himself while defeating that interest. His conclusion rested on the defendants’ ignorance of who supplied the goods and the resulting disadvantage of treating Boulton as their contracting partner. It did not depend on a claim that every sale necessarily involves unique personal services. (Id. at 566.)

Channell required Boulton to establish an agreement with himself. He had executed an order to his predecessor without explaining the change; by the time the invoice arrived, the defendants might no longer have been able to return the goods. Thus receipt and nonreturn did not supply the missing assent on these facts. The court made the rule absolute. It did not adjudicate every possible implied obligation or independently award the goods’ value outside the pleaded price action. (Id. at 566–567.)
