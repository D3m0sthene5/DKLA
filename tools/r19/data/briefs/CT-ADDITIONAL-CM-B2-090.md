# Martin Rispens & Son v. Hall Farms, Inc.
621 N.E.2d 1078 (Ind. 1993) | Contracts

## Court, procedural posture, and result
The Indiana Supreme Court decided this transfer proceeding on September 22, 1993. Justice Krahulik wrote the majority, joined by Chief Justice Shepard and Justices DeBruler and Givan. Justice Dickson concurred in the other rulings but dissented from dismissal of the strict-liability claim. The court granted transfer, vacated the Court of Appeals opinion, and remanded with instructions to enter summary judgment against Hall Farms on every claim except express-warranty claims against both defendants and the implied warranty of merchantability claim against retailer Rispens. These surviving claims remained for further proceedings; the decision did not establish that either defendant had breached a warranty or award crop damages. (1078, 1080–1081, 1091–1092.)

## Seed purchase and alleged crop injury
Hall Farms operated roughly 1,400 acres in Knox County and had successfully used Prince Charles watermelon seed since 1982 or 1983. In August 1988 it ordered forty pounds from Rispens at $85.40 per pound. Rispens delivered the seeds in sealed one-pound cans in February 1989. Petoseed grew the seed, with the pertinent lots originating in China and Mexico. Hall Farms germinated the seed in greenhouses in April. Yellow lesions appeared on about fifteen seedlings on April 25. Purdue plant pathologist Richard Latin determined that the problem was neither the initially suspected gummy stem blight nor a fungus. The lesions did not inhibit seedling growth or cause plant deaths. The asymptomatic seedlings went into the fields in early May. (1080–1081.)

A small purple blotch appeared on a watermelon in early July, spread rapidly by mid-July, and had ruined a significant portion of the crop by harvest. Most damaged melons remained in the fields and were plowed under in September. Volunteer plants the next summer were destroyed before Latin could examine them; the farm experienced no fruit blotch in 1990, including in previously affected fields. Based on Latin's view that bacteria arrived through the seed, Hall Farms contended that Petoseed knew or should have known that its source fields were infected. That knowledge and negligence remained allegations, not findings of intentional sale of diseased seed. (1081.)

Hall Farms sued the retailer and grower for express and implied warranty breaches, negligence, and strict liability. The trial court denied summary judgment. On interlocutory appeal, the Court of Appeals directed judgment on most claims, preserved a strict-liability claim against Petoseed and one express-warranty claim against Rispens, and treated both defendants' seed-price limitations as effective for contract and tort claims. Hall Farms sought restoration of the trial court's broader denial of judgment. The Supreme Court resolved the theories separately rather than treating crop loss as automatically recoverable under all three. (1081.)

## Express warranties: identify the promise before deciding breach
The court applied Article 2 of the UCC. Where the relied-on representations are entirely written, whether they create express warranties is a question for the court. Affirmations of fact or promises can create warranties without using a formal warranty label, but seller opinion and commendation alone do not. Petoseed's description of its product as top-quality seed was puffery. Its additional promise of high vitality, vigor, and germination, however, described performance and created an express warranty. Although the seedlings germinated and grew, that did not conclusively establish compliance: Hall Farms contended that disease impaired their capacity for natural growth and survival. Breach of that performance promise required factual determination. (1082–1083 & n. 1.)

Rispens' purchase order promised seed properly fitted for seeding purposes, with language about screening and removing distinguishable foreign seeds. Both parties accepted that it created a warranty but disputed whether it concerned only seed identity or also freedom from latent disease. Ambiguity made its interpretation a factual question. The additional phrase strictly high-grade seeds likewise required investigation of whether seed-industry meaning promised freedom from disease or merely praised the product. The appellate court had improperly required Hall Farms to supply enough evidence of that meaning to defeat judgment. Rispens, as the summary-judgment movant, had not established the absence of a material factual issue. The court therefore preserved express-warranty litigation against both sellers without declaring every favorable adjective enforceable. (1083.)

## Merchantability and awareness of trade usage
The implied-warranty claim against Petoseed was outside the requested transfer issue because Hall Farms did not challenge the appellate ruling that Petoseed's label excluded it. The court additionally noted the absence of privity as a separate obstacle under the historical Indiana rule. Rispens' written disclaimer, by contrast, omitted merchantability, appeared on the reverse of an order that did not require a signature on that side, and was inconspicuous. It did not satisfy the applicable written-disclaimer requirements. (1083–1084 & n. 2.)

An implied warranty could nevertheless be modified or excluded by usage of trade. The court explained that actual knowledge is not invariably necessary: a usage can bind parties engaged in the relevant trade or those who are or should be aware of it. But it distinguished the parties' trades here. Rispens sold seed; Hall Farms planted seed and produced crops. Consequently, Rispens had to establish that Hall Farms knew or should have known of the asserted disclaimer usage rather than simply proving that seed merchants followed it. (1084.)

Rispens supplied affidavits, including one from the American Seed Trade Association's executive vice-president, asserting uniform seed-merchant disclaimer practice. Hall Farms did not contradict that industry's practice. Rispens also submitted disclaiming purchase orders from another seller with whom Hall Farms had dealt. Mark Hall testified that he had never read such disclaimers. The evidence left a factual dispute about whether the farm should be charged with knowledge. Summary judgment on Rispens' implied warranty therefore was inappropriate. The holding did not find that no usage existed, that farmers never have reason to know seed-industry usage, or that actual reading is always indispensable. (1084–1085.)

## Remedy limitations: permissible allocation still requires assent
Both defendants attempted to cap compensation at the seed's purchase price. Petoseed's can label also excluded consequential and incidental loss and claimed application across legal theories. Rispens' reverse-side conditions similarly limited damages to the price. The court distinguished a limitation's validity in principle from whether the buyer actually agreed to it. (1085–1088 & n. 3.)

The limits did not fail of their essential purpose merely because the crop loss far exceeded the price or the defect was latent. Their purpose was to restrict monetary liability to the seed price, and that purpose would be fulfilled. The court distinguished an exclusive repair remedy that fails because repairs cannot restore the promised performance. It also rejected the proposition that a latent defect alone made the allocation substantively unconscionable. Parties could allocate that risk at contracting. A seller could not knowingly supply nonconforming goods while hiding that knowledge, but the evidence did not conclusively show such knowledge by either defendant. (1085–1087.)

Assent remained unresolved. Mark Hall read the top of Petoseed's can, which contained the claimed warranty, but testified that he did not read its side limitation. Rispens' limitation appeared on an unsigned reverse side that he likewise had not read. Considering these circumstances, the court found factual issues about mutual assent and refused summary judgment enforcing the caps. Thus, rejecting essential-purpose and automatic-unconscionability objections did not amount to finally enforcing the limits against Hall Farms. (1087–1088.)

## Tort claims and the partial dissent
The majority expressly reserved whether these contract terms could limit tort liability, treating that issue as moot in light of its disposition of the strict-liability claims. Under the then-applicable Indiana statute, physical harm included sudden, major property damage, but excluded gradually evolving property damage and its economic losses. The majority characterized the crop disease's development over months as gradual and held that it was not the required sudden, calamitous event. It also regarded the claimed loss as damage to the product itself, appropriately addressed through warranty. Both defendants received summary judgment on strict liability. Footnote 5 nevertheless rejected the intermediate court's separate suggestion that a distributor could escape strict liability merely because fault evidence concerned its manufacturer: an otherwise liable distributor's indemnity rights would be addressed after liability. (1088–1089 & nn. 4–6.)

Petoseed also received summary judgment on negligence because the claimed loss arose from inferior product performance and consisted of economic loss. The majority relied on the UCC's allocation of commercial risk and the need to avoid circumventing negotiated warranty limits through negligence. It preserved the distinction for personal injury or physical harm to property other than the product itself. It did not decide the parties' evidence-authentication disputes. Hall Farms had not sought transfer concerning the earlier negligence judgment for Rispens. (1089–1091 & n. 7.)

Dickson would have allowed a factfinder to decide whether transmission from infected seed to healthy plants or seed involved sudden, major damage. The record did not establish whether all the seed was infected, whether other seed's produce was damaged, or precisely how transmission occurred. He also rejected treating the crop as simply the purchased product: defendants sold seed, while the ensuing crop incorporated the farm's growing process, care, and nutrients. He concurred in every other respect. His view did not become the court's strict-liability holding. (1091–1092.)
