# Strike 3 Holdings, LLC v. Doe, Subscriber Assigned IP Address 108.51.209.163
2018 WL 385418 (D.D.C. Jan. 11, 2018), No. 17-cv-2347 (TJK), ECF No. 8 | Civil Procedure | Early discovery and protection of an unidentified subscriber

## The identification problem before ordinary discovery

Strike 3 Holdings owned copyrights in adult entertainment films. It alleged that an unidentified person had used BitTorrent to download and distribute those films without permission. The complaint identified the defendant by an Internet Protocol address, 108.51.209.163, rather than a personal name. Strike 3 further alleged that geolocation technology traced the address associated with the downloads to a physical location in the District of Columbia. These were allegations supporting an early discovery request, not adjudicated findings that a particular subscriber had infringed copyright. ECF No. 8, opinion p. 1.

The missing name created a practical barrier to litigation. Strike 3 could not identify and serve the defendant, investigate the defendant's role, or arrange the ordinary discovery conference without learning who subscribed to the connection. According to a declaration supporting its motion, the internet service provider was the only entity able to correlate the relevant IP address with a subscriber. Strike 3 therefore moved for leave to serve a third-party subpoena before the Rule 26(f) conference. Its memorandum represented that it sought only the subscriber's true name and address and would use the information to prosecute the complaint. Id. pp. 1, 3.

Judge Timothy J. Kelly addressed that preliminary request in a memorandum opinion on January 11, 2018. The question was whether limited identification discovery should begin early, and what safeguards should accompany it. There was no trial, copyright merits judgment, damages award, or final determination that the subscriber was the person who downloaded the films.

## Good cause and a basis for personal jurisdiction

Rule 26(d)(1) ordinarily prohibited a party from seeking discovery before the Rule 26(f) conference unless an exception, including a court order, authorized it. The District of Columbia decisions used a good-cause standard to decide requests for discovery before that conference. The court described good cause as existing when the requested discovery was necessary for the suit to progress. The rule supplied discretion to change the discovery sequence; it did not entitle a copyright plaintiff to unrestricted discovery merely upon filing a complaint. Id. p. 2.

Necessity alone was not enough. A plaintiff also needed a good-faith belief that the requested discovery would enable it to show personal jurisdiction over the unknown defendant. The Copyright Act did not provide an unlimited basis for personal jurisdiction over alleged infringers. The court consequently looked to the reach of District of Columbia law. Citing the D.C. Circuit's *AF Holdings* decision, it explained that jurisdiction might be exercised if the defendant resided in the District or downloaded the copyrighted work there. That jurisdictional discussion set the threshold for authorizing discovery; it did not finally establish the residence or conduct of an as-yet unidentified defendant. Id. p. 2.

The two requirements served different purposes. The good-cause inquiry asked why discovery needed to precede the normal conference. The jurisdictional inquiry asked why this court, rather than an arbitrary forum, had a plausible connection to the unknown defendant. A plaintiff who could not identify the defendant without an ISP's assistance still needed a reason to believe that the resulting identification could support jurisdiction here.

## Why this limited subpoena could proceed

Strike 3 met the necessity requirement because the case could not move forward without identifying someone who could be served. The declaration linked the information sought to a particular source: the ISP could match the address associated with the alleged infringement to its subscriber. The request therefore sought information necessary to overcome the immediate procedural obstacle, rather than broad discovery into the merits before the defendant had appeared. Id. p. 3.

Strike 3 also met the good-faith jurisdictional threshold. Its geolocation evidence placed the IP address in the District. The court relied on appellate and district precedent recognizing that such an estimate could justify a good-faith belief in personal jurisdiction over an unknown defendant. This was a conclusion about the adequacy of the plaintiff's showing for early discovery, not a finding that geolocation invariably proves jurisdiction. Exercising its broad discretion over discovery sequence, the court authorized limited discovery directed to the ISP. Id.

## Why identifying the subscriber required protection

The court separately considered the risks that disclosure would create. An IP address identifies an internet connection, and its subscriber need not be the infringer. A family member, houseguest, neighbor, or customer using a business's connection might have downloaded the material. The court cited decisions warning that an innocent subscriber could be misidentified and burdened with defending unfounded allegations. Id. p. 4.

The adult nature of the copyrighted material introduced an additional risk. Public association with allegations involving adult films could pressure an innocent subscriber to accept an unjust settlement to avoid publicity. The concern was not an adjudication that Strike 3 had engaged in coercion in this case. It was a reason to establish safeguards before disclosure. Strike 3 itself had noted that protective orders were appropriate in similar cases and invited the court to impose one if warranted. The court found that protections were needed against annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, and undue burden or expense. Id.

## Exact disposition and safeguards

The court granted the motion for leave to serve a third-party subpoena before the Rule 26(f) conference, while conditioning discovery on protections for the subscriber. Strike 3 could serve a Rule 45 subpoena seeking information sufficient to identify the subscriber associated with the specified address. It had to provide the ISP with the memorandum opinion and accompanying order when it served the subpoena. Id. pp. 4-5.

The ISP had to give the subscriber written notice, which could include email, at least **twenty business days** before releasing identifying information. That notice had to include the subpoena, memorandum opinion, and accompanying order. The subpoena's return date could be no earlier than **forty-five calendar days** after service. A motion to quash had to be filed before that return date. The distinction between business and calendar days matters; these were not interchangeable waiting periods. Id. p. 5.

The ISP also had to preserve subpoenaed information while a timely motion to quash was being resolved. Strike 3 could use disclosed information solely to protect the rights asserted in its complaint. Finally, the court required Strike 3 to file a status report by February 20, 2018, outlining the progress of the authorized discovery. The opinion stated that a separate order would accompany it. Id. The practical result was permission to obtain identifying information under specified conditions, with an opportunity for the subscriber to contest the subpoena. It was not permission to treat identification as proof of infringement.
