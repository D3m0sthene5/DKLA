# Bank of California, N.A. v. Superior Court

**Civil Procedure · CP-082**  
16 Cal. 2d 516, 106 P.2d 879 (1940) · Supreme Court of California, in bank · November 7, 1940

## Facts and procedural route

Sara Boyd died in June 1937 with an estate worth approximately $225,000. Her will, admitted to probate on July 8, provided about $60,000 in specific gifts to individuals and charitable institutions and left the residue to St. Luke's Hospital. The Bank of California became executor. Some beneficiaries lived in other states or abroad. Bertha Smedley, Boyd's niece and a legatee, later sued to enforce an alleged contract under which Boyd had promised her the entire estate. She sought ownership after debts and expenses, conveyances from the defendants, and relief quieting her title. 16 Cal. 2d at 518–19.

Smedley named the executor and every beneficiary as defendants but served only the bank and St. Luke's. When trial began in November 1939, those appearing defendants moved under California Code of Civil Procedure § 389 to require service on and joinder of the others. They argued that all beneficiaries were necessary and indispensable because the requested decree challenged everyone's interest, threatened multiple suits, and imposed future expense on the executor. The superior court denied the motion. The bank and hospital sought a writ of prohibition to stop trial until the absent defendants were brought before the court. The Supreme Court therefore addressed the trial court's power to proceed, rather than the merits of Smedley's contract or an ordinary appellate challenge to discretionary case management. 16 Cal. 2d at 519–20.

## Question and legal distinctions

Were the unserved beneficiaries indispensable parties whose absence deprived the superior court of jurisdiction to adjudicate the dispute before it? Chief Justice Gibson's unanimous reported opinion answered no.

The historical § 389 allowed a court to decide a controversy among present parties when it could avoid prejudice to others or save their rights; when complete determination required other parties, it directed the court to bring them in. The opinion traced that provision to equity's concern with complete relief and avoidance of fragmented litigation. But it distinguished two categories commonly blurred by the phrase “necessary and indispensable.” Necessary parties should ordinarily be joined for a complete settlement, yet their interests may be separable enough that a valid decree between present parties remains possible. Indispensable parties have interests inevitably affected by any decree the court could render, so the court cannot proceed without them. 16 Cal. 2d at 520–23.

For example, dividing a common fund can necessarily reduce what remains for absent claimants. Cancelling a lease or a voter's registration can directly destroy an absent person's right. Those situations differ from a decree whose operation can be confined to the parties actually before the court. The distinction mattered especially because prohibition required a jurisdictional defect; a debatable exercise of joinder discretion would not suffice.

## Why the beneficiaries' interests were separable

The court examined the legal operation of an action to enforce a promise to leave property by will. Such an action did not make the equity court rewrite the will or direct probate distribution. Instead, equity could impose a constructive trust on property received by an individual distributee and require that distributee to convey it to the promisee. This was the practical equivalent of specific performance, directed personally at a recipient and that recipient's property. 16 Cal. 2d at 524–25.

Each recipient's obligation was consequently distinct. A decree requiring an appearing recipient to surrender the property received would not bind an absent recipient or determine that absent person's title. Although everyone shared an interest in the alleged contract's validity and complete resolution would ordinarily favor bringing everyone in, that common issue did not itself make each beneficiary indispensable. The court relied on *Rundell v. McDonald*, where a constructive trust imposed on one heir's title did not affect coheirs dismissed from the action. 16 Cal. 2d at 525–26.

The plaintiffs' broad prayer did not alter this result. Requesting relief beyond what the court could validly award did not eliminate jurisdiction over the relief it could award. The court could restrict its decree to the present defendants and preserve the absent beneficiaries' rights; the Supreme Court would not assume that it would adopt an erroneous theory and bind nonparties. Likewise, inconvenience and possible later litigation were reasons to consider broader joinder, rather than proof that the existing proceeding lacked jurisdiction. 16 Cal. 2d at 526.

## What remained undecided and exact judgment

The Supreme Court deliberately did **not** decide whether refusing joinder was an abuse of discretion. If the other beneficiaries could readily be served, joinder might have been appropriate. If service abroad or outside California would cause great difficulty and indefinite delay, proceeding could be reasonable. The full opinion describes St. Luke's share as over seventy-five percent of the estate, explaining why its presence captured most of the economic dispute; that consideration did not itself determine jurisdiction. These issues belonged first to the trial court and could be reviewed through an appropriate procedure, rather than prohibition. 16 Cal. 2d at 526–27.

The court **discharged the previously issued alternative writ and denied the peremptory writ**. The superior court could continue with the parties before it. Gibson wrote; Edmonds, Curtis, Carter, Shenk, and Justices pro tempore Moore and York concurred. No dissent, separate opinion, or judicial footnotes are reported. Nothing in that disposition established that Smedley proved the alleged contract or owned the entire estate.

## Significance and source scope

The essential inquiry is the effect a permissible decree would have on absent interests, rather than whether absent people have some connection with the controversy. The case also separates jurisdictional indispensability from equitable joinder discretion. Those are the opinion's historical California categories, not a substitute for checking the text and interpretation of a later joinder rule.
