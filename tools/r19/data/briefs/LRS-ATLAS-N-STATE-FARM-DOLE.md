# State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co. v. Dole
802 F.2d 474 (D.C. Cir. 1986) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and setting

The D.C. Circuit dismissed challenges to a contingent future rescission of a federal automobile-safety standard as unripe, while deciding and rejecting New York's challenges to the standard's immediately operative choices among protective devices. Judge Starr wrote for the court, with Judge Scalia joining. Judge Mikva concurred in much of the ripeness disposition but dissented from the treatment of New York's challenge to combining state seatbelt laws with federal passive restraints and from the majority's decisions on the permissible protective devices. The court denied New York's petition insofar as it challenged the failure to require airbags or nondetachable belts; it dismissed every other petition and the remainder of New York's petition as unripe. This was direct review of a rule, rather than an appeal from a trial judgment. 802 F.2d at 476–477, 489–490.

## Regulatory history and the rule challenged

The National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 1966 directed the Secretary to establish practicable federal standards meeting the need for motor-vehicle safety. Standard 208 initially required manual seatbelts. Because occupants often did not use them, the Department later required passive protection: devices providing protection without an occupant's affirmative choice to buckle up. Examples included airbags, safer padded interiors, and automatic belts. A detachable automatic belt could be disconnected and left disconnected; a nondetachable belt could ordinarily be defeated only by disabling it, such as cutting it. The Department's earlier passive-restraint requirements underwent several revisions. Its 1981 rescission was the subject of the Supreme Court's 1983 *Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Association v. State Farm* decision, which found the agency's explanation inadequate and required further consideration. The present case concerned the new rule issued on remand, not the rescission invalidated in 1983. Id. at 476–478 & nn.1–5.

After a proposed rule and supplemental comment proceedings, Secretary Dole published the July 17, 1984 final rule. It allowed manufacturers to satisfy the passive-restraint requirement through airbags, padded interiors, or detachable or nondetachable automatic belts. The phase-in required protection in ten percent of cars manufactured after September 1, 1986, then twenty-five percent after September 1, 1987, forty percent after September 1, 1988, and all cars after September 1, 1989. During the initial years, a car using an airbag or passive interior earned an additional half-car credit toward a manufacturer's percentage, encouraging development of protection other than automatic belts. Id. at 478 & n.7.

The controversial feature was the “trap door.” The passive-restraint requirement would be rescinded if, by April 1, 1989, states containing two-thirds of the national population had enacted qualifying mandatory seatbelt-usage laws, or MULs. Qualifying laws had to require properly fastened restraints for covered outboard front-seat occupants while the vehicle moved forward, permit only medical waivers, take effect no later than September 1, 1989, and contain specified enforcement features. Those features included a minimum $25 penalty for each violator, treatment of violations in civil-damages mitigation, compliance promotion, and an evaluation program. The Secretary could consider waiving requirements for substantially complying laws adopted before August 1, 1984. Once she determined that the population threshold had been met, rescission would occur immediately. Id. at 478 & nn.6–7.

## Parties, arguments, and review framework

Insurers, insurance-industry organizations, insurance commissioners, New York officials, and two individuals sought review. They argued that the automatic-rescission provision lacked a rational basis and conflicted with the Act. The objections included inadequate consideration of keeping both MULs and federal passive restraints, insufficient justification for treating MULs covering two-thirds of the population as an adequate substitute, and an irrational waiver rule. Statutory objections concerned the loss of uniform national standards, replacement of federal requirements with state laws, and the Secretary's asserted lack of authority to influence state safety legislation. New York additionally challenged the permission to use detachable belts, the failure to require airbags in every car, and the failure to require airbags together with nondetachable belts. Id. at 477 & n.2, 480 & n.9, 484–486.

The court used *Abbott Laboratories*' two-part ripeness inquiry: fitness of the issues for judicial decision and hardship from withholding review. Fitness included finality, whether the question was legal, the value of a more concrete setting, and the possibility that adjudication would prove unnecessary. A final rule and a developed record did not themselves eliminate institutional reasons to postpone review. Hardship had to be immediate, direct, and significant; uncertain planning consequences or chains of possible future events did not ordinarily suffice. The majority described the doctrine's constitutional and prudential roots without requiring a precise boundary between them for this case. Id. at 478–480 & n.8.

For the ripe merits challenges, the court applied arbitrary-and-capricious review under the Act and APA §706(2)(A). The agency needed a satisfactory explanation connecting facts to its choice. The court could not invent missing agency reasons or substitute its preferred safety policy, but could uphold an explanation whose path was reasonably discernible. Relevant defects included ignoring an important aspect of the problem, relying on impermissible considerations, contradicting the record, or offering an implausible explanation. Id. at 486 & n.22.

## Why the rescission challenges were unripe

The administrative record was developed enough to evaluate the rescission provision, but actual rescission appeared unlikely. Twenty states covering approximately sixty percent of the population had MULs, yet apparently none satisfied the rule's requirements. New York's earlier law might count under the waiver; even on that assumption, additional states covering at least fifty-nine percent of the population would have to adopt complying laws. Some states were deliberately adopting laws that would not count toward rescission. The court treated the possibility of the trap door opening as a contingency that might never arise, making immediate adjudication potentially unnecessary. It did not hold that the rescission provision was lawful. Id. at 480–481 & nn.10–13.

The asserted hardships did not outweigh that institutional interest. Additional deaths depended on rescission actually occurring, and petitioners could seek review and a stay if it became imminent. Their claim that uncertainty would deter manufacturers from developing adequate passive restraints lacked evidence and overlooked the near-term obligation to install them and the rule's extra-credit incentive. The court declined to infer industry behavior from speculation. Id. at 481–482 & nn.14–15.

The state-legislative arguments also failed on the circumstances presented. Most allegedly affected states were not petitioners. New York had already enacted its MUL and offered only a hypothetical future need to strengthen it. Other states' possible choice between strong MULs and preserving a federal requirement was remote, and some had included provisions nullifying their laws if the federal government counted them toward rescission. The majority contrasted these possibilities with the immediate compliance costs and exposure to civil or criminal penalties in *Abbott Laboratories*. The insurers' actual uncertainty about how to lobby for strong laws while preserving federal restraints was insufficient planning uncertainty. Id. at 482–483 & nn.16–18.

The uniformity and federal-standard objections likewise depended on a future rescission; the operative federal standard was nationally uniform. New York's objection that the Secretary was already coercing state legislative choices required separate analysis, but New York identified no proposed or pending legislation currently affected. The court expressly reserved whether a concrete effect on an ongoing legislative process would satisfy ripeness. Nor did the statutory sixty-day filing period compel immediate merits adjudication: petitioners had to file timely, while the court retained responsibility for deciding ripeness. Id. at 484–485 & nn.19–20.

## Why New York's device challenges were ripe and failed

The choice of permissible protective devices was final, needed no further administrative action or factual development, and would affect cars being produced immediately under the phase-in. If the rule inadequately protected occupants, postponing review could directly harm New York citizens. The court therefore reached those issues. It treated the proposed combination of MULs and passive restraints differently, as part of the unripe attack on ending their existing coexistence through the trap door. Id. at 485–486 & n.21.

First, the Secretary reasonably allowed detachable belts. Her explanation relied on adverse public reaction to compulsory nondetachable restraints, including surveys suggesting that ten to twenty percent of the public might cut them, and difficulty reaching a center front seat past such belts. New York offered alternatives to the center-seat problem, but the majority found no clear error of judgment in the Secretary's assessment. The Act allowed reasonableness, practicability, cost, and public acceptance to enter the safety judgment; it did not invariably require the technology offering the greatest safety benefit. Id. at 486–487 & nn.23–24.

Second, the Secretary considered requiring airbags and reasonably chose incentives instead. She estimated an additional $320 per car over manual belts and an $800 replacement cost, which might discourage replacement after deployment. The rule discussed fatalities and injuries, so it had not simply disregarded human safety. Conflicting estimates did not compel acceptance of New York's preferred lower figures. The Secretary also considered consumers' willingness to pay and fears of inadvertent deployment, deployment chemicals, and protection without a belt. Even largely unfounded fears could affect public acceptance. Contrary surveys and comments supported addressing those concerns through experience with multiple permissible devices. The majority deferred to that assessment of conflicting evidence. Id. at 487–489 & nn.25–27.

Third, the majority found that the Secretary had considered requiring airbags together with nondetachable belts. Although the explanation lacked ideal clarity, a subsection discussing airbags “and/or” nondetachable belts, its treatment of the devices' weaknesses, and its reference to earlier analysis made the agency's path discernible. Combining the devices would not remove the concerns about cost and public acceptability. That explanation passed the governing standard. Id. at 489 & n.28.

## Mikva's partial concurrence and dissent

Mikva agreed that most challenges to the Secretary's authority were unripe, but regarded New York's combination-of-MULs-and-passive-restraints argument as a ripe challenge to the rule's present design. In his view, the agency had chosen an either/or scheme without adequately evaluating the potentially greater safety benefits of combining the two. A one-sentence response did not answer the regulatory-impact analysis indicating that the combination could be cost-beneficial. He also considered the effort to influence state regulation of driver behavior beyond the statute's grant of power to establish vehicle and equipment standards. The majority instead treated that combination claim as an attack on contingent rescission. Id. at 489–494.

On nondetachable belts, Mikva thought the agency had discussed disadvantages without balancing the relevant safety advantages or considering evidence limiting the center-seat problem. On airbags, he criticized reliance on replacement costs without examining the circumstances of deployment, cars destroyed in accidents, and the willingness of occupants previously protected by airbags to replace them. He stressed contrary public-acceptance evidence and Congress's prioritization of safety. Finally, he rejected the inference that a heading and discussions of individual devices showed actual analysis of their combined use: airbags and belts could compensate for one another's weaknesses in different crashes. He would have found the challenged device decisions arbitrary and capricious. Those views explain the disagreement over the rigor of reasoned-decision review; they are not the court's holding. Id. at 494–498.
