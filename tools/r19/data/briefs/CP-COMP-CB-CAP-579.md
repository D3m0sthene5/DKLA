# V.L. v. E.L.

**577 U.S. 404 (2016) — Supreme Court of the United States — March 7, 2016.** The Court issued a per curiam summary disposition, with no recorded dissent or separate opinion. The assigned account is Civil Procedure Note 3, printed page 1365, physical PDF page 687. The issue is recognition of an adoption judgment across state lines, not an original federal adjudication of an adoption petition.

## Family history and the Georgia judgment

V.L. and E.L., two women, were in a relationship from approximately 1995 through 2011. Through assisted reproductive technology, E.L. gave birth to S.L. in 2002 and to twins N.L. and H.L. in 2004. The women raised the three children together from birth. They later sought to give V.L.'s relationship with the children legal status through adoption. To facilitate the proceeding, they rented a house in Alpharetta, Georgia, and V.L. filed a petition in the Superior Court of Fulton County. 577 U.S. at 405.

E.L. appeared and expressly consented to V.L.'s adoption as a second parent, without surrendering her own parental rights. The Georgia court determined that the applicable state requirements had been met and entered a final decree recognizing both women as legal parents. The Supreme Court's eventual decision did not redecide the correctness of that determination under Georgia adoption law. Its concern was whether Alabama could treat the decree as jurisdictionally invalid. Id. at 405–406.

## Alabama proceedings and the parties' positions

The relationship ended in 2011 while the women lived in Alabama, and V.L. moved out of their shared home. She later petitioned in the Circuit Court of Jefferson County, alleging that E.L. had denied access to the children and interfered with her parental rights. She asked the court to register the Georgia judgment and award some measure of custody or visitation. The case was transferred to the Jefferson County Family Court, which awarded scheduled visitation. The denial-of-access assertions were V.L.'s allegations; the Supreme Court did not decide those factual disputes. Id. at 406.

E.L. challenged the visitation order. Among her arguments was that the Georgia court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction to grant an adoption while preserving her existing parental rights. The Alabama Court of Civil Appeals rejected that jurisdictional argument but held that an evidentiary hearing was required before visitation could be awarded. It remanded for that hearing. The Alabama Supreme Court then reversed on the separate ground that the Georgia court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction, so Alabama need not give the adoption decree full faith and credit. Id.

## Question and governing rule

The question was whether Alabama could refuse recognition by treating Georgia's statutory adoption requirements as limits on subject-matter jurisdiction. The Supreme Court held that it could not on this record. The Full Faith and Credit Clause requires recognition of a sister state's valid judgments. A court cannot refuse recognition because it disagrees with the rendering court's reasoning or believes the judgment was incorrect on its merits. Id. at 406–407.

A genuine jurisdictional defect is different: a state need not recognize a judgment entered without authority over the subject matter or relevant parties. The recognizing court may therefore inquire into jurisdiction, but that inquiry is limited. When the judgment appears on its face to be the record of a court of general jurisdiction, jurisdiction is presumed unless the record or extrinsic evidence disproves it. Merits disagreement cannot substitute for the required jurisdictional showing. Id. at 407.

## The statutory distinction that controlled

Georgia Code § 19–8–2(a) gave the superior courts exclusive jurisdiction over all adoption matters. That express grant encompassed the general class of proceeding that produced the decree. The Superior Court therefore possessed adjudicatory authority to hear the petition and enter an adoption judgment, whatever one might think of its conclusion that this particular adoption satisfied the law. Id. at 407–408.

The Alabama Supreme Court instead relied on § 19–8–5(a), which generally required living parents or guardians to surrender their rights before a third-party adoption. It interpreted that provision to prohibit an adoption that preserved E.L.'s rights, and then classified the perceived defect as jurisdictional. Its explanation relied on the statutory nature of adoption and on Georgia authorities describing adoption requirements as mandatory and subject to strict construction. Id. at 408.

That classification failed. Section 19–8–5(a) did not speak in jurisdictional terms, and no Georgia appellate court had construed it as jurisdictional. Georgia law itself distinguished authority to decide a class of cases from the question whether relief should be granted in a particular case. Unlike the express jurisdictional grant, the surrender provision supplied a rule for deciding an adoption petition. A mandatory rule of decision does not become a jurisdictional limit merely because it is mandatory or strictly construed. Treating every adoption requirement that way would collapse the distinction between an erroneous decision and an adjudication made without power to decide. Id. at 408–409.

The Court also emphasized caution in construing ambiguous statutory words to leave a judgment perpetually open to collateral dispute. That concern is particularly strong where full faith and credit is involved. The decree facially came from a competent Georgia court, and no established Georgia law rebutted the jurisdictional presumption. Alabama therefore had to recognize it. Id. at 409–410.

## Exact judgment and limits

The Court **granted certiorari, reversed the Alabama Supreme Court's judgment, and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion**. This was a summary disposition, rather than a ruling after merits argument recorded in the report. No separate writing or numerical division was reported. Id. at 410.

The decision protects the final Georgia adoption judgment against the particular jurisdictional attack. It does not itself set a custody schedule, order a particular visitation arrangement, decide the previously identified evidentiary-hearing question, or establish that every possible adoption-law defect is nonjurisdictional. It also does not decide whether Georgia correctly applied the surrender requirement on the merits. The controlling distinction is between adjudicatory authority and a rule governing entitlement to relief. Id. at 406–410.
