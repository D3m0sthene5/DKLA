# McKinnon v. Benedict
38 Wis. 2d 607, 157 N.W.2d 665 (1968) | Contracts

## Court and posture

Justice Heffernan wrote for the Wisconsin Supreme Court on April 9, 1968. The court partly reversed an injunction restricting the Benedicts’ use of their resort, but affirmed relief for their separate trespass on the McKinnons’ land and remanded costs and related matters. No separate opinion appears. Rehearing was denied with costs on June 4. The casebook’s abbreviated “Reversed” disposition concerns the contractual restriction; the complete decision has a mixed result. The question was equitable enforcement against the contracting parties, not a damages action for breach or enforcement of a land covenant against later owners. 38 Wis. 2d at 615–616, 624–626.

## The bargain and the later resort changes

The Benedicts sought to buy Bent’s Camp, roughly eighty acres on Mamie Lake surrounded by the approximately 1,170-acre McKinnon tract. The camp had fourteen cabins and a lodge, with its desirable resort area near the lake and a less attractive undeveloped portion across County Trunk B. The financially constrained buyers were referred to McKinnon, who lent $5,000 toward their $60,000 land-contract purchase. His August 31, 1960 letter required an interest-free note secured by a first mortgage on the buyers’ Michigan cottage. It also contemplated a recordable agreement preventing tree cutting in specified areas and construction of improvements closer to his property than existing buildings for twenty-five years. Land west of the road and a separate woodlot were excluded. Complete CaseMine judicial reproduction, factual statement preceding “Heffernan, J.”; assigned casebook 641.

The letter also offered assistance: trees for planting, designated areas for free firewood, an attempt to solve Mrs. Vair’s fifty-year cabin lease at $5 annually, efforts to generate customers, and help organizing the operation. The Benedicts signed, received the advance, and repaid it in spring 1961 after about seven months. Their seller’s land contract imposed additional restrictions while the purchase obligation remained outstanding. Between 1961 and 1964 they spent $20,000 improving cabins, but resort income declined and payments became difficult. McKinnon’s effort to solve the lease problem failed; the record showed almost no generated business and no attempt to organize the operation. Factual statement; majority at 620–621.

To stabilize the business, the buyers developed eighteen trailer spaces in fall 1964 at approximately $8,000 and a tent campsite the next spring at approximately $1,200. When the seasonal residents returned in June 1965, the McKinnons sued to prohibit those improvements. They invoked the letter agreement, alleged zoning and trailer-ordinance problems, and asserted trespass on a point of land they owned inside the surrounding resort property. Following a bench trial, the county court restrained further grading and trailer or camper rentals, limited resort use through August 31, 1985, and purported to bind subsequent purchasers. The Benedicts appealed. Factual statement; 38 Wis. 2d at 615–616.

## Equitable relief required more than a contract right

The attempted restriction of subsequent purchasers was erroneous even under the respondents’ own characterization: they conceded this was a simple contract action between the original parties. As to those parties, the court confined its inquiry to injunction as specific enforcement. The plaintiffs had not commenced an action at law for damages and claimed no adequate legal remedy. Their assertion of inadequate damages did not automatically entitle them to an injunction. Id. at 615–619.

Equitable remedies are discretionary under settled principles, including whether their hardship outweighs their benefits. Restatement (First) of Contracts §367 identifies grossly inadequate consideration or unfair terms, disproportionate hardship, and sharp practice, misrepresentation, or mistake as grounds for refusing specific performance. These are alternatives; proof of deliberate misconduct is not essential whenever hardship and unfairness are established. An injunction can impose a burden far exceeding the plaintiff’s cognizable benefit, unlike damages compensating a proven loss. The court applied those principles to this restrictive-use agreement. Id. at 616–619.

The opinion also invoked a policy disfavoring restrictions on land use and the need for reasonable restrictions protecting legitimate interests. The casebook’s editorial footnote b flags that the free-use passage attributed to Stein came from that decision’s dissent, rather than its majority. That attribution problem should not be converted into an unqualified description of Stein’s holding. Here the operative reasoning is McKinnon’s own application of equitable hardship, adequacy, and reasonableness. Id. at 619; assigned 642 & editorial n.b.

## Why this restriction failed the equitable test

Literal enforcement would preserve the resort’s 1960 status for twenty-five years, limiting development even of ordinary additional cottages to a narrow existing area. The unrestricted land across the road was farthest from the lake and least useful for resort expansion. The buyers’ effort to make the resort financially stable through lawful trailer and tent uses would be severely constrained. A private restriction of those uses required consideration reasonably related to the owner’s sacrificed opportunities. Id. at 619–620.

The monetary benefit was not a $5,000 gift. It was seven months’ interest-free use of secured money, which the court valued at about $145 using the land contract’s 5% rate. The other assistance produced only a failed attempt on the cheap long-term lease and a small group’s brief stay. The court called the inadequacy gross enough to bar extraordinary equitable enforcement. When counsel emphasized that the buyers could not have purchased without the advance, the court viewed that necessity as evidence of their inability to negotiate at arm’s length, rather than a reason to ignore the resulting imbalance. Id. at 620–621.

The benefits to the neighbors were comparatively small. Testimony indicated that the trailer park could not be seen from their house and that summer foliage concealed the tent campsite during their principal period of residence. Footnote 1 explains that McKinnon’s $50,000 depreciation testimony was admissible but had slight probative force, especially because his real-estate expert could not quantify depreciation. The court did not treat that asserted amount as proven damage justifying the full injunction. Id. at 621–622 & n.1.

The record showed no sharp practice, dishonesty, or overreaching by McKinnon. Nevertheless, his legal, investment, corporate, and governmental experience substantially exceeded the retail jeweler’s financial and business position. The buyers apparently overvalued his promises. Taken together, grossly inadequate consideration, a small protected benefit, oppressive restrictions, and bargaining circumstances made enforcement unfair even viewed at the agreement’s inception. The court withheld the injunction without holding that every harsh agreement necessarily lacks enforceability at law. Id. at 622–623.

## Ordinance and trespass issues in the complete decision

The neighbors’ attacks on the town trailer-park ordinance and permits did not independently justify prohibiting this development. The clerk’s testimony and the ordinance document supported the statutory presumption of proper enactment. The challenged annual license had expired, making its particular location-description issue moot. Adding campground language through the clerk’s assistance did not invalidate the application; required plans could be demanded for renewal. Footnote 2 adds that invalidating the regulatory ordinance would undermine the neighbors’ strategy by removing the regulation, subject to zoning requirements. Id. at 623–624 & n.2.

The separate trespass claim succeeded. The evidence supported the neighbors’ title to the point projecting into the lake and the finding that the buyers had trespassed there. The court affirmed damages and an injunction concerning that land. The $750 tree-planting item restored screening previously supplied by a small mound and its vegetation rather than improperly improving the plaintiffs’ position. Contractual freedom to use the buyers’ own resort did not give them permission to grade the neighbors’ land. Id. at 624–625.

The $1,355.70 counsel-fee item was not attributable solely to trespass and could not stand in the amount awarded. The neighbors did not prevail in the overall action and could recover proper costs only to the extent attributable to trespass and preventing further trespass; assessment belonged to the trial court. The buyers’ claimed damages from the improvident resort injunction likewise remained for that court. Id. at 625–626.

## Exact disposition and source scope

The judgment was affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded consistently with the opinion: no equitable enforcement of the resort-use agreement, preserved trespass relief, and further proceedings on costs and any injunction-related damages.
