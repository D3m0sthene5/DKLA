# Groves v. John Wunder Co.
205 Minn. 163, 286 N.W. 235 (1939) | Contracts

## Transaction, breach, and trial judgment
S. J. Groves & Sons owned twenty-four acres of suburban Minneapolis land zoned for heavy industry, with existing or readily available railroad access. The neighborhood’s limited development made the sand and gravel deposit the property’s principal immediate asset. Groves operated an excavating and screening plant there; Wunder operated a nearby competing plant. Their August 1927 transaction gave Wunder a seven-year lease, the screening plant, and rights to remove sand and gravel, while eliminating Groves as a competitor. Wunder paid $105,000 and promised to leave the site at a uniform grade substantially matching the existing roadway, using stripped overburden to establish and maintain that grade. Frank Groves sued as assignee and successor to the original company’s rights. 205 Minn. at 164–165.

Wunder removed the richest gravel and left the land broken, rugged, and uneven. The trial findings established failure to perform the grading covenant. Under the interpretation used at trial, completion required moving 288,495 cubic yards of overburden off the property at a reasonable cost above $60,000. The land’s value if properly graded was only $12,160. The trial court used a market-value measure and entered judgment of slightly more than $15,000 including interest, rather than awarding completion cost. The plaintiff appealed the measure of damages. Liability for breach was not the disputed appellate question. Id. at 165; see also Olson dissent, id. at 172–173.

## Issue and prevailing rule
The court had to decide whether the promised grading was compensable at its reasonable completion cost despite that cost exceeding the value of the completed land. Stone’s prevailing opinion held that cost of performance was the appropriate measure for this deliberate failure to perform a construction obligation, subject to determining exactly what work the contract required on remand. Id. at 165–171.

The aim was to give the promisee, so far as money could do so, the promised accomplishment. A landowner could purchase an improvement for reasons not exhausted by current market value, including future development or personal preferences. A contractor who had received the agreed consideration could not escape the cost of the promised work simply because the land was worth little. Stone characterized this breach as willful and in bad faith and distinguished cases of good-faith substantial performance. Id. at 165–168.

## Reasoning
The prevailing opinion separated contractual loss from ordinary deterioration of land through a tort. Tort valuation generally compensates the decrease in an asset’s money value; this contract entitled the owner to a specified alteration of the property. The loss was the promised work, not merely a smaller sale price. Drawing on grading and farm-improvement precedents, the court considered reasonable performance cost the value of that lost accomplishment. It rejected the suggestion that municipal construction agreements alone received such treatment: private parties with equivalent contractual rights should receive equivalent remedies. Id. at 166–170.

The court also defended an owner’s right to spend money on an improvement that would add little or even reduce resale value. Its discussion of a monument to an owner’s taste illustrated why the promisor’s opinion of economic benefit did not replace the bargain. It did not find that this site contained such a monument; the illustration supported a general account of the performance interest. For industrial land on the edge of the Twin Cities, limiting recovery to current value also risked defeating the owner’s ability to contract for future development. Id. at 168–170.

The court acknowledged the Restatement’s economic-waste limitation but construed it narrowly. The objection addressed destruction of an already completed or substantially completed physical structure in order to cure a defect. Here the grading had not been performed, and completing it would not wreck a nearly finished promised structure. A large difference between completion cost and land value did not itself establish that kind of waste. The majority therefore rejected the argument that completion-cost compensation necessarily constituted an improper windfall. Id. at 170–171.

## Remand and unresolved interpretation
The court reversed and ordered a new trial. It did not render a fixed award above $60,000. The first remand question was what the grading language actually demanded. The parties had litigated under an interpretation requiring removal and disposal elsewhere of enormous quantities of overburden, but the agreement said that overburden should be used to establish and maintain the grade. A uniform slope and grade were required; whether every point had to be lowered to roadway level, even if that required hauling material away, remained for initial determination below. The court expressly withheld an interpretation because the issue had not been argued on appeal. Id. at 171.

## Olson’s dissent and court participation
Justice Julius J. Olson dissented, joined by Justice Holt. Justices Hilton and Loring did not participate, Hilton because of illness. Olson explained that the prevailing opinion represented a majority of the sitting quorum, although a minority of the full membership, and accepted its binding effect. His opening discussion prevents mistaking the prevailing opinion for a unanimous full-court decision. Id. at 171–172, 185.

Olson regarded the damage inquiry as compensation for actual loss, not punishment for the motive behind a breach. He disputed the majority’s use of willfulness, observing that the findings did not establish a willful and fraudulent variation of completed work and that no substantial-performance claim was involved: the grading had simply not been done. He thought an award exceeding $60,000 for land worth about $12,000 after performance placed the owner in a better position than performance would have produced. There was no affirmative showing of a unique personal use or special nonmarket purpose; in his view the land was being prepared for general sale. Id. at 172–179.

The dissent accepted that reasonable repair cost ordinarily supplies compensation, but favored diminution in value when reconstruction expense was disproportionate to its benefit. It examined the precedents separately, including cases involving satisfaction clauses, nominal recovery, manure spreading, and an unwanted ornamental fountain. Olson argued that those authorities did not eliminate the requirement to identify the promisee’s actual compensable loss on the particular record. He also distinguished public works, which ordinarily lack a market value against which to measure loss, from privately owned land. He would have affirmed the existing judgment. These were dissenting positions, not the rule adopted on remand. Id. at 177–185.

## Significance and sources
Groves frames the central tension between the cost of obtaining the promised performance and the market value that performance would add. Its holding also depends on the characterization of deliberate nonperformance and a limited conception of economic waste. The unresolved interpretation of the grading covenant is essential to understanding what the reversal actually authorized.
