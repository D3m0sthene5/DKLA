# Macedonia Church v. Lancaster Hotel Limited Partnership
560 F. Supp. 2d 175 (D. Conn. June 19, 2008) | Contracts

## Facts and contractual setting
Macedonia Church sought hotel rooms for a July 9–10, 2004 church retreat at the Lancaster Host. Its representatives negotiated room rates and reservations on the church’s behalf. The proposed transaction would make the church financially responsible for deposits and payment, while individual members would occupy the rooms. The individual plaintiffs alleged racial discrimination against them as African-Americans and as members of an African-American church: the hotel allegedly failed to send the promised agreement, failed to communicate a reservations problem, and ultimately asserted that sufficient rooms were unavailable. The amended pleading also described injury to the church’s own operations and mission. These were allegations evaluated on a dismissal motion, rather than findings that discrimination had occurred. (177–180.)

The assigned editors supplement the 2008 ruling with background from a later, separately identified 2010 opinion. That introduction recounts negotiations beginning in 2003, a disputed 2004 proposal, changes in dates and room numbers, and the hotel’s June refusal. It also describes a Caucasian friend obtaining a proposal for another church group for the same weekend, with an increased room request accepted afterward. That chronology supplies useful context but is not all recited in this 2008 judicial opinion. The brief keeps its source distinct from the allegations and rulings actually before Judge Thompson. (Assigned printed 1208–1210, editorial note b.)

## Litigation and competing positions
In April 2006, the defendants had sought dismissal of claims by everyone except four individuals who had actually visited the hotel. The court previously dismissed the church’s own claims without prejudice to specifying its injuries, dismissed its claim for compensatory damages on members’ behalf, and denied other portions of the motion without prejudice or because the identity of the prospective contracting parties was insufficiently clear. Following a September 2007 order for a proposed amended complaint and a defense reply, the renewed motion focused on individual standing and, consequently, the church’s ability to rely on members’ standing for associational claims. The defendants no longer disputed the adequacy of the church’s allegations of injury to itself. (177–179.)

The individual plaintiffs offered two routes to contractual rights under 42 U.S.C. § 1981: they were prospective contracting parties themselves, or they were intended beneficiaries of the church’s proposed agreement. The hotel invoked Domino’s Pizza, Inc. v. McDonald, arguing that the individuals were merely agents of the church and had no rights in its contract. It additionally maintained that no executed contract existed and that it could not intend beneficiary rights for individuals whose identities it did not know. (179–180, 182–184.)

## Governing rules and holding
Section 1981 protects the making and enforcement of contracts, including performance and enjoyment of contractual benefits. A plaintiff must identify discrimination affecting contractual rights belonging to that plaintiff. McDonald expressly left open whether an intended beneficiary may possess sufficient rights; it did not itself decide that question affirmatively. Judge Thompson relied on Second Circuit precedent recognizing that intended beneficiaries may assert § 1981 rights, together with other circuits’ applications. (179–181.)

The court used Connecticut contract law, selected through the forum-state approach under § 1988, to identify the claimed rights. The test is whether both parties intended that the promisor assume a direct obligation to the beneficiary, determined from the agreement and surrounding circumstances. Express beneficiary language is unnecessary, but foreseeable benefit alone is insufficient. The court also read Restatement §§ 302 and 304 as supporting the distinction between intended and incidental beneficiaries. (181–182 & n.2; 184 & n.3.)

The individuals were not prospective parties: the church negotiated the rate, was to receive the agreement, and alleged that it would undertake the financial commitments. Those who negotiated acted as its representatives. Nevertheless, the allegations adequately established intended beneficiary rights in the proposed transaction. Its very purpose was to provide lodging to the individual retreat participants. The hotel would undertake a direct obligation to accommodate them; the individuals would receive the precise performance the church was purchasing. Accordingly, they could pursue § 1981 claims as intended beneficiaries. (180 & n.1; 182, 186.)

## Reasoning and rejected objections
Requiring a completed contract would defeat § 1981’s protection of contract formation. Its protection extends to would-be contractors whose rights would arise under the proposed relationship. The court analogized to a daughter purchasing spa services for her mother and to a mother ordering restaurant food for her children: performance for the third person is integral to the transaction. It distinguished decisions in which there was insufficient interference with making a contract, rather than any categorical rule requiring execution. Here the pleading specifically alleged conduct preventing the church from obtaining the rooms. (182–184.)

Nor did ignorance of every participant’s name reduce the members to incidental beneficiaries. Both sides allegedly understood that the church was reserving rooms for these retreat participants. Their lodging was the transaction’s intended performance, rather than a collateral advantage foreseeable from a bargain serving someone else. In addition, the pleading alleged that the hotel itself failed to supply its customary form for listing the guests. The court contrasted a shopper’s niece receiving a free sample coupon and a motorist benefiting from a fiancée’s automobile-club membership: those persons’ independent service rights were not contemplated in the original bargains. (184–186 & nn.3–4.)

## Judgment and limits
Judge Alvin W. Thompson denied the defendants’ renewed motion to dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction, Doc. No. 170. This was a single district judge’s ruling, with no separate writing or vote division. It sustained the plaintiffs’ ability to proceed on the pleaded contractual rights; it did not decide ultimate discrimination, damages, whether an enforceable hotel agreement was consummated, or the outcome of later proceedings. (186.)
