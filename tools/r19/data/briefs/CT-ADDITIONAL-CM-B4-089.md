# Thompson v. Witherspoon
197 Md. App. 69, 12 A.3d 685 (2011) | Contracts

## Holding and judgment
On February 1, 2011, Maryland's Court of Special Appeals held that owners and beneficiaries of a life-insurance policy could not be compelled to arbitrate their claims against a broker and UBS under brokerage agreements signed by the insured parents. The claimants neither directly benefited from those agreements nor asserted claims sufficiently dependent on their provisions. The judgment compelling arbitration was vacated and the case remanded, with costs payable by appellees. Kehoe wrote for the Davis, Kehoe, and specially assigned Sharer panel; no separate writing is reported. Davis participated in adoption as a specially assigned judge after participating in the hearing and conference as an active judge. (Official cover; slip 1–2, 17–21.)

## Separate insurance and brokerage arrangements
According to the complaint, the parents obtained a $4 million second-to-die policy in September 1990. Their children were the policy's owners and beneficiaries. The death benefit would become payable only after the second insured died. Witherspoon, the parents' son-in-law, was both a PaineWebber employee and a Manulife producer, broker, and agent. The children alleged that he represented them and their parents in acquisition, and they authorized policy correspondence to be sent to him. (Slip 2–3 & nn. 4–5.)

The annual premium was $105,000, initially paid by the parents as gifts for the children. The complaint alleged that premiums were not paid during 1996–2003 on Witherspoon's advice or through his actions; gift funds instead paid other expenses, including schooling for his children. Manulife allegedly borrowed against policy value to cover premiums and interest without the owners' knowledge. After the father's death in 2005, the children discovered approximately $900,000 in loans and accrued interest. These were allegations relevant to the arbitration dispute, not adjudicated findings of misconduct. (Slip 3–4.)

The owners alleged misrepresentation that premiums were being paid, concealment, unauthorized facilitation of loans, conversion, negligence, and constructive fraud. They asserted duties arising from their own ownership, broker-client dealings, and familial relationship. UBS allegedly bore responsibility through respondeat superior and its own duties to preserve their interests. Claims against Manulife and its successor were also pending, but those entities were not parties to this appeal. (Slip 1 & n. 2; 4–5 & n. 6.)

The parents established UBS accounts in 2003 and signed Master Account and InsightOne Brokerage Account agreements. Broad clauses required arbitration of controversies with UBS and its employees or agents concerning accounts, transactions, disputes, or performance of these or other agreements, including earlier ones. The children had not signed those brokerage agreements, and their insurance policy contained no arbitration clause. The defendants nevertheless argued that the policy transaction benefited the children and that the connection to the parents' advisory relationship estopped them from rejecting arbitration. A claim that the policy collateralized the accounts was disputed, not relied upon below, and not pursued on appeal. (Slip 5–6 & n. 7.)

## Order below, appealability, and governing approach
The circuit court reasoned that the asserted duties depended on the contractual understanding between the parents and their adviser, making the owners' claims intertwined with that relationship. It granted arbitration motions and stayed proceedings against the other defendants. The appellate court treated the arbitration ruling as an appealable adjudication of arbitrability even though claims against Manulife remained and there was no separate finality certification. An action to compel arbitration could have been brought separately, and the ruling conclusively resolved that distinct question. (Slip 7–8.)

Review was de novo because the relevant arbitration facts were undisputed. Although the agreements selected New York law, neither side developed a New York-law argument or identified a material difference, so the court applied Maryland law where state law mattered. It described the relationship between the state and federal arbitration statutes and, for this nonsignatory question, applied the federal arbitrability authorities then used by Maryland decisions. That historical choice of analytical framework is reported without certifying its present status. (Slip 8–10.)

## Expansive scope was not consent to arbitrate
The defendants' emphasis on the breadth of the clauses addressed what claims an enforceable agreement might cover. It did not establish that these particular claimants were bound at all. Likewise, arguments about resolving ambiguity through arbitration did not answer the threshold question whether the children consented or were estopped from denying a contractual obligation to arbitrate. The court resolved that question; no clear and unmistakable contrary delegation had been established. (Slip 10–12.)

The opinion recognized incorporation, assumption, agency, veil piercing, and estoppel as possible nonsignatory theories. Only estoppel was asserted here. Its premise was that a person should not rely on a contract for advantages while repudiating its corresponding burdens. The defendants invoked both intertwined claims and direct benefits. (Slip 12–13.)

## Why the estoppel comparisons did not fit
The court distinguished decisions permitting a nonsignatory to enforce terms against a signatory whose claims depended on the signed agreement. In Notre Dame, coordinated architectural and engineering contracts supported enforcement of an accrual provision along the contractual chain; that case did not concern arbitration. In Case Handyman, homeowners had signed the construction agreement, and their claims against a related nonsignatory depended on asserted rights under it. Case Handyman's judgment had been vacated on procedural grounds; its reasoning was cited as persuasive rather than an operative unvacated judgment. Here the direction of enforcement was reversed, and the children did not derive their asserted claims from the UBS agreements. (Slip 1 n. 1; 13–16.)

A direct benefit had to flow from the agreement containing the arbitration provision, rather than merely from the relationship between its parties. The UBS agreements did not promise that the insurance policy would be issued to these owners; the policy preceded them by years. The court viewed their connection as coincidence rather than causation. Possible future inheritance benefits from advice to the parents were too attenuated. Nor did the brokerage terms reveal an intention to make a contractual promise to the children that would establish third-party-beneficiary status. (Slip 16–18.)

Calling the claims torts was not itself decisive. The court examined their substance: alleged breaches of duties owed to the claimants as policy owners and beneficiaries. References to advice given to the parents supplied factual background, not dependence on brokerage provisions addressing duties to the children. A defendant could not create inseparability merely by invoking its contract as a defense. The factual and legal connection was insufficient under either the reliance or intertwining formulation. (Slip 18–20.)

## Reserved question, result, and assigned account
The court reserved whether nonsignatory relatives could be bound when their claims derive from and closely track those of a coplaintiff who must arbitrate. That circumstance was not present. It vacated the order and remanded for further proceedings, rather than adjudicating liability or damages on the underlying insurance and broker claims. (Slip 20–21 & n. 8.)

The assigned paragraph at printed 1197 correctly contrasts benefits under a policy with arbitration obligations in a different agreement. Its compressed description of beneficiaries and investment advice is developed here through the actual policy ownership, unpaid-premium allegations, loans, and separate 2003 account agreements. The surrounding general discussion of direct-benefit estoppel does not substitute for the case's distinct nonsignatory and contract-identity analysis.
