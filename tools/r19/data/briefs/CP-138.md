# Jones v. Morris Plan Bank of Portsmouth

**Civil Procedure · CP-138 · 168 Va. 284, 191 S.E. 608 (1937) · June 10, 1937**

Jones holds that a creditor who obtains judgment and satisfaction for only part of an automatically accelerated installment obligation cannot retain the remaining debt as a basis for repossessing the security. All installments were due when the bank first sued. That single claim could not be divided, and the court treated title to the automobile as passing to the buyer when the bank lost its right to pursue the balance. The conversion judgment for the bank was reversed, with remand limited to damages.

William Jones bought a Plymouth sedan for $595. He traded in a car valued at $245 and financed the balance with a charge of $78.40, executing a $428.40 note payable in twelve monthly installments of $35.70. The note provided that nonpayment of any installment immediately made the unpaid whole due. A conditional-sales agreement reserved title until payment and secured the note. The dealer assigned the agreement and endorsed the note to Morris Plan Bank. The opening narrative gives the balance as $428, while the stated note and installment amounts are $428.40 and $35.70. (168 Va. at 287–88.)

Jones missed the May and June installments. The bank sued only for those two payments in Suffolk’s Civil and Police Court. Jones did not appear, the bank obtained judgment, and Jones paid the resulting execution in full. The bank then sued for July’s installment. Jones pleaded res judicata, and the bank took a nonsuit. On September 7, 1935, while the car was parked outside Jones’s home and he retained its key, the bank’s agent took it during the night without his consent. The bank sold the car and applied the proceeds to the note. Jones brought this conversion action. After he presented his evidence, the trial court struck it on the bank’s motion, producing a defense verdict. (Id. at 288–89.)

Jones argued that default automatically matured the whole note, so the bank’s first judgment extinguished any claim for the omitted installments. Because the title-retention agreement existed only to secure the debt, the extinguishment also ended that agreement and left him as owner when the bank seized the car. The bank answered that conversion required Jones to prove ownership, that the full price had never been paid, and that retained title therefore remained in the bank. It also characterized the note and agreement as divisible obligations allowing successive installment suits. The bank did not dispute that the acceleration clause had matured every installment. (Id. at 289–90.)

Justice Gregory’s opinion rejected the divisibility argument. An ordinary installment obligation may support separate actions as different installments mature, but this note contained an unconditional acceleration clause, without the usual option allowing the holder to choose acceleration. Every installment had become payable before the initial suit. The note and conditional-sales agreement together formed one contract, and the agreement’s sole function was to retain title until the note was discharged. (Id. at 290.)

The court used identity of supporting evidence as an important test of an indivisible demand. The evidence supporting the two installments would also support the entire accelerated balance. A party cannot choose part of one presently enforceable claim, obtain judgment, and then litigate the residue. The rule protects defendants against repeated expense and harassment and gives litigation an end; it applies despite changing the form of the later demand. The full opinion distinguishes installments that accrue separately from installments already due at the commencement of an action. The relevant bar here attached to the entire matured obligation. (Id. at 290–93.)

The bank could not avoid that bar by asserting the unpaid remainder defensively in Jones’s conversion action. Once its first judgment had been satisfied and the omitted installment claims were barred, it no longer had the debt-based right to retain title. The court therefore held Jones owned the car when the bank took and sold it. It **reversed and remanded solely to determine the amount of damages**, rather than awarding a particular sum itself. (Id. at 293.)

All justices were recorded as present; Gregory delivered the opinion, and the report contains no separate writing or dissent and no judicial footnotes. The assigned principal spans printed pages 1275–1277. Later editorial discussion of separable coupons, continuing conduct, and later breaches does not change the decisive feature of Jones: one note with automatic acceleration before the first suit. This brief describes that historical result without an unchecked claim about current secured-transactions or preclusion law.
