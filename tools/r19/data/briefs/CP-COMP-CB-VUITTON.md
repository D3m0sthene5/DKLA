# In re Vuitton et Fils S.A.

**606 F.2d 1 (2d Cir. 1979) · United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit · July 25, 1979 · Civil Procedure**

The Second Circuit granted mandamus directing the district court to issue an appropriate temporary restraining order without notice. The unusual justification was that advance notice would allow a counterfeiting network to move the disputed goods and frustrate effective relief. The panel required a short, narrowly drawn order complying with Rule 65 and left its precise terms to the district court. The assigned account appears in CivPro, physical PDF page 603, printed page 1191, before Notes and Questions.

## Facts and procedural history

Vuitton was a French seller of expensive leather goods bearing a distinctive trademark registered in 1932. Retailers sold allegedly counterfeit products that copied the surface appearance of Vuitton luggage and handbags at much lower prices. Vuitton had brought eighty-four actions nationally, fifty-three within the Second Circuit, and conducted many additional investigations. Its experience suggested a network of counterfeit distributors: once a seller learned of threatened litigation, inventory could be transferred to another seller whose identity Vuitton did not know. Sparse records and cash transactions made the goods difficult to trace afterward. These were the factual grounds offered in counsel's affidavit, not a finding that every retailer engaged in a common enterprise. **606 F.2d at 1–3.**

On January 16, 1979, Vuitton sued Dame Belt & Bag Co. and Morty Edelstein for trademark infringement and unfair competition, seeking preliminary and permanent injunctions and damages. Counsel supplied an affidavit explaining why process had not been served and why notice of an application for a temporary restraining order should not be required. At the January 17 hearing, counsel requested preservation of the alleged counterfeit inventory for a few hours. Judge Charles L. Brieant, Jr., declined relief because Vuitton concededly could give notice to these defendants. **Id. at 2–3.**

The denial of that temporary order was not appealable under the circuit authority the panel applied. The district court also refused certification under §1292(b). Vuitton petitioned for mandamus, asking the appellate court to require a no-notice order. The district case later changed: note 1 records dismissal of the action against Dame Belt by notice dated January 30; note 2 explains that Edelstein subsequently told the clerk's office that he did not wish to present his views and did not care how the panel ruled. The panel addressed the possible mootness concern by treating the issue as capable of repetition yet evading review. **Id. at 2–3 & nn.1–2.**

## Why possible notice did not settle the issue

The historical Rule 65(b) required specific facts establishing immediate and irreparable injury before the opponent could be heard, together with counsel's written certification concerning efforts to give notice and the reasons notice should not be required. The second requirement does not say that the opponent must be physically impossible to reach. It allows a supported explanation that giving notice would itself defeat the preservation the order is intended to accomplish. **Id. at 3–4.**

The panel acknowledged the general commitment to notice and a chance to be heard. An ex parte order must preserve the status quo and avert irreparable harm only for the time necessary to arrange a hearing. Prior circuit warnings against orders issued without even telephone notice did not establish an absolute prohibition; they insisted on careful compliance with the rule. Here, the goods' disappearance before a hearing could leave the court unable to provide effective relief. **Id. at 3–4.**

The panel also found the required likelihood of product confusion. The disputed items were virtually identical in appearance to the genuine products, and their marketing aimed to induce buyers to believe they were purchasing Vuitton goods. Under the trademark doctrine the court applied in 1979, substantial likelihood of confusion itself supplied irreparable injury. Note 3 qualifies the apparent identity: an expert could distinguish the counterfeits by their inferior workmanship, and counsel had presented samples at oral argument. The court's reasoning concerned confusion among purchasers rather than impossibility of expert identification. **Id. at 4; id. at 2 n.3.** This is the historical rule used in this decision, not a certification of present trademark injunction standards.

The affidavit's account of rapid transfers through a poorly documented distribution network then explained why notice should be dispensed with in this particular case. Notice would predictably encourage movement of the very inventory the injunction needed to preserve. The panel did not authorize a broad injunction merely because counterfeiting had been alleged. Its conclusion assumed compliance with the other requirements of Rule 65 and required both narrow scope and brief duration. **Id. at 4–5.**

## Extraordinary appellate intervention and disposition

Note 5 explains the mandamus justification. Without a prompt order and without certified interlocutory review, these cases would rarely progress beyond the complaint stage in a form that allowed an effective appellate ruling on the notice issue. The panel also observed differing practices among district judges: some granted comparable temporary orders, while Judge Brieant regarded applications of this kind as vexatious. The recurring factual pattern and the threatened defeat of later appellate review supported supervisory intervention under the All Writs Act. The panel drew on mandamus's corrective and instructional function, while characterizing this case as extraordinary. **Id. at 3 & n.5.**

The precise result was to **grant the petition and direct the district court to issue an appropriate ex parte temporary restraining order**, with the district judge determining its terms. The order had to protect defendants through limited scope and short duration. The appellate court did not itself enter a permanent injunction, award damages, or decide the final infringement merits. **Id. at 3, 5.** The decision is per curiam by Judges Moore, Friendly, and Meskill; the report contains no separate writing or numerical vote annotation.

## Assigned-source qualification

The assigned account attributes the no-notice order to the presence of many defendants not yet identified. The actual opinion provides a more specific reason: the named defendants could receive notice, but notice risked transfer of their inventory to other, unidentified sellers. The latter circumstance made effective relief vulnerable; inability to contact the named opponents was not the premise. **Assigned physical 603, printed 1191; actual 606 F.2d at 2–5.**
