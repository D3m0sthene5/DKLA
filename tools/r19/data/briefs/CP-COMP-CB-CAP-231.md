# Factors Etc., Inc. v. Pro Arts, Inc. — appellate prediction and remand relief

**652 F.2d 278 (2d Cir. 1981), June 29, 1981; 562 F. Supp. 304 (S.D.N.Y. 1983), April 14, 1983.** These are distinct decisions: Judge Newman's appellate majority, joined by District Judge Robert L. Carter sitting by designation, with Judge Mansfield dissenting; and Judge Tenney's later district order. The assigned account is CivPro physical page 281, printed pages 525–526, Note 7. It develops the 1981 disagreement and describes the 1983 result. Both complete opinions supply this brief; intermediate decisions are chronology, not additional claims of full reading.

During his life, Elvis Presley assigned exclusive commercial rights to his name and likeness to Boxcar Enterprises, a Tennessee corporation. Two days after his death, Boxcar licensed Factors, a Delaware corporation, to use those interests in merchandise. The initial term was eighteen months with renewal options, a 5% royalty and minimum payments. Pro Arts separately purchased the copyright to a photographer's Presley photograph, then sold a memorial poster through retailers including Stop and Shop's Bradlee division in New York. The dispute concerned the commercial publicity right in Presley's identity, distinct from copyright ownership of that particular image. (652 F.2d at 279.)

Factors obtained a preliminary injunction in New York, which the Second Circuit affirmed in 1978. Meanwhile, the Memphis Development Foundation sued Factors in Tennessee over sale of miniature Presley statues financing a public monument. Factors obtained an injunction there too, but the Sixth Circuit ultimately reversed permanent relief, predicting that Tennessee law did not recognize a publicity right surviving death. The New York district court nevertheless granted Factors summary judgment and a permanent injunction. Pro Arts appealed, relying on the Sixth Circuit and raising collateral estoppel and other objections. (652 F.2d at 279–280.)

## 1981: which prediction controls?

The first step under *Klaxon* was to apply New York's conflicts rules. The earlier preliminary-injunction appeal had assumed New York substantive law without litigating the conflicts question. That assumption did not prevent reconsideration on final adjudication once the parties disputed choice of law. Factors relied on New York sales and its communications industry; Pro Arts emphasized Presley's Tennessee domicile, Boxcar's incorporation, the contracting location, and the agreement's Tennessee-law clause. All three judges agreed that New York would use Tennessee law to determine whether Boxcar possessed a surviving right it could convey. That issue could be distinguished from whether particular conduct infringed a right elsewhere. (652 F.2d at 280–281 & n.3.)

No Tennessee statute or decision answered survivability. The real disagreement was how much weight a federal court outside Tennessee's circuit should give the Sixth Circuit's prediction. Newman did not resolve the question by independently choosing whichever general common-law policy seemed preferable. Under *Erie*, the Sixth Circuit was attempting to identify Tennessee law, even when forced to reason from broader policy in the absence of local guidance. It did not possess authority to create a nationwide general common law. (652 F.2d at 281–282.)

The majority adopted strong, qualified deference to the court of appeals whose circuit included the relevant state. Its principal reasons were orderly state-law development and fairness through predictable legal rules. Divergent federal predictions could give Tennessee parties inconsistent guidance and disrupt their arrangements. A single prediction could guide conduct and focus legislative attention, pending authoritative state action. Newman candidly said he might favor a surviving right if sitting on Tennessee's highest court or originally deciding the question in the Sixth Circuit; that preferred policy was not the governing institutional question. (652 F.2d at 282–283.)

This deference was **not automatic binding authority in all circumstances**. A later authoritative state enactment or decision could supersede a circuit prediction; overlooked existing state decisions could require departure. On a first-impression issue, the majority would at least defer unless clear signals from the state's highest court convincingly showed the prediction wrong. It found no such signals here, accepted *Memphis Development*, and concluded that Boxcar had no post-death publicity right to convey. The judgment was **reversed**. Collateral estoppel, copyright preemption, and the possible territorial limits of publicity protection were not decided by the majority. (652 F.2d at 281–284 & nn.4–8.)

The assigned account attributes the result to the Sixth Circuit's familiarity with Tennessee law. The complete majority indeed discusses familiarity-based deference in the authorities, but makes institutional uniformity central to its new rule. Footnote 7 expressly prefers territorial responsibility over the particular author's Tennessee heritage because that author disclaimed a reliable basis for predicting the state courts' predisposition. The assignment should not be read as the majority merely presuming that these judges personally knew the answer. (652 F.2d at 281–283 & n.7.)

Mansfield agreed on Tennessee law but rejected geographically privileged deference when the prior opinion had derived nothing from Tennessee authority or practice. The ordinary expertise rationale made little sense where the circuit itself said it had no state-law guidance. In his view, sound competing predictions foster common-law development; consistency should not require adopting a rule the deciding judges thought unsound. A legislature might act sooner in response to conflict rather than uniformity. (652 F.2d at 284–287.)

On the merits, he would treat the commercially developed publicity interest as transferable property surviving its owner, unlike a purely personal privacy interest. Lifetime investment and exploitation supported inheritance and continued economic incentives. He would require some demonstrated recognition and development of the commercial value, which Presley's arrangement supplied. The protection could accommodate news, biography, art, and First Amendment concerns rather than indiscriminately control every public portrayal. (652 F.2d at 287–288 & nn.3–6.)

Mansfield also would reject the remaining defenses. The parallel litigation and early New York adjudication made nonmutual preclusion inappropriate here, rather than treating Factors as simply starting a new suit after losing the same issue. Copyright protected particular expression, whereas publicity protected appropriation of commercial persona; he found no preemption. Nationwide recognition of survivability need not prevent tailoring relief where local infringement rules differed. He would **affirm** the permanent injunction. Those positions were dissenting reasoning, not holdings of the majority. (652 F.2d at 288–290.)

## 1983: dismissal and wrongful-injunction damages

The later district order exposes an important limit in the assignment's closing description. After the appellate reversal, Factors cited *Commerce Union Bank*, a Tennessee chancery decision recognizing survivability. Tenney stayed proceedings so Factors could seek recall of the appellate mandate. The Second Circuit refused, citing another Tennessee chancery decision, *Lancaster*, rejecting survivability; it found consideration of the first decision unnecessary in light of the second. On the resulting district motions, Factors **did not oppose** lifting the stay or summary judgment for defendants, but opposed damages. Thus the 1983 opinion does not describe a fresh, uncomplicated district choice to apply one newly authoritative Tennessee holding. It records conflicting state decisions, an unrecalled federal mandate, and unopposed entry of dismissal. (562 F. Supp. at 305–306.)

The substantial contested issue was the earlier $100,000 injunction bond, discharged when the district court made the injunction permanent. Factors argued that discharge and defendants' failure specifically to appeal it eliminated authority to award damages; it also urged discretionary denial because plaintiffs acted in good faith, had obtained appellate approval of preliminary relief, and could not reasonably foresee the later deference rule. Defendants argued that reversal also undid discharge and that plaintiffs had enjoyed an unjustified merchandise monopoly. (562 F. Supp. at 305–306 & n.1.)

Tenney distinguished separate actions whose only jurisdictional basis was the bond from a damages motion in the original enjoining action. Relying on *Arkadelphia* and the court's retained authority in its interlocutory judgment, he concluded that bond discharge did not preclude damages here. He did not definitively hold that every open reversal implicitly reinstates a discharged bond: the opinion separately grounded continued jurisdiction and noted that the appellate mandate did not foreclose necessary proceedings. (562 F. Supp. at 306–308 & nn.2–4.)

Despite agreeing that the litigation's course was unusual and the appellate deference decision unforeseen, he declined to deny recovery for those reasons. Defendants had prevailed while plaintiffs enjoyed exclusivity to which they ultimately were not entitled. Damages nevertheless had to be **proximately caused by the wrongful injunction**, and absent malicious prosecution were limited to the bond's $100,000 amount. This was a ceiling, not a damages award of that amount. (562 F. Supp. at 308–309 & n.5.)

The exact order vacated the stay, directed summary judgment dismissing the complaint, and referred the amount of wrongful-injunction damages to Magistrate Kent Sinclair for hearings and recommendation. It did not itself quantify damages. These two decisions show both the consequences of a federal state-law prediction and later remedial proceedings under the mandate. No certiorari denial is treated as an endorsement of the reasoning, and no current-law or citator conclusion is asserted. Independent-review status appears in the accompanying register.
