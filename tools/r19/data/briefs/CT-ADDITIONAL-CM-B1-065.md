# Yaros v. Trustees of the University of Pennsylvania

742 A.2d 1118 (Pa. Super. Ct. Dec. 9, 1999) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B1-065

The Superior Court affirmed an order enforcing a $750,000 settlement accepted after closing arguments but before the jury's deliberations and defense verdict. The university had not communicated a deadline requiring acceptance before closings, and its instruction to counsel to get back to it indicated that the oral offer survived the conversation. Whether seventy minutes was reasonable depended on this negotiation's circumstances; it was not governed by a rule that every offer made before closing argument automatically expires when argument starts. Judge Orie Melvin wrote for the panel with Ford Elliott and Stevens. The complete report contains no separate writing. (742 A.2d at 1118–1125, ¶¶1, 6–17.)

## The negotiations within the negligence trial

Yaros sued after falling at the university's skating rink. Testimony began January 26, 1998, before Judge Paul Ribner. The university offered $750,000; her lawyer, Richard Haaz, told opposing counsel John Orlando that she would accept $1.5 million until she testified, after which she would not settle. Two defense witnesses testified before Yaros, and no settlement was reached at that stage. That earlier expressly stated event condition later became important when the university argued that the final offer carried an implied event deadline. (Id. at 1119–1120, ¶2.)

On January 29, after the evidence concluded, Orlando again offered $750,000 during a ten-minute recess before closings. He told Haaz to get back to him, looked at the clock, and placed his palms sideways. Neither a time limit nor an expiration at the start of closing arguments was communicated. Haaz left to speak with his client; Orlando left for the restroom. Haaz returned without Yaros, who was herself in the restroom, and obtained two minutes from the judge to consult her. Orlando assumed they had not yet discussed the offer. When she returned, they did not confer and arguments began immediately. The court had asked counsel to finish by 5 p.m. so it could charge the jury the next day. (Id. at 1120, ¶3.)

During the university's closing, Yaros authorized acceptance. Haaz delivered his rebuttal, then told the court at a sidebar that she had accepted. Orlando replied that he did not know whether the offer was still there. The next day, before deliberations, Yaros orally moved to enforce the settlement. Judge Ribner denied the motion pending evidentiary hearings and the verdict; the jury subsequently found for the university. After trial she renewed the enforcement request and sought post-trial relief. Ribner held one hearing; on his retirement the matter went to Judge Sandra Mazer Moss, who held further hearings in January 1999 and granted enforcement on February 22. The university appealed that order. (Id. at 1120, ¶3.)

## The oral-conversation rule did not end this offer

The university challenged the governing legal standard, the asserted rejection, the lapse of a reasonable acceptance time, and the weight of the factual findings. Legal conclusions received plenary review; factual findings received the deference applicable to a trial judge deciding disputed evidence. Settlement enforcement followed contract law, with what the parties said, did, and intended through those acts treated as factual matters when disputed. (Id. at 1120–1122, ¶¶4–8; 1124, ¶13.)

The trial court applied the rule that an offer with no expiration remains outstanding for a reasonable time. The university invoked the more specific conversation rule in Restatement (Second) § 41 comment d: face-to-face or telephone bargaining ordinarily ends an oral offer with the conversation unless a contrary intention appears. The appellate court agreed that comment d initially fit the setting more closely, but emphasized its exception. Words or circumstances showing an expected intervening step can invite delayed acceptance. An oral offer's duration is therefore not invariably confined to the conversation. (Id. at 1121, ¶¶5–7.)

Here Orlando's direction to get back to him contemplated that Haaz would consult Yaros and return with an answer. The offer plainly continued after counsel's conversation. The remaining question was how long it continued. The university's intended cutoff before closing arguments was not made clear by a glance at the clock, hand gestures, or the general urgency of trial. Indeed, Orlando's own uncertainty after closings undercut the assertion that Yaros necessarily understood an unmistakable deadline. Note 1 reproduces testimony from Orlando and university liability administrator Erika Gross confirming that they had not set or communicated a time limit. Note 2 explains that Orlando used the same hand gesture when expressing uncertainty to the court, weakening the claim that it uniquely signaled a brief acceptance window. (Id. at 1122–1123, ¶¶7–8 & nn.1–2.)

The prior negotiation did not establish a convention of automatic event conditions. Yaros's earlier condition had been expressly stated and her proposal remained open while several witnesses testified. The final university offer lacked the corresponding explanation. The course of dealing therefore supported requiring evidence of this offer's terms rather than importing an unstated cutoff. (Id. at 1123, ¶9.)

## Continuing the trial did not amount to rejection

The university alternatively argued that failing to confer immediately and proceeding through closings manifested rejection. The court recognized that words or conduct can justify an inference that an offeree does not intend to accept. Specific advance notice that particular conduct means rejection is not always indispensable. But not every act permits that inference. In Smaligo the offeror had expressly told the plaintiffs that going forward would count as rejection; those plaintiffs then proceeded to arbitration. No comparable notice accompanied this offer. (Id. at 1123–1124, ¶¶10–11.)

The court declined to create a rule treating commencement of closing arguments as automatic rejection. Such a rule could let an offeror assert, only after acceptance, that an unspecified and uncommunicated trial event had ended the offer. Orlando also believed Yaros had not yet learned of the offer. On that understanding, her participation in arguments could not reasonably show a deliberate rejection of a proposal she supposedly did not know existed. (Id. at 1123–1124, ¶11.)

## Seventy minutes and the later verdict

Reasonable time ordinarily depends on the transaction, relationship, course of dealing, and business usages. A court can decide the issue as law when recurring transactions present the same data or when the answer admits no genuine doubt. But litigation's familiar sequence did not make settlement negotiations uniform. The distinctive timing and communication here warranted a factual determination, and competent evidence supported the trial court's finding of a reasonable interval. The appellate court would not overturn that finding as capricious or against the evidence. (Id. at 1124, ¶¶12–14.)

The university urged that urgency made the precise seventy-minute interval irrelevant and that significant closing arguments necessarily ended the opportunity. The court answered that even a significant trial event is only one consideration in reasonable time. Settlements occur at many stages. Note 3 reproduces the trial court's language about verdict, nonsuit, directed verdict, and the significance of closings; the appellate court did not turn that quotation into a universal guarantee that every offer survives until a verdict. It upheld this particular acceptance on the record before it. (Id. at 1125, ¶¶15–16 & n.3.)

The exact disposition was affirmance of the settlement-enforcement order. The court did not affirm the defense verdict as the controlling resolution of the parties' obligations, hold the university negligent, or direct a new tort trial. Acceptance was communicated before deliberations and the defense verdict; the judgment of the later jury did not remove the previously formed settlement. (Id. at 1120, ¶3; 1125, ¶¶16–17.)
