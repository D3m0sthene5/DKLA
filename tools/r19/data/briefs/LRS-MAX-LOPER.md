# Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo

**Course:** Legislation, Regulation & the State  
**Citation:** 144 S. Ct. 2244 (2024)  
**Court and date:** Supreme Court of the United States, June 28, 2024  
**Opinion:** Chief Justice Roberts, joined by Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett. Thomas and Gorsuch separately concurred. Kagan dissented, joined by Sotomayor and, only in the consolidated Relentless case, Jackson. Jackson did not participate in Loper Bright.  
**Assigned source:** LRS November pages 1–165 scan, PDF 158–165, including a duplicate of printed page 938 at PDF 158–159; continuation in November pages 165–end scan, PDF 1–17, through printed page 961 before Notes.  
**Brief ID:** LRS-MAX-LOPER

## Case orientation

The Court overruled Chevron's requirement that courts defer to reasonable agency interpretations merely because a statute is ambiguous. Under APA § 706, a reviewing court must exercise independent judgment in determining statutory meaning and whether the agency acted within its authority. Agencies' experience can persuade, and courts must respect actual congressional delegations of discretion. The Court vacated and remanded both circuit judgments without deciding whether the challenged fishery-monitoring rule was authorized on the merits. (Roberts slip opinion at 6–7 note 2, 16–18, 35.)

## Facts and regulatory setting

The Magnuson-Stevens Act extended federal fishery-management jurisdiction and established regional councils. NMFS administered the Act under the Secretary of Commerce. Fishery-management plans could require observers aboard domestic vessels. The statute expressly addressed observer costs for foreign vessels, certain limited-access-privilege programs, and the North Pacific fishery. It did not contain equivalent terms expressly addressing Atlantic herring fishermen's observer costs.

When government-funded coverage became uncertain, the New England council proposed industry funding. NMFS's 2020 rule sought observer coverage on 50 percent of trips by specified Atlantic herring permit holders. If NMFS required an observer but did not assign a federally funded one, the vessel had to contract with a certified third-party observer. NMFS estimated costs up to $710 daily and modeled reductions in annual owner returns up to 20 percent. Those were agency estimates, not findings that every petitioner actually incurred those losses. (Roberts at 1–4.)

## Proceedings and the consolidated cases

Four family fishing businesses challenged the rule in Loper Bright. The district court upheld it, and a divided D.C. Circuit affirmed using Chevron: statutory uncertainty led to deference to what the majority considered a reasonable agency construction. Judge Walker dissented, reading the statute to foreclose the payment obligation.

Relentless involved three businesses operating two vessels on longer trips that could participate in multiple fisheries. They challenged the same rule. The First Circuit also upheld it under Chevron. The Supreme Court took both cases to decide whether Chevron should be overruled or clarified, rather than to resolve the rule's substantive validity in the first instance. The resulting opinion addressed both judgments. (Roberts at 4–7, note 2.)

## The former Chevron framework

Chevron's first step asked whether Congress had directly answered the precise statutory question, using traditional interpretive tools. If so, the statutory answer controlled. If the statute was silent or ambiguous, step two directed the court to accept a permissible agency reading, even when its own interpretation would differ. Subsequent cases justified that framework through presumed congressional delegation, agency expertise, political accountability, and policymaking responsibilities. Brand X could permit an agency interpretation to supersede an earlier judicial construction unless the court had declared the statute unambiguous. (Roberts at 18–22.)

The disputed feature was this binding effect. The Court did not abolish consulting agencies' explanations or the deferential standards applicable to factual findings and discretionary policymaking.

## Independent judgment under the APA

Section 706 directs reviewing courts to decide relevant questions of law, interpret statutory and constitutional provisions, and determine the meaning or applicability of agency action. The majority read that instruction against the historical judicial duty of independent interpretation. Early decisions accorded substantial respect to contemporaneous, consistent executive interpretations, while preserving judicial judgment. Skidmore similarly treated administrative experience, reasoning, consistency, and thoroughness as persuasive considerations rather than controlling authority. (Roberts at 7–13, 16–17.)

The majority distinguished New Deal cases such as Gray and Hearst as involving delegated or factbound applications, rather than a general requirement to accept agency statutory constructions. It also acknowledged that the period's decisions were not entirely consistent. Kagan disputed this historical account; the majority's description should not be presented as an uncontested scholarly fact. (Roberts at 11–15, notes 3–4; Kagan at 15–23.)

The APA expressly makes certain factfinding and policymaking review deferential, but prescribes no equivalent mandatory deference for deciding statutory meaning. In the majority's view, its instruction to decide legal questions codified independent judicial judgment. Courts therefore cannot substitute a merely reasonable agency construction for the reading they determine is best. Statutory ambiguity alone does not establish that Congress delegated interpretive authority. Ambiguity may be accidental or reflect an unresolved issue rather than a choice to empower the agency. (Roberts at 13–16, 21–23.)

## What remains for agencies

An agency's informed interpretation remains relevant under Skidmore, particularly when contemporaneous with enactment and consistent over time. A court may use the agency's technical knowledge and the parties' evidence to resolve complex legal questions. Such assistance does not itself bind the court's legal interpretation. (Roberts at 16–17, 24–26.)

Congress can delegate discretion. Statutes may expressly authorize an agency to define a term, fill statutory details, or exercise judgment under flexible language such as appropriate or reasonable. The court must independently determine that the statute delegates that authority, identify its boundaries, and ensure reasoned agency decisionmaking within them. The opinion did not require that every delegation use one particular express formula. Neither did it authorize courts to replace choices that Congress actually entrusted to agencies with judges' policy preferences. (Roberts at 17–18, notes 5–6, 26.)

## Why the majority overruled Chevron

The majority rejected the broad delegation presumption as inconsistent with the APA and ordinary interpretation. Expertise may help identify facts and technical context, but does not automatically establish superior legal authority. Uniformity cannot justify adopting an incorrect statutory reading. Political accountability matters when Congress gives an agency policy discretion; it does not transform every unresolved question of legal meaning into such a delegation. (Roberts at 22–26.)

The majority also found Chevron's qualifications increasingly unstable: Mead's force-of-law requirement, procedural-validity restrictions, major questions, and other exceptions complicated identifying when deference applied. The Court itself had not relied on Chevron deference since 2016, although lower courts remained bound by the precedent. That history supported overruling; it did not mean Chevron had legally expired in 2016. (Roberts at 27–29.)

On stare decisis, the majority emphasized deficient reasoning, workability, and reliance. Chevron had not grappled with § 706, ambiguity was difficult to identify consistently, and agencies could change controlling interpretations over time. In its view, those features undermined claims that the doctrine reliably stabilized legal expectations. (Roberts at 29–34.)

## Prior holdings are not automatically undone

The Court expressly preserved statutory stare decisis for earlier holdings that particular agency actions were lawful, including Chevron's own Clean Air Act result. Mere reliance on Chevron is not a special justification sufficient to overrule such a holding. This protects precedents concerning specific actions; it is not a promise that every unlitigated regulation remains valid or that no older holding can ever be revisited for a distinct sufficient reason. (Roberts at 34–35.)

## Separate writings

Thomas joined the majority but argued that Chevron also violated constitutional separation of powers. It required judges to surrender their Article III interpretive responsibility and allowed executive agencies to exercise powers not vested in them. This additional constitutional rationale was Thomas's concurrence, not the majority's categorical constitutional holding. (Thomas at 1–4 and its footnote.)

Gorsuch emphasized three stare decisis considerations: written law controls over mistaken judicial precedent; precedent should be assessed against the broader historical body of law; and judicial opinions must be read in light of the disputes actually decided. He argued that Chevron conflicted with earlier independent-judgment principles, the APA, impartial adjudication, and fair notice. His examples involving veterans and immigrants illustrated perceived harms of shifting agency interpretations, rather than deciding those litigants' claims here. He understood overruling Chevron as restoring ordinary adjudication, not ensuring that agencies would always lose. (Gorsuch at 1–34 and all notes.)

Kagan, joined by Sotomayor and by Jackson only in Relentless, would retain Chevron. Congress necessarily leaves gaps in complex regulatory statutes. Agencies' technical knowledge, accumulated experience, and political accountability ordinarily make them better choices to resolve those gaps. Chevron's presumption could be defeated by statutory context, and step one required rigorous interpretation before deference. Its refinements were sensible limits on an otherwise useful allocation of responsibility. (Kagan at 1–14.)

Kagan disputed that § 706 specifies independent rather than deferential review of statutory interpretation. A court reviewing reasonableness still decides a legal question. She read the APA's historical backdrop, including Gray and Hearst, as permitting deference rather than forbidding it. She also defended statutory stare decisis, congressional opportunities to change Chevron, and decades of reliance by lawmakers, agencies, courts, businesses, and the public. Ambiguity triggers exist elsewhere in law, and the replacement regime also requires difficult judgments about persuasive weight and delegated discretion. (Kagan at 15–30.)

Finally, Kagan welcomed the majority's assurance about earlier judgments but considered it insufficient for reliance on agency interpretations never challenged in court. She feared substantial disruption and judicial assumption of technical and policy choices Congress had entrusted to agencies. Those were dissenting predictions, not additional holdings. (Kagan at 30–33.)

## Judgment and exam use

Both circuit judgments were vacated and remanded for proceedings consistent with independent judicial interpretation. Loper Bright's judgment was 6–2 because Jackson did not participate; Relentless's was 6–3. The opinion did not decide whether NMFS could lawfully require these fishermen to fund observers.

Separate four questions: the statute's best meaning; whether that meaning delegates discretion; whether the agency stayed within the delegation and acted reasonably; and what force an earlier judicial holding retains. Ambiguity alone no longer commands deference. Persuasive agency expertise and actual delegated policy authority remain part of the analysis.
