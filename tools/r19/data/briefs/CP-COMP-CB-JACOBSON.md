# Jacobson v. Miller

**41 Mich. 90, 1 N.W. 1013 (1879) · Supreme Court of Michigan · June 11, 1879 · Civil Procedure**

A judgment for earlier rent installments did not conclusively establish execution of the lease in a later suit for different installments, where execution had been admitted only by a pleading omission in the first case. The court reversed the second judgment, awarded costs, and ordered a new trial. The assigned account is CivPro Note 2, physical PDF page 648, printed page 1284.

## Two rent demands and the disputed lease

Miller first sued Gunzburg and Jacobson in 1877 for rent allegedly accruing between October 15, 1875, and April 9, 1877. The asserted lease covered premises in Grand Rapids for three years at $1,400 annual rent, payable monthly in advance. Defendants pleaded the general issue but did not deny execution of the lease under oath. Circuit Court Rule 79 therefore prevented them from disputing its execution in that case. Jacobson offered evidence that Gunzburg alone occupied the premises, although the appellate record did not clearly establish the significance he then claimed for that evidence. Miller obtained judgment. **41 Mich. at 91–92.**

Miller later sued for rent accruing from July 1877 through April 1878. This time Jacobson submitted an affidavit denying that he had signed, executed, or delivered the lease, or authorized anyone else to do so. That denial ordinarily required Miller to prove execution. Miller instead introduced the first suit's files, verdict, and judgment, which the trial judge treated as conclusive proof of the lease's execution. The judge reasoned that a judgment for rent necessarily resolved that foundational fact, even though the first case had received the lease without evidence for or against execution. **Id. at 92–93.**

The judge also rejected Jacobson's proposed evidence of a December 1875 arrangement under which Miller allegedly accepted Gunzburg as the tenant. An attempted showing of a later surrender also failed under the court's rulings. Miller again recovered judgment, and Jacobson sought review. **Id. at 93.**

## The difference between the adjudicated demand and a litigated issue

Justice Cooley distinguished two kinds of conclusiveness. A judgment settles the subject matter of the litigation: the right one party asserted against the other and asked the court to adjudicate. It also can settle a point of fact or law necessarily adjudicated in deciding that controversy. The first principle prevents a later suit from reopening the same adjudicated demand, including through a defense that should have been raised originally. The second requires attention to what the first proceeding actually put in dispute and determined. **Id. at 93–95.**

That distinction allowed Cooley to preserve finality without giving every allegation in an earlier case perpetual conclusiveness. A judgment settling the earlier rent demand stood whether correct or erroneous, contested or obtained by default. Jacobson could not reopen liability for those earlier installments by belatedly disputing the lease. But the present suit concerned later rent, a different demand. The question was whether execution had been adjudicated as an issue binding in this different controversy. **Id. at 94–97.**

The answer was no. By failing to submit the required sworn denial, defendants conceded execution for the purposes of the first litigation and removed it from the issues court and jury could consider. Even a special finding on execution would have been surplusage; a finding denying execution would have been outside the permitted issues. The prior judgment could therefore rely on the procedural admission without resolving the fact for every later action. **Id. at 95–99.**

If execution actually had been contested and determined in the first action, that determination could bind the parties in later litigation under the same lease. Conversely, a party's opportunity to contest a fact does not alone show that it was actually determined when the second action concerns a different demand. Cooley drew on Cromwell and distinguished precedents in which the disputed issue really had been resolved. He also criticized an inconsistent conversion case: a judgment rejecting a defense as legally insufficient could not be transformed into a finding denying the factual allegation that the court had accepted for that ruling. Finality protects the judgment made, not an adjudication that could or should have been made instead. **Id. at 95–99.**

## Surrender was left for the new trial

The court did not decide that Jacobson never executed the lease or that a surrender conclusively terminated liability. If Miller failed to prove execution at the new trial, surrender would become irrelevant. If execution was established, surrender might matter. The record did not clearly show which surrender issues had actually been litigated earlier, and some alleged transactions occurred after the first suit began, making them unavailable for determination in that proceeding. **Id. at 99–100.**

Jacobson's evidence that Miller received a store key and offered the premises for rent could bear on surrender, but those facts were not conclusive. Other circumstances might qualify or neutralize their significance. The appellate court accordingly declined to prescribe instructions in advance of the fuller factual record expected at a new trial. **Id. at 100.**

## Judgment, concurrence, and assigned question

The court **reversed with costs and ordered a new trial**. Chief Justice Campbell and Justice Marston concurred in Cooley's opinion. Justice Graves separately concurred, explaining that the first proceeding's effect on execution was comparable to an agreement to decide that particular case independently of whether the instrument had genuinely been executed. He agreed that the omission did not remove the sworn-denial option in the later action. **Id. at 100–101.** All four reported members agreed with the result; no dissent appears in the source.

The textbook places the account near a discussion of common-law compulsory counterclaims and asks whether possible landlord reliance should preclude the defense. Those questions should not obscure the actual holding: Jacobson was permitted to dispute execution when the later installments were sought. The court preserved the earlier judgment rather than allowing an attack on that existing liability. **Assigned physical 648, printed 1284; actual 41 Mich. at 95–101.**
