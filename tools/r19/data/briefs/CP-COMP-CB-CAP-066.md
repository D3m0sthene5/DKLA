# Rush v. Savchuk

444 U.S. 320 (1980) | Civil Procedure | Supreme Court of the United States

## Identity and assigned scope

No. 78-952, argued October 3, 1979, decided January 21, 1980. Justice Marshall wrote for Chief Justice Burger and Justices Stewart, White, Blackmun, Powell, and Rehnquist. Brennan and Stevens dissented separately. The supplied CivPro textbook presents the decision in Shaffer's Note 3, PDF page 135, printed page 222. The complete source includes Marshall's twenty-two footnotes, Stevens's dissent and note, and Brennan's incorporated joint dissent at 444 U.S. 299–313.

## Parties, facts, and relief

Savchuk was injured as a passenger in a car driven by Rush in Elkhart, Indiana, on January 13, 1972. Both were Indiana residents at the time. Rush's father owned the car, and State Farm insured it under an Indiana liability policy. Indiana's guest statute would have barred Savchuk's claim. Savchuk moved with his parents to Minnesota in June 1973 and sued there on May 28, 1974, after Indiana's two-year limitations period had expired. His requested $125,000 damages were later reduced to the policy's $50,000 limit. These were amounts sought, not damages awarded. (322–323 & nn. 1–5.)

Rush had no Minnesota contacts that would support personal jurisdiction. Savchuk instead sought quasi in rem jurisdiction by garnishing State Farm's contractual obligation to defend and indemnify Rush. State Farm did business in Minnesota and nationwide. Rush was personally served in Indiana. The insurer denied owing anything to Rush; Savchuk sought permission for a supplemental complaint adding it as garnishee. The garnishee was a party under that procedure, rather than the defendant against whom Savchuk asserted the tort claim. (322–324 & n. 6.)

## Procedural history

Rush and State Farm moved to dismiss. The trial court denied their motion and permitted the supplemental complaint. Minnesota's Supreme Court affirmed in Savchuk I, 245 N.W.2d 624 (1976), treating the obligation as attachable property and emphasizing notice, the policy limit, insurer control, and the state's interests. The U.S. Supreme Court vacated and remanded for reconsideration after Shaffer. Minnesota again upheld jurisdiction in Savchuk II, 272 N.W.2d 888 (1978), reasoning that the policy was closely connected to the litigation and the procedure served resident plaintiffs. The present appeal challenged that renewed judgment. (324–325.)

## Issue, rule, and holding

Can the insurer's Minnesota business and the attachment of its defense-and-indemnity obligation support jurisdiction over an insured who otherwise has no contacts with Minnesota, for an Indiana accident?

No. Under International Shoe and Shaffer, jurisdiction requires a constitutionally sufficient relationship among the particular defendant, the forum, and the litigation. Attachable property does not eliminate that inquiry. State Farm's contacts could not supply Rush's missing contacts merely because it insured him and would conduct the defense. The constitutional requirements must be satisfied for each defendant; forum contacts cannot simply be aggregated among the defending parties. (327–333.)

## Judgment

The Court reversed the Minnesota Supreme Court's judgment. It did not determine negligence, damages, insurance coverage, or the constitutional validity of applying Minnesota substantive tort law. The latter issue was expressly outside the appeal. (325 n. 8; 333.)

## Majority reasoning

Seider-type jurisdiction combined two legal fictions: a debt was located wherever its debtor could be found, and a corporation was present wherever it did business. On that account, State Farm's policy obligation was deemed Minnesota property, supposedly furnishing jurisdiction over Rush. The majority assumed the obligation was garnishable under state law without deciding that question. Its constitutional significance remained a separate issue. (325–328 & n. 14.)

The insurer's business decision was not purposeful Minnesota activity by Rush. Rush had no control over it and would not reasonably expect an Indiana insurance purchase to expose him to suit wherever a later plaintiff moved. Nor was the insurance policy the subject matter of the negligence claim or connected to its operative accident facts. It concerned how litigation would be defended and paid. Imagining the same obligation to be simultaneously present throughout State Farm's nationwide business did not create a meaningful contact with each state. (328–330.)

The suggested direct-action analogy also failed. Minnesota's procedure first needed power over the insured to bring the insurer in as garnishee; it did not independently authorize the tort victim to sue the insurer alone. The majority distinguished Watson's direct-action statute, which allowed an insurer-only suit and required an accident, injury, or insured domicile in the forum. Treating Rush as nominal did not cure the missing prerequisite. (330–331 & nn. 18–19.)

Insurance did not necessarily eliminate the insured's own stake. The majority noted reputational consequences, possible aggregate claims beyond coverage, and insurability effects. These were illustrative possibilities, not findings that Rush personally faced each one. More fundamentally, the plaintiff's Minnesota residence and the state's regulatory or remedial interests could not replace Rush's missing forum connection. Relationships between parties may matter in assessing their ties, but the insurer's activities were not automatically attributable to him. (331–333 & nn. 20–22.)

## Brennan's incorporated dissent

Brennan's dissent applies to both Rush and World-Wide Volkswagen; the Rush report expressly refers to its location at 299. He read International Shoe as an inquiry into practical fairness, giving greater weight to forum interests and the actual burden of defending. Modern communication and transportation reduced the justification for concentrating almost exclusively on defendants' direct forum activities. He still required an adequate relationship among the parties, litigation, and forum and did not propose jurisdiction without limits. (299–302, 308–313.)

For Rush specifically, Minnesota had a bona fide resident plaintiff, an interest in providing redress and regulating insurers, and a targeted garnishment statute. State Farm was amenable to suit there, controlled the defense, and would bear liability up to the policy limit. Brennan viewed Rush's additional burden as slight and the nationwide insurance network as a benefit to the insured. He disputed both the majority's treatment of the policy as unrelated to the suit and its description of State Farm's Minnesota activity as wholly adventitious to Rush. His qualifications reserved possible different results for a nonresident plaintiff, actual financial liability, or significant noneconomic consequences to the insured. (302–305 & nn. 3–7; 312 n. 20.)

## Stevens's dissent

Stevens treated the Minnesota procedure as functionally a direct action against the insurer. In his view it could proceed for a resident plaintiff even though the accident occurred elsewhere, provided Minnesota exercised no power over Rush individually. His understanding excluded compulsory participation through contempt, execution against Rush personally, and subsequent collateral-estoppel use against him. Policy cooperation duties were distinct from the forum's coercive authority. Because no such personal use of the judgment was presented, he would not require dismissal. He regarded possible reputational harm to an individual with no forum contacts as too remote to control the issue. (333–334 & dissenting note.)
