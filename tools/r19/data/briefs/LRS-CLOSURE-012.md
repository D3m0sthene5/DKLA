# Clark-Cowlitz Joint Operating Agency v. FERC
826 F.2d 1074 (D.C. Cir. 1987) (en banc) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, questions, and result

On August 11, 1987, the en banc D.C. Circuit decided No. 83-2231 by a 4–3 division among participating judges. Starr wrote the court's opinion; Mikva dissented with Robinson and Edwards. Ruth Bader Ginsburg, Bork, Starr, and Buckley formed the majority. Scalia had participated at argument but did not participate in the opinion; Senior Judge Wright subsequently recused himself. The court upheld FERC's authority to abandon its earlier interpretation of the municipal preference in hydroelectric relicensing and apply the new reading in this contest. It also held that the new reading was permissible. But it remanded FERC's economic justification for the particular license award as insufficiently reasoned, affirming the order in all other respects. (1075–1076 & participation notes; 1090–1092.)

## License contest and changed interpretation

Pacific Power & Light owned and operated the Merwin hydroelectric project on Washington's Lewis River between Clark and Cowlitz Counties. The original fifty-year license was issued in 1929; Pacific Power's predecessor transferred it to Pacific Power in 1941. Pacific Power applied for a new license in 1976. Public utility districts then formed Clark-Cowlitz to compete, and it applied in 1977, claiming the Federal Power Act's preference for state and municipal applicants whose plans were equally well adapted. (1076–1077 & n. 1.)

Because many project licenses were approaching expiration, FERC convened a declaratory proceeding on whether that preference applied when incumbent licensees sought relicensing. Both competitors participated. In City of Bountiful in 1980, FERC held that it did. The Eleventh Circuit upheld that interpretation under a deferential standard in Alabama Power in 1982. The interpretation was reasonable, but the appellate court had not decided that it was the only permissible construction. (1077–1078 & n. 2.)

Meanwhile FERC reconsidered. In a Supreme Court certiorari brief the Solicitor General disclosed the Commission's changed view and sought further consideration below; certiorari was denied. Merwin's individual proceedings became the first relicensing hearing after Bountiful. An ALJ found the competitors equally well adapted and ruled for Clark-Cowlitz, but the ruling remained subject to Commission review. In its 1983 Merwin decision, FERC overruled Bountiful and awarded the license to Pacific Power. It alternatively found the plans not equally well adapted because Pacific Power's customers would face greater replacement-power costs if it lost the project than Clark-Cowlitz's would if its application failed. (1077–1078, 1084–1085 & n. 10; dissent 1095–1097.)

While judicial review was pending, Congress's 1986 Electric Consumers Protection Act eliminated the municipal preference for relicensing but expressly excluded the Merwin proceeding. That exclusion preserved this controversy; it did not dictate its outcome. (1078 & n. 3; 1085–1086 & n. 12.)

## Preclusion did not freeze FERC's reading

Clark-Cowlitz argued that the declaratory order and its judicial affirmance barred the reinterpretation as to the participants. The majority distinguished the issue actually resolved earlier—whether Bountiful's interpretation was reasonable—from whether FERC's later contrary reading was also reasonable. An ambiguous statute might admit both. Eleventh Circuit dicta criticizing the alternative did not acquire preclusive force because that criticism was unnecessary to the judgment. Clark-Cowlitz and FERC also had supported the same position in the earlier litigation, rather than litigating the relevant issue adversely to each other. (1078–1081 & n. 4.)

Note 5 answered Mikva's emphasis on precluding Pacific Power itself. The decisionmaker changed its position, rather than Pacific Power litigating that Bountiful did not apply. Even assuming party preclusion was the proper focus, pure legal rulings could be reconsidered to avoid inequitable administration of law. Administrative adjudications warranted flexible treatment; binding Pacific Power here would treat the Merwin parties differently from similar participants now governed by the congressional amendment. That was part of the majority's justification, not a holding that agency declaratory orders can never have preclusive effect. (1080–1081 & n. 5.)

## Retroactivity and the five factors

The majority started from the general rule that an agency interpreting law in adjudication may apply its new interpretation in the proceeding before it. An exception could be warranted to prevent manifest injustice. Retail, Wholesale supplied five nonexclusive considerations: whether the case was one of first impression; an abrupt departure from established practice versus filling an unsettled area; actual reliance; the burden imposed; and the statutory interest in applying the new rule despite reliance. The court evaluated them rather than treating the agency's choice as automatically controlling. (1081–1082.)

The first factor favored application because Merwin was the proceeding announcing the changed rule. Note 6 explained that “first impression” in this test did not necessarily mean an issue never previously addressed: it could mean the case first announcing the new principle, in contrast to a later case applying it. Allowing the party who helped bring about a change to benefit encouraged challenges to outworn doctrine; the other factors could outweigh that consideration. Mikva disputed this understanding. (1081–1083 & n. 6.)

For the second factor, Bountiful was a single recent pronouncement on a formerly dormant issue, not a long-established practice repeatedly applied and confirmed. FERC changed course before its judicial-review process had fully concluded. The new rule did not fill a complete void, but the Commission believed its earlier reading contradicted congressional intent and risked locking the mistake into decades-long licenses. On balance, this favored application of the new interpretation. (1082–1083 & nn. 7–8.)

For reliance, Clark-Cowlitz's formation and initial application predated Bountiful. The majority allowed, at most, a short period of optimism following appellate approval before the Solicitor General disclosed the forthcoming change. That disclosure made later reliance on continued adherence unreasonable, notwithstanding FERC's separate concern about litigation preclusion. Hope of prevailing was not equivalent to justified reliance. The opinion describes this reasonable-reliance interval as no more than six months; its inconsistent references to the month of the earlier appellate decision do not alter the stated analysis. (1083–1084 & n. 9.)

For burden, Clark-Cowlitz lost an attractive competitive preference but retained the right to seek the license on the merits. It had never received a final license; the ALJ's favorable ruling was subject to review. There was no new liability for past conduct, fine, or damages. The majority regarded this burden as marginal compared with other retroactivity cases, rather than treating Clark-Cowlitz as deprived of an already vested license. (1084–1085 & n. 10.)

For statutory interest, applying Bountiful would preserve a thirty-year advantage FERC now believed inconsistent with Congress's best-qualified-applicant objective. That interest outweighed the brief reliance period. Additional fairness considerations favored treating Clark-Cowlitz like eight other municipal applicants with pending matters rather than granting it a special benefit because its hearing happened first. Note 12 expressly disclaimed treating the new legislation as directly resolving Merwin. (1085–1086 & nn. 11–12.)

## Permissible statutory reading

Under the then-governing Chevron framework, the court used ordinary interpretive tools and found that Congress had not clearly answered whether the municipal preference applied when an incumbent sought renewal. Section 7(a) referred to licenses to new licensees under § 15, while § 15 distinguished new from original licensees. FERC reasonably treated an incumbent as an original licensee and applied the preference only where an incumbent was not competing. Contrary readings either disregarded that distinction or replaced issuance language with language about entertaining applications that Congress had used elsewhere. The agency could tell from the outset whether an original licensee participated; it was not postponing the preference until after choosing a winner. (1086–1088 & nn. 13–14.)

The court acknowledged tension with the phrase “as between other applicants” and the force of the alternative reading. But a court could not replace a reasonable agency construction merely because another sounded more natural. Legislative history was inconclusive, with no compelling direction against FERC's reading. The new interpretation reasonably accommodated private investment and public interests: municipalities could compete against an incumbent but needed superior qualifications to displace it, rather than a tie-breaking advantage based only on identity. This was not an independent incumbent renewal expectancy. (1088–1090 & nn. 15–16.)

## Economic discretion did not excuse incomplete analysis

FERC could consider economic consequences beyond the applicants' technical plans. The second clause's permissive “may,” contrasted with the first clause's “shall,” and omission of a plan-equalization provision supported discretion to consider extrinsic factors. Section 10(a)'s comprehensive public-interest standard and the Commission's technical expertise supported that reading. (1090–1091 & n. 17.)

FERC nevertheless failed adequately to explain the economics here. It reasoned that Pacific Power would need expensive thermal or Bonneville new-resources power if displaced, while Clark-Cowlitz could continue buying lower-priced Bonneville priority-firm power if unsuccessful. The claimed net cost favored Pacific Power's customers. But that customer-only balance risked creating an effective incumbent preference, ignored that an award to Clark-Cowlitz could free subsidized Bonneville power for other regional customers, and did not account for the region's unchanged total energy needs and power sources despite a change in project control. The court required FERC to confront those implications, not adopt a particular economic theory. (1091–1092.)

The exact disposition was a remand for further elucidation of the determination that Pacific Power's higher alternative costs justified the award. All other aspects were affirmed. The court did not direct the license to Clark-Cowlitz or simply uphold the award without qualification. (1092.)

## Mikva's dissent

Mikva rejected the majority's starting presumption favoring retroactivity. He understood Chenery and Retail, Wholesale to require independent balancing without a tilted scale. Agencies had the alternative of prospective rulemaking; courts owed no special deference on the legal question of retroactivity, and an agency needed to explain its choice, including less drastic alternatives. He characterized Merwin as a case of second impression reversing an established standard and argued that a rule could be firmly established even if not old or repeatedly applied. (1092–1095.)

He also emphasized the concrete reliance omitted or minimized by the majority: FERC ordered pending matters to proceed under Bountiful, Clark-Cowlitz pressed for hearings and invested in discovery, experts, briefs, and litigation, and the parties treated the preference as settled. Changes in Commission personnel, a closed meeting, and the attempt to secure certiorari did not adequately notify the parties that FERC would reverse the rule in Merwin; FERC's own suggestion that Bountiful would bind participants if certiorari were denied supported continued reliance. Loss of the license contest's decisive advantage burdened an entity formed for that specific project. Congress's express Merwin exception, in his view, eliminated any statutory need to deny this applicant the prior standard, and FERC had supplied no retroactivity explanation at all. At minimum, he would remand for that explanation. (1095–1099.)

On preclusion he focused on the competing parties' adjudication of a ripe controversy, not merely whether FERC was barred by its prior advocacy. Pacific Power should not escape a final declaratory resolution because the decisionmaker changed its mind. He warned that otherwise administrative declaratory proceedings could become costly dress rehearsals. On statutory interpretation he considered the preference an instruction for selecting the winner, not simply issuing the eventual license, and read the “other applicants” clause within the three identified situations rather than as an independent catch-all. He concluded that the retroactive reversal was unlawful, inadequately reasoned, preclusively barred, and supported in part by an unreasonable statutory interpretation. (1099–1101.)
