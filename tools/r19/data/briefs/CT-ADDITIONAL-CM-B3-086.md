# Kooleraire Service & Installation Corp. v. Board of Education
28 N.Y.2d 101, 268 N.E.2d 782 (1971), decided March 3, 1971 | Contracts

## Court, transaction, and trial posture
Judge Bergan wrote for the New York Court of Appeals. Chief Judge Fuld and Judges Scileppi, Breitel, Jasen, and Gibson concurred; Judge Burke did not participate. The six participating judges agreed, and the report contains no separate writing or judicial footnote. (101, 105–107.)

Kooleraire was a prequalified bidder for heating and ventilation work at J.H.S. 56 in Manhattan. Its low bid was $418,175. The Board awarded the contract on July 21, 1965; Kooleraire executed it on August 12 and the Board on August 13. Article 44 made the agreement nonbinding until the comptroller endorsed a certificate that an unexpended, unapplied appropriation balance was sufficient to meet the estimated expense. Kooleraire began preliminary work. On November 17 the Board rescinded. No certification had occurred, although uncontradicted trial proof showed adequate funds and certification and registration of a later contract for the same purpose. The Board itself had asked the comptroller to withhold registration, a fact it did not dispute. (105.)

Kooleraire sued for breach. The Board pleaded two affirmative defenses: lack of certification under the contract and Administrative Code section 93c-3.0, and justification for rescission based on alleged false statements in Kooleraire’s prequalification application. The trial court deliberately tried only the certificate issue, reserving the factual merits of the second defense if further trial became necessary. It entered judgment for the Board solely because the certificate was absent. The Appellate Division and trial court agreed that the contractual provision, rather than the cited statute, governed the certification question. (105–106.)

## Issue, arguments, and prevention
The narrow issue was whether the Board could invoke a condition’s nonoccurrence after requesting the comptroller not to perform the certification duty, even though sufficient funds existed. The Board contended that no binding contract had arisen and that its obstruction was justified by the contractor’s alleged misconduct. The contractor argued that certification served only to confirm available funds, and that the Board could not convert its own obstruction into a defense. The published counsel accounts also advanced formation and waiver theories; the court’s reasoning resolved the limited certification/prevention issue. (102–106.)

The court interpreted article 44 by its purpose. The parties could reasonably have intended a certificate confirming adequate funds; nothing in the instrument suggested an independent comptroller veto. A party ordinarily cannot rely on another’s failure to satisfy a condition precedent when it has frustrated or prevented that condition. Here funds existed, but the Board induced noncertification and sought to use the missing certificate to defeat contractual liability for a different reason. That maneuver repurposed a funding safeguard into a means of avoiding the agreement. (106–107.)

The prevention rule did not make all obstruction unjustifiable. The other party’s conduct can justify it, but this record contained only the Board’s assertion of false prequalification statements. Their truth and legal significance had not been tried, and no request had been made to litigate justification within the trial’s limited scope. The Board could litigate that defense on its merits later; it could not obtain a finding of justified prevention from an evidentiary record that deliberately omitted the relevant issue. (106–107.)

The court distinguished Albany Supply & Equipment Co. v. City of Cohoes, which involved a statute making the contract invalid and an effort by the contractor and officials to evade that statute. Kooleraire instead involved a contractual funding certification, with sufficient money actually available. The prevention analysis did not announce a general power to disregard statutory municipal-contract requirements. Related municipal decisions supplied context but were not treated as precisely controlling the same facts. (107.)

## Judgment and significance
The Court of Appeals reversed the order with costs and remitted the case to Supreme Court, New York County, for further proceedings. It awarded no quantified damages and did not resolve whether the asserted prequalification misconduct ultimately justified rescission. The contractor defeated the certification defense on the record tried; the reserved defense remained available for litigation. (107.)

Kooleraire illustrates that even a condition framed as necessary to a contract’s effective existence must be interpreted in relation to its purpose and the parties’ conduct. A funding condition cannot ordinarily be defeated deliberately and then invoked as a substitute for proving a disputed substantive defense. The opinion also shows why prevention and ultimate liability must remain distinct when the trial addressed only one preliminary issue.
