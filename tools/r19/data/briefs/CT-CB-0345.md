# Channel Home Centers, Division of Grace Retail Corp. v. Grossman
795 F.2d 291 (3d Cir. 1986) | Contracts

## The proposed anchor store and the signed letter
Channel operated home-improvement stores and was considering space in Cedarbrook Mall in Cheltenham Township, near Philadelphia. Developer Frank Grossman and his sons controlled the entities acquiring and leasing the mall. During the relevant negotiations, Baker Investment Corporation was the equitable owner, Equitable Life Assurance Society held legal title, and the closing was expected in February 1985. The developers hoped to revive a struggling mall through rehabilitation and new tenants. Channel would be a fourth anchor store and could help make the acquisition attractive to financing sources. 795 F.2d at 292–293.

After a November 28, 1984 tour and preliminary discussions, Channel's officers indicated interest in leasing the site. Grossman requested a letter of intent that he could show banks or others. Channel prepared and signed a detailed letter dated December 11, which Grossman promptly signed and returned. It described approximately 70,400 square feet, a twenty-five-year term, stepped base rents, percentage rent, renewal options, tax and maintenance responsibilities, and work the landlord would perform. Execution of a mutually satisfactory lease remained subject to approval by Channel's corporate parent, satisfactory title, and necessary sign permits or variances. The full letter also said that expenditures before execution of the lease would be at the spending party's own risk. Id. at 293–294 & n.2.

Most important, the letter stated that, to induce Channel to proceed, Grossman would withdraw the store from the rental market and negotiate only the described transaction to completion. That provision was the asserted commitment. Channel conceded that the letter did not itself bind the parties to the final lease. It argued instead that the parties had made an enforceable agreement about how they would conduct the negotiations while working toward one. Grossman maintained that an agreement to negotiate was unenforceable without agreement on the underlying transaction and that Channel had supplied no consideration. Id. at 298–301.

## Performance toward the lease and the competing tenant
Both sides undertook steps consistent with pursuing the transaction. Channel directed its parent's legal department to prepare the lease, secured its own real-estate committee's approval, measured the premises, developed marketing and construction plans, arranged delivery schedules, and purchased necessary materials or equipment. Its submissions indicated expenditures of about $25,000. The developers applied for zoning permission for signs. These activities were evidence of the parties' intentions; they did not conclusively establish formation of either the final lease or the preliminary contract. Id. at 294–295, 300.

On January 11, 1985, Channel's lawyer sent a forty-one-page draft lease and requested documents for exhibits. The developers supplied title and site information on January 16 and said that work on Channel's location had commenced. A January 17 letter expressed anticipation of executing the lease soon. During a January 23 call, the parties discussed concerns including a use clause and arranging a further conference. They later disputed who was supposed to initiate that conference. Id. at 294–295.

Meanwhile, Mr. Good Buys, a direct competitor of Channel, contacted Grossman around January 22 and toured Channel's proposed space on January 25. On February 6, Grossman announced that negotiations with Channel had terminated because Channel had failed to submit a signed, mutually acceptable lease within thirty days of the December 11 letter. The letter contained no thirty-day deadline. Grossman testified that Channel's officers had orally agreed to send a draft within that time; they denied agreeing to any finite deadline. The February 6 notice was the first written evidence of the alleged time restriction. On February 7, Grossman executed a lease with Mr. Good Buys at substantially higher base rents, although the competing lease did not require Channel's proposed percentage rent. Channel's parent approved its proposed lease on February 13. Id. at 295–296 & n.3.

The chronology supported Channel's claim that the defendants negotiated with a rival while they had promised exclusivity. It did not conclusively settle whether the preliminary obligation existed, whether an agreed deadline had expired, or what relief would follow from a breach.

## A premature merits judgment and a waived procedural objection
Channel filed its diversity action on February 15 in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania, asserting breach and seeking preliminary relief. A temporary restraining order protected its position pending a February 25 preliminary-injunction hearing. The hearing lasted about ten minutes and principally covered limited deposition material. Without advance notice, the district court then treated the preliminary hearing as a trial on the merits under Rule 65(a)(2), denied relief, and entered judgment against Channel on March 26. It concluded that the letter lacked binding intent and consideration and did not satisfy the statute of frauds for a lease. Id. at 296–297.

Channel sought reconsideration and explained that a final trial required further discovery, including evidence concerning financing and the other anchor tenants. On May 7, the district court adhered to its judgment but offered another evidentiary hearing if Channel requested it within fifteen days. Channel did not request one. On appeal it said that an additional hearing without discovery would be useless, but it had not asked the district court for further discovery or more time in response to the offer. Id. at 297–298.

The Third Circuit strongly disapproved of the original procedure. Rule 65(a)(2) requires clear notice early enough to give the parties a full opportunity to present their cases before a preliminary hearing becomes a final trial. The district court had committed error by providing no advance notice. Nevertheless, Channel's failure to use the later opportunity or ask for the discovery it thought necessary waived this procedural objection. The appellate court therefore did not reverse on that ground. Its reversal rested on the substantive treatment of the preliminary agreement. Id. at 297–298.

## A negotiation contract can be different from the contemplated lease
Writing for Judges Weis and Higginbotham as well as himself, Judge Becker addressed Pennsylvania law. No Pennsylvania court had squarely considered the particular agreement-to-negotiate question. Looking to contract principles and decisions from other jurisdictions, the panel predicted that Pennsylvania would enforce such an agreement if it otherwise satisfied the requirements for a contract: manifested intent to be bound, sufficiently definite terms, and consideration. The holding concerns an expressly undertaken negotiation obligation, not a universal duty of good faith at every precontractual stage. The court separately distinguished the common-law duty of good faith in performing an existing contract. Id. at 298–299 & n.8.

The district court's analysis had focused on the wrong potential contract. Channel did not ask to enforce the letter as a completed lease. Thus the failure to satisfy the statute of frauds governing leases did not defeat the different promise actually asserted. The final transaction's contingencies and unfinished provisions did not necessarily prevent a binding, narrower agreement to withdraw the premises from the market and negotiate exclusively in good faith. Id. at 298–299 & n.7.

Intent required examining the whole document and the circumstances surrounding its adoption. The explicit exclusivity language supported Channel's position, as did the parties' reciprocal efforts to satisfy contingencies and their communications about completing the lease. The letter's detail gave the negotiation promise context and made its subject identifiable. Taken together, this evidence could support a finding that the parties intended the preliminary commitment to bind them even while leaving the final lease for later approval and agreement. The court did not hold that the evidence compelled that finding. Conflicting evidence about intent remained for the factfinder. Id. at 299–301 & n.9.

## Consideration without a payment
Grossman argued that Channel paid no money or thing of value for the negotiation promise. The court rejected that understanding of consideration. Channel executed and tendered the letter because Grossman wanted a document he could use to help obtain permanent financing for his purchase. Securing a serious prospective anchor tenant's written expression of interest could benefit the developer. A bargained-for benefit need not be a cash payment. Id. at 300–301.

The exchange was therefore intelligible on both sides. Grossman wanted the signed letter for his financing effort; Channel, aware of that purpose, sought the developer's signed commitment to remove the space from the market and negotiate only with it. The record supported a finding that Channel's execution and delivery of the letter supplied a bargained-for benefit in return for that promise. The court did not make actual receipt of a bank loan based on the letter the necessary consideration. Nor did it resolve the case solely through Channel's expenditures: the consideration analysis identified the requested and exchanged letter itself. Id. at 300–301.

The panel did not reach the district court's rejection of alternative unilateral-contract and promissory-estoppel analyses because its treatment of the mutual negotiation agreement made that unnecessary. The case thus should not be represented as a holding that reliance alone made the final lease enforceable. Id. at 297 n.6.

## Holding, remand, and limits
The Third Circuit held that the record could support a binding agreement to negotiate in good faith, that the described negotiation promise was sufficiently definite, and that the asserted exchange supplied consideration. It reversed the district court's judgment and remanded for a full trial. Id. at 301.

At least two issues remained: whether the parties actually intended to bind themselves to the preliminary agreement, and whether they agreed to a thirty-day time limit. If Channel had undertaken to deliver a draft within thirty days and failed to do so, the obligation could have terminated. If there was no fixed deadline, a reasonable time would apply, requiring a factual determination in context. The court encouraged additional, expedited discovery and did not foreclose other formation questions. Id. at 301 & n.10.

The opinion did not award possession of the space, damages, or specific performance of a completed lease. It established that a court cannot reject an express negotiation commitment merely because the ultimate contract is unfinished. For studying preliminary agreements, its practical contribution is to identify the particular obligation asserted, test that obligation for intent, definiteness, and consideration, and preserve the distinction between enforceability in principle and the factual determinations necessary for final liability.
