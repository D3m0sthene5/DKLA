# Wojtas v. Capital Guardian Trust Co.

**477 F.3d 924 (7th Cir. 2007) — United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, February 15, 2007, No. 05-4248.** Judge Sykes wrote for the panel of Chief Judge Easterbrook and Judges Manion and Sykes; no separate opinion is reported. Civil Procedure: voluntary dismissal under Rule 41(a)(2), a limitations defense, and waiver by failure to respond. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 528, printed 1037–1038.

## Facts and procedural posture

Bonnie Wojtas owned an IRA held by Capital Guardian Trust, later known as Capital Bank & Trust. In April 1999 she directed Capital to transfer the investments to an IRA managed by Insurance Planning & Design. An employee of the successor company converted the funds and was later convicted of mail fraud. Bonnie and her husband, who claimed a marital-property interest in the money, sued the original custodian for negligence and breach of fiduciary duty. They alleged that Capital should have checked whether the successor they selected was qualified by the IRS to act as custodian. Capital removed their state suit to federal court in the Western District of Wisconsin on diversity grounds. (925.)

Capital answered and moved for judgment on the pleadings under Rule 12(c). It argued that both claims rested on the same alleged fiduciary breach and were barred by Wisconsin’s two-year limitations period. The complaint came more than two years after the plaintiffs said they learned of their injury through the successor employee’s indictment. Alternatively, Capital argued it owed no duty to investigate their selected successor. (925.)

The plaintiffs answered the no-duty argument but offered no response to the limitations argument. They instead requested dismissal without prejudice under Rule 41(a)(2), proposing to refile in Illinois under a longer limitations period. The district court denied voluntary dismissal and entered judgment on the pleadings, dismissing the suit with prejudice as untimely. They appealed both decisions. (925–926.)

## The unanswered limitations argument

On appeal, the plaintiffs argued their negligence claim remained timely under Wisconsin’s six-year property-injury limitations provision because Capital’s motion supposedly addressed only the fiduciary-duty claim. The court rejected that description: the motion unambiguously sought dismissal of **both** claims and argued that negligence merely relabeled the same alleged breach. (926.)

The plaintiffs’ failure to oppose that argument constituted waiver, in the court’s terminology. Their silence supported the district court’s inference that they acquiesced in the proposed limitations treatment. The appellate decision thus upheld dismissal on the grounds raised and left unanswered; it did not separately decide Capital’s alternative contention that no investigative duty existed. Nor should the plaintiffs’ requested six-year period be presented as the court’s governing rule. (925–926.)

## Holding: loss of a vested defense was plain legal prejudice

Because Capital had answered, the requested Rule 41(a)(2) dismissal required a court order. The district court had discretion both over whether to allow dismissal and over its terms. Allowing dismissal when it would cause the defendant plain legal prejudice would abuse that discretion. (927.)

The plaintiffs relied on *Bolten*, where voluntary dismissal allowed refiling in a state with a longer limitations period. The court gave two answers. First, *Bolten*’s earlier treatment of dismissal as a right, with discretion confined to conditions, had been expressly rejected by later circuit decisions. Second, the relevant state-law consequences differed. The limitations bar in *Bolten* had concerned the remedy in that forum. Wisconsin law extinguished the cause of action as well as the remedy and created a vested right in the defendant to insist on the bar. (927.)

Capital would lose that substantive protection if the plaintiffs could obtain dismissal without prejudice and avoid the Wisconsin bar through refiling. That loss constituted plain legal prejudice. The problem therefore extended beyond the ordinary expense and annoyance of defending a second lawsuit. The district court did not abuse its discretion by refusing dismissal. (927–928.)

## Disposition and limits

The court **affirmed** denial of voluntary dismissal and the judgment on the pleadings dismissing both claims with prejudice on limitations grounds. It did not order dismissal subject to reimbursement, remand for a factual trial, or decide whether the original custodian committed an actionable breach. (925, 928.)

The opinion should not be reduced to a universal proposition that a plaintiff may never seek dismissal to pursue a longer limitations period elsewhere. Its reasoning includes Wisconsin’s extinction of the right, Capital’s vested defense, and the plaintiffs’ waiver of the argument that both pleaded claims fell under the shorter period. Conversely, *Bolten* did not provide an unconditional right to dismissal after an answer. (926–928.)
