# Williams v. Curtin
807 F.2d 1046 (D.C. Cir. 1986) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and disposition
The D.C. Circuit decided the case on December 23, 1986, after argument on September 26. Senior District Judge Jameson, sitting by designation from Montana, wrote for a panel with Circuit Judges Silberman and Williams. The report contains no dissent or separate opinion. The court affirmed the district court's judgment sustaining a Department of Agriculture reparation decision in Curtin's favor. The affirmed award was $47,435.10, with the additional interest and attorney-fee components described by the district court; the appellate court ordered no new trial or recalculation. (1046–1047, 1049, 1052.)

## Cabbage sale, market change, and reduced quantity
Ray G. Williams of Georgia packaged, loaded, and sold produce grown by others. Thomas A. Curtin of New York bought, stored, and sold cabbage primarily for coleslaw processing. Both held licenses under the Perishable Agricultural Commodities Act, or PACA. In January 1982 Curtin visited Williams in Georgia. They agreed on roughly thirty acres of cabbage, expected to yield 600–800 tons, at $136 per ton, with f.o.b. delivery between May 1 and June 15. Curtin stated a preference for large heads: twelve or fewer in a fifty-pound sack. He advanced $4,000. Williams arranged for the Baker brothers to grow the crop on about thirty-three acres, splitting sales profits with them. Plant spacing sought to produce the desired large cabbage. (1048.)

After further telephone discussions and Curtin's requests for a writing, Williams sent a signed contract dated March 8 on March 29. With Curtin's changes, the final writing referred to approximately thirty acres or 600–800 tons of slaw cabbage. It priced loose or bin-box loads at $120 per ton and bagged loads at $136, provided for $10,000 in growing-season advances to be deducted from purchases, and required Williams to assist in arranging trucks. The writing used slaw cabbage rather than an express numerical head-size limitation. (1048.)

Cabbage prices then rose sharply, from roughly $3–$4 per fifty-pound sack in January to as much as $12 in May. On May 6 Curtin unexpectedly visited Williams. They inspected the field and found that most heads were not reaching the desired size. Curtin said he wanted all the cabbage he could get and would be satisfied with 400 tons. Williams did not object that only large heads qualified. Curtin advanced another $6,000, which Williams accepted. The later discussion reduced the agreed volume and also supplied evidence of how the parties understood the original size issue. (1048, 1050 & n. 4.)

The field ultimately produced 518.4 tons. Williams delivered only the 152.42 tons of large cabbage to Curtin's designated locations and marketed the smaller heads elsewhere. Cabbage supplied to Curtin averaged $3.07 per sack, compared with about $10 for cabbage sold to other buyers. Curtin had to buy on the open market to meet commitments, at an average $400 per ton. Williams claimed an unpaid $24,121.02 for the delivered cabbage, representing $34,121.02 less the $10,000 advances, and commenced a PACA proceeding when Curtin refused payment. Curtin counterclaimed for the short delivery. (1049.)

## Agency findings and district-court review
Williams maintained that slaw cabbage meant large cabbage to members of the trade. Curtin maintained that it meant cabbage suitable for coleslaw and that all the crop qualified under 1982 conditions. The Agriculture Department's judicial officer found all of the crop suitable for coleslaw and found no commonly used slaw-cabbage term in coleslaw production or marketing establishing the claimed size restriction. It calculated Curtin's cover loss using the difference between the 152.42 tons received and the modified 400-ton obligation. This was not an award calculated from the entire 518.4-ton yield or the initial 600–800-ton expectation. (1049.)

PACA provided a district-court trial de novo, with the Secretary's findings and orders serving as prima facie evidence. The district court adopted the supported findings after considering testimony and exhibits, including additional witnesses, and upheld the award. It expressly regarded the May conversation as a modification reducing quantity to 400 tons. Williams appealed, arguing that the court had neglected actual intention, should have found an obligation to deliver only large heads, and should have tested usage exclusively within Georgia. (1047–1049 & nn. 1–3.)

## Intention shown by conduct and adopted findings
The appellate court rejected the premise that the district court failed to consider intention merely because it did not make an independent express finding using that word. It had adopted the judicial officer's particular findings about what the contract required. Subsequent discussions and conduct were strong evidence of the parties' understanding: when both could see that insufficient large cabbage was growing, the discussion concerned reducing overall tonnage, and Williams did not insist that size excluded smaller heads. Acceptance of the second substantial advance reinforced that understanding. Curtin's preference for large cabbage was therefore distinguishable from a contractual condition restricting performance to those heads. The adopted intent findings were not clearly erroneous, and remand was unnecessary. (1050.)

## Trade usage in the relevant interstate transaction
Williams relied on UCC § 1-205(5), directing use of an applicable local usage for performance occurring in that locality. Because planting, growing, cutting, sizing, and bagging occurred in Georgia, he argued that only Georgia usage mattered. The court instead identified the pertinent performance as delivery into an interstate market. Both parties lived and dealt in separate states, both were licensed for interstate produce commerce, their negotiations crossed state lines, and Williams regularly sold to out-of-state buyers. The transaction therefore was not a subtrade consisting exclusively of the Georgia market. The court applied the usage inquiry to the particular interstate coleslaw-processing market in which the sale occurred. It drew on the UCC definition requiring regular observance sufficient to justify an expectation in the transaction, and the official comment concerning what the language fairly means to the parties in that locality, vocation, or trade. (1051–1052.)

The evidence independently supported rejection of Williams' alleged size usage. Only he had testified to it before the agency. The district court found Curtin's experienced processing witnesses more persuasive, while two of Williams' additional witnesses had prior dealings with Curtin and preferred not to do business with him. Earlier transactions between these parties used large cabbage rather than slaw cabbage. Footnote 7 also invoked PACA authority requiring proof through numerous instances of practice rather than a witness's opinion. The finding that the asserted regular usage was unproved was supported by substantial evidence and not clearly erroneous. Thus the decision did not adjudicate priority between two conclusively proven conflicting usages. (1049 n. 3, 1051 n. 6, 1052 & n. 7.)

Georgia and New York had enacted identical relevant versions of § 1-205. The court accordingly did not need to decide the choice-of-law issue; its analysis remained the same under either. This reservation should not be replaced with a holding that Georgia law, New York law, or the law of interstate trade categorically governed every aspect of the contract. (1051 n. 5.)
