# Turner v. Rogers

**564 U.S. 431 (2011) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 20, 2011.** Civil Procedure. The assigned account is CivPro physical page 172, printed page 297, Note 5’s Turner discussion after Lassiter and before the appended notes. Justice Breyer wrote for Justices Kennedy, Ginsburg, Sotomayor, and Kagan. Justice Thomas dissented, joined throughout by Justice Scalia; Chief Justice Roberts and Justice Alito joined only Parts I–B and II of that dissent. The judgment was 5–4.

## Facts and procedural history

In June 2003 a South Carolina family court ordered Michael Turner to pay Rebecca Rogers $51.73 per week in child support. Rogers’s father, Larry Price, later had custody of the child and was also a respondent in the Supreme Court. Over the following three years Turner repeatedly failed to pay. Four contempt findings produced 90-day sentences, but he paid the amounts due either before confinement or after a few days in jail. On the fifth occasion he did not pay and served six months. 564 U.S. at 436.

After his release, Turner remained in arrears. A March 2006 show-cause order eventually led, after postponement because he failed to appear, to a January 3, 2008 hearing. Both Turner and Rogers appeared without counsel. The clerk reported $5,728.76 in arrears. Asked whether he had anything to say, Turner described drug use, intermittent payments and work, a broken back, and applications for disability benefits. He apologized and asked for another chance. The judge briefly heard from Rogers and then found willful contempt, imposed 12 months’ imprisonment, allowed purging by reducing the balance to zero, and placed a lien on benefits. 564 U.S. at 436–437.

The court did not expressly find that Turner could pay. His wife had voluntarily submitted a disability application, but the judge did not pursue questions about his finances. A standardized contempt form offered alternatives for whether he was employed and able to pay; the judge left those alternatives unselected. With pro bono counsel, Turner appealed while imprisoned, asserting a constitutional right to counsel. After he completed the sentence, the South Carolina Supreme Court rejected that claim because the proceeding was civil contempt and did not require every safeguard applicable in criminal cases. The United States Supreme Court granted review because lower courts disagreed about counsel in child-support contempt proceedings. 564 U.S. at 437–439.

## Threshold question: mootness

Respondents argued that completion of the sentence ended the controversy, with no alleged collateral consequences to keep it alive. The Court relied instead on the capable-of-repetition-yet-evading-review exception. A period of confinement lasting no more than one year was too short to complete state litigation and Supreme Court review. Repetition was likely for Turner personally: he had repeatedly defaulted and been confined, faced another contempt proceeding within months of release, served another six months, and had substantial arrears with a further hearing scheduled. This was not merely the possibility that someone else might face the same issue. The Court also distinguished failure to seek a stay of a federal contempt order from Turner’s state proceedings, which were not readily subject to immediate Supreme Court review. 564 U.S. at 439–441. Thomas agreed that the case was not moot. Id. at 450 n.1.

## Issue and governing distinction

Does due process automatically require appointed counsel for an indigent parent facing incarceration for civil contempt based on unpaid child support? If not, what protection was required in Turner’s circumstances?

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel governing criminal prosecutions does not itself control civil contempt. Civil contempt aims to coerce compliance: a person who complies is purged of contempt and released. A person clearly unable to comply cannot properly be imprisoned under that rationale. Accurate determination of ability to pay therefore protects liberty and preserves the distinction between coercive civil confinement and criminal punishment, which carries additional protections. 564 U.S. at 441–442, 445.

Prior civil-counsel cases did not establish an automatic entitlement whenever incarceration was possible. Lassiter’s physical-liberty discussion identified the setting in which a right had been recognized, without declaring that every proceeding threatening confinement required counsel. Gagnon allowed case-specific consideration of counsel at probation revocation, and the controlling Vitek position allowed suitable professional assistance rather than necessarily a lawyer. The Court accordingly used the Mathews framework to consider the liberty interest, error risk and value of additional or substitute safeguards, and countervailing interests. 564 U.S. at 442–445.

## Holding and reasoning

The Court declined to require counsel automatically in proceedings of the kind before it, while requiring adequate alternative safeguards. Freedom from imprisonment was a powerful private interest. Wrongly finding ability to pay could produce wrongful confinement and deprive the alleged contemnor of the safeguards associated with criminal punishment. Nevertheless, three considerations favored an alternative to a categorical appointed-lawyer rule. 564 U.S. at 445–448.

First, the usual decisive question—ability to pay—could often be resolved through relatively straightforward financial inquiry. Courts already assessed indigence to determine eligibility for appointed counsel before providing the lawyer. This did not mean that every financial question was simple or that legal assistance could never be necessary. Second, the opposing parent was herself unrepresented. Providing counsel only to the obligor could create an imbalance, add formality and delay, and impede support urgently needed by the family. The majority expressly considered the interests of children and custodial parents, rather than treating efficiency solely as the State’s budgetary interest. 564 U.S. at 446–447.

Third, the United States proposed a coordinated set of substitute safeguards: notice that ability to pay is a critical issue; a form or equivalent means of obtaining relevant financial information; an opportunity at the hearing to answer statements and questions about finances; and an express judicial finding of ability to pay. Used together, those measures could substantially reduce wrongful confinement without the disadvantages the Court associated with an automatic counsel requirement. The Court did not prescribe them as the only possible implementation; it acknowledged that equivalent safeguards or appropriate nonlegal assistance could sometimes ensure fairness. 564 U.S. at 447–448.

The holding was limited. Where the custodial parent or other custodian receiving support is unrepresented and equivalent safeguards are provided, due process does not automatically require counsel, even when imprisonment for up to a year is possible. The Court expressly left unresolved proceedings seeking reimbursement owed to the government, which would likely appear through a lawyer or competent representative, and unusually complex cases in which only a trained advocate could fairly represent the defendant. 564 U.S. at 448–449.

Turner nevertheless prevailed. He received neither counsel nor the substitute protections described. He lacked clear notice that ability to pay was the critical issue; no one supplied a financial-information form or equivalent procedure; and the judge made no ability-to-pay finding before imprisonment. The presence of a voluntarily submitted disability application did not lead the majority to find the required process satisfied. His incarceration therefore violated due process. The Court did not decide that he actually lacked resources, erase his child-support obligation, or establish a categorical counsel entitlement for all civil contemnors. 564 U.S. at 438, 449.

## Dissent

Thomas would affirm. Part I–A, joined only by Scalia, used an original-understanding and historical-practice analysis to reject a right to appointed counsel in civil contempt. Part I–B, joined also by Roberts and Alito, argued that even modern precedent did not categorically extend appointed counsel to all civil proceedings threatening confinement. It emphasized the Sixth Amendment’s express coverage and the narrow treatment of comparable proceedings in the Court’s cases. 564 U.S. at 450–455 & n.2.

Part II, joined by all four dissenters, objected to deciding the alternative-safeguards issue. Turner had litigated a right to counsel, not a separate claim challenging these procedures. The United States raised the alternative theory as an amicus; the state courts had not passed on it, and the parties had not developed a record or fully tested its boundaries. Thomas’s third footnote stressed that a financial form had reached the court and questioned whether the resulting dispute was merely a fact-specific assessment of the hearing. The dissent distinguished adopting an amicus’s argument on an existing issue from vacating a state judgment on a new constitutional ground. 564 U.S. at 455–458 & nn.3–4.

Part III, joined only by Scalia, further criticized Mathews as insufficiently attentive to children and custodial parents. Thomas described persistent enforcement difficulties and the effectiveness of threatened confinement in producing payments, including Turner’s earlier payments after contempt findings. He did not purport to resolve the policy wisdom of particular enforcement measures or the majority’s preferred safeguards. Those judgments remained for lawmakers. The majority had already expressly taken recipient-family interests into account, so this disagreement concerns the adequacy of its analysis rather than an undisputed absence of consideration. 564 U.S. at 447, 458–461 & nn.5–6.

## Judgment and assigned significance

The Court **vacated the South Carolina Supreme Court’s judgment and remanded** for consistent proceedings. 564 U.S. at 449. The textbook correctly pairs the rejection of an automatic counsel requirement with Turner’s successful challenge to incarceration without adequate safeguards. Its short quotation should be read with the express government-creditor and complex-case reservations. This brief describes the historical decision without certifying later law.
