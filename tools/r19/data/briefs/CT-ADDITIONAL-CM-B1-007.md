# Dahl v. HEM Pharmaceuticals Corporation
7 F.3d 1399 (9th Cir. 1993) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
The Ninth Circuit affirmed a mandatory preliminary injunction requiring HEM to provide a year of Ampligen treatment to patients who had completed its experimental study. It also rejected patient Carol Graham’s cross-appeal seeking a lifetime supply and declined to sanction HEM for its appeal. Judge Kleinfeld wrote for a panel with Judges Norris and Beezer; no separate opinion or division is recorded. The decision reviews provisional relief, not a final damages judgment or final approval of the drug. (1401–1405; Parts II–IV.)

## Facts and procedure
HEM tested Ampligen as an investigational treatment for chronic fatigue syndrome. Its clinical trial included ninety-two patients and used a double-blind design: some received Ampligen and others saline solution, with neither patient nor doctor knowing the assignment. The drug was slowly injected into a vein. Participants signed consent forms acknowledging the experimental treatment and its risks. Although they could withdraw, continued participation required accepting treatment risks, avoiding other drugs and pregnancy, and undergoing uncomfortable testing. (1401–1402; Part I.)

HEM offered a year of free treatment after completion of the double-blind study. The consent form conditioned the continuation on statistical analysis showing efficacy compared with placebo and completion of termination procedures. Former placebo recipients would be offered Ampligen under the same protocol; former drug recipients would be offered continuation. After testing ended, however, HEM stopped supplying the medication. Dahl and seventeen others sued for injunctive and other relief. The Nevada district court ordered twelve months of continued treatment through a preliminary injunction and later held HEM in civil contempt for initial noncompliance. HEM appealed the injunction; Graham cross-appealed its duration. (1401–1403; Parts I–II.)

Meanwhile, the FDA rejected HEM’s request for a “treatment IND,” which would have expanded use to patients outside the clinical trials, because the submitted evidence was incomplete and raised serious safety concerns, including liver toxicity, abdominal pain, and irregular heartbeat. But it expressly permitted an open-label study to continue. In that study both patient and doctor would know that Ampligen was being administered. Footnote 1 explains that the parties treated an FDA Talk Paper, formally prepared to guide public communications, as equivalent to the agency’s decision. The court did not mistake this limited clinical permission for approval of unrestricted prescription use. (1402, 1404; Part I; n.1.)

## Jurisdiction and standard of review
The court had interlocutory appellate jurisdiction over the preliminary injunction under 28 U.S.C. §1292. It raised mootness itself and obtained supplemental briefs because the ordered year might have expired before decision. The controversy remained live: Graham still challenged the limit, Hyneman had not begun treatment, and Rogers-Dickie had begun late enough that treatment remained. Moreover, the outstanding civil contempt fine depended on the injunction’s validity even for patients who had finished their treatment. The contempt order itself was not before the court. (1402–1403; Part II.)

The district court had to weigh probable success against possible irreparable injury. Appellate review asked whether it abused its discretion, applied an erroneous legal standard, or relied on clearly erroneous findings. Because the order compelled supply and injections, mandatory preliminary relief received heightened scrutiny and required facts and law clearly favoring the patients. These are the standards the court applied in 1993; the brief does not certify the present injunction standard. (1403; Part III.)

## HEM’s arguments and the court’s reasoning
HEM first urged primary jurisdiction: safety and efficacy belonged to the FDA, so the judicial proceedings should await the administrative process. The court described primary jurisdiction as protecting a regulatory scheme by initial agency consideration of matters within its assigned authority and expertise. Here, enforcing the promised continuation neither determined unrestricted drug approval nor displaced the FDA’s clinical-study decisions. (1403–1404; Part III.A.)

The efficacy condition did not require final FDA approval before patients could receive the promised year. The FDA had not made a conclusive efficacy finding on the record; HEM offered no evidence negating the contractual statistical condition and itself sought drug approval. Continuation under the same protocol would augment, rather than frustrate, the permitted study. Reading the promise to require an interruption until final marketing approval would disconnect the follow-up year from the study in which the patients had participated. (1403–1404; Part III.A.)

Safety remained relevant to equitable relief. The court acknowledged the FDA’s concerns and stated that a court might err by compelling a promised treatment that the FDA had prohibited entirely. But this FDA action expressly allowed open-label use. The requested treatment would go to informed patients insisting on access and would occur within that permitted phase. The hold on expanded treatment outside trials therefore did not conflict with the injunction. The decision does not establish that contract rights override an agency’s actual prohibition. (1404; Part III.A.)

HEM’s consideration argument failed for a different reason. It asserted that voluntary participation and a right to withdraw meant the patients had undertaken no binding obligation. That argument assumed an exchange of promises. HEM instead sought actual participation: the patients completed the study, undergoing experimental injections and intrusive testing in return for the offered treatment. Their performance accepted a unilateral offer and supplied the bargained-for detriment. Freedom to withdraw before completion did not erase the performance actually rendered. (1404–1405; Part III.B.)

The court compared Hamer v. Sidway, where the nephew completed the requested abstention in exchange for the uncle’s promise. Footnote 2 used the Brooklyn Bridge hypothetical to explain the same distinction: the offeror bargains for the completed act, not necessarily a promise to perform it. HEM obtained precisely the actual testing participation it sought; mutuality of promises was unnecessary. (1405; n.2.)

## Graham’s claim, sanctions, and limits
Graham claimed that HEM had separately promised her lifetime treatment. After considering testimony and depositions, the district judge found that no such promise was made and disbelieved her contrary account. The appellate court found no clear error and left the one-year limit intact. This was a failure of proof concerning the alleged promise, rather than a ruling that a lifetime supply could never be promised. (1405; Part IV.)

Although HEM’s consideration theory approached frivolousness, its primary-jurisdiction argument was serious because of the FDA’s role and safety concerns. The court denied Dahl’s request for appellate sanctions. Its final disposition was affirmance of the preliminary injunction and rejection of the requested extension, with no separate appellate reversal or modification of the contempt order. (1405; Parts III.B–IV.)
