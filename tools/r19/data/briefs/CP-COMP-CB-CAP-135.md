# Bank of the United States v. Planters’ Bank of Georgia

**22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 904 (1824) · Supreme Court of the United States · March 20, 1824**  
**Civil Procedure — federal bank charter, assignment restrictions, and state ownership of a corporation**

The Court held that the Bank of the United States could pursue payment of notes in the federal Circuit Court even though some parties’ members shared state citizenship and Georgia owned an interest in the defendant bank. The federal Bank’s charter supplied the right to sue independently of the ordinary diversity grant. Chief Justice Marshall wrote the Court’s opinion; Justice Johnson disagreed on jurisdiction and separately examined the other objections. The assigned account is CivPro PDF page 188, printed page 331, Note 2. It emphasizes the connection with *Osborn*, while the complete report supplies the additional corporate-state and assignment issues.

## Notes, pleadings, and the certified questions

The Bank of the United States sued Planters’ Bank of Georgia on promissory notes payable to a named person or bearer. Its petition alleged that the notes had been transferred, assigned, and delivered to it, making it their lawful bearer and entitling it to payment. Under Georgia practice, this petition took the place of a declaration. **904–905.**

Planters’ Bank pleaded against jurisdiction. It alleged that Georgia and private individuals were members of the defendant corporation, that some private members shared state citizenship with members of the plaintiff Bank, and that the original payees were Georgia citizens who could not themselves have brought the action in the federal Circuit Court. It argued that transferring the notes could not create a right to sue there that the original holders lacked. **905.**

The federal Bank demurred, challenging the legal sufficiency of that plea. The Circuit Court judges divided on whether the declaration’s allegations established jurisdiction and whether, on these pleadings, the plaintiffs were entitled to judgment. The case reached the Supreme Court on that certificate of division, and it was argued alongside *Osborn*. Johnson described the division as pro forma to secure fuller consideration of important questions. This was not an appellate affirmance of a completed damages judgment after a trial on disputed evidence. **904–905, 910.**

## The charter supplied jurisdiction

Marshall treated the first question as already fully considered in *Osborn v. Bank of the United States*. Rather than repeat its reasoning, he held that the declaration sufficiently established jurisdiction. The assigned passage places this short statement beside *Osborn*’s account of the federal charter as an original ingredient: the Bank’s legal existence and capacity to make and enforce contracts depended on federal law. **905; assigned page 188, including the preceding retained Osborn discussion.**

The opinion’s later discussion made the operative distinction explicit. The Bank was not suing because the defendant’s citizenship differed from that of all its members. It sued under the charter’s independent authorization to bring its debt actions in federal Circuit Courts. Reading that power as nevertheless requiring diverse citizenship would largely destroy it, because the Bank had members throughout the commercial States. **909–910.**

## Georgia’s investment did not make Georgia the defendant

Planters’ Bank argued that Georgia’s membership brought the suit within the Supreme Court’s original jurisdiction over state-party litigation or within the Eleventh Amendment. Marshall rejected the premise that suing the corporation amounted to suing Georgia as a State. To invoke original jurisdiction on this ground, the State had to be a party on the record in its state capacity. The corporation, not its individual members, was the defendant, and its property would satisfy the judgment. **906–907.**

The Court reasoned that a government entering a trading company acted as a corporator in that company’s transactions. It did not impart its sovereign privileges to the corporation merely by investing in it. Georgia’s interest therefore did not make Planters’ Bank immune from federal suit. Marshall drew a parallel to the United States’ shareholding in the federal Bank: suits by or against that Bank were not treated as suits by or against the United States. **907–908.**

These propositions describe the Court’s historical analysis of this corporation and charter. They are not a current-law certification that every state investment or every governmental commercial activity defeats sovereign immunity.

## The restriction on assigned claims did not defeat the charter grant

Section 11 of the Judiciary Act limited ordinary federal jurisdiction over an assignee’s suit on a promissory note or other chose in action when the original holder could not have sued there. Marshall explained its purpose: without the restriction, citizens of the same State could transfer ordinary debts to out-of-state holders and expose local debtors to federal litigation. **908–909.**

But the federal Bank’s authority did not derive from that ordinary Judiciary Act grant. It derived from its charter. The Court therefore held that the assignment restriction did not defeat the Bank’s charter-based right to sue without regard to citizenship. It deliberately proceeded on that general question without deciding whether these particular bearer notes constituted original promises directly enforceable by their bearer. **908–910.**

## Johnson’s disagreement

Johnson referred to his *Osborn* opinion for his jurisdictional position and regarded it as controlling his judgment here. He then argued that *Bank v. Deveaux* required attention to the individual corporators’ identities. If the members were the real parties for jurisdictional purposes, shared citizenship and Georgia’s own immunity could not simply disappear because the defendant was incorporated. He acknowledged that, absent *Deveaux*, this suit would not fall within the Eleventh Amendment’s purview, but considered himself bound by that precedent until overruled. **910–913.**

On assignment, Johnson emphasized that the demurrer admitted the pleaded facts and that acquisition by delivery could constitute an assignment under federal law. The record did not establish that these notes had entered circulation as currency in a manner warranting a special treatment. He nevertheless recognized that, if the charter truly granted an unrestricted right to sue on all contracts, it would override the statutory restriction. His objection was to interpreting Congress as having intended that broad grant. **913–914.**

## Exact result and source limits

The Court certified **both answers for the plaintiff**: the declaration sufficiently established Circuit Court jurisdiction, and the plaintiffs were entitled to judgment on the pleadings. The certificate was ordered transmitted to the Circuit Court. **914.** No numerical vote is stated in the report. The complete eleven-page report, including Johnson’s entire writing and the final certificate, contains no judicial footnotes or appendix.
