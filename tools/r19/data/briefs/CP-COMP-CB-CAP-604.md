# Marek v. Chesny

**473 U.S. 1 (1985) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 27, 1985.** Chief Justice Burger delivered the Court’s opinion, joined by White, Powell, Rehnquist, Stevens, and O’Connor. Powell and Rehnquist each concurred separately while also joining that opinion. Brennan dissented, joined by Marshall and Blackmun. The judgment was 6–3. Civil Procedure: Rule 68 offers of judgment, lump-sum offers, and §1988 attorney’s fees. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical pages 708–709, the Marek account at printed 1409–1410 and all five attached editorial notes at 1410–1412.

## Facts, offer, and fee proceedings

Three police officers responding to a domestic-disturbance call shot and killed Chesny’s adult son. Chesny sued both individually and as administrator of the estate, asserting federal civil-rights claims under §1983 and state tort claims. Before trial, the officers made a timely $100,000 offer expressly including accrued costs and attorney’s fees. Chesny did not accept it. At trial he recovered $5,000 on the state wrongful-death claim, $52,000 for the §1983 violation, and $3,000 in punitive damages: $60,000 in all. (3–4.)

Chesny then requested $171,692.47 in costs including attorney’s fees. The district court refused the portion incurred after the rejected offer, invoking Rule 68. The parties subsequently agreed that $32,000 fairly represented allowable preoffer costs including fees. The postoffer request was consequently approximately $139,692.47. Importantly, the district court did not require Chesny to reimburse any costs incurred by the officers, and the officers did not challenge that ruling. The Supreme Court thus reviewed denial of Chesny’s own postoffer recovery, not an award of the officers’ fees against him. (4, n.1; 12.)

The Seventh Circuit reversed. It viewed mechanically combining Rule 68 with §1988 as undermining the civil-rights fee statute by making plaintiffs and lawyers hesitate to reject even inadequate offers. The Supreme Court reversed that appellate judgment. It considered both whether the officers’ all-inclusive offer was valid and whether attorney’s fees authorized as part of costs by §1988 fell within Rule 68. (4–5.)

## Holdings

First, Rule 68 did not require the officers to allocate separate amounts to substantive relief and accrued costs. An all-inclusive offer could validly limit their total settlement exposure. Second, Rule 68’s costs incorporated costs properly awardable under the relevant substantive statute or other authority. Because §1988 expressly authorized attorney’s fees as part of costs in a §1983 action, those fees were subject to Rule 68. The officers were not liable for Chesny’s postoffer costs including fees when his recovery, properly compared, was less favorable than their offer. (5–12.)

The comparison was not $100,000 against only the $60,000 verdict. Preoffer allowable costs also mattered, bringing the relevant recovery to $92,000. Chesny argued for counting claimed postoffer costs as well, but the Court rejected that approach: those amounts offset the expense of continuing litigation and were not part of the comparison. Adding them would undermine the rejected offer’s operation. (7; 11.)

## Why a lump-sum offer was valid

Chesny argued that the rule’s requirement of an offer with costs then accrued required itemizing damages and costs. Burger focused instead on the judgment the offer would permit. If an offer expressly included costs or specified a cost amount, the resulting judgment would include them. If the offer was silent about costs, the court had to add an appropriate accrued-cost amount. So long as the offer did not implicitly or explicitly exclude costs from the judgment, itemization was unnecessary. (5–6.)

Permitting a total-liability offer promoted settlement because defendants might be unwilling to bind themselves while leaving additional fees unquantified. The majority also considered plaintiffs able to assess the offer by comparing the proposed amount against the damages and accrued costs they knew or could ascertain. Uncertainty about the eventual trial recovery exists in any settlement decision; separating offer components would not eliminate it. These were the Court’s assessments of incentives and information, later disputed in the dissent. (6–7.)

## Why §1988 fees counted as Rule 68 costs

The Court examined the legal setting when the rules were adopted in 1938. Although the ordinary American rule generally excluded attorney’s fees from costs, existing statutes made exceptions, including fee awards expressly described as part of costs. The committee’s materials identified numerous such statutes. Rule 68 nevertheless supplied no independent definition of costs. Burger inferred that it incorporated the costs properly awardable in the particular action rather than imposing a single definition excluding fees. (7–9.)

Section 1983 supplied the civil-rights cause of action; **§1988 supplied the relevant authorization for attorney’s fees as part of costs**. That statutory wording made the fees part of the Rule 68 comparison and consequences, absent contrary congressional expression. Note 2 distinguishes *Roadway Express*: the sanctions statute involved there had its own definition of costs, unlike Rule 68’s incorporation of the otherwise applicable definition. (9–10, n.2.)

Burger rejected the argument that this construction frustrated effective access to court. Section 1988 supported meritorious civil-rights actions; Rule 68 encouraged their settlement. Requiring counsel to reconsider continued litigation after a favorable offer was, for the majority, compatible with both policies. Settlement could produce compensation earlier and avoid burdens or an unfavorable trial outcome. The Court also invoked the relationship between reasonable fees and success achieved: in this case, the substantial postoffer expenditure produced a recovery $8,000 below the rejected offer once preoffer costs were included. (10–12.)

The Court’s rule was mechanical once applicable; it did not rest on a separate finding that all postoffer work was unreasonable under §1988. Nor did it hold that every attorney’s-fee statute counts fees as costs. Its reasoning depended on the applicable statutory definition and contrary congressional expressions. (9–12.)

## Powell and Rehnquist’s concurrences

Powell acknowledged that his separate opinion in *Delta Air Lines v. August* favored identifying substantive relief separately from accrued costs and fees. Although he still considered identification the better practice, he joined the Court’s clarification of the rule’s requirements and emphasized the importance of a settled interpretation supporting settlement. His earlier position was not a qualification on his vote to join Burger here. (12–13.)

Rehnquist likewise explicitly withdrew his Delta dissent’s view that Rule 68 costs excluded attorney’s fees. Further examination persuaded him that the earlier view was wrong, and he joined Burger’s opinion. His short concurrence contains no additional test limiting the majority’s holding. (13.)

## Brennan’s dissent

Brennan argued that costs should have a uniform meaning drawn from the traditional taxable items in §1920. History, the clerical cost-taxing procedure, and other rules’ express treatment of attorney’s fees supported distinguishing fees from costs. He considered the majority’s treatment of *Roadway Express* conclusory and argued that varying Rule 68’s effects according to minor differences in fee-statute wording would defeat the rules’ uniformity. (13–27, dissent nn.1–37.)

The dissent emphasized that §1988 prescribed an informed reasonableness inquiry rather than a cutoff determined solely by a rejected offer and trial result. Unproductive postoffer work could already be excluded under §1988; indeed, on the appellate remand the district court might have rejected much or all of Chesny’s request. But the majority’s rule also foreclosed otherwise reasonable work without considering all relevant circumstances. Brennan maintained that this could occur even where litigation produced excellent results of broader public importance. (28–30, nn.38–42.)

He disputed the majority’s claim of neutrality because Rule 68 was available to those defending claims rather than to claimants. Early low offers before adequate discovery could pressure plaintiffs into uninformed settlements. Additional difficulties involved valuing injunctive relief, reconciling the short acceptance period with class-action protections, and conflicts between named representatives and absent class members. These were anticipated consequences and hypotheticals, not facts about Chesny’s individual damages case. (31–35, nn.43–50.)

Brennan also regarded §1988’s fee entitlement as substantive. In his view, construing a procedural rule to abridge it violated the Rules Enabling Act and the limits on judicial alteration of fee allocations. He reviewed proposed judicial and congressional amendments, including their reasonableness, discovery, and class-action safeguards. None of those proposals was the operative rule adopted in this decision; they supported his argument that Congress and the rulemaking process should resolve the issue. (35–43, nn.51–64.)

## Dissenting statutory appendix

Brennan’s full appendix groups fee statutes into three categories: fees described as costs, fees described separately from costs, and potentially ambiguous formulations referring to costs and expenses including fees. Its 63, 49, and 7 numbered entries illustrate the variety of statutory drafting and the resulting disparities he predicted. The list supplies evidence for the dissent’s criticism, not Court holdings resolving each statute’s application. Note 65 expressly says the enumeration is incomplete and does not suggest that all listed statutes are necessarily governed by Rule 68. The complete appendix, including that limitation and the final category, forms part of the reviewed opinion. (43–51, n.65.)

## Judgment and assigned comparison

The Supreme Court **reversed the Seventh Circuit** and held the officers not liable for Chesny’s approximately $139,692 postoffer request. The underlying damages judgment was not reversed. The preoffer costs agreement and the unchallenged refusal to shift the officers’ costs remain distinct from the question actually decided. (4, n.1; 12.)

The textbook accurately describes the verdict, offer, and general statutory-incorporation reasoning, but one sentence attributes the fee-as-costs language to §1983. The actual opinion places it in **§1988**, while §1983 supplies the claim. The assigned dissent summary omits the full historical, rulemaking, practical, and appendix analysis developed above. All five attached editorial notes have also been read: they raise questions about one-sided settlement incentives, public judgments and confidentiality, objections to settlement, access to facts affecting nonparties, and judicial neutrality in mediation. Those are assigned editorial concerns rather than additional holdings of Marek. This brief explains the historical 1985 decision without certifying later amendments or current law.
