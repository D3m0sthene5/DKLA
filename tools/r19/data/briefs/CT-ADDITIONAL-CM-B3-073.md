# Pennsylvania Exchange Bank v. United States
170 F. Supp. 629 (Ct. Cl. 1959) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and disposition
Judge Whitaker wrote the Court of Claims opinion dated March 4, 1959, as amended April 3. Chief Judge Jones, Judges Laramore and Madden, and Circuit Judge Bastian sitting by designation concurred without separate writings. Assignees for the creditors of Joseph Lerner & Son sued for an unpaid government-contract balance; the United States counterclaimed. On cross-motions, the court denied the assignees’ summary-judgment and counterclaim-dismissal motions, granted the government summary judgment, and directed dismissal of the petition. The government’s recovery required deductions, including an unascertained value to be determined by a commissioner absent stipulation. This was a liability ruling with a specified recovery formula, not a final fixed-dollar award. (629, 633–634.)

## The industrial-preparedness bargain
The Army Signal Corps wanted manufacturers prepared to supply specified components promptly in a national emergency. Lerner’s February 29, 1952 contract concerned “microwave magic tees,” which controlled the direction of microwave impulses. The undertaking had four steps. Step I required information, planning, qualification approval, and a pilot manufacturing run. Step II required production planning short of procuring tools and materials or actual volume manufacture, assuming a mobilization schedule of 2,821 units in one year. Step III required acquiring the additional tools and manufacturing aids necessary for that schedule; these cost $37,907.37. (630 & n. 1.)

Step IV contemplated volume production only after a government order in a national emergency. The government did not promise to order any specified number of units; footnote 2 corrected its counsel’s contrary assertion. After the first three steps, Lerner had to preserve the acquired information and maintain its tools in operable condition for six years. The point was readiness for possible production, rather than a guarantee that an emergency would occur or that orders would be placed. (630 & n. 2.)

Payment for Steps I and II was initially $117,226.16 subject to a prompt-payment discount, then redetermined to $135,754.48 because costs exceeded the estimate. By October 1, 1953, Lerner had substantially completed Steps I through III. The government had paid $92,843.12 on the first two and reimbursed the tools’ cost except for $1,922.23. On that date Lerner made an assignment for creditors in lieu of bankruptcy, about a year and a half after entering the agreement. Its assignees sought the unpaid balances as compensation for the completed preparation. (630–631.)

## Competing theories of the completed steps
The government pleaded alternative counterclaims. Its first treated the whole contract as indivisible: inability to remain available for Step IV destroyed the bargain, excused further payment, and supported recovery of the $92,843.12 previously paid for Steps I and II. Its second valued the lost standby availability at $85,858.63, allowed the outstanding sums for completed preparation, and sought a net $41,025.04 with interest. These were alternative pleaded measures, not two cumulative damages awards. (631.)

The court examined the contract’s purpose rather than assuming that separate steps and listed prices made it divisible. Procurement Specification 15 emphasized preparing each particular manufacturer to produce through its own methods; it expressly disclaimed collecting information to be passed to another manufacturer. The first three steps were therefore incidental to the ultimate readiness to undertake Step IV. Their expense would not have been incurred independently of that objective. The duty to remain ready was an essential element of one bargain, and the preparation could not be separated from it. (631–632.)

## The assignment disabled promised performance
Preserving records and tools implicitly required this manufacturer to remain ready, willing, and able to use them. The court concluded that Lerner’s insolvency and assignment destroyed its ability to undertake the promised volume production. The government lost its bargain even if no emergency ever arose: it had purchased continuing preparedness, not solely actual deliveries after an emergency. Maintaining the mobilization plan would require preparing another manufacturer. The court treated the disabling assignment as a present total breach, applying the implied obligation not to render oneself incapable of performance through insolvency or acts of bankruptcy. The conclusion was tied to this contract’s continuing readiness undertaking, rather than mere dissatisfaction with a contractor’s financial condition. (632.)

## Consideration and the unpromised future orders
The assignees argued that the government had merely reimbursed the first three steps, paying nothing separately for standby service or Step IV, and had promised no purchases. The court agreed that the government had made no purchase commitment but rejected the consideration objection. Government-funded preparation gave Lerner a manufacturing capability it previously lacked and an opportunity to earn profits if orders arrived. Lerner knowingly accepted the uncertainty of that opportunity. The government spent its money to obtain both that preparation and the promise to remain ready; without the latter promise it would not have paid for the former. (633.)

Tennessee Soap involved failure to deliver goods under an agreement lacking mutual purchase commitments. Here the adjudicated breach was the separately supported readiness obligation. The court did not decide what obligations might arise if the government later ordered tees and Lerner refused delivery. Its statement that mutuality was not the question presented must be read with that reservation, not as a holding that every uncommitted future procurement arrangement was enforceable. (633.)

## Recovery formula and remaining valuation
The court described the loss in terms of the cost, as of October 1, 1953, of preparing another manufacturer to stand ready. Its actual order allowed recovery against the bank and Roseman only to the extent that they held Lerner assets as assignees. The starting sum was $92,843.12. It required deduction of any value to the government of Step I articles delivered, plus the $1,922.23 unpaid tool-cost balance; the government had repossessed those tools. The record did not establish the delivered articles’ value. Unless stipulated, that sole valuation issue had to be referred to a commissioner under Rule 38(c). Neither the government’s alternative net counterclaim nor the textbook’s approximate preparation-payment total was the final awarded amount. (633–634.)

## Assigned account and source scope
The assigned “Case of the Magic Tees” account, printed pages 873–874, correctly emphasizes the continuing-readiness purpose, indivisibility, and total breach. Its approximately $128,000 already paid aggregates preparation payments, while the opinion’s $92,843.12 recovery starting point concerns Steps I and II and expressly accounts for other benefits and tools. The full report also supplies the consideration analysis, the reservation concerning future orders, the limitation to assignee-held assets, and the remaining valuation. Those details explain why separately priced preparations did not create an independent right to the balance here.
