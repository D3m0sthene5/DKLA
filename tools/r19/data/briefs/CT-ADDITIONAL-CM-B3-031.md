# Joyce Beverages of New York, Inc. v. Royal Crown Cola Co.
555 F. Supp. 271 (S.D.N.Y. 1983) | Contracts

## Court and precise disposition
Judge Milton Pollack of the Southern District of New York decided this application on January 18, 1983. Joyce sought a preliminary injunction preventing Royal Crown from terminating its franchises and appointing another distributor. After accelerated discovery and an evidentiary hearing, the court denied preliminary relief in all respects. It found neither irreparable injury nor the requisite showing on the contract or antitrust merits, and the balance of hardships favored Royal Crown. The opinion supplies Rule 52(a) findings and conclusions for that provisional application; it does not enter a final damages judgment or dispose of every claim and counterclaim. There are no separate writings. (555 F. Supp. at 271–272, 274–275, 280.)

## Contracts and the competing cola
Royal Crown licensed trademarks and sold secret concentrates to independent bottlers, which manufactured, promoted, and distributed the finished drinks. Joyce had long-term, sometimes potentially perpetual, exclusive territorial rights covering parts of New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut. Its older Royal Crown and Diet-Rite agreements required best efforts to build, maintain, and expand sales satisfactorily to Royal Crown. Updated agreements for RC-100 and Decaffeinated RC expressly prohibited substantially or reasonably similar drinks and required best efforts to achieve maximum distribution and sales. Royal Crown’s cola brands represented about five percent of national cola sales in 1981. Joyce also distributed lemon-lime Seven-Up and other non-cola products. (Id. at 272–273.)

The conflict arose when the Seven-Up Company introduced LIKE, a decaffeinated cola. Royal Crown warned Joyce early in 1982 that it would have to choose between LIKE and Royal Crown’s colas. Joyce’s Chicago and Washington affiliates signed LIKE agreements in July that excluded competing colas. For New York, Seven-Up initially proposed the same exclusivity, which Joyce rejected. In November it offered partial coexistence: Joyce could retain its existing cola licenses, but any cola it later discontinued would fall under a prohibition on taking other colas alongside LIKE. Seven-Up demanded a decision by Thanksgiving. (Id. at 273–274.)

Royal Crown reiterated its objection at a November 8 meeting and in a later letter warning that it would terminate the Royal Crown agreements if Joyce chose LIKE. Joyce nevertheless signed on November 22. Its president told Royal Crown on November 30 that no decision had been made, then acknowledged the agreement the next day while refusing to surrender Royal Crown’s licenses. Seven-Up expected introduction by February 7, 1983, and warned that otherwise it need not fulfill its obligations. Joyce began soliciting retailers to carry LIKE and preparing the launch. It sued December 21; the preliminary-injunction hearing began January 11, 1983. (Id. at 274–275.)

## Best efforts under these circumstances
Joyce conceded that the contemplated conduct violated the Decaffeinated RC agreement unless antitrust law supplied a defense. It disputed whether the older best-efforts language required exclusivity and claimed it could represent both products evenhandedly. Royal Crown argued that competing obligations over pricing, advertising, placement, and promotion would destroy the loyalty and cooperation it had purchased. (Id. at 275–276.)

The court expressly declined a categorical rule that taking a competitive product line always breaches best efforts. It instead found a breach from the hearing record. Bottlers compete for limited retail shelf space, displays, advertising features, and promotional opportunities. Joyce was already persuading established retailers to devote budgets and space to LIKE, planned large free giveaways to induce its promotion, and would control the competing brands’ local pricing and discounts. Those activities would necessarily dilute its effort for Royal Crown. Preserving confidential competing marketing strategies posed an additional conflict. Keeping Royal Crown merely alive fell short of an undertaking to expand its sales vigorously. (Id. at 275–277.)

The caffeine campaign sharpened the conflict. Seven-Up’s national advertising disparaged caffeine; Joyce’s Chicago and Washington affiliates had sponsored that campaign, and Seven-Up expected its use in New York. Joyce had not committed to reject it there. Advertising that sought to draw consumers away from Royal Crown’s caffeinated products threatened the reputation and sales Joyce had undertaken to promote. The court also found implausible Joyce’s suggestion that it could match LIKE’s giveaways with equivalent Royal Crown discounts regardless of whether those discounts were timely or needed. Footnote 1 explained that Bloor v. Falstaff did not equate evenhanded effort with best effort. (Id. at 275–276 & n. 1.)

Industry usage reinforced the factual conclusion. The record showed a longstanding practice of one cola franchise per bottler. Royal Crown’s newer express exclusivity language reflected and solidified the parties’ and industry’s existing understanding; it was not shown to introduce a new obligation. Footnote 3 identified trade usage as a source for interpreting and supplementing the agreement. The court thus read best efforts as exclusive efforts in these circumstances, while repeating that the bare signature of another cola agreement was not by itself decisive. It found a material breach by Joyce and concluded that Royal Crown’s proposed cancellation would not breach its own obligations. (Id. at 277 & n. 3.)

## Antitrust alternative
Joyce alternatively invoked Clayton Act section 3 and Sherman Act section 1. The court examined whether the exclusivity arrangement substantially foreclosed market opportunities and considered its economic justification. Footnotes 4 and 5 addressed the rule of reason, foreclosure, and the cited exclusive-dealing decisions. On this record, the arrangement did not substantially lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly. (Id. at 277–278 & nn. 4–5.)

LIKE had viable routes into the area through other distributors or other arrangements. Seven-Up’s witness described Joyce as its first choice, not its only choice; Seven-Up also had the resources, contacts, and backing of Philip Morris. The court rejected Joyce’s proposed distributor qualifications as an unsupported effort to define Joyce itself as indispensable. Nor had Joyce shown that replacing Royal Crown with LIKE would cause other licensors to terminate their agreements or prevent adequate sales volume. (Id. at 278–279.)

Separate bottlers for Royal Crown and LIKE would preserve vigorous competition among those two brands, Coke, and Pepsi. Common distribution by Joyce would impair that rivalry; its own president stated that the two brands would never compete with each other under its control. Royal Crown was a small competitor behind Coke and Pepsi, rather than a dominant firm using the arrangement to foreclose entry. The court found no likelihood of success or fair ground for litigation on the antitrust alternative in this provisional record. (Id. at 279.)

## Injury, equities, and assigned account
The expected transition was a difficult adjustment, not destruction of Joyce’s business. Its own and Seven-Up’s projections anticipated LIKE replacing Royal Crown volume and ultimately exceeding it. Joyce would retain its other product lines, territorial licenses, and existing customer relationships. The court found no threatened loss of goodwill or irreparable injury. An injunction, meanwhile, would force Royal Crown to remain dependent on a distributor the court found unable and unwilling to promote it rigorously. Joyce sought to change its operations while freezing Royal Crown’s ability to respond. Those hardships weighed against preliminary relief. (Id. at 280.)
