# Fuentes v. Shevin; Parham v. Cortese

**Civil Procedure · CP-ATLAS-FUENTES · 407 U.S. 67 (1972) · June 12, 1972**

Fuentes invalidated the Florida and Pennsylvania procedures that let private claimants obtain state-assisted prejudgment seizure of another person's possessions without a prior opportunity to contest the claim. The protection extended to temporary loss of possession of ordinary household goods, even though ownership or the right to continued possession remained disputed. Justice Stewart wrote for four participating Justices; Justice White dissented with Chief Justice Burger and Justice Blackmun. Justices Powell and Rehnquist did not participate. The Court **vacated both federal district-court judgments and remanded**. It did not decide the underlying payment disputes, award damages, or eliminate every form of repossession before final judgment. (407 U.S. at 68, 84–97.)

## The transactions and the two proceedings

Margarita Fuentes bought a gas stove, a service policy, and later a stereo from Firestone on installment contracts. The goods cost approximately $500, with more than $100 in financing charges. Firestone retained title, while the contracts entitled her to possession until default. She paid for more than a year; with approximately $200 remaining, a dispute developed over servicing the stove. Firestone claimed she had stopped making the required payments, filed a small-claims repossession action, and simultaneously obtained a replevin writ. A clerk issued it from completed forms without first hearing Fuentes. A deputy sheriff and a Firestone agent seized the stove and stereo later that day. (Id. at 70–71.)

The Pennsylvania companion litigation involved other household goods bought on credit, including a bed and a table, and also Rosa Washington's dispute with her former husband, a deputy sheriff, over their child's clothes, furniture, and toys. Washington's possessory interest arose under ordinary property law rather than an installment contract. The distinction matters: the Court's protection concerned significant possession, not simply contractual buyers' rights. (Id. at 71–72, 86–87 & n.16.)

Fuentes and the Pennsylvania appellants brought federal constitutional challenges under § 1983 and the jurisdictional provision then identified as § 1343(3), seeking declaratory and injunctive relief against the summary seizure procedures. Three-judge district courts sustained both states' laws, and the Supreme Court heard their direct appeals together. Majority note 3 distinguishes a challenge to the summary seizure process from an injunction against pending or future state judicial proceedings themselves. The Fourth Amendment challenge was raised but remained undecided because the due process holding disposed of the relevant issue. (Id. at 71–72 & nn.2–5, 96 n.32.)

## What the statutes allowed

Florida required the applicant to file a repossession complaint asserting entitlement to the goods and a bond of at least twice their value. The writ issued without a convincing preliminary demonstration that the goods were wrongfully detained. It commanded seizure and service of the complaint; officials could forcibly enter a building if delivery was demanded and refused. Thus notice of the lawsuit accompanied the loss of possession instead of providing a chance to prevent it. The sheriff was to hold the goods for three days, during which the possessor could recover them by posting a counterbond of twice their value. Otherwise, the applicant received the goods pending adjudication of the repossession action. (Id. at 73–75 & n.6.)

Pennsylvania permitted a similar application to a prothonotary, supported by a valuation affidavit and double-value bond. Unlike Florida, the route actually used did not require the applicant to file a complaint asserting the right to possession or pursue a later merits hearing. The dispossessed party could demand that the applicant file a complaint, initiating the process needed for a judicial resolution, or post a counterbond within the specified period to recover possession. None of the Pennsylvania appellants had invoked the procedure to compel a complaint. These differences made the states' procedures distinct, but both allowed seizure before an adversarial evaluation. (Id. at 75–78 & nn.7–9.)

## Why a hearing had to precede these seizures

The Court treated timely notice and a chance to be heard as safeguards against mistaken or unjustified deprivation. Their value lay in letting the possessor answer the applicant before state power altered possession. Returning the goods or paying damages later could not prevent the period of deprivation that had already occurred. The requirement therefore addressed the timing of an opportunity, not merely the eventual availability of a lawsuit. The form of hearing could vary with the interests and circumstances, but the challenged procedures supplied none before seizure. (Id. at 80–84.)

The bond and possible damages did not perform the same function. They might deter an entirely baseless application, but they principally tested the applicant's willingness to act on a self-interested belief that the claim was sound. A sincere claimant could still be wrong, and an informed neutral evaluation required more than that confidence. Note 13 adds that a claimant might expect an uninformed consumer never to contest even an unjustified seizure. The safeguards could affect the form of a hearing; they did not remove the need for a prior opportunity in these cases. (Id. at 83–84 & n.13.)

The Court also rejected several attempts to put the interests outside constitutional protection. A temporary loss remained a deprivation; having to surrender other property to obtain a recovery bond did not make the original seizure harmless. The right protected significant possession and use, even when the seller retained legal title. Nor did its protection depend on proving in advance that the possessor would win the contract dispute. Finally, a stove, stereo, or other consumer item did not have to be an absolute necessity like wages or welfare to count as property. The importance and duration of the loss could affect appropriate procedure, but did not justify dispensing with an opportunity altogether. (Id. at 84–90 & nn.14–21.)

The opinion's account of common-law replevin reinforced that conclusion. Traditional replevin commonly returned goods that a landlord had distrained from a tenant, and a disputed ownership claim could produce a summary official determination before delivery. Modern creditor seizure of goods allegedly wrongfully detained was not an automatic continuation of an entirely one-sided historical process. The Court distinguished those earlier forms rather than treating the antiquity of the word “replevin” as constitutional approval of these statutes. (Id. at 78–80 & nn.10–12.)

## Extraordinary situations and contractual waiver

Some exceptional seizures could precede a hearing. The Court identified an important governmental or public interest, a special need for prompt action, and strict state control through an official applying a narrowly drawn standard. Its examples involved taxes, wartime needs, bank failure, and public health. Note 23 separately discusses attachment to secure jurisdiction and limits what can be inferred from an unexplained earlier disposition. Ordinary private debt collection did not supply the comparable public interest. The statutes were not limited to demonstrated danger that the debtor would destroy or conceal goods; they allowed a claimant to activate state force without official review of the claim or need for immediate action. (Id. at 90–94 & nn.22–30.)

The sellers also relied on contract language permitting them to take back or repossess goods on default. The Court distinguished a specifically negotiated corporate waiver considered in Overmyer from these small-print consumer forms. It did not need to decide every issue about unequal bargaining power or knowing waiver: the language never clearly waived a prior hearing in the first place. A substantive right to repossess after default did not identify whether repossession would occur after judgment, by self-help, or by a prejudgment writ, with or without a hearing. (Id. at 94–96 & n.31.)

The resulting holding left states room to provide efficient hearings that genuinely tested the validity or probable validity of the asserted right to repossess. It did not require a complete merits trial before every provisional measure, and note 29 explains that a noticed possessor could choose not to use the opportunity. Note 30 distinguishes search warrants, including their public purpose, need for prompt action, and probable-cause safeguards; the opinion did not demand an adversary hearing before a search warrant. (Id. at 92–97 & nn.29–33.)

## White's dissent

White first would have remanded for reconsideration under Younger and its companion decisions because, in his view, state replevin proceedings were already pending. He regarded the Pennsylvania writ process as commencing a state action even without the applicant filing a complaint and considered the existing state avenues sufficient for constitutional objections. This was a procedural objection to federal intervention, not the majority's adopted disposition. (Id. at 97–99.)

On due process, he emphasized the creditor's stake in preventing use and deterioration of its security while ownership and default were litigated. The bond protected the debtor against ultimate loss, and commercial incentives made knowingly false or premature repossession unattractive. His footnote reports that Fuentes and the Parhams had stipulated to delinquency, while Epps's default remained disputed; it also describes the dissolution of Washington's temporary restraining order after the lower court found the representations supporting it incorrect. Those details underlay his assessment of the risk, while the majority insisted that an eventual lack of a successful defense did not extinguish the procedural right. (Id. at 99–102 & White's note at 101; majority at 87.)

White preferred a context-sensitive accommodation of both parties' property interests and suggested requiring the debtor to make the payments on which possession depended. He doubted that the added hearing would materially improve protection, expected greater credit costs or reduced availability, and relied on recent legislative and Uniform Commercial Code treatment of secured transactions. His suggestion that clearer contract terms might avoid the decision was a dissenting prediction, not a majority holding validating any particular future waiver or self-help provision. (Id. at 102–103.)
