# Day v. Edwards

**5 T.R. 648–649 (K.B. 1794); assigned parallel citation 101 Eng. Rep. 361**  
**Court of King's Bench | June 25, 1794 | Lord Kenyon, Chief Justice**  
**Civil Procedure - forms of action: immediate injury, trespass, and case**  
**Assigned:** CivPro PDF 303, printed 569, own-caption paragraph under “Case Captures Negligence.”

## Facts and procedural posture

Day alleged that he possessed a four-wheeled carriage, called a landaulet, drawn by a horse along the King's highway. Edwards also possessed a cart and horse, which were under his direction. According to Day's declaration, Edwards drove furiously, negligently, and improperly, causing his cart to strike Day's carriage with great force, overturn it, and damage it. These were pleaded allegations, not factual findings following a negligence trial. (5 T.R. 648.)

Day selected an action on the case. Edwards responded with a **special demurrer**, objecting to the declaration's legal sufficiency and form. He contended that the described harm was an immediate trespass to Day's property, so Day should have brought trespass *vi et armis*, rather than case. The demurrer also criticized the way the declaration recited, rather than positively averred, the facts against Edwards, and its omission of the traditional allegations of force and arms and breach of the peace. Day joined issue on the demurrer, bringing the legal objection before the court. The published report gives counsel's argument and the court's resolution, rather than an appellate review of a completed damages trial. (648.)

## Arguments and the issue

The central question was whether calling Edwards's driving negligent allowed Day to use case when the declaration described Edwards's cart immediately striking and damaging Day's carriage. Day's counsel, Wood, argued that driving along the highway was lawful in itself. Injury resulting from exercising that lawful activity improperly was consequential damage, for which case was the appropriate action. He distinguished a declaration directly alleging that the defendant drove against the plaintiff from one alleging furious driving along the highway in consequence of which harm occurred. (648–649.)

Edwards's counsel, Baldwin, was stopped by the court when supporting the demurrer; the report does not reproduce a developed oral argument from him. The grounds recorded in the demurrer supply Edwards's position. The court's account of stopping counsel should not be turned into an invented evidentiary hearing or a ruling about which facts were proved. (648.)

## Rule and reasoning

Lord Kenyon treated **the relation between the defendant's act and the injury** as controlling. An injury produced by the immediate act complained of belonged in trespass. An injury merely consequential on that act belonged in case. The classification did not turn on counsel's characterization of the underlying activity as lawful or of its performance as negligent. (649.)

He illustrated the distinction with a log thrown into the highway. If the log struck the plaintiff in the act of being thrown, the injury was immediate and trespass would lie. If the log later lay in the road and the plaintiff stumbled over it, case would address the consequential injury. The example separates an injury inflicted by the operative act from a later injury caused by the condition that act created. It is Kenyon's illustration of the form-of-action rule, not a separate factual event in Day's collision. (649.)

Applying that distinction, Kenyon concluded that Day complained of the immediate act. The declaration alleged that Edwards personally drove the cart so that it struck Day's carriage and overturned it. Describing the driving as negligent, or inserting the phrase “in consequence,” did not convert that direct impact into the sort of later consequential harm described by the log example. Day should therefore have brought trespass. (648–649.)

The judgment resolves the improper selection of case on this declaration. The short opinion does not separately analyze every technical ground in the special demurrer, and does not determine the elements or damages of a properly pleaded trespass action. Most importantly, it does not find that Edwards drove carefully or that Day suffered no damage. Edwards won because the chosen legal form did not fit the injury pleaded.

## Judgment, participation, and significance

The report ends with a **per curiam judgment for Edwards**, the defendant. It gives Kenyon's explanation and reports no concurrence, dissent, or numerical vote. The two lettered notes at the end are editorial references: note (a), attached to counsel's argument, points to Slater v. Baker and Scott v. Shepherd; note (b), attached to the judgment, points to Savignac v. Roome and the later Ogle v. Barnes report. They are not additional judicial reasons or separately decided matters within Day. (649.)

The assignment places Day among cases in which the immediate-versus-consequential distinction encountered negligence and accidents. Its account accurately emphasizes counsel's unsuccessful effort to shift the analysis from direct impact to negligent exercise of a lawful activity. The precise historical lesson is that **negligence alone did not make case the proper form under Kenyon's rule in 1794**. The surrounding textbook discussion follows subsequent developments in other decisions; those different judgments must be examined separately before treating later flexibility as Day's own holding. This brief describes the historical pleading system, rather than presenting its forms of action as current procedural requirements.
