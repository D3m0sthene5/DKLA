Temple v. Synthes
Temple v. Synthes Corp., Ltd.
Citation
Temple v. Synthes Corp., Ltd., 498 U.S. 5, 111 S. Ct. 315, 112 L. Ed. 2d 263 (1990), No. 90-295. Supreme
Court of the United States, decided November 5, 1990, on the petition for certiorari. The decision is per
curiam, meaning issued in the Court’s name without an identified individual author. The report gives no
argued date and reports no separate opinions. Casebook pp. 40-41 (repository PDF p. 50). Full judicial
opinion.
Parties
Temple, a Mississippi resident, was the plaintiff in federal court and the petitioner in the Supreme Court.
Synthes, Ltd. (U.S.A.), the Pennsylvania corporation that manufactured the implanted device, was the
federal defendant and respondent. Dr. S. Henry LaRocca and St. Charles General Hospital were defendants
in separate state proceedings, not parties to the federal action. Temple sought to maintain his federal
product-liability suit against the manufacturer without joining, or adding as defendants, the doctor and
hospital.
Material Facts
In October 1986, LaRocca operated on Temple at St. Charles General Hospital in New Orleans. A device
consisting of a plate and screws, manufactured by Synthes, was implanted in Temple’s lower spine. After
the surgery, screws broke off inside his back. Temple alleged that the device had been defectively designed
and manufactured, while separately pursuing malpractice and negligence claims against the doctor and
hospital. These were allegations about different potential contributors to his injury, not liability findings by
the Supreme Court. 498 U.S. at 5-6.
Procedural History
Temple sued Synthes in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Louisiana under diversity
jurisdiction, the federal court’s authority over qualifying disputes between citizens of different states. At the
same time, he pursued a state administrative proceeding against the doctor and hospital; when that
proceeding ended, he sued them in Louisiana state court. Synthes did not file a third-party complaint under
Rule 14(a), which would have sought to bring another person into the federal suit through a claim by
Synthes. It instead moved to dismiss Temple’s suit under Rule 19 for failure to join necessary parties. Id. at
6.
The District Court ordered Temple to add the doctor and hospital within twenty days. It emphasized judicial
economy, or the efficient use of court resources, and relied on Provident Tradesmens Bank & Trust Co. v.
Patterson. When Temple did not comply, it dismissed with prejudice, an order intended to end that claim
rather than permit a fresh filing. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, reasoning that overlapping proceedings could
prejudice defendants who blamed one another for the injury. It denied rehearing. The Supreme Court granted
review and reversed. The rehearing denial is supplied by the full opinion rather than the excerpt. Id. at 6-8.
Issue
Must a plaintiff suing a product manufacturer join a doctor and hospital that are potential joint tortfeasors, or
persons who may share legal responsibility for the same injury, simply because their separate lawsuits
involve overlapping facts and competing accounts of fault?

Rule and Holding
Potential joint tortfeasors are merely permissive parties in these circumstances: they may be joined, but their
presence is not required under Rule 19(a). Rule 19(b), which addresses whether an action may proceed when
a party who should be joined cannot be joined, is not reached unless Rule 19(a)’s threshold is satisfied. The
doctor and hospital did not satisfy that threshold merely because they might share responsibility with
Synthes. It was an abuse of discretion to order their joinder and dismiss when Temple refused. Id. at 7-8.
Judgment
The Court granted certiorari, reversed the Fifth Circuit’s judgment, and remanded for further proceedings
consistent with its opinion. The ruling restored the possibility of proceeding against Synthes alone; it did not
decide whether the device was defective or which defendant caused Temple’s injury. Id. at 8.
Reasoning
The Court began with the undisputed characterization of the manufacturer, doctor, and hospital as potential
joint tortfeasors. It stated: “It has long been the rule that it is not necessary for all joint tortfeasors to be
named as defendants in a single lawsuit.” The 1966 revision of Rule 19 had not displaced that principle. The
Advisory Committee’s explanation of Rule 19(a) expressly treated a tortfeasor subject to ordinary
joint-and-several liability as permissive in an action against another with similar liability. Joint-and-several
liability describes a regime in which a responsible defendant can be liable for the whole covered injury
rather than only a separately apportioned share. The Court found no contrary rule in the Louisiana tort law
relevant to the case. Id. at 7.
The lower courts’ efficiency concerns could not replace the rule’s threshold inquiry. The District Court
emphasized complete and efficient resolution, and the Fifth Circuit anticipated competing efforts to attribute
fault. Those were the lower courts’ reasons for compelled joinder, not the Supreme Court’s holding. Because
the absent doctor and hospital were merely permissive parties, the conditions for moving to Rule 19(b)’s
further analysis were missing. Id. at 6-8.
The full opinion contains an explanation of Provident omitted from the excerpt. That earlier insurance
dispute proceeded on the assumption that an absent policyholder satisfied Rule 19(a), but the policyholder
could not be added without destroying diversity jurisdiction. Rule 19(b) therefore supplied the appropriate
next inquiry there, and the Court allowed the suit to proceed even without that party. Temple’s case never
reached that stage: the doctor and hospital did not satisfy Rule 19(a) in the first place. The comparison
explains why a general interest in limiting multiple litigation did not control this case. Id. at 7-8.
Separate Opinions
The reported decision contains no concurrence or dissent. No individual author is identified.
Notes
