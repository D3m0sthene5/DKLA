# Chicot County Drainage District v. Baxter State Bank

**308 U.S. 371 (1940) · Supreme Court of the United States · January 2, 1940**  
**Civil Procedure — collateral attack, jurisdictional finality, and a later unconstitutional-statute decision**

Bondholders who had notice and an opportunity to challenge a debt-readjustment proceeding could not evade its final decree by later attacking the statute’s constitutionality. The statute’s subsequent invalidation in another case did not automatically erase this completed adjudication. Chief Justice Hughes wrote the Court’s opinion; no separate writing or numerical vote appears in the complete report. CivPro assigns the account and retained judicial explanation in PDF page 218, printed page 392, Note 2, before the *Travelers* discussion.

## The old bonds and the readjustment decree

Chicot County Drainage District, organized under Arkansas law, issued bonds in 1924 and defaulted in 1932. Baxter State Bank and the other respondents later sued in federal district court on fourteen $1,000 bonds. The District defended on the basis of a prior debt-readjustment decree entered by the same federal court in March 1936 under the federal Municipal Debt Readjustments Act of May 24, 1934. **372–373 & nn.1–2.**

That decree recited approval of a fair and equitable plan by holders of more than two-thirds of the outstanding indebtedness. The District issued $193,500 in new bonds to the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, which had purchased and exchanged a much larger amount of outstanding obligations. Funds were deposited for the remaining old obligations. Holders had one year to present those obligations for participation in the plan and the fund; otherwise, they would be barred. Subject to that presentation opportunity, the decree canceled the old bonds and enjoined claims on them. **373.**

In the later bond action, the evidence established the respondents’ ownership and notice of the readjustment proceeding. The record and final decree were introduced, and the parties stipulated to a trial without a jury. The district court nevertheless ruled for the bondholders, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed. The lower courts considered the decree void because the Supreme Court had subsequently invalidated the 1934 statute in *Ashton v. Cameron County District*, a case involving a Texas district. **373–374.**

## Why later invalidation did not erase the decree

The bondholders’ theory treated an unconstitutional statute as though it had never existed: no valid law meant no jurisdiction and therefore no binding decree. Hughes rejected that reasoning as too absolute. A statute’s actual existence before invalidation can be an operative fact with consequences for conduct, status, settled rights, and final adjudications. A later declaration cannot necessarily erase the past. The Court did not announce a universal rule covering all retroactive effects of invalidation; it confined its decision to the res judicata question presented. **374–375 & n.3.**

Apart from the later constitutional decision, the elements of the District’s preclusion defense were present. The earlier proceeding complied with the statute, its regularity was unchallenged, and the bondholders were parties with notice and a full opportunity to object. They could have challenged the plan’s fairness, the decree’s terms, or the statute’s validity. They apparently did not raise the constitutional question, and no direct review of the decree was sought. Their failure to assert an available objection did not preserve it for a separate action attacking the decree. **375.**

The assignment says the notified parties chose not to appear. The actual opinion expressly establishes their party status, notice, opportunity to object, and failure to raise invalidity. Its account does not independently narrate an appearance history. The brief relies on those facts expressly stated by the Court rather than turning the editorial shorthand into an additional judicial finding.

## A limited court can determine its own jurisdiction

The bondholders stressed that the federal court sat in bankruptcy and possessed only statutory jurisdiction. Hughes agreed that lower federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction, but rejected the inference that every jurisdictional error makes their judgments nullities. A court receiving parties in accordance with due process has authority to determine whether it may entertain the case, including authority to construe the statute under which it acts. That determination remains subject to direct review; it does not become automatically open to collateral attack. **376–377.**

The rule applied both to allegations that a court exceeded a valid statute’s boundaries and to allegations that the enabling statute was unconstitutional. The latter was still a question for judicial resolution in light of the party and the statute’s application. The District suggested that its status differed from that of the district in *Ashton*, because it acted for property owners rather than as a political subdivision. The Court mentioned that argument only to illustrate a question the original court could have resolved, not as a new holding that this application of the statute was constitutional. **377 & n.4.**

If the bondholders had litigated validity and lost, that determination would have been final apart from direct appeal. They could not obtain a better position by remaining silent when the issue was available. Res judicata extended to available matters that could have been presented to sustain or defeat the right adjudicated, not merely issues actually argued. **377–378 & n.5.**

## Judgment and limits

The Supreme Court **reversed and remanded to the district court with directions to dismiss the complaint**, **378**. It did not reapprove the 1934 statute as a general matter or treat every jurisdictional defect as harmless. The holding protected this decree against these parties’ later attack, given their notice, opportunity, and the lack of direct review. The assignment’s nearby discussion of exceptions and *Kalb* is useful context but does not expand this case into a categorical rule against all collateral challenges.
