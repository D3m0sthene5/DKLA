# Arbaugh v. Y & H Corp.
546 U.S. 500 (2006) | Civil Procedure

## Decision and assigned account

Justice Ginsburg delivered the Court's February 22, 2006 opinion, joined by every participating Justice. Justice Alito did not participate, making the judgment 8–0. The Court held that Title VII's fifteen-employee threshold was an element of the claim, rather than a restriction on federal subject-matter jurisdiction. It reversed the Fifth Circuit and remanded. The assigned account is Note 7 in Civil Procedure PDF page 175, printed pages 303–304. The complete official report spans pages 500–516, with the Court's opinion at 503–516, eleven judicial notes, a counsel/amicus note, and no separate writing or appendix. [Complete official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-546/pdf/USREPORTS-546-500.pdf).

## A trial victory erased by a late workforce objection

Jenifer Arbaugh worked as a bartender and waitress at the Moonlight Cafe, a New Orleans restaurant owned by Y & H Corporation, from May 2000 through February 2001. She alleged that an owner, Yalcin Hatipoglu, sexually harassed her and caused her constructive discharge. In November 2001 she sued under Title VII and Louisiana law, invoking federal-question jurisdiction and supplemental jurisdiction over the state claims. 546 U.S. at 507–508 & n.6.

Y & H admitted the jurisdictional allegations while contesting the merits. The parties' pretrial order likewise acknowledged federal jurisdiction, and it did not identify the number of employees as a disputed fact or legal issue. No workforce objection arose before or during the trial. After the parties consented to a magistrate judge's adjudication, a two-day jury trial produced a verdict finding sexual harassment and constructive discharge under federal and state law. Arbaugh received $5,000 in backpay, $5,000 in compensatory damages, and $30,000 in punitive damages. Judgment entered on November 5, 2002. Id. at 507–508.

Two weeks later, Y & H moved under Rule 12(h)(3) to dismiss the entire action for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. It asserted for the first time that it lacked the fifteen employees necessary to qualify as a Title VII employer. Posttrial discovery focused on eight delivery drivers, two owner-managers, and their shareholder spouses. Including either the drivers or the four owners with the payroll employees would have satisfied the threshold. The trial court concluded that none of those disputed categories counted as employees. Id. at 508–509.

Although the trial court described the timing as unfair and wasteful, it considered jurisdictional dismissal mandatory. It vacated the verdict judgment, dismissed the federal claim with prejudice, and dismissed the state claims without prejudice. The Fifth Circuit affirmed under its precedent categorizing the employee minimum as jurisdictional. The Supreme Court took the case to resolve the circuit conflict over that classification, rather than to recalculate the disputed workers' status. Id. at 508–510.

## Statutory coverage and jurisdiction occupy different provisions

Title VII prohibited specified employment discrimination and separately defined an employer as a person in an industry affecting commerce with fifteen or more employees for each working day in twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding year. The minimum appeared in the statute's definitions section, alongside twelve other defined terms. Notes 1–3 identify excluded employer categories, the reduction from the original twenty-five-worker minimum, and the other definitions. Id. at 504–505 & nn.1–3.

Federal-question jurisdiction came from 28 U.S.C. § 1331 and Title VII's own authorization for district courts to adjudicate actions brought under the Act. The latter originally ensured a forum when § 1331 still required more than $10,000 in controversy. Congress removed that monetary condition in 1980, leaving Title VII's provision to reinforce access to a federal forum. Neither jurisdictional grant incorporated the employee minimum. Id. at 505–506 & n.4.

Section 1367 also permitted adjudication of related state claims forming the same Article III case or controversy. Arbaugh had used that route for complete relief. Its presence made the distinction consequential for more than the federal claim: a jurisdictional dismissal erased the basis for the entire action, whereas a merits dismissal ordinarily left discretion to retain related state claims. Id. at 506 & n.5, 514.

## Why characterization changes the litigation rules

A true subject-matter defect cannot be waived or forfeited; courts must address it even without a party's objection. Under the rules discussed in the opinion, it could be raised after trial and judgment. By contrast, Rule 12(h)(2)'s failure-to-state-a-claim defense survived through trial on the merits, but not beyond it. Y & H's stipulations and failure to contest employee coverage during trial therefore mattered if the threshold was substantive. Id. at 506–507, 510–511.

The Court identified three practical consequences. First, treating the threshold as jurisdictional would impose an independent judicial duty to investigate employer size, although Title VII supplied no indication that Congress intended such a duty. Second, jurisdictional facts sometimes could be resolved by the judge, whereas contested facts about an essential claim element belonged to the jury. Third, jurisdictional dismissal would require dismissal of the entire complaint, including already-tried state claims, rather than permit discretionary supplemental jurisdiction. These consequences reinforced the need to classify the statute carefully; they did not allow courts to disregard a restriction Congress actually made jurisdictional. Id. at 513–515.

## Earlier uses of “jurisdiction” did not decide this question

The Court acknowledged imprecise jurisdictional terminology in its own decisions. A label used without considering whether an issue actually concerned adjudicatory authority had no precedential force on that question. *Hishon* decided that Title VII reached partnership decisions while expressly leaving the dismissal classification unresolved. *Arabian American Oil* concerned geographic coverage, and its references to definitions as jurisdictional were incidental to that issue. Id. at 510–513.

Y & H also relied on *Walters*, where the Court determined the employee count. Note 7 explained that the relevant facts apparently were stipulated, so resolving the legal counting standard did not establish that the requirement was jurisdictional. Note 9 distinguished an EEOC administrative-authority discussion from federal-court subject-matter jurisdiction. Note 8 identified Congress's later amendment concerning U.S. citizens working abroad; it did not convert the old opinion's passing terminology into a controlling jurisdictional holding. Id. at 512–513 & nn.7–9.

## The clear-statement rule and its limits

The Court adopted an administrable rule: Congress must clearly classify a threshold restriction on statutory coverage as jurisdictional for courts to treat it that way. Without such classification, the restriction is nonjurisdictional. Congress remained free to impose jurisdictional requirements; the opinion contrasted diversity jurisdiction's monetary floor and catalogued other express jurisdictional limits in note 11. Id. at 514–516 & n.11.

Title VII's employee minimum appeared in a separate definitions provision that neither spoke in jurisdictional terms nor referred to district-court jurisdiction. The minimum therefore remained a substantive element of the plaintiff's claim. This did not abolish the threshold or determine that Y & H actually had fifteen employees. It meant that the defendant could not use an untimely merits objection to undo the judgment as jurisdictionally void. Id. at 515–516.

The rule also did not eliminate the requirement of a colorable federal claim. Note 10 preserved dismissal for want of jurisdiction where an asserted federal claim was manufactured solely to obtain jurisdiction or was wholly insubstantial and frivolous. Arbaugh's claim plainly did not fit that category. Id. at 513 n.10.

## Exact judgment and connection to the assignment

The Court reversed the Fifth Circuit and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion. It did not itself issue a new damages award or decide whether the drivers and owners satisfied the employee definition. The Court's classification foreclosed this belated jurisdictional attack on the $40,000 judgment. Id. at 511, 516.

The assigned account accurately explains that the workforce objection came too late because it concerned substantive coverage. The full opinion supplies the precise posttrial sequence, separate jurisdictional grants, consequences for related state claims, earlier cases' limited precedential value, and the colorability exception. The later *Sebelius* discussion beginning on the same assigned page is the editors' separate comparison, rather than part of Arbaugh's opinion. Assigned printed 303–304.
