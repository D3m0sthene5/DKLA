# Menorah Chapels at Millburn v. Needle
386 N.J. Super. 100, 899 A.2d 316 (App. Div. 2006) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and disposition
Judge Payne wrote the New Jersey Appellate Division opinion decided June 8, 2006, on a panel with Judges Skillman and Axelrad. No separate writing appears. The funeral home had obtained summary judgment on its collection claim, dismissal of Emanuel Needle’s counterclaim, and litigation expenses. The appellate court reversed the treatment of the funeral agreement as divisible and restored the contractual counterclaim for trial. It preserved dismissal of the untimely tort claims and affirmed a litigation-readiness sanction, while vacating the separate contractual fee award. The final direction was reversal and remand consistent with those differentiated rulings, not an appellate award of emotional-distress damages. (100, 104–107, 113–119.)

## Funeral arrangements and incomplete vigil
Needle arranged services when his father-in-law died on a Friday. The deceased was an Orthodox Jew, and the funeral was set for Sunday, February 21, 1999, after the Sabbath. Menorah advertised a Jewish funeral chapel and offered requested Orthodox ritual services. Its documents specified shrouds and a continuous vigil over the body, and the selected-services statement listed six shifts of watchers, or shomerim, for $900. (105 & n. 1.)

Menorah subcontracted the watchers to a burial society. Allegedly because of the Sabbath, only three shifts appeared, beginning Saturday evening after the Sabbath ended. The body had consequently been left unattended contrary to the requested custom. Needle and his family learned of the absence shortly before the funeral began. The fact of the missing contracted services could be evaluated without deciding the religious correctness of a performed ritual. (106, 109–110.)

## Collection suit, counterclaim, and fees
In October 1999 Menorah sued Needle in the Special Civil Part for the funeral cost with a $390 deduction for the missing shifts. Needle denied liability and counterclaimed for negligence and breach of warranty causing family emotional distress. Although he did not expressly label a contract cause of action, the court could infer it from the warranty claim and denials. After dismissal without prejudice, Menorah filed a new complaint on April 9, 2002 and Needle repeated his counterclaim. (106.)

Needle was unprepared when the case came to trial October 16, 2002. An adjournment was conditioned on $4,877.02 in fees and costs for Menorah’s trial preparation. Later he sought dismissal and referral to a religious court, while Menorah sought summary judgment on the counterclaim. The motion judge declined referral, characterized the sale as divisible, and dismissed the counterclaim partly for alleged discovery noncompliance, limitations, and insufficient fraud pleading. After further proceedings, including withdrawal of claims for reimbursement of partially performed watcher services, Menorah obtained summary judgment on its complaint, plus $11,627.02 in contractual attorney’s fees and $1,641.35 in costs. Needle appealed these rulings. (106–107.)

## Civil adjudication applied neutral principles
The court agreed that the Free Exercise and Establishment Clauses did not require abstention here. It distinguished civil decisions about doctrine, ecclesiastical governance, ministerial functions, and regulatory entanglement from application of neutral contract rules. The parties’ agreement selected New Jersey law and permitted Superior Court litigation. More fundamentally, the dispute between secular parties concerned whether the promised watchers appeared at all, rather than the doctrinal adequacy of how a ritual was performed. The claimed consequential harm was distress to survivors, not a religious injury to the deceased requiring theological evaluation. The court therefore allowed adjudication of both the collection action and counterclaim on those facts. (108–110.)

## Itemized pricing did not establish divisibility
Intent, gathered from the agreement and circumstances, determined whether the parties had exchanged one entire undertaking or separate transactions. Menorah relied on independent prices for each item. The court explained that mandatory funeral-service itemization was a consumer-protection disclosure, not dispositive proof that the parties intended each item to be independently enforceable despite failure of the rest. A unit rate alone did not establish willingness to accept partial performance at the same proportional price. (110–112 & n. 2.)

The record did not support treating material failures—such as failure to transport remains or supply the selected coffin—as automatically curable by deducting their listed prices. The same approach could not simply be presumed for the vigil. The court left a jury to determine whether full provision of watchers was a material condition. Its rejection of severability therefore did not itself fix the breach’s materiality, the client’s damages, or the funeral home’s ultimate recovery. (112–113.)

If the jury found a material breach preventing contractual recovery, Menorah nevertheless might recover the value of its remaining services in quantum meruit, allowing for the damages caused by the breach. The listed unit prices were not an automatic measure: missing a material component might reduce the worth of the other services to the recipient. A fraction of delivered performance did not necessarily have the same fraction of the value expected from an intact funeral undertaking. (113.)

## The contract counterclaim could seek emotional-distress damages
The record failed to establish a sufficient basis for dismissal with prejudice as a discovery sanction. It did not clearly identify the requests, feasibility of compliance, relevance, prejudice, timely service, or compliance with required sanction procedures. A lesser sanction should suffice where possible. The court vacated that ground of dismissal in the interest of justice. Negligence, misrepresentation, and fraud claims remained untimely under the two-year period even allowing tolling during the initial action; Needle concentrated his appeal on contract. (113–114.)

The contractual claim could include consequential emotional-distress damages when foreseeable at contracting. Funeral services are intended to offer comfort and solace, so a breach could, on suitable facts, produce precisely that harm. Menorah had promised to provide or arrange the watcher services; it had not merely promised to disburse cash to others. The court rejected applying the heightened severity test for intentional infliction of emotional distress to this contract claim and rejected difficulty of monetary valuation as a reason for dismissal. These rulings made a trial possible; they did not prove every asserted injury or grant a dollar award. (114–118.)

## Two different fee consequences
The $4,877.02 readiness sanction was within the trial court’s discretion and remained in place. Although Needle’s expert had died, he had not confirmed that his earlier adjournment request was granted. He returned from Israel the morning of trial unprepared, after Menorah had incurred preparation expense. The opinion’s chronology distinguishes that earlier ungranted request from the conditioned adjournment ultimately allowed when he was not ready. (106, 118.)

The later contractual collection-fee award was vacated with the underlying contract rulings, without prejudice to an appropriate award if Menorah prevailed on its contractual claim after retrial. Contractual fees would not be available on a recovery solely in quantum meruit. The counterclaim’s contract component and collection dispute thus returned for further proceedings, while the tort dismissals and readiness sanction remained. (118–119.)

## Assigned discrepancies and reading scope
The assigned Faithless Servants account on printed page 882 dates the case 2008; the original report gives June 8, 2006. It describes discovery sometime after the funeral and an estate refusing payment and filing suits; the actual opinion describes discovery shortly before the funeral, Menorah suing Needle, and Needle counterclaiming. Its condensed statement that breach and emotional-distress entitlement were held also overstates the finality: materiality and fact-supported damages were left for trial. The assigned discussion usefully poses restitution-offset questions, but the brief follows the original procedural and factual account.
