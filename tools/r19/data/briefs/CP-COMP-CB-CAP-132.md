# Marshall v. Marshall

**547 U.S. 293 (2006) — Supreme Court of the United States, May 1, 2006.** Justice Ginsburg wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer, and Alito. Justice Stevens concurred in part and in the judgment. All nine agreed on the result; Stevens disputed preserving a separate probate exception. Assigned account: *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 186, printed page 326, “Note on Judicially Created Exceptions to Diversity Jurisdiction.”

## Competing proceedings and the alleged interference

Vickie Lynn Marshall, also known as Anna Nicole Smith, married J. Howard Marshall II on June 27, 1994. He died August 4, 1995. He had given her substantial gifts during their relationship but left her nothing in his will. Vickie maintained that he intended to provide for her through a catchall trust. His estate plan instead benefited his son, E. Pierce Marshall, through a living trust and a will pouring other assets into that trust at death. **300.**

While Texas probate proceedings continued, Vickie filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in California in January 1996. Pierce filed a proof of claim alleging that she had defamed him through her lawyers’ public accusations of forgery, fraud, and overreaching. He sought a determination that the asserted debt was nondischargeable. Vickie asserted truth as a defense and counterclaimed for tortious interference with the gift she expected. She alleged that Pierce isolated his father, misrepresented matters to him, and transferred property contrary to his wishes. The counterclaim converted her objection into an adversary proceeding. **300–301 & n.1.**

The bankruptcy court granted Vickie summary judgment on Pierce’s defamation claim and, after trial, awarded her more than $449 million compensatory damages, reduced by any Texas probate recovery, plus $25 million punitive damages. It treated the objection and counterclaim as core proceedings permitting final judgment. Pierce’s posttrial challenge argued that only the Texas probate proceeding could hear her claim. The bankruptcy court rejected the challenge as waived and also reasoned that adjudicating rights without taking possession of probate property was permissible. **301–302.**

In Texas, Vickie initially contested the will and asserted tortious interference but voluntarily dismissed those claims after the bankruptcy judgment. The probate court, following a jury trial, declared the trust and will valid. The federal district court later rejected the bankruptcy court’s waiver analysis but held that the probate exception did not exclude Vickie’s tort. It classified the claim as noncore, treated the bankruptcy findings as proposed rather than final, and independently reviewed them. It found that Pierce had conspired to suppress or destroy a trust instrument and falsify documents to strip his father’s assets. It awarded approximately $44.3 million compensatory damages and the same amount punitive damages. These were lower-court findings and awards, not fresh Supreme Court determinations of wrongdoing. **302–304 & n.2.**

## Appellate question and holding

The Ninth Circuit reversed on jurisdiction alone. It extended the probate exception to questions ordinarily addressed in deciding an estate-planning instrument’s validity, even though Vickie’s tort claim did not itself involve probate or administration. It also regarded the Texas probate court’s assertion of exclusive jurisdiction as binding on the federal court. **304–305.**

The Supreme Court held that neither rationale defeated federal jurisdiction. The probate exception reserves probate or annulment of a will and administration of an estate to state probate courts, and prevents federal courts from disposing of property in a state probate court’s custody. It does not exclude otherwise jurisdictionally proper personal claims merely because they relate to an estate. Vickie sought a personal damages judgment against Pierce, not probate of the will or control of estate property. **311–314.**

## Narrowing the exception rather than enlarging it

The Court used *Ankenbrandt*’s treatment of the domestic-relations exception as an analogy. Although the historical justification was uncertain, that decision had preserved a longstanding statutory construction while limiting it to divorce, alimony, and custody decrees rather than ordinary tort claims. Specialized state proficiency did not justify removing every related tort from federal adjudication. **305–308.**

Here, federal jurisdiction rested on the bankruptcy grant in § 1334. The Court did not resolve the historical debate about English chancery powers or decide whether an uncodified probate exception applied to § 1334 at all. Vickie’s claim fell outside the recognized exception even assuming its application. Footnote 3 separately noted that the bankruptcy court had rejected a mandatory-abstention request as untimely; the Supreme Court did not decide whether a timely request would require abstention. **308–309 & n.3.**

The central precedent, *Markham v. Allen*, allowed a federal judgment establishing rights in an estate while protecting the state court’s custody of its property. Some lower courts expanded *Markham*’s phrase “interfere with the probate proceedings” into a broad bar against probate-related matters. The Court instead read it with the narrower property-custody language accompanying it. The principle was essentially the ordinary rule against two courts simultaneously exercising control over the same property. Some redundancy was preferable to an incoherent expansion. **309–312 & n.4.**

Applying that rule, Vickie’s tort involved allegedly fraudulent interference with a gift, conduct that ordinary federal and state trial courts could evaluate. She sought neither to annul the will nor to reach property held by the probate court. State probate expertise did not provide a sound reason to expand the exception. **312.**

## State exclusivity, preclusion, and reserved questions

Texas law governed the substantive elements of the tort, but Texas could not reserve adjudication of that transitory claim exclusively to its probate courts and thereby diminish federal jurisdiction. A court’s jurisdiction comes from the law creating that court. Creating a specialized state tribunal does not eliminate an otherwise proper federal forum. **313–314.**

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Durfee v. Duke* confused different questions. A fully and fairly litigated state judgment determining that state court’s own jurisdiction can ordinarily receive full faith and credit. Here, however, the contested issue was the federal courts’ jurisdiction, which Texas could not decide away by announcing exclusive competence over this tort. **314.**

That conclusion did not settle preclusion. Footnote 5 distinguished decisions barring inheritance-interference claims through ordinary preclusion from decisions rejecting jurisdiction. The parties disputed whether the particular sequence and scope of the Texas and federal proceedings precluded Vickie’s claim. The Texas judgment became final February 11, 2002, before the district court’s independent final judgment on March 7, 2002. The Ninth Circuit had not reached claim or issue preclusion, nor whether the counterclaim was core. All those questions remained open on remand. **313 n.5, 314–315.**

## Stevens’s concurrence and disposition

Stevens agreed that the case belonged in federal court but would abandon the probate exception altogether. He disputed its uncertain historical foundation and thought even the majority’s narrowed description could exclude controversies federal courts had previously heard, including some will-annulment and estate-accounting disputes. Ordinary principles—such as requiring a dispute between parties and respecting another court’s custody of property—could adequately constrain jurisdiction without a special probate category. His view was a concurrence, not the Court’s elimination of the exception. **315–318 & concurrence nn.1–2.**

The Court **reversed and remanded the Ninth Circuit’s judgment**. It removed the probate-jurisdiction obstacle without finally reinstating an unassailable damages award or resolving the reserved bankruptcy and preclusion issues. **315.**
