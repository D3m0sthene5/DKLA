# Holman Erection Company v. Orville E. Madsen & Sons, Inc.

330 N.W.2d 693 (Minn. 1983) | Contracts

## Decision and setting

The Minnesota Supreme Court held that a general contractor’s use of a subcontractor’s price and listing of that subcontractor in its successful public bid did not, without more, accept the subcontractor’s offer. It affirmed summary judgment dismissing the subcontractor’s claim for lost profits. Justice Yetka wrote the March 4, 1983 opinion. The report supplies one court opinion, with no separate writing, judicial footnote, or numerical voting lineup. 693, 695–699.

## Facts and bidding sequence

The City of Moorhead sought bids for a wastewater treatment facility, to be opened publicly on January 22, 1981. Madsen invited potential subcontractors, including Holman, to bid. Two or three hours before the prime bid deadline, Holman telephoned its price and proposed scope to Madsen’s Wisconsin office. Someone recorded the information and relayed it to Madsen’s representatives in Moorhead. Holman submitted the identical bid to six other general contractors competing for the same project. Its preparation expense was not allocated to any particular general contractor. 693–694.

Madsen used Holman’s figures and its own past experience to prepare the prime bid. The city required bidders to identify proposed subcontractors, so Madsen listed Holman for steel erection, approximately two percent of the project. The listing requirement came from the awarding authority, not a statute requiring such a listing. Madsen received the general contract on February 2. There had been no further communication with Holman promising it the work. 694, 696.

After award, Madsen negotiated details and executed individual subcontracts. It instead contacted Van Knight Steel Erection, asked about that firm’s minority-business qualifications, and eventually selected it for steel erection. Federal minority-business regulations required an effort to assign part of the public project to minority businesses. Van Knight qualified and offered significant materials and some labor outside Holman’s bid, although its price was slightly higher. Holman learned that it was the low steel-erection bidder and had been listed in Madsen’s prime bid, then telephoned Madsen. It was told another firm would get the work. These two calls—submission and later inquiry—were the parties’ only communications. 694, 698–699.

## Procedure and arguments

Holman sued in Clay County District Court for the profits it alleged it lost when Madsen awarded the work elsewhere. It asserted that use of its price and the public listing constituted acceptance and created a contract. The district court granted Madsen summary judgment. The underlying facts were undisputed; Holman contended that a factfinder could nevertheless infer formation from them. The supreme court considered both formation and the propriety of summary judgment. 693–695, 699.

Holman argued that the listing had no other reasonable explanation, that a general contractor knowing the prime bid was public should expect the listed subcontractor to infer acceptance, and that fairness required reciprocal obligations if reliance could bind the subcontractor. The court rejected an automatic-contract inference, rather than holding that acceptance must always use a particular form. 695.

## Objective assent and the authorities

Acceptance required an objective manifestation of assent. Written words, oral words, and conduct could each suffice, but the actor had to know or have reason to know that its conduct could communicate assent. The court applied Restatement (Second) §19 and comment c. Here, a listing of a proposed subcontractor, without an additional acceptance communication, was not such a manifestation. 695.

The court reviewed decisions from Idaho, California, Washington, Arizona, and federal appellate courts rejecting formation merely from use or listing of a subcontract bid. Some dealt with mistaken listings, licensing difficulties, or statutorily required listings. Holman’s attempt to distinguish those circumstances did not undermine their contract reasoning. In particular, the California authorities distinguished contract rights from a potentially separate statutory remedy for an impermissible substitution. The court did not transform that possible remedy into a Minnesota contract right. Nor did Madsen’s absence of mistake or Holman’s apparent capability establish assent. Holman had shown no additional communication, detrimental reliance, or loss caused by the substitution apart from the hoped-for profits. 695–697.

## Why reliance did not create reciprocal obligations

Minnesota precedent could bind a subcontractor to its bid through promissory estoppel when a general contractor justifiably relied on it and became committed to the prime contract. That obligation rested on reliance, not on a finding that use of the bid formed a reciprocal contract. The court rejected the proposed symmetry because the parties’ reliance positions differed. 695, 697–698.

A general contractor who receives the prime award is committed to its price; a subcontractor’s subsequent withdrawal can therefore cause it a financial loss. In contrast, Holman prepared one bid for several general contractors and incurred its ordinary bidding overhead regardless of which contractor won or whether it ultimately received the subcontract. It incurred no further expense in reliance on Madsen’s listing. The disappointed prospect of the work did not supply the detrimental reliance necessary for estoppel here. 698.

## Selection flexibility and rejected alternatives

The court also considered commentators’ proposals to bind general contractors at the listing stage, including presumptive formation subject to exceptions and an analogy to the UCC’s flexible acceptance rule. Those were proposed alternatives, not rules the court adopted or an Article 2 holding about this construction agreement. Their policy goals included preventing bid shopping, improving certainty, and retaining room to negotiate unresolved details. 697.

The court recognized bid shopping as undesirable. But subcontractors’ practice of submitting bids shortly before the prime deadline produced a hectic process in which price and broad scope could be compiled quickly while detailed terms remained for later clarification. Selection also depended on reliability, work quality, capacity, and minority-business obligations, rather than price alone. Here, binding Madsen to every listed subcontractor could have prevented adjustment necessary to satisfy those obligations, potentially cost it the prime contract, and increased the city’s expense. Van Knight’s qualifications and broader supply commitment illustrated the need for flexibility. The court left the proposed general change to the legislature. 698–699.

## Exact result and assigned scope

Because the undisputed facts did not establish acceptance, no permissible inference from them changed the legal outcome. The court **affirmed** summary judgment dismissing Holman’s claim. It awarded no lost profits and ordered no trial. The decision concerns mere use or listing under these circumstances; it does not say a general contractor can never bind itself through further assent or other legally sufficient circumstances. 699.
