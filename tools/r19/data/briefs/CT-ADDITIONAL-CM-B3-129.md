# Lewis v. Fisher Service Company
329 S.C. 78, 495 S.E.2d 440 (1998) | Contracts

## Decision and certified posture
Justice Toal wrote the South Carolina Supreme Court’s unanimous January 12, 1998 decision; Chief Justice Finney and Justices Moore, Waller, and Burnett concurred. Answering federal certified questions, the court allowed after-acquired evidence to defeat liability altogether in an employee-handbook contract action, but only upon clear and convincing proof that the misconduct was sufficiently severe that the employer actually would have fired the employee on that ground alone. It did not itself vacate the federal jury verdict, enter judgment for either litigant, or decide whether this employer met that standard. The exact disposition was “Certified Questions Answered.” Pinpoints below use the South Carolina report. (78–79, 87–88.)

## Handbook, disputed recording, and two trials
Fisher hired Douglas Lewis as a machinist in 1984 and gave him a Practices and Policies manual on his first day. The handbook promised progressive discipline but allowed immediate dismissal for violations of very serious and widely recognized behavior standards. In April 1991, when Lewis applied to become a quality-control inspector, he used a pocket recorder during an interview with supervisor Andy Simpson. The recording was later played to other employees in the break area. Fisher treated the incident as serious enough to bypass progressive discipline and discharged him. (79.)

Lewis sued for breach of the handbook, alleging demotion and discharge contrary to its discipline provisions. He maintained that he had told Simpson of the recording and that another employee played it. Fisher claimed the recording was secret and Lewis himself played it. A June 1993 jury awarded $400,000 in actual damages representing back and front pay. That verdict was set aside for reasons unrelated to the certification, and a June 1994 retrial produced a $355,000 verdict. (79–80.)

Fisher moved for judgment as a matter of law or a new trial. The district court considered secret recording of a superior serious enough to bypass progressive discipline but concluded that, viewing the evidence favorably to Lewis, the jury could find the Simpson recording disclosed and its later playback performed by someone else. That original incident therefore did not require overturning the verdict on the stated analysis. (80.)

## Other recordings discovered after discharge
In deposition after filing suit, Lewis admitted secretly recording a conversation with general manager David Suk and probably one with shop manager Jeff Klatt before termination. Unlike the Simpson incident, nondisclosure of these recordings was undisputed. Suk testified that secret recording of management was serious misconduct, that he did not know Lewis had recorded him, and that he would have immediately fired Lewis had he known. These later-discovered, earlier acts supplied the proposed defense; they were not the conduct Fisher knew about when it originally discharged him. (80–81.)

The district court certified whether South Carolina recognized that defense in handbook cases and whether it could eliminate liability, cut off recovery at discovery, or cut off recovery at judgment. The questions arose to assist disposition of the pending post-trial motion, not in a direct state appeal from the federal verdict. (81.)

## Private contract rights versus public-policy protections
The court traced the dispute over complete defenses through Summers and Wallace, then explained McKennon’s rejection of a complete bar in an ADEA discrimination action. McKennon balanced the statutory purposes of deterrence and compensation against legitimate employer interests through remedies, generally permitting back pay until discovery while limiting reinstatement and front pay. Lewis’s sole judicial footnote recounts that remedial discussion; it does not impose that damages-only rule on the handbook case. South Carolina had adopted McKennon’s approach in Baber’s statutory whistleblower action. (82–84 & n. 1.)

Reviewing other jurisdictions, the court found a persuasive distinction between private employment-contract claims and claims implicating major public interests, such as discrimination, whistleblowing, or workers’ compensation rights. Public protections justified refusing to let after-acquired misconduct erase liability for the employer’s earlier statutory wrong. Handbook contract litigation, in the court’s analysis, concerned private rights and did not present those same overriding purposes. It therefore declined to impose an absolute prohibition on using the evidence to defeat contractual liability. This distinction addressed the certified cause of action; it did not abolish Baber’s different statutory treatment. (84–86.)

## Severity and a heightened burden of proof
The court nevertheless recognized the incentive for employers to search an employee’s record for minor missteps after being sued. An employer might previously tolerate occasional tardiness yet later claim it would have caused discharge. Unrestricted use could make contractual promises effectively unenforceable. (87.)

The court imposed two protections. First, the misconduct must be severe enough that the employee in fact would have been terminated on that ground alone if it had been known at discharge; conduct that would not actually have caused termination was inadmissible for this purpose. Second, the employer must prove the requisite facts by clear and convincing evidence, rather than a preponderance. The combination excluded doubtful or insignificant wrongdoing while admitting serious wrongdoing genuinely sufficient for discharge. Accordingly, a mere assertion that the employer could have fired the employee, or that the conduct violated some workplace rule, was insufficient. (87–88.)

## Application left to the certifying court and assignment
The answers authorize a complete defense when those requirements are established. They do not find Suk’s testimony conclusive, determine all relevant credibility issues, or replace the federal court’s disposition of the pending motion. The $400,000 and $355,000 figures describe the earlier verdicts, not amounts awarded or affirmed by this certified-answer decision. (80–81, 87–88.)
