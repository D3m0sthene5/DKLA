# Daar v. Yellow Cab Co.

**67 Cal. 2d 695, 433 P.2d 732, 63 Cal. Rptr. 724 (1967) — November 15, 1967 — Civil Procedure**

Justice Sullivan wrote for the California Supreme Court. Chief Justice Traynor and Justices McComb, Peters, Tobriner, Mosk, and Burke concurred, making the reported decision unanimous. The court sustained the legal sufficiency of a proposed class action at the pleading stage. It did not order a temporary reduction in taxicab fares, despite the textbook’s description of that remedy under this citation.

## Facts and procedural history

David Daar sued on November 20, 1964, for alleged overcharges by Yellow Cab during the preceding four years. Yellow Cab Company of California was the only appearing defendant, although the complaint used additional fictitious names. Daar pleaded two classes: riders who paid with coupons from Yellow Cab script books and riders who paid cash. He alleged that the company’s written franchise required fares at rates fixed by the Los Angeles Public Utilities Commission, but its meters were adjusted to charge higher rates. The proportion of overcharge was allegedly uniform, and company information could establish the total. These were allegations accepted for testing the complaint, not findings of proven misconduct. (699–703 & nn.4–8.)

Script books and individual coupons bore corresponding serial numbers. The complaint alleged that company records could identify purchasers and users and determine their overcharges. For cash riders, it alleged that the amount and nature of the overcharge were known to Yellow Cab and ascertainable at trial. Daar claimed to have spent $100 on script books and $100 in cash fares, with an unknown personal overcharge. Each count alleged aggregate overcharges exceeding $100,000 and sought damages and an accounting. Small individual losses allegedly made separate lawsuits economically infeasible. (700–703 & nn.5–8.)

Yellow Cab demurred for failure to state a claim or a proper class action, nonjoinder, uncertainty, and lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The superior court’s January 28, 1965 minute order sustained the jurisdictional demurrer without leave to amend and transferred the case to municipal court. It effectively treated Daar’s own small claim as all that remained. No formal judgment appeared in the record. (698–699 n.1, 703.)

## Appealability and governing requirements

An order sustaining a demurrer ordinarily was nonappealable. Here, however, the transfer and rejection of class treatment effectively dismissed the claims of every member except Daar. The Supreme Court assessed the order’s legal effect rather than its label and treated it as a final judgment for purposes of appeal. Otherwise the destruction of the class action would evade review. Daar clarified that he was appealing that specific order; attempted appeals from other unspecified rulings were dismissed. (698–699 & n.1, 717.)

Code of Civil Procedure § 382 reflected equity’s doctrine of representative litigation. Despite statutory language framed in alternatives, precedent required both an ascertainable class and a well-defined community of interest in the questions of law and fact. The court explained those requirements through the relationship between shared liability questions, individualized facts, and the binding effect of a class judgment. (703–706.)

Identifying every person at the outset was different from showing that an ascertainable class existed. Individuals whose entitlement depended on entirely separate factual premises could not simply be bound by someone else’s suit. But an inability presently to name all people affected by common issues did not automatically foreclose representative litigation. A later claimant ordinarily would still have to identify himself and establish his own entitlement to a share. Permitting the action to proceed also did not foreclose closer scrutiny of representation and preclusion in a later collateral challenge. (704–706 & n.9.)

## Community of interest and individual recovery

Yellow Cab argued that community had to extend to common relief, not merely common issues of liability. The court rejected a categorical requirement of a common fund or a single indivisible recovery. Individual calculations could be subsidiary to common questions, much as beneficiaries’ shares could be determined after litigation concerning a trust fund. What mattered was whether numerous substantial individual questions would remain, or whether the common issues were sufficiently important to justify one representative adjudication. (706–709, 713.)

The court distinguished cases in which each person’s right to recover depended on unique facts, such as wrongful exclusion from a football game or unemployment benefits dependent on each claimant’s status and conduct. By contrast, precedent allowed claims involving identical secured instruments or a common method of retaining cotton seed where basic liability questions were shared and individual amounts could be determined mathematically. The comparisons supplied a practical test rather than declaring that every group of people invoking one statute formed a proper class. (710–713.)

The 1966 federal Rule 23 revision supplied a useful comparison: it removed the former express common-relief language and added criteria substantially consistent with the court’s approach. But the court was applying California § 382, not treating the federal rule as the directly controlling procedural law. Defendant did not allege a conflict between Daar and class members or challenge his membership; its objection was that their claims were separate and distinct. (707 n.10, 708–709 & nn.11–13.)

## Application to the complaint

A demurrer admitted properly pleaded material facts, not bare legal conclusions. Taking the allegations as pleaded, both groups faced a uniform overcharge arising from the same basic pricing practice. An accounting could establish amounts from company records and information; the complaint did not require thousands of separate trials on liability. The fact that amounts ultimately differed was not by itself disqualifying. The basic questions would dominate individual lawsuits, while separate actions would burden parties and courts and would likely cost more than each rider could recover. Without a class procedure, Yellow Cab might retain the benefits of the alleged wrongdoing. (713–715.)

Defendant insisted every rider would have to appear and prove particular rides, fares, and damages. Given the alleged ability to ascertain the full overcharges from company information, the court rejected that premise at this stage. This was a ruling on the sufficiency of allegations, not a completed factual determination that records actually yielded every passenger’s individual amount. (715–716.)

Yellow Cab also requested judicial notice of fare structures, waiting time, quarter-mile increments, and tipping practices to discredit the uniform-overcharge allegation. Although judicially noticed facts could be considered on demurrer, these proposed facts were not beyond reasonable dispute within the governing standard. The court declined to take notice and defeat the pleading on that basis. (716.)

## Exact judgment and the textbook’s remedial discrepancy

The court reversed the January 28 order and directed the superior court to overrule the demurrer on every specified ground except uncertainty. The trial court had never ruled on the special demurrer for uncertainty, and the Supreme Court left that issue for it to decide. Other attempted appeals were dismissed, and Daar recovered appeal costs. Thus the result reopened the class action; it neither awarded overcharge damages nor finally disposed of every pleading objection. (717 & n.16.)
