# Barnes v. Yahoo!, Inc.
570 F.3d 1096 (9th Cir. 2009), amended June 22 and further corrected September 28, 2009 | Contracts

## Court, posture, and precise result

Judge O’Scannlain wrote for Judges Graber and Callahan on an appeal from dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6). The court affirmed dismissal of an Oregon negligent-undertaking theory, reversed dismissal of the contract theory based on promissory estoppel, and remanded; each party bore its own costs. It decided the reach of Yahoo’s § 230(c)(1) defense, without deciding that Barnes had actually established an enforceable promise or that Yahoo lacked a different defense under § 230(c)(2). The panel’s June amendment deleted Part II of the original May opinion, renumbered the remaining parts, and replaced the federal-claims footnote. It unanimously denied both parties’ rehearing petitions; no active judge requested an en banc vote. The September order corrected “liability” to “liability shield.” This brief uses the amended opinion and that correction, rather than the superseded text. (Official amended slip 7411, 7414–7416, 7430, 7436; September order 13929.)

## Alleged events and litigation

After Cecilia Barnes ended a relationship in late 2004, her former boyfriend allegedly posted unauthorized Yahoo profiles containing nude photographs taken without her knowledge and an apparent solicitation for sex. Posing as Barnes in chat rooms, he directed men to the profiles, which included her workplace’s physical and electronic addresses and telephone number. Strangers then contacted and visited her workplace expecting sexual encounters. These were allegations accepted for purposes of dismissal, not findings after trial; Yahoo expressly indicated that it would contest them. (Slip 7416–7417 & n.1.)

Barnes first mailed Yahoo her photo identification and a signed statement denying involvement and requesting removal, following Yahoo’s procedure. After a month without a response, she wrote again; the following month she sent two more requests. A local television report was imminent when Yahoo’s communications director, Ms. Osako, called and asked Barnes to fax her earlier statements directly. Osako said she would personally take them to the division responsible for unauthorized profiles and that the division would take care of the problem. Barnes alleged that she relied on this assurance and took no further action. After approximately two more months, she sued in Oregon state court. The profiles disappeared shortly after the lawsuit, apparently permanently. Yahoo removed the case to federal court. (Slip 7417–7418.)

Although the complaint was imprecise, the court construed it to assert negligent undertaking under Restatement (Second) of Torts § 323 and an enforceable promise under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90. The district court dismissed on Yahoo’s § 230(c)(1) defense. Barnes argued that her theories arose from Yahoo’s undertaking and promise, rather than its responsibility for another person’s content. (Slip 7418, 7424–7425.)

## Statutory rule and the unsuccessful tort theory

Section 230(c)(1) prevents treating a provider or user of an interactive computer service as the publisher or speaker of information supplied by another information content provider. Together with § 230(e)(3), it protects an eligible provider against an inconsistent state-law claim that would impose liability in that publishing capacity. Yahoo’s eligibility and the former boyfriend’s responsibility for the profiles were not disputed. The decisive question was the source of the asserted duty. Changing a claim’s label from defamation to negligence cannot avoid the statute if liability still depends on reviewing, editing, publishing, or withdrawing another person’s content. The amended footnote expressly limited this restatement to the state-law claims presented, without deciding how the discussion would apply to a federal claim. (Slip 7419–7424 & nn.4–8.)

Barnes’s tort theory asserted that Yahoo, once undertaking removal, had to exercise reasonable care. The court rejected the proposed distinction: the particular undertaking was removal of third-party profiles, itself a publisher’s function. A statutory bar would be ineffective if a plaintiff could evade it merely by describing the failure to withdraw publication as negligent performance of services. The opinion’s comparison to liability for leaving defamatory graffiti on a bar wall illustrated this publishing duty; it did not adjudicate a separate defamation count. The court also reserved whether Barnes otherwise pleaded the Oregon tort properly. (Slip 7424–7427 & nn.9–10.)

Barnes’s distributor argument did not require settling all the distinctions between primary and secondary publishers in defamation law. The statute operated more broadly than that particular tort and could not be confined to the precise historical case that prompted enactment. Nor did its encouragement of voluntary screening limit § 230(c)(1) to providers that actively screened content. Section 230(c)(2) supplied a further, distinct shield for certain good-faith restrictions of objectionable material, potentially including providers outside subsection (c)(1). (Slip 7427–7430 & n.11.)

## Why the promise theory survived the statutory defense

Oregon promissory estoppel required a promise, reasonably foreseeable inducement of the kind of conduct that occurred, actual reliance, and a substantial change of position. The opinion treated it principally as a means of enforcing a contract without consideration. Ordinary principles of objective commitment therefore remained important: the promise had to be sufficiently definite to justify understanding that a commitment had been made. The discussion of contract law generally did not displace Oregon law on remand. Neither the availability nor the measure of any damages was decided; footnote 13 noted that courts differed between expectation and reliance remedies. (Slip 7431–7433 & nn.12–13.)

The contractual duty arose from an enforceable commitment voluntarily assumed as a promisor, rather than from the provider’s publishing status. Promising to remove content could therefore generate a different legal obligation even though performance would involve an ordinary publishing act. The court acknowledged the historical relationship between assumpsit, negligent undertaking, and promissory estoppel, but considered the promise’s legally significant commitment sufficient to distinguish the contract theory. (Slip 7433–7434 & n.14.)

This distinction did not convert every helpful response or monitoring policy into a contract. A court could not infer the required definite promise merely from an attempt to remove material. Objective manifestations of an intention to be bound remained necessary, and Yahoo could disclaim that intention. Formation defenses and defects such as duress, incapacity, and unconscionability likewise remained available. An enforceable voluntary promise could depart from the statutory baseline governing strangers, but the appellate court had not determined that Osako’s words actually satisfied all the Oregon requirements. It held only that § 230(c)(1) did not itself bar this contract theory. (Slip 7435–7436 & n.15.)
