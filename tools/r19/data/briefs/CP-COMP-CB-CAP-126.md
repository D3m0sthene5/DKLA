# Blair Holdings Corp. v. Rubinstein

**133 F. Supp. 496 (S.D.N.Y. 1955) — United States District Court for the Southern District of New York, July 19, 1955.** District Judge Bicks decided the renewed jurisdictional motion. No separate writing or panel vote is involved. The assigned account is CivPro, physical PDF page 182, printed pages 316–317.

## The parties and jurisdictional dispute

Blair Holdings, a corporation organized under New York law, sued Serge Rubinstein and other defendants. It relied solely on diversity jurisdiction, alleging that Rubinstein was not a citizen of the United States or New York. The reported opinion does not develop the underlying substantive transaction or decide the merits of Blair's claim. Its question was whether the plaintiff had established the foreign citizenship necessary to litigate that claim against Rubinstein in federal court. (497–498.)

Rubinstein had been born a subject of the Russian Czar and had later acquired Portuguese citizenship. The evidence, however, raised whether either citizenship continued when suit began. His family had fled Russia in 1918 without Soviet permission. The court treated the applicable Soviet citizenship-forfeiture rules as ending his Soviet citizenship; a Portuguese consular certificate established that Portugal had cancelled his Portuguese citizenship and had never reinstated it. Other evidence included a Nansen passport associated with stateless persons and his registration as stateless with the United States Department of Justice. (498–499.)

## Procedure and the burden of proof

Judge Edelstein had initially held the dismissal motion in abeyance because affidavits did not satisfactorily resolve citizenship. Blair was allowed jurisdictional discovery, but Edelstein also explained that a negative allegation—that Rubinstein was not American—did not positively identify him as a citizen or subject of a foreign State. That earlier reported ruling was procedural history, not the judgment now being decided. (497–498 & n.1.)

Blair then took depositions of Rubinstein and his mother, producing 388 pages and 35 exhibits. After the depositions ended in July 1954, a renewed motion was denied as premature because Blair sought more time to investigate possible citizenship in Russia, Portugal, Austria, France, or Israel. On the second renewal, Blair no longer sought further investigation or a continuance. Judge Bicks therefore treated its jurisdictional presentation as complete and decided the motion. (498.)

Rubinstein died shortly after the motion was argued. The parties agreed that the claimed cause of action survived, and his executors were substituted. Footnote 2 explains that jurisdiction was assessed at commencement: if it existed then, his later death would not destroy it. The court's inquiry was thus not whether the executors independently supplied a new basis for jurisdiction. (498 n.2.)

## Issue, rule, and holding

Was Rubinstein a citizen or subject of a foreign State under the then-applicable §1332(a)(2), or could Blair rely simply on his being a non-American alien? Blair had to establish jurisdiction by a preponderance of the evidence. Difficulty investigating an internationally mobile person's nationality did not relax that burden. The historical statute required a controversy between citizens of a State and foreign States or their citizens or subjects; its amount requirement then exceeded $3,000. The opinion denied jurisdiction because Blair failed to establish that Rubinstein was a citizen or subject of a qualifying foreign State. (498 & n.3; 499–502.)

## Why earlier citizenship did not suffice

Blair relied on the presumption that established citizenship continues. The court accepted that principle but held the presumption rebutted for both Russia and Portugal. The Russian departure, applicable forfeiture law, later Portuguese citizenship, and stateless documentation undermined Soviet nationality. Blair's expert did not establish a sufficient basis for a contrary conclusion; footnote 4 records the expert's qualified statement that the facts were not established in enough detail to reach a final conclusion. (498–499 & n.4.)

For Portugal, Blair regarded the consular cancellation certificate with suspicion but did not deny that it had issued. The court invoked each country's authority to determine its nationals and the presumption of regular foreign administrative procedure. It accepted the certificate as sufficient proof of cancellation without reinstatement. With both presumptions overcome, Blair lacked affirmative proof of either nationality. The Nansen passport's issuance as recognition of stateless status was described in the opinion as alleged; the broader jurisdictional conclusion rested on the whole evidentiary presentation, not that document alone. (499.)

## Why “alien” did not mean every non-American

Blair alternatively argued that any alien, including a stateless person, qualified. Bicks rejected that reading. Inferior federal courts exercise jurisdiction Congress confers, not every possible controversy a party might seek to place within Article III. The statutory grant was strictly construed, and its words required a connection to a particular foreign sovereign. (499–500.)

The court discussed foreign-party jurisdiction's historical concerns: protection of foreigners under treaties and avoidance of disputes with other sovereigns arising from treatment of their nationals. Against that background, references to aliens and foreigners were shorthand for citizens or subjects of a specific foreign State. Although the Judiciary Act of 1789 used the term “alien,” the court read that term in its constitutional setting and relied on earlier pleading decisions requiring identification of a foreign sovereign. Congress's 1875 legislation adopted more exact citizenship language, substantially continued in the 1948 Code. (500–501 & nn.5–8.)

Blair's authorities using broader terminology involved persons actually connected to a foreign State. They did not establish jurisdiction over a stateless person. The court also acknowledged qualifications to its historical examples: footnote 9 noted a later contrary characterization of Palestine, and footnote 10 explained Congress's extension of diversity jurisdiction to District of Columbia and territorial citizens. Those discussions were not rulings on the contemporary citizenship of every person in those categories. (501–502 & nn.9–10.)

## The remaining objections and judgment

Blair claimed that denying federal jurisdiction gave Rubinstein immunity. The court answered that it withheld a particular forum, not substantive accountability: Blair could pursue its claim in state court. It also rejected Blair's analogy to expansive sovereign-immunity interpretations of the Eleventh Amendment. Preserving a sovereign's immunity was a different problem from enlarging Congress's diversity grant to stateless persons. (501–502 & nn.11–12.)

The court **granted the motion to dismiss the complaint as against Rubinstein**, now represented by his executors. It did not announce a disposition of all claims against every named defendant. (502.) The assignment accurately presents the citizenship problem; its question whether stateless persons need greater protection against bias is an editorial policy question, not an exception the court adopted.
