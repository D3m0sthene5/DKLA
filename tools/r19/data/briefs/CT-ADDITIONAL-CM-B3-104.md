# State ex rel. Schilling v. Baird; State ex rel. Klingler v. Baird
65 Wis. 2d 394, 222 N.W.2d 666 (Wis. 1974) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural setting
The Wisconsin Supreme Court affirmed the remand orders in these two deputy sheriffs’ back-pay actions on October 29, 1974. Justice Connor T. Hansen wrote the opinion; the report contains no separate writing or recorded division. The case resolves mitigation credits and prejudgment interest following reinstatement, rather than deciding anew whether the suspensions were lawful. It is the sequel to the 1972 decision reported at 56 Wis. 2d 460, which had reversed an award allowing full back pay without deductions for outside earnings. (394–397, 402.)

## Facts, amounts, and competing positions
Schilling and Klingler successfully appealed their suspensions to the circuit court and were reinstated. After the earlier Supreme Court remand, the parties stipulated that Klingler’s back pay was $11,499.16 and that the employer should receive a $1,000 credit for his self-employment during part of the suspension. That particular credit was no longer contested. They stipulated that Schilling’s gross back pay was $11,510.38 and that he had earned $3,869.13 during a portion of his suspension, but disagreed about whether those earnings should be deducted. The trial court deducted the full amount, leaving $7,641.25. It found that both officers had made reasonable and diligent efforts to find other work and denied prejudgment interest. (396.)

The employer challenged the adequacy of Klingler’s search between September 14, 1970, and January 1, 1971, and Schilling’s search between September 14, 1970, and March 1971. Klingler’s later self-employment was conceded; Schilling later obtained work in Wisconsin and Arizona, moving to Arizona in June 1971. The employer sought further mitigation credits for the earlier periods and invited the court to take judicial notice of available employment in Wisconsin, especially suburban Milwaukee. The officers disputed the credits and sought interest from the dates their wages would otherwise have been paid. (397–399, 402.)

Schilling’s separate argument relied on his midnight-to-8 a.m. sheriff’s shift: because his substitute earnings were obtained outside that shift, he maintained that none should reduce his recovery. The record also disclosed that, before suspension in September 1970, he had earned $3,369.24 in additional sheriff’s-office overtime that year. That overtime figure describes his earlier sheriff’s employment, not the $3,869.13 subsequently earned elsewhere. These newly clarified circumstances helped explain the trial court’s difficulty applying the earlier mandate. (400–401.)

## Mitigation duty and burden of proof
A discharged or suspended employee must seek other employment, and the employer may receive credit for earnings actually obtained or that could have been obtained. The employee need not seek or accept work of a different or inferior kind. But the employer bears the burden of establishing both a lack of reasonable and diligent efforts and the availability of suitable work. Whether opportunities existed is a factual question bearing on proof of a failure to mitigate, rather than on the existence of the underlying duty. (397–398.)

The officers were not necessarily limited, as a matter of law, to seeking police jobs: Klingler had prior experience with excavating and snow removal, and Schilling with roofing. Nevertheless, the employer introduced no evidence that alternative work was available to either officer during the disputed periods. It therefore failed to carry its burden. After reviewing the record, the court also concluded that the trial court’s findings concerning diligent efforts were not against the great weight and clear preponderance of the evidence. The Supreme Court did not recite the entire evidentiary record or substitute a newly found job-search chronology for those findings. (398–399.)

General judicial notice could not supply the missing proof. Although matters of common knowledge may be noticed, the court declined to assume that employment of like character, on similar and noninferior terms, in a reasonably convenient location, was available to these particular employees. This distinguishes an abstract possibility of work from evidence sufficient to reduce a specific back-pay claim. (399–400.)

## Actual substitute earnings and the earlier mandate
The 1972 mandate, reproduced in footnote 1, called for mutual consideration of whether earnings accrued during the officer’s regular duty hours and the amount earned outside those hours immediately before suspension. It did not make the clock alone decisive. The Supreme Court approved the trial court’s rejection of Schilling’s argument: otherwise he could avoid third-shift work, earn elsewhere, and retain all substitute earnings alongside unreduced back pay. The passage quoted in the casebook is the trial court’s explanation as reproduced and approved by the Supreme Court. (400 & n. 1, 401.)

The operative conclusion was that the employer received credit for Schilling’s suspension-period earnings from employment unrelated to outside or part-time employment he had held before suspension. Accordingly, the $3,869.13 deduction stood. The decision does not say that every preexisting side job or every source of income must automatically reduce damages. Its distinction preserves the difference between replacement earnings and an independent earning stream that could have continued alongside the original employment. (401.)

## Prejudgment interest and disposition
Interest required a fixed, determined, or readily determinable amount. The court recognized the usual qualification that an unliquidated setoff, counterclaim, or cross-claim does not necessarily defeat interest on an otherwise sufficiently certain balance. It held, however, that the mitigation credit here was not such a separate setoff or claim. The officers had disputed any allowance for outside earnings and had never supplied the employer with their suspension-period earnings information. Consequently, the employer could not reasonably calculate and tender the amount due to stop interest. The genuine dispute and missing information supported denial of prejudgment interest; the holding is more specific than a rule denying interest whenever a defendant contests payment. (401–402 & n. 2.)

The final disposition was “Orders affirmed,” preserving the trial court’s mitigation findings, the actual-earnings deductions, and denial of prejudgment interest. No new award of the full gross figures or fresh reversal of the reinstatements was ordered. (402.)
