# ConnectU LLC v. Zuckerberg

**482 F. Supp. 2d 3 (D. Mass. 2007) — March 28, 2007 adoption order.** District Judge Woodlock adopted Magistrate Judge Collings’s March 2 report recommending dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The report, reproduced with the order, supplies the reasoning. The CivPro account is on physical page 180, printed page 313. This brief concerns that district-court disposition, including its supplementary domicile finding, rather than later appellate reasoning.

## Background and procedural problem

ConnectU LLC sued Mark Zuckerberg, several other individual defendants, and TheFacebook, Inc. on September 2, 2004. Its initial complaint alleged state-law claims and invoked diversity jurisdiction. ConnectU’s founders included Cameron and Tyler Winklevoss and Divya Narendra; their efforts to launch a competing website explain the business setting of the membership dispute. The jurisdictional decision did not determine responsibility for the underlying alleged misconduct. **5–10, 25–27.**

On October 28, 2004, ConnectU amended as of right to add a federal copyright-infringement claim. The report explained that ConnectU had not yet obtained its copyright-registration certificate when the original complaint was filed; the certificate issued October 15. Defendants challenged jurisdiction under Rule 12(b)(1). ConnectU argued that federal-question jurisdiction in the amended complaint made any initial diversity problem moot. The court rejected that position and investigated the facts existing at the original filing. **5–9, nn.2–3.**

Initially the pleadings and admissible evidence did not establish Zuckerberg’s or Narendra’s citizenship. Limited jurisdictional discovery and a June 2006 evidentiary hearing followed. The parties stipulated that Narendra was a New York citizen. The court found Zuckerberg was also a New York citizen at filing, making Narendra’s membership in ConnectU potentially decisive. A second hearing in October addressed membership; the report ultimately resolved the case on a broader ground: for diversity purposes, the LLC had no members at filing. **9–11, 14–15, 27.**

## Time of filing and attempts to cure jurisdiction

Diversity ordinarily depends on the parties’ citizenship when suit begins. Section 1653 permits correction of defective jurisdictional allegations; it does not create jurisdictional facts absent at commencement. Thus an allegation can be corrected when jurisdiction actually existed, but a later federal claim cannot, on these facts, supply jurisdiction retrospectively. The court distinguished authorities in which the original case already had jurisdiction or its factual allegations already supported a federal claim. Rule 15(c)’s relation-back provision did not apply here. **6–9, nn.2–3.**

A limited liability company takes its members’ citizenship, rather than the corporate rule based on incorporation and principal place of business. Consequently, ConnectU’s description of itself as a Delaware “limited liability corporation” did not resolve its citizenship. The court had to identify its members under Delaware law and apply federal jurisdictional rules to their status at filing. **9, 15–21.**

Defendants relied on an operating agreement signed August 5, 2005, naming Narendra and three Winklevosses as members effective April 6, 2004. Delaware law permitted such contractual retroactivity. Nevertheless, the report held that a later agreement could not alter the historical facts controlling federal diversity. Otherwise, the outcome could depend on whether the jurisdictional inquiry happened before or after execution of the later agreement, undermining the stability of the time-of-filing rule. It distinguished changes in parties and precedents concerning assignments or agreements that already existed when litigation began. **15–20.**

## Earlier evidentiary arguments and the membership finding

The defendants first argued that the integrated operating agreement barred contrary evidence under the parol-evidence rule. The court rejected treating the later instrument as determinative of membership on the earlier filing date. It also rejected judicial estoppel based on assertions of membership made in California litigation. The California defendants had advanced alternative grounds for defeating personal jurisdiction, and the unexplained ruling granting their motion did not establish that the court accepted the particular membership position. Narendra also offered an explanation for the apparent inconsistency. Those issues called for factual examination rather than automatic preclusion. **11–14, nn.9–11.**

The report then applied Delaware’s statutory admission provisions. ConnectU’s formation certificate identified the entity, registered office, and agent, but no members. The later operating agreement could not fix citizenship retrospectively. Nor did the record establish admissions before suit under the statutory alternatives involving the agreement, existing members’ consent, and company records. The court consequently concluded that no one was a member at formation or by September 2, 2004 for this jurisdictional analysis. This did not invalidate the LLC’s existence under Delaware law. **20–22, nn.17–22.**

The court also considered intentions and conduct as an alternative analysis. The founders described urgent efforts to launch the website, informal understandings, and little attention to the legal implications of membership. A bank signature card and Connecticut business application named Cameron and Tyler as members, but their creation and the other testimony made them neither dispositive nor particularly persuasive evidence of admission. Later tax documents were similarly unhelpful in establishing the earlier facts. The report therefore reached the same result even if the proposed conduct evidence were considered. An LLC with no members had no state citizenship through which to satisfy §1332(a)(1): it was “stateless” for this purpose. **24–27, n.24.**

## Zuckerberg’s domicile: presence was not enough

Although the memberless-LLC holding was sufficient, the report detailed the supporting determination that Zuckerberg’s domicile remained New York. Domicile combines a true fixed home with intent to return, and the relevant date was September 2, 2004. No single factor was controlling. New York voter registration received little weight because Zuckerberg did not recall registering and had never voted. **27–32.**

Zuckerberg had grown up in New York, attended school away from home, and gone to California in summer 2004 with friends to work on Facebook. He took one bag while leaving possessions at Harvard or his parents’ home. His personal bank and credit accounts remained in New York, he used his parents’ address as a permanent address, and he retained a New York driver’s license until 2006. California addresses on business documents, leases, and his continued presence there were relevant evidence, but did not establish the necessary intent by the filing date. **28–31, nn.25–31.**

The court credited his testimony that, at filing, he intended to remain in California for a semester or two and then return to Harvard. Funding and rapid growth later changed the practical situation, but later events were not substituted for his earlier intent. The report found New York domicile, rather than merely inferring it from New York birth or a mailing address. **29–32.**

## Disposition and source limits

Collings recommended allowing defendants’ motion to dismiss because diversity did not exist at commencement; Woodlock adopted that report on March 28. **5, 32.** No ruling on the underlying website or copyright merits followed from this jurisdictional disposition. The report left the Massachusetts registration issue aside and contains thirty-one judicial footnotes; the adopting order adds no separate reasoning. **10–14, nn.8, 11; 31–32.**

The assigned account poses the domicile question and labels the district decision “reversed by” 522 F.3d 82 (1st Cir. 2008). That parenthetical is assigned procedural-history information; the complete appellate opinion has not been used to certify a later holding in this district-stage brief.
