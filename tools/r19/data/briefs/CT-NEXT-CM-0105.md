# Prasad v. Pinnacle Property Management Services, LLC
No. 17-cv-02794-VKD, 2018 WL 4599645 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 25, 2018), Dkt. 40 | Contracts

## Employment claims and arbitration posture

Stephanie Prasad applied online and was hired in May 2016 as a property manager at a Mountain View apartment complex. Her employment ended less than a year later. She alleged that type I diabetes sometimes required accommodations, long hours aggravated her condition, and, after a two-week medical leave, she returned to find another employee in her position. She regarded reassignment as a lower-paid temporary roving manager as a demotion. These employment allegations had not been adjudicated. Her eleven claims combined seven wage-and-hour, penalty, and unfair-business-practice claims pursued on class, collective, or representative bases with four individual disability, accommodation, interactive-process, and emotional-distress claims. Slip opinion at 1–2.

Pinnacle moved to enforce an Issue Resolution Agreement requiring final binding arbitration of disputes arising from applications, employment, or termination. Its rules allowed only one employee’s claims in each proceeding and withheld authority to conduct class arbitration without mutual agreement. Prasad initially denied signing the agreement, but an earlier order found that she entered it. The court deferred the remaining enforceability issues while the Supreme Court reviewed the Ninth Circuit’s Morris decision. After Epic Systems reversed Morris in May 2018, the stay was lifted for supplemental briefing. Prasad conceded that Epic defeated her attack on the concerted-action waiver but maintained her other unconscionability objections. Pinnacle argued that any defective terms could be severed. Id. at 2–4.

Judge Virginia K. DeMarchi decided the motion with the parties’ consent to magistrate-judge jurisdiction. The FAA’s applicability and the agreement’s coverage of these disputes were unchallenged. The question was whether California’s generally applicable unconscionability doctrine defeated enforcement or required narrower relief. The court did not revisit formation or decide the merits of the employment claims. Id. at 1 n.1, 4–5.

## Formation pressure and the sliding scale

California law required both procedural and substantive unconscionability, with stronger evidence of one permitting less of the other. Procedural unconscionability concerned oppression or surprise; substantive unconscionability concerned unjustifiably harsh or one-sided terms. Adhesion began the inquiry but did not itself invalidate the agreement. Id. at 5–8.

The court found some procedural unconscionability. Applicants could not submit their applications without agreeing to arbitration. Although the form permitted withdrawal within three days, withdrawal also ended employment consideration, so it did not provide a meaningful way to retain the job opportunity while declining arbitration. The employer had superior bargaining power. Prasad’s lack of recollection did not undo the previous assent finding, but the form’s presentation of arbitration without its potential disadvantages contributed to an uninformed choice. Pinnacle’s point that she could read the form on her own time did not answer that concern. The consequence was closer scrutiny of the terms, rather than automatic nonenforcement. Id. at 6–8.

## The six challenged provisions

The court rejected the class-waiver challenge following Prasad’s post-Epic concession. It found the employee-only one-year deadline unconscionable because it shortened potentially longer statutory periods without a corresponding restriction on the employer or a justification for the asymmetry. Tolling and continuing-violation exceptions did not cure that defect. Prasad’s own timely filing was irrelevant because fairness was assessed when the agreement was made. Id. at 9–10.

The $50 filing requirement also failed. The employee had to submit the request and fee to “American Management Services”; counsel acknowledged that this was Pinnacle, despite a suggestion to the contrary in its reply. The employer then had thirty days to respond before arbitration proceeded. Counsel’s assurance that the money would pay arbitration expenses was not apparent from the writing. The comparison to a $400 court filing fee missed the problem: this was payment to the very opponent against whom the employee sought relief, an expense she would not bear in court. Id. at 10–12.

The rules advanced arbitration costs through Pinnacle but ultimately divided them equally, with the employee’s liability capped at $100 apart from the filing fee. Applying Ingle strictly, the court found this unconscionable even though the cap established only a modicum of unfairness. Separately, Pinnacle could amend or terminate the rules each December 31 on thirty days’ written notice, preserving the rules applicable when a claim was filed. Notice and the protection against retroactive modification reduced the unfairness. Nevertheless, the court followed Ingle over contrary district-court reasoning and held that unilateral control in this adhesive agreement deprived employees of bargaining power. Id. at 12–14.

The confidentiality clause survived. It covered the arbitration and record, subject to statutory restrictions, mutual agreement, subsequent proceedings, and governmental or legal process. Unlike the broad clauses in Davis and Pokorny, it did not prevent even acknowledging a controversy or discussing its underlying basis with anyone outside the proceeding. Poublon, relying on California appellate authority concerning substantially identical language, supplied the closer comparison. The court therefore rejected Prasad’s claim that every such confidentiality term improperly entrenched the employer’s informational advantage. Id. at 14–17.

## Severance and exact order

Finding four defective provisions did not dictate that the entire agreement was permeated with illegality. The inquiry was whether its central purpose remained lawful and whether defects could be removed without adding contractual promises. In Armendariz, lack of a bilateral obligation could not be cured by deletion alone. Here both employer and employee claims were subject to arbitration, no improper restriction on available relief had been presented, and the invalid deadline, fees, cost division, and amendment provisions were collateral to a lawful alternate forum before AAA or JAMS. The court exercised its discretion to sever them and enforce the remainder. Id. at 17–19.

It compelled arbitration of Prasad’s individual claims and held that she could not maintain the original class, collective, and representative claims. A concurrent separate order allowed amendment to assert a PAGA claim; she agreed that that claim should remain stayed pending individual arbitration. The September 25 arbitration order stayed the action and directed administrative closure, expressly explaining that this removed it from the active docket rather than finally adjudicating the lawsuit. Either party could seek reopening if circumstances changed, and a status report was due within ten days after arbitration ended. Id. at 4 n.2, 18–19. The PAGA discussion states the court’s 2018 procedural treatment, not a certification of later arbitration doctrine.
