# Allegheny College v. National Chautauqua County Bank of Jamestown
246 N.Y. 369, 159 N.E. 173 (1927) | Contracts

## Court and result
Chief Judge Cardozo wrote the New York Court of Appeals' November 22, 1927 majority opinion, joined by Judges Pound, Crane, Lehman, and O'Brien. Judge Kellogg dissented, joined by Judge Andrews. By five to two, the court reversed the Appellate Division and Trial Term judgments and ordered judgment for the college as prayed in its complaint, with costs in all courts. Acceptance of part of a charitable pledge implied a return promise to maintain and publicize the donor's memorial, supplying consideration for the unpaid balance. The decision discusses charitable promissory estoppel at length but does not rest this recovery on that alternative doctrine. (370, 375–379, 382; subsequent locators use the archived judicial reproduction, independently compared with the original report.)

## Pledge, memorial condition, and attempted repudiation
Allegheny College, a liberal-learning institution in Meadville, Pennsylvania, was conducting a $1.25 million endowment campaign. Mary Yates Johnston signed and delivered a $5,000 estate pledge on June 15, 1921. Its face referred to her interest in Christian education and to others' subscriptions, instructed payment from her estate thirty days after death, and permitted endowment use or the instructions on the reverse. Spaces for an interest rate and commencement date were blank. The reverse required a Mary Yates Johnston Memorial Fund whose proceeds would educate students preparing for ministry in the United States or abroad. It also conditioned validity on first satisfying the provisions of her existing will. (Lines 173–192.)

Although payment was deferred until after death, Johnston paid $1,000 on account in December 1923. The college set it aside as a scholarship fund for prospective ministers. She notified the college in July 1924 that she repudiated the pledge. Thirty days after her death the college sued her executor for the remaining $4,000. The two lower judgments favored the executor, and the Court of Appeals reversed them and directed judgment for the college. The court did not determine a freely revocable pledge in isolation: it considered the written memorial condition together with the college's acceptance of part payment. (Lines 193–194, 211–215, 231.)

## Consideration and the charitable-subscription background
Cardozo acknowledged New York cases declaring unsupported charitable promises unenforceable and other cases finding enforceability where older consideration formulations might suggest none. Detriment induced by a promise alone was not a complete account of traditional consideration. The promise and detriment must each purport to induce the other, at least in part; merely changing position in anticipation of a gift does not itself establish a bargain. The majority therefore did not equate every reliance loss with traditional consideration. (Lines 195–202.)

The majority also described promissory estoppel as an exception or substitute and said it had been recognized in the charitable-subscription cases. It declined to decide how far that doctrine modified New York's general consideration law. Barnes v. Perine illustrated expense incurred with the donor's knowledge and reasonable expectation of performance despite the absence of an original request. Other cases reflected similar expansion and public-policy concerns about disappointing the expectations of those engaged in a common charitable enterprise. Cardozo nevertheless disclaimed abandonment or obsolescence of consideration. This historical discussion supplied the background for interpreting subscription and acceptance, while leaving room to fit the actual transaction within conventional consideration. (Lines 203–211.)

## Acceptance implied a requested memorial undertaking
The donor wanted her name perpetuated. Upon taking the $1,000 installment, the college assumed an obligation to take customary and reasonably necessary steps to maintain the memorial in the spirit of the gift. It could not retain that payment while disclaiming personal responsibility to give effect to the memorial condition. The implied undertaking included identifying the donor in scholarship announcements and customary informational materials, at least to applicants. Applying the money to education while presenting it as anonymous would defeat an obligation the donor had requested and could justify her refusal of further installments. (Lines 212–218.)

This duty went beyond applying funds to a corporate purpose or holding them subject to a restricted use. It benefited the donor through remembrance, and the court did not measure the duty's monetary value against the $5,000 promise. Even a small requested obligation can furnish consideration. Acceptance thus produced a bilateral agreement: a subscription promise on one side and an implied-in-fact return promise on the other. Conduct can communicate such a promise even without an express verbal undertaking. (Lines 219–222.)

The majority inferred a commitment to make the whole scholarship plan effective, rather than allowing the college to retain the initial payment and decide only after the donor's death whether to complete her memorial. Collection of the whole fund and later attachment of her name were part of that contemplated plan. Mere restitution if the college refused the remainder would not answer the issue; a duty to restore upon failure of consideration presupposed a promised consideration that could fail. (Lines 223–224, 227–228.)

## A condition can be a requested exchange, but need not always be one
Cardozo expressly recognized that restrictions on a donated sum sometimes are only conditions on a gratuitous promise, rather than consideration. Benefit to the promisor is a useful, nonconclusive aid in distinguishing those situations. Here the donor's memorial objective, initial payment, and college's acceptance supported the inference that she requested a corresponding undertaking. She could not obtain that benefit through partial payment and then defeat the expected remainder. (Lines 215, 225–228.)

Because the implied memorial promise supplied consideration, the court did not decide whether promissory estoppel separately arose from the college's assumption of a special educational duty in reliance on later payments sufficient to complete the scholarship. The case therefore does not establish that every restricted charitable pledge is enforceable without consideration or actual reliance. It establishes the enforceability of this pledge on the majority's interpretation of a bilateral exchange. (Lines 229–231.)

## Kellogg's dissent
Kellogg regarded the memorial language as a donor's wish, not a request to trade for a promise. The written grounds named Christian education and other subscribers, which he considered legally inadequate; he objected to inventing another consideration. Johnston herself called the contribution a gift. Even assuming an offer, he read it as demanding actual memorializing acts rather than the college's promise of future acts. Those acts had not occurred. On his analysis, no unilateral contract was accepted, and contemplated performance after her death could not accept an offer that death had withdrawn. A different implied promise could not substitute for the exact consideration requested. (Lines 233–240.)

He also disputed Cardozo's interpretation of the charitable precedents. Keuka College could be explained by requested acts actually performed, and the other cases might share that ordinary unilateral-contract foundation. Even if promissory estoppel explained them, he regarded it as an old anomaly rather than a newly eroding consideration requirement. Finally, he understood the current majority to be finding consideration, not dispensing with it; his disagreement was that this transaction supplied none. Andrews joined that dissent. (Lines 241–246.)
