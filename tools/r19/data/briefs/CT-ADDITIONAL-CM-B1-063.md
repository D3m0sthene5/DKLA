# Loring v. City of Boston
48 Mass. (7 Met.) 409 (March Term 1844) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural setting

The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court held that Boston’s reward offer had ceased before Loring and his co-plaintiff acted on it, and ordered a nonsuit under the parties’ agreed submission. Three years and eight months was beyond a reasonable duration for this temporary offer responding to a particular arson emergency. Shaw C.J. wrote the sole opinion; the report supplies no numerical vote or separate writing. The decision was not an appellate affirmance of a jury verdict: the parties had withdrawn the case from the jury and agreed that the city would be defaulted or the plaintiffs nonsuited according to the full court’s determination. (409, 411, 414.)

## Advertisements and services performed

The trial report states that Mayor Samuel A. Eliot advertised a $500 reward on May 26, 1837, for apprehension and conviction of anyone setting fire to a building within the city. The following day, a second advertisement doubled the reward to $1,000, describing repeated successful incendiary attempts and the need for vigorous prevention. Publication continued for about one week. No city vote or mayoral notice formally revoked the offer or specified its duration. Similar rewards had previously been paid, and a similar reward was being advertised at the time of trial. (410.)

After a January 1841 Washington Street fire destroyed the Amory House and other buildings, the plaintiffs suspected Samuel Marriott. They announced their intention to pursue him to obtain the advertised reward, followed him to New York with an identifying witness, arrested him, and returned him to Boston. They supplied witnesses and evidence, secured an indictment, and helped obtain his March 1841 conviction for burning the Amory House. Marriott received a ten-year prison sentence. The plaintiffs then brought assumpsit for the $1,000 reward; their writ was dated September 30, 1841. (409–411.)

Boston’s fire-department chief testified that alarms had been frequent before the 1837 advertisements but that few fires occurred from that period through the end of 1841. The only submitted question was whether the earlier offer remained effective when the Amory House burned. The report did not deny that the plaintiffs had performed the apprehension and prosecution services; the dispute concerned whether an offer still existed to be accepted by that performance. (410–411.)

## Reward contracts and lapse

Shaw accepted the general principle that a public reward is an offer capable of acceptance through the requested service, creating an enforceable contract supported by consideration. The city’s defense therefore did not depend on a rule that public advertisements can never be contractual. Rather, it maintained that an emergency advertisement published almost four years before the services could not reasonably be treated as a perpetual offer. The court examined the terms, purpose, and circumstances to determine its duration. (411–412.)

An omitted express deadline did not make the offer unlimited. The advertisement was a temporary executive response to a stated emergency, not a standing municipal ordinance. A reasonable reader would not regard it as continuing for ten or twenty years merely because no formal revocation appeared. The plaintiffs’ argument that the statute of limitations supplied the only temporal limit was also mistaken: a limitations period for suit would begin when the services created the contract and the cause of action accrued, not when the unaccepted offer was published. That theory could leave the offer open for decades before the suit period even began. (412.)

The court therefore implied a reasonable duration. With the circumstances proved, determining reasonableness was a question of law in this case. The purposes of the reward were to make the public and officers vigilant in detecting offenders and perhaps to deter offenders who expected detection. Those purposes depended on the reward being publicly known and remembered. A long-discontinued advertisement, known only to someone who happened to find an old newspaper, would no longer ordinarily operate as the intended incentive. (412–413.)

The annual tenure of the officers who issued the advertisement offered some indication of its temporary character, but did not impose a rigid end-of-term deadline: they acted for the continuing city and might publish a reward near the end of their own terms. Likewise, cessation of advertising was not itself a revocation. It was nevertheless relevant evidence that the emergency had passed and officials no longer conspicuously held the offer out as continuing. The court thus distinguished lapse through implied reasonable duration from affirmative withdrawal of an otherwise effective offer. (413–414.)

## Holding and limits

The court declined to identify a precise universal deadline for reward offers. It held only that, in these circumstances, three years and eight months was too long. By then the emergency had passed, the public generally could be presumed to have forgotten the offer, and the plaintiffs could not reasonably understand it as still continuing. It had ceased before their acceptance and performance, so no express reward contract supported the action. The agreed result was “Plaintiffs nonsuit.” (414.)

This does not make the plaintiffs’ successful prosecution valueless or deny that they actually wanted the reward. Their subjective motivation could not revive the expired offer. Nor does the holding fix the same duration for every reward, or treat the end of newspaper publication as automatic revocation. (413–414.)
