# Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. G.W. Thomas Drayage & Rigging Co., Inc.
69 Cal.2d 33, 442 P.2d 641 (Cal. 1968) | Contracts | Extrinsic evidence, reasonable susceptibility, and indemnity

## Turbine damage and the asserted indemnity

In 1960 G.W. Thomas contracted to furnish labor and equipment to remove and replace the upper metal cover of Pacific Gas & Electric's steam turbine. Thomas undertook the work at its own risk and expense and promised indemnity for losses, damages, expenses, and liability arising from injury to property connected with performance. It also agreed to obtain at least $50,000 in property-liability insurance, name PG&E as an additional insured, and provide a cross-liability clause extending coverage to PG&E's property. While the work was underway, the cover fell and damaged the exposed turbine rotor. PG&E sought the $25,144.51 it later spent on repairs. It dismissed its negligence count during trial and obtained judgment under the contractual indemnity theory. (69 Cal.2d at 36.)

Thomas offered admissions by PG&E's agents, its conduct under similar contracts with PG&E, and other evidence to show that the indemnity concerned only third-party property, excluding PG&E's own property. The trial judge recognized that the wording was characteristic of third-party indemnification and could easily carry that intended meaning. Nevertheless, the judge concluded that the writing's plain language also covered PG&E's property and excluded outside evidence inconsistent with that interpretation. Thomas appealed from the resulting judgment. The Supreme Court did not find Thomas free from negligence or finally determine the parties' actual intended coverage. (Id. at 35–36, 40–41.)

## Apparent clarity does not settle the admissibility inquiry

Chief Justice Traynor rejected a test turning solely on whether a judge finds a document clear on its face. The proper question is whether the offered evidence bears on a meaning the language can reasonably carry. Treating a judge's linguistic experience as the only permissible source of meaning assumes a precision and stability that ordinary language lacks. Contractual rights arise from the parties' lawful mutual intention as expressed in the agreement, requiring a determination of what they meant by their words in context. Words depend on users, surrounding circumstances, purposes, and verbal context; they do not possess universally fixed referents comparable to formal mathematical symbols. (Id. at 37–39; original n.5.)

The prohibition on adding to, subtracting from, or varying an integrated writing remains. But the court must ascertain the written terms' meaning before deciding whether the proposed evidence would impermissibly alter them. A facially clear phrase may have been used by the parties in a sense different from the judge's initial reading. This is not confined to specialized trade usage, although the opinion's sixth footnote identifies examples involving such usage. The decision therefore concerns interpretation, rather than a general right to evade written terms by asserting an inconsistent private intention. (Id. at 39–40; original n.6.)

## Preliminary consideration and final admission are distinct

Rational interpretation requires preliminary consideration of credible evidence offered to establish the parties' intention, including the agreement's object, subject matter, nature, and surrounding circumstances. If, in that context, the language can fairly carry either proposed meaning, evidence relevant to those meanings is admissible. If the proposed meaning falls outside the language's reasonable susceptibility, the evidence does not become admissible merely because someone claims to have intended it. The court's seventh footnote allows conditional receipt or a reserved ruling when the admissibility of an individual item cannot yet be determined in light of the whole offer. Its eighth explains that describing this process as resolving ambiguity is harmless if one recognizes that external evidence may expose the ambiguity. (Id. at 39–40; original nn.7–8.)

Here, even without the offer, the indemnity clause was reasonably susceptible to third-party coverage alone. The offered evidence was consequently relevant both to identifying that possible meaning and to showing that it was the meaning actually intended. Excluding it required reversal. The first footnote also addressed preservation: although the offer might ordinarily have been too general, repeated rulings categorically excluding interpretation evidence excused a more formal offer of proof. This was not a holding that specificity of an offer never matters. (Id. at 36, 40–41; original n.1.)

## The original ninth footnote supplies an alternative ground

The assigned excerpt renumbers this discussion as footnote 6. In the complete opinion it is footnote 9. Traynor explained that exclusion would have been erroneous even under a facial-ambiguity rule: the trial court itself acknowledged third-party-indemnity usage, and statutes and dictionaries used indemnity in differing senses. The word “all” did not compel first-party coverage, because it could describe every claim within the category defined by indemnity. References to loss, damage, and expense could encompass amounts paid to settle third-party claims, rather than independently insure PG&E's property. (Id. at 41; original n.9.)

Similarly, undertaking work at the contractor's own risk could release PG&E from injury to the contractor's property without making the contractor an insurer of PG&E's property. The insurance requirement did not conclusively establish whether coverage was intended for every injury or only injuries caused by the contractor's negligence. These points show why this clause could carry Thomas's proposed meaning; they do not conclusively choose that meaning. They also prevent presenting this particular judgment as resting exclusively on rejection of facial ambiguity, since the opinion expressly supplied an alternative under the more restrictive approach. (Id.; original n.9.)

## Two additional rulings for retrial

The complete opinion addresses issues omitted from the assigned judicial excerpt. The jury had been instructed that PG&E could recover unless four conditions all existed: PG&E maintained independent operations on the premises, the damage was unrelated to Thomas's performance, PG&E was actively negligent, and that negligence involved matters under PG&E's exclusive control. The court found the cumulative instruction wrong. Under the historical rule applied, a generally worded indemnity did not cover consequences of the indemnitee's own actively negligent acts; active negligence causing the harm was sufficient, without all four conditions. The court did not determine that PG&E actually was actively negligent. (Id. at 41–42.)

The second ruling distinguished permissible uses of repair invoices from impermissible proof of the repairs themselves. PG&E introduced the repairer's invoices, payment drafts, and testimony of payment. A mechanical engineer, relying on photographs, described repairs he considered necessary and reasonable and testified that the invoiced amounts were reasonable. Invoices alone were hearsay as proof that liability was incurred, payment made, or charges reasonable. Once a witness testified to payment, the invoices could corroborate that testimony, and paid charges supplied evidence of reasonableness. Their admission for those limited purposes was proper. (Id. at 42–43.)

But the invoice items were also read to prove the specific work actually performed. No qualified witness authenticated them as accurate records of that work, and no other evidence established which repairs were made. That use was erroneous. The original tenth footnote explained that proper business-record testimony might supply an exception. Because the expert's opinion concerning reasonableness depended on inadmissible proof that those repairs actually occurred, Thomas's objections to that opinion should have been sustained. This was a distinct evidentiary defect, not an across-the-board holding that repair bills could never be received. (Id. at 43–44; original n.10.)

## Judgment and complete source

The California Supreme Court reversed the judgment on July 11, 1968. Traynor was joined by Peters, Mosk, Burke, Sullivan, and retired Justice Peek, serving by assignment. McComb dissented without a separate written opinion. The result was six to one and contemplated retrial; it did not enter a final judgment declaring Thomas owed nothing. (Id. at 44 and panel footnote.)
