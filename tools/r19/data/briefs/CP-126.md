# Griggs v. Miller

**CP-126 · Civil Procedure · 374 S.W.2d 119 (Mo. 1963) · September 9, 1963; modified October 7, 1963; supplemental opinion January 10, 1964 · Bohling’s opinion adopted per curiam**

An execution officer must protect both creditor and debtor and cannot sell an entire divisible farm merely because doing so is convenient. The court conditioned cancellation of an improper execution sale on restitution, then modified those conditions and ultimately found them satisfied. The complete report therefore goes beyond the original conditional decree retained in the assigned excerpt.

## Judgments, sale, and ejectment proceedings

Ray Crouch obtained a $1,966.69 judgment against W.A. Brookshire in July 1959, affirmed in December 1960. No supersedeas bond stayed execution. The sheriff levied on Brookshire’s 322-acre farm, advertised it for the Crouch execution, and sold it on January 16, 1961, for $20,600. A different creditor, Dorothy Contestible as administratrix, held a $17,000 wrongful-death judgment. The sheriff received its writ and levied before the farm sale, but never advertised a sale under that execution. 374 S.W.2d at 120–121, 124.

Purchaser Bill Griggs sued for possession and withholding damages three days after the sale. Brookshire denied his claim and counterclaimed to cancel the sale and sheriff’s deed; Sheriff Powell became a third-party defendant. The parties and court treated the dispute as equitable. The trial court favored Griggs, denied the counterclaim, and awarded $2,483.24 through judgment plus $250 per month while possession was withheld. After Brookshire appealed and was imprisoned, Miller became trustee of his estate and substituted as litigant. Id. at 120–121.

The Supreme Court rejected preliminary attacks on the ejectment pleading and an attempt to disqualify the transferred judge after an earlier disqualification. Prior possession was unnecessary for the pleaded ejectment claim, and the governing rule permitted only one judicial disqualification for each party. The court also noted that a new damages cause against the sheriff had been struck from a reply and confined its appellate treatment to contested issues presented by the pleadings below. Id. at 121, 125.

## Execution protections and application

The governing rules allowed a debtor to identify sufficient property for levy and elect the order of sale through timely written notice. They directed division of real estate susceptible of division and sale of only enough to satisfy the execution, unless the debtor requested sale of the whole tract. The officer retained judgment, but had to exercise the care of a reasonably prudent person and protect against sacrificing property. Id. at 122, 124.

Brookshire wrote the sheriff five days before sale, identifying the northeast forty acres and requesting that the sale be confined to them. He also protested publicly. The farm consisted of approximately eight forties and could have been offered in parcels. The sheriff acknowledged that forty acres might have been worth far more than the Crouch debt, but did not intend to offer even eighty. He knew of substantial cattle and other property and chose the farm because it was easier. The court considered this record against rules designed to afford reasonable protection to the debtor. Id. at 123–125.

Liens appearing unreleased in the records did not justify treating the debtor’s interest as nearly worthless. Evidence showed that a $15,000 note, a $10,000 note, and a federal tax claim had been adjusted or discharged. Remaining state and county taxes reduced the interest to approximately $46,000 even under the purchaser’s lower valuation. The court accepted that valuation treatment rather than simply subtracting every recorded lien. Id. at 122–123.

Receiving the second writ produced a constructive levy on property already held, but not authority to sell under it without the required advertisement. The farm had been advertised to satisfy the Crouch judgment, interest, and costs, totaling $2,308.16. Brookshire learned of the other levy only shortly before sale, and the sheriff testified that he sold under the Crouch execution. The larger unadvertised judgment therefore could not justify selling more land than proper under the advertised execution. Although the purchaser paid $20,600 and the sheriff promptly distributed it toward both debts, the sale of the undivided farm violated the protective requirements and called for equitable cancellation. Id. at 124–125.

## Original decree, modifications, and final disposition

The September opinion initially required a $20,600 deposit with 6% annual interest from sale within thirty days. Timely payment would lead to reversal and cancellation of the sale and deed; otherwise the decree would remain affirmed. It initially assessed costs against Brookshire’s estate. Id. at 125. Those are the terms appearing in the assigned principal excerpt, whose closing bracket expressly omits the further opinions.

On October 7, the court extended the period to 120 days from September 9 because the trustee needed to arrange a loan. It also required restitution of $1,610.63 in taxes Griggs had paid to prevent a tax sale, with 6% interest from July 6, 1962, and divided costs equally between appellant and respondents. The trustee’s argument that alleged purchaser wrongdoing defeated restitution failed on the findings, its late introduction as a point in the reply brief, and the undisputed use of the purchase money to discharge lawful obligations. Equitable cancellation could not let the debtor recover the property while retaining the benefit of paid debts. Id. at 125–126.

The January 10, 1964 supplemental opinion recorded that the trustee had deposited principal and interest totaling $26,019.26 on January 7; Griggs received payment and filed satisfaction, and appellate costs were paid. The conditions were fully satisfied. The court therefore reversed and remanded with directions to cancel the sheriff’s sale and deed and take further proceedings necessary to revest title in the trustee. Id. at 126–127.

The court adopted Special Commissioner Bohling’s original opinion with all judges concurring; subsequent rulings were per curiam and supplemental, not dissenting writings. The complete report contains no judicial footnotes. The case links enforcement safeguards with equitable restitution: an irregular sale could be undone, but restoration of the debtor’s land required restoration of benefits received through the sale. Its rule numbers and terms are presented historically, without relying on the assignment’s later recodification references as proof of current law.
