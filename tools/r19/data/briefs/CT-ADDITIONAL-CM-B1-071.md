# Anthony Pools v. Sheehan

295 Md. 285, 455 A.2d 434 (Md. Jan. 25, 1983) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B1-071

Maryland's Court of Appeals, then its highest court, affirmed the intermediate court's reversal and new-trial disposition. Although construction services predominated in the swimming-pool contract, the separately identifiable consumer diving board carried an implied warranty of merchantability. Maryland's special consumer-warranty statute made the contractual disclaimer ineffective. The court also adopted the intermediate court's strict-liability instruction analysis. It did not find the board defective, award damages, or affirm the original defense verdict. Judge Rodowsky wrote for the six listed judges, Murphy, Smith, Eldridge, Cole, Davidson, and Rodowsky; no separate writing appears. (295 Md. at 285–289, 298–299.)

## The construction bargain and the injury

John and Pilar Sheehan engaged Anthony Pools to design and construct a backyard pool at their Maryland home. The inground gunite pool measured sixteen by forty feet, with depths from three to eight feet. Curved alcoves at the short sides gave it the described Grecian shape. Anthony also designed, manufactured, and installed a six-foot diving board centered over the alcove at the deep end. Its work was completed by mid-June 1976. At an August 21 pool party, John testified that he used the board for the first time: after leaving the water and walking toward its pool end, he slipped from its right side and hit the coping. (Id. at 286–287.)

The Sheehans alleged that skid-resistant material on the board stopped about an inch short of its side edges, breaching merchantability. They also offered evidence that the board, particularly its placement over the alcove, was unreasonably dangerous. Those were liability theories supported by their presentation, not appellate findings that either defect or causation had been established. (Id. at 287.)

Their May 25, 1976 agreement was a large, two-sided printed form combining a retail installment contract with a swimming-pool construction agreement. It included contractor-license information, cancellation and lending disclosures, and forty-seven listed specifications or options. Anthony agreed both to construct the pool and to sell the related equipment to the buyer. Its duties included engineering, permits, excavation, reinforcing steel, gunite, plaster, tile, coping, filtering equipment, and the specified diving board. The reverse contained an exclusion of implied warranties. Note 2 identifies an unexplained price discrepancy: the construction portion states $7,980, while the disclosure gives $9,489.31 before financing charges. Neither the record nor the agreement allocated a separate price to each item or portion of work. (Id. at 289–290 & n.2.)

## The appeal concerned two grounds for a new trial

At the close of the plaintiffs' evidence, the trial court directed a verdict for Anthony on warranty because the agreement conspicuously made its express warranties exclusive of others. The strict-liability claim went to the jury, which returned a defense verdict. The Court of Special Appeals reversed and remanded for a new trial. It regarded the swimming-pool package as consumer goods and therefore treated the warranty exclusion as ineffective; it separately found an omission in the jury instructions concerning the plaintiff's conduct. (Id. at 287–288.)

Anthony obtained certiorari on the warranty question, the instruction concerning inadvertent use, and the intermediate court's statement that contributory negligence was not a strict-liability defense. The highest court agreed that the directed verdict was erroneous but took a different route to that conclusion. The warranty claim before it was merchantability under § 2-314; the plaintiffs were not pressing an express warranty or fitness for a particular purpose. (Id. at 288–289.)

## Ordinary Article 2 classification did not resolve Maryland's consumer-warranty question

Article 2 ordinarily applies to transactions in goods, defined by movability when identified to the contract. Merchantability is implied where the seller is a merchant in goods of the kind, unless validly excluded or modified. The ordinary disclaimer provision, § 2-316, permits specified exclusions. Maryland had enacted a further provision, § 2-316.1: the ordinary exclusion section did not apply to consumer goods, services, or both, and language excluding implied consumer warranties or their remedies was unenforceable. The quoted provision also preserved a seller's recovery from the manufacturer for resulting warranty damages. Consumer goods were defined by their primarily personal, family, or household use. (Id. at 286 n.1, 289.)

The court had previously adopted the predominant-purpose test in Burton to choose the limitations period for a landscaping bargain. Under that approach, a transaction predominantly selling goods is governed as a sale despite incidental labor; the converse applies when services predominate. Here the Sheehans were buying construction of an inground, reinforced, finished pool, rather than purchasing individual rods, gunite, plaster, or tiles. Construction labor and services predominated, and the board was incidental to that project. The court did not evade the problem by reclassifying the entire undertaking as a sale of goods. (Id. at 290–291, 293–294.)

The court reviewed differing pool decisions. Gulash required evidence showing a goods-predominant bargain; Riffe applied warranties where goods predominated and installation was needed to make them usable; the divided Ben Construction decision treated an installed pool as labor and services. Chian had excluded an inground concrete pool from Article 2's perfect-tender rule. These variations showed why the precise transaction and particular statutory issue mattered. Chian's reasoning, reproduced in note 4, distinguished Maryland's consumer-warranty statute from ordinary Title 2 applications. (Id. at 291–293 & nn.3–4.)

The board nevertheless retained an identity distinct from the pool construction. The contract expressly promised sale of equipment, labeled the Sheehans buyers, and offered the board as an optional accessory. It was movable when identified, not structurally integrated, and remained detachable from its support. Had the Sheehans bought that board in a separate purchase, a merchantability warranty would have accompanied it. A rule eliminating the warranty solely because the board was bought along with construction services required a further justification. (Id. at 293–294.)

## The statutory policy supported a limited gravamen approach

The court considered policy arguments concerning risk distribution, consumer reliance, and difficulties proving negligence. Newmark had recognized warranty protection for a lotion supplied during a beauty service, while distinguishing commercial transactions from professional services. Worrell involved a defective fitting supplied in connection with other work. Hawkland's suggested gravamen approach distinguished injury from a defective item from injury caused by deficient labor. Those authorities supplied an analytical vehicle; the Maryland enactment furnished the decisive local policy. (Id. at 294–297.)

An all-or-nothing classification would mean that a consumer good in a services-predominant transaction had no implied Article 2 warranty to begin with. But § 2-316.1 expressly embraced consumer goods and services and prevented exclusion of implied consumer warranties. The court understood that enactment as contemplating warranties on consumer goods supplied within such hybrid transactions. Treating the construction classification as dispositive would frustrate that policy. (Id. at 297–298.)

Its holding was specific: when a commercial transaction sells consumer goods that retain that character after the promised performance, and claimed monetary loss or personal injury arises from a defect in those goods, Maryland's implied-warranty provisions apply to those goods even though consumer services predominate. The board met those conditions. Its merchantability warranty could not be disclaimed, so the directed verdict based on the exclusion was error. The court reserved whether goods consumed in rendering a service also received this protection. Note 5 further reserved whether this limited goods holding was coextensive with the statute's reach into consumer services. It did not abolish predominant-purpose analysis for every Article 2 issue or impose a warranty on every service. (Id. at 298 & n.5.)

## The adopted strict-liability analysis required instructions distinguishing carelessness from assumption of risk

The highest court confined this part of its review to the omission in the instructions and expressly adopted Part III of the intermediate opinion, 50 Md. App. 614, 620–626. That section addressed the defense's emphasis on John stepping near the board's edge and turning to speak while walking. Without guidance, a jury could mistake ordinary carelessness for a complete bar, even though the plaintiffs still had to prove a defective, unreasonably dangerous product and causation. (295 Md. at 287–288, 299; 50 Md. App. at 620–626.)

The intermediate court explained that inadvertence, failure to discover a defect, or failure to guard against it does not itself bar strict-liability recovery. A plaintiff who discovers a defect, understands its danger, and nevertheless voluntarily and unreasonably encounters that danger may instead assume the risk. That inquiry is subjective and requires actual awareness and understanding, not merely conduct that an ordinarily careful person would have avoided. The plaintiffs' specific request and the defense's argument made an appropriate inadvertence instruction necessary; giving it also called for an appropriate assumption-of-risk instruction. Neither appellate court found that John actually satisfied that defense. The new jury was to receive the correct distinctions. (50 Md. App. at 620–626 & nn.5–11; 295 Md. at 288, 299.)

The adopted section also retained the requirement that a product defect proximately cause injury. Rejecting an ordinary contributory-negligence bar therefore did not make every pool accident actionable. The highest court affirmed the intermediate court's judgment, with costs payable by Anthony Industries. Both the warranty-directed-verdict error and the strict-liability instruction error supported the new trial; no final injury award was entered. (295 Md. at 298–299.)
