# Miami Coca-Cola Bottling Co. v. Orange Crush Co.
296 F. 693 (5th Cir. 1924) | Contracts

## The license and the lawsuit
Orange Crush granted Miami Coca-Cola the exclusive right within a designated territory to manufacture orange crush, bottle it, and distribute it under Orange Crush's trademark. Orange Crush promised to supply its concentrate at stated prices and undertake certain advertising. Miami Coca-Cola agreed to purchase a specified quantity of concentrate, maintain a bottling plant, solicit orders, promote sales, and develop increasing sales volume. The license was described as perpetual, but Miami Coca-Cola could cancel the agreement at any time. The report supplies no additional notice period or restriction on that cancellation power. (296 F. at 693–694.)

Miami Coca-Cola's bill alleged that it purchased concentrate, manufactured the beverage, and was performing its obligations when, about a year after formation, Orange Crush gave written notice that it would no longer be bound. These are the pleading's allegations, rather than findings made after a damages trial. Miami Coca-Cola sought an injunction against cancellation and specific performance of the license. District Judge Rhydon M. Call dismissed the bill; the Fifth Circuit reviewed that equity decree. Judge Bryan wrote the February19,1924 opinion on a panel consisting of Judges Walker and Bryan. The report contains no dissent, concurrence, or judicial footnotes. (Id. at 693–694.)

## Why the written undertakings did not bind future performance
The court agreed that the executory agreement lacked mutuality. Although the license listed substantial activities for the bottler, the unrestricted cancellation proviso let it withdraw at any time. The promised consideration was a promise for a promise; Miami Coca-Cola's ability to cancel meant that it had not committed itself to the future performance the licensor sought in exchange. The court consequently held the agreement unenforceable insofar as it remained executory. This reasoning depends on the cancellation power, not simply on the absence of particular tasks: the opinion had already described purchasing concentrate, operating a plant, soliciting orders, and promoting sales. (Id. at 694.)

Miami Coca-Cola could not preserve the arrangement by treating it as a continuing option. An enforceable option needed supporting consideration, and the court found none binding the grant of that continuing privilege. Its cited contract authorities supported the conclusion that a party's unrestricted ability to terminate defeated an executory exchange premised on reciprocal promises. The opinion did not identify a separately paid option price or other independent consideration for irrevocability. (Id.)

## Past performance and prospective equity were different questions
The court expressly distinguished the period already performed. It conceded that Orange Crush might be liable for damages for that period, but those damages had an adequate remedy at law. That concession did not adjudicate a damages amount or award one; the suit under review requested prospective equitable relief. A remedy for accrued performance would therefore not establish entitlement to compulsory continuation of the license. (Id.)

The court supplied an additional equitable explanation through Express Co. v. Railroad Co.: equity would not intervene where a party held the power of revocation, because that party could render the court's action a nullity. In this agreement, the bottler could cancel the very ongoing arrangement it wanted the court to require the licensor to perform. The court treated that asymmetry as a reason to refuse specific performance, consistent with its consideration analysis. (Id.)

## Exact result and assigned significance
The Fifth Circuit affirmed the decree dismissing the equity bill. It did not grant an injunction, order future supplies, or finally reject every possible damages claim arising from the completed period. (Id. at 694.)

The assigned termination-clause note uses this decision to illustrate an unrestricted power to withdraw. Its ensuing discussion of restricted cancellation and the Uniform Commercial Code is editorial material, not a holding of this 1924 opinion. The case's historical reasoning should not be converted into a present-day rule about every termination provision or every sales agreement without examining governing law and the provision's actual restrictions.
