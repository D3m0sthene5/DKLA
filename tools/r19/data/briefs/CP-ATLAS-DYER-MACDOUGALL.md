# Dyer v. MacDougall

**201 F.2d 265 (2d Cir. 1952) · United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit · December 31, 1952**  
**Civil Procedure · Summary judgment, demeanor evidence, and finality under Rule 54(b)**

## Facts and available evidence

Dyer’s amended complaint contained four defamation counts arising from his dealings with the Queensboro Corporation. Only the third and fourth counts were dismissed by the judgment under review. The third alleged that Albert E. MacDougall told a lawyer, Almirall, that a letter Dyer sent to the corporation’s shareholders was a blackmailing letter. The fourth alleged that MacDougall’s wife, acting as his agent, made a similar statement to Dorothy Russell Hope, the sister of Dyer’s wife. Separate counts concerned a statement at a directors’ meeting and a written letter; those remained pending. 266–267.

The defendants supported their motion with affidavits from both MacDougalls and Almirall and with Hope’s deposition, which Dyer had taken. Each alleged speaker denied making the statement, and each alleged hearer denied hearing it. Dyer’s own affidavits supplied no admissible evidence of either utterance. He was not present when the alleged slanders occurred. His proposed case consequently depended on calling witnesses whose available accounts all denied the necessary fact. 266–268.

The district judge offered Dyer the opportunity to depose the MacDougalls and Almirall and to take a second deposition from Hope. The hearing was adjourned by consent to permit that discovery. After several months, Dyer declined to proceed with those depositions. He nevertheless argued that examining the witnesses in a courtroom might expose the falsity of their denials or produce admissions unavailable from their written accounts. 267–269.

## Procedure and appellate jurisdiction

The defendants initially moved before answering to dismiss counts two, three, and four. They later withdrew the motion as to count two. In December 1951 the district judge decided that counts three and four presented no trial-worthy issue, and judgment was entered on January 7, 1952. Dyer did not appeal within the ordinary 30-day period. He subsequently requested an extension and reargument. On March 4, the judge adhered to the dismissal but certified that judgment was expressly directed and that there was no reason for delay. Dyer filed his notice of appeal that day. 266–267.

The defendants challenged appellate jurisdiction, but the court denied their motion to dismiss the appeal. Under the amended Rule 54(b), the partial disposition of a multiple-claim action did not become appealable until the required determination gave it finality. Each alleged defamatory utterance was a separate wrong, distinct in words, occasion, or speaker; their common connection to Dyer’s employment did not collapse them into a single claim. The first two counts remained pending, and March 4 was the first day on which the necessary determination permitted this appeal. Thus, the appeal was timely without needing to treat the ordinary January judgment as already appealable. 267–268.

That ruling concerns the historical version of Rule 54(b) discussed in the opinion. It is not a general rule that an untimely appeal can always be revived by a requested extension.

## Issue, holding, and judgment

The merits question was whether Dyer had a genuine issue for trial when every available witness denied the utterance and he offered no admissible affirmative proof, relying instead on the possibility that their demeanor or further examination might convince a jury of the opposite. The court held that summary judgment was proper on this record and **affirmed the dismissal of counts three and four**. It did not dispose of the other pending defamation counts. 268–269.

Judge Learned Hand wrote the principal opinion on a panel with Chief Judge Swan and Judge Frank. All three agreed with affirmance; Frank wrote separately to reject Hand’s broad rationale and to rest his concurrence on the case’s unusual facts. There was no dissent. 266, 269–272.

## Hand’s reasoning

The moving defendants bore the burden of showing the absence of a genuine issue. But Dyer would bear the trial burden of proving that the slanders were uttered. If the motion established that his proof would not suffice to go to a jury, summary judgment was appropriate. Since he declined the offered discovery, the court assumed the witnesses would repeat their affidavits and deposition at trial, subject to its separately considered possibility of obtaining new admissions. 268.

The court first examined Dyer’s proposed hearsay chain. He swore that his wife said Hope had told her she could forgive him everything except the shareholder letter. Dyer did not submit the wife’s affidavit, but Hand considered the argument even assuming such an affidavit were present. Hope’s supposed declaration still would be hearsay. The allegedly defamatory telephone statement was not fixed closely enough in time to support a spontaneous-exclamation exception; even assuming a one-day interval, the circumstances did not justify treating Hope’s later statement as an utterance made while deliberation remained suspended by sudden emotional stress. 268.

Under the evidentiary rules discussed in this 1952 opinion, calling Hope as Dyer’s own witness would not make the wife’s contrary version admissible. Rule 43(b)’s provision concerning an adverse party did not extend to Hope, who was not a party. Thus, the proposed account did not give Dyer substantive evidence of either slander or a means of overcoming the witnesses’ uniform denials. The brief states the court’s historical analysis rather than claiming these provisions are the present Federal Rules of Evidence. 268.

Hand acknowledged that demeanor is evidence. Hesitation, discomfort, arrogance, or defiance can make a witness’s denial appear fabricated and sometimes suggest that the opposite occurred. He therefore did not rest on the proposition that demeanor is never probative or that every denial must be believed. 268–269 & n.1.

Nevertheless, he concluded that this plaintiff could not reach a jury solely through the theoretical chance of favorable demeanor impressions. Otherwise, a judge’s decision about a directed verdict could depend on impressions that disappeared from the printed record and could not be effectively reviewed on appeal. Hand chose protection of appellate review over the exceedingly rare possibility that demeanor alone would rationally support the plaintiff’s case. The opinion distinguished the issue before it—the equivalent of directing a verdict—from the judge’s potentially broader power to set a verdict aside. 269 & n.2.

Hand separately considered whether live questioning could extract **additional substantive admissions**, rather than merely expose unfavorable demeanor. He did not categorically exclude that possibility. A witness’s evasiveness or recalcitrance at deposition might demonstrate a reason to believe that appearing before a judge would produce more testimony. Dyer had refused the available depositions, however, so he had no demonstrated basis for that expectation. The court would not assume it in the abstract. 269.

## Frank’s concurrence: disagreement about the rationale

Frank objected to the appellate-review argument. He read Hand’s approach as allowing a judge in a nonjury trial to rely on demeanor while preventing a jury from doing the same, making summary judgment depend on whether a jury had been requested. He regarded that distinction as unfortunate and unnecessary. 269–271 & nn.1–4.

In Frank’s view, the argument also overstated the obstacle to reviewing directed verdicts. A trial judge considering a directed verdict must disregard the judge’s own views about witnesses’ credibility and assume the evidence and reasonable inferences favor the opponent. An appellate court makes the same assumption, so the judge’s unrecorded reaction does not defeat review. On a new-trial motion, by contrast, a judge may weigh testimony and credibility; Frank emphasized that difference in the authorities and extended footnotes he discussed. 271–272 & nn.5–9.

He also rejected an invariable rule that a plaintiff can never prevail when the sole available witness denies the plaintiff’s allegation but the factfinder is thoroughly convinced by that witness’s demeanor that the denial is false. Such a rule could cause serious injustice. 272.

Frank nevertheless agreed to affirm here because the facts were exceptionally narrow. The plaintiff claimed statements made outside his presence, knew that all available witnesses denied them, and could supply no independent account. In those peculiar circumstances, only bringing suit publicized the alleged slander, and Frank would not allow a trial whose sole possible foundation was disbelief of witnesses all testifying against the plaintiff. His concurrence supports the result without embracing Hand’s generalized appellate-review explanation. 272.
