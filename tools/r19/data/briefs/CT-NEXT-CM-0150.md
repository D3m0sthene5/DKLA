# United States for the Use of Coastal Steel Erectors, Inc. v. Algernon Blair, Inc.
479 F.2d 638 (4th Cir. 1973) | Contracts

## Facts and trial result
Algernon Blair contracted with the United States to build a naval hospital in Charleston County, South Carolina. It subcontracted steel erection and associated equipment supply to Coastal Steel Erectors. Coastal used its own cranes to handle and place steel. Blair refused to pay crane rental, maintaining that the subcontract did not require payment. Coastal stopped work after completing approximately twenty-eight percent of its obligations. Blair hired another subcontractor to finish. Coastal sued Blair and its payment-bond surety, United States Fidelity and Guaranty, in the United States’ name under the Miller Act for the labor and equipment it had already supplied. 479 F.2d at 640.

The district court found that the subcontract required payment for crane use and that Blair’s refusal was a material breach justifying Coastal’s termination. Those findings were not challenged on appeal. The court estimated that approximately $37,000 remained payable for Coastal’s performance under the contract, after payments received. It also found that Coastal would have lost more than $37,000 had it finished. Treating the expected loss as a deduction from contract damages, it denied recovery. The appeal therefore concerned an alternative remedy for a justified cessation after the other party’s breach, rather than whether Coastal itself wrongfully abandoned the project. Id.

## Issue and rule
The question was whether a partly performing subcontractor could elect quantum meruit recovery for labor and equipment already supplied even though completion would have made the contract unprofitable and contract damages would yield no award. The court held that it could. Restitution measured the reasonable value of the performance conferred on Blair, without deducting the loss Coastal would have incurred on the unperformed balance. Id. at 640–641.

The contract price could be evidence of reasonable value, but did not set the measure or cap recovery under the rule adopted in this opinion. The relevant value was the amount for which services could have been purchased from someone in Coastal’s position at the time and place of performance. Amounts already paid remained deductible so Coastal would not recover the same benefit twice. Id. at 641–642 & nn. 7–9.

## Reasoning
The court distinguished the expectation question—what financial position completion of this particular bargain would have produced—from the restitution question—what reasonable value Coastal had already supplied at its own expense and Blair retained without full payment. Blair’s material breach entitled Coastal to stop and elect recovery of that value. The anticipated loss from the remaining work did not turn the completed labor and equipment use into a benefit Blair could retain unpaid. Id. at 640–641.

The opinion relied on Miller Act and construction precedents recognizing a promisee’s option to forgo contract damages and seek reasonable value after the other party’s breach. This remedy could be pursued against the payment-bond surety as well as the prime contractor, consistent with the Act’s protective construction. Lack of precision in the complaint’s theory did not preclude quantum meruit, and a claimant could plead it alongside breach-of-contract damages. Those procedural points permitted the alternative theory; they did not authorize cumulative collection of duplicative awards. Id. at 640–641 & nn. 3–5.

The quoted restitution rationale identified both Coastal’s expenditure and Blair’s retained gain as reasons for relief. It did not convert every expense Coastal incurred into an automatically recoverable amount. The court still required a valuation of services and equipment use at the relevant market standard. Footnote 7 acknowledged the possibility that restitution could exceed the value of the promised return under an advantageous bargain, consistent with the opinion’s refusal to make contract price a ceiling for this partial-performance claim. Id. at 641 & nn. 6–8.

The action’s governing framework also matters. Footnote 2 states that federal substantive law controls a Miller Act action when federal and state law differ, while observing that the court believed the outcome would be the same under the pertinent state law. This was not simply an Erie prediction from a diversity dispute. Nor did the opinion decide the separate situation of a claimant who had fully performed and sought restitution in place of the agreed payment. Id. at 640 & n. 2; 641–642.

## Disposition and judicial participation
Craven wrote for Chief Judge Haynsworth, Senior Judge Bryan, and Craven; the report contains no separate writing. The court reversed and remanded with instructions to determine accurately the reasonable value of the labor and equipment use Coastal furnished, then enter judgment for Coastal after subtracting prior contractual payments. It did not award $37,000 as the final restitution figure. The district court’s approximation had sufficed for its losing-contract analysis but was not a precise reasonable-value finding, as footnote 9 explains. Id. at 639–640, 642 & n. 9.
