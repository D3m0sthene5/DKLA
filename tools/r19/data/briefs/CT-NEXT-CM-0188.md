# Kingston v. Markward & Karafilis, Inc.
134 Mich. App. 164, 350 N.W.2d 842 (Mich. Ct. App. April 30, 1984) | Contracts

## Facts and construction arrangements
Chrysler employee Charles G. Kingston died on December 11, 1976 while helping replace metal covers on an oil reservoir. The cover on which he stood slipped, and he fell into the oil. W.J.C. Kaufmann had manufactured the plates during the plant’s 1969–1970 renovation under a contract with Chrysler. Incorporated general conditions indemnified the owner, identified as Chrysler, against property damage and personal injury associated with the contractor’s work. Chrysler separately retained Markward & Karafilis for a building addition. (167–168.)

In April 1969, Chrysler informed Kaufmann that its contract was assigned to Markward, which would assume overall administration and coordination, while Chrysler would continue direct payments. Kaufmann acknowledged the assignment subject to protection of its retention money. A June purchase-order change expressly assigned coordination and responsibility, again retaining direct payment by Chrysler. The plates were already completed in May. In July, Markward warned the architect that the covers were unanchored and dangerous but did not inform Kaufmann. Stops were outside the original specifications and would have been additional work. Later meetings addressed the loose covers, but protective stops apparently were never added. (168–169.)

## Litigation and competing claims
The estate sued Markward, Kaufmann, and the architect. Markward and Kaufmann sought common-law indemnity from Chrysler; Markward also claimed contractual indemnity from Kaufmann through the supposed assignment. Chrysler itself tendered its defense to Kaufmann under the original indemnity provision. Thus, both Chrysler and Markward treated the same clause as protecting themselves. (169–170.)

Although the trial judge denied Markward’s request for a conditional directed verdict on indemnity, the eventual jury instructions treated an indemnity agreement between Markward and Kaufmann as undisputed. They required indemnity if Kaufmann’s negligence fully caused the death or if both contractors’ negligence caused it. The jury awarded the estate $3 million jointly against both contractors, total indemnity to Markward, and no recovery against Chrysler. Kaufmann objected to the instructions and unsuccessfully sought judgment notwithstanding the verdict on indemnity. It later paid $1 million for the estate’s covenant not to enforce the judgment against it. Both contractors appealed: Kaufmann challenged the indemnity obligation, while Markward contended it should have received a directed verdict. (170–171.)

## Assignment rule and holding
Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 317 generally permits assignment of rights, but excludes an assignment materially changing the obligor’s duty, increasing its burden or risk, impairing its chance of return performance, or reducing that performance’s value. Materiality depends on the contract and surrounding circumstances. Corbin’s explanation is especially important for an aleatory promise: where performance depends on an uncertain event, the promisee cannot assign in a way that materially increases the likelihood that the event will occur and the duty become payable. (171–173.)

Any purported assignment of this indemnity right to Markward was void because it materially increased Kaufmann’s risk. Chrysler, as the injured worker’s employer, had workers’ compensation protection against employee tort suits. Markward lacked that protection against Chrysler employees. Substitution of Markward as indemnitee therefore exposed Kaufmann to a far wider range of claims than the original owner-indemnity bargain contemplated. The assignment would change the risk of having to perform, even if the clause’s literal words were unchanged. (173–175.)

## Additional reasoning
The documents also suggested that Chrysler intended a limited transfer of supervision and coordination, rather than a complete transfer of the contractual relationship. Chrysler retained entitlement to the work, payment responsibility, and its own claimed indemnity rights. It could not unilaterally create two indemnitees. If both Chrysler and Markward could enforce, the risk would multiply; if Markward replaced Chrysler, the absence of employer immunity would still materially increase it. Either understanding defeated the asserted transfer. (174–175.)

The court did not resolve a general presumption that assignment of an entire bilateral executory agreement also delegates duties. Michigan precedent had expressly reserved that question, and here the documents showed no intended complete assignment. Nor did the court need to decide Kaufmann’s remaining arguments about the clause’s operation once the supposed assignment failed. Kaufmann’s acknowledgment of the administrative assignment did not establish the broadened indemnity obligation asserted in litigation. (168–169, 174–175.)

## Remaining issues and exact judgment
Markward’s own demand for a directed verdict necessarily failed because it possessed no enforceable assigned indemnity right. Its mistrial argument also failed: plaintiff’s counsel’s irrelevant questions about an officer’s moral duty should not have been admitted, but were insufficiently egregious to require a mistrial. Finally, the court declined to reverse for exclusion of a contributory-negligence defense. A later change allowing comparative negligence in safety-device cases required some evidence of the decedent’s negligence; the record supplied neither evidence nor an inference of it. The court did not decide retroactivity because the evidentiary prerequisite was absent. (175–176.)

The court reversed denial of Kaufmann’s judgment-notwithstanding-the-verdict motion and granted that motion on indemnity. It affirmed denial of Markward’s directed-verdict motion, affirmed the estate’s $3 million verdict, and preserved both contractors’ joint and several liability for that award. Costs were awarded to Kaufmann. Judge Shepherd wrote for the panel of Shepherd, M.J. Kelly, and Hood; no separate opinion or division is reported. (177.)
