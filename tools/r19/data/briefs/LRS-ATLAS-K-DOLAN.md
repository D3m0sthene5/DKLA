# Dolan v. United States Postal Service
546 U.S. 481 (2006), reversing 377 F.3d 285 (3d Cir. 2004) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Accident, claim, and district proceedings

Barbara Dolan alleged that on August 25, 2001, she tripped over letters, packages, and periodicals a Postal Service employee had left on her porch, causing serious injury. She pursued the required administrative claim, which was denied on April 18, 2002. On October 15, 2002, she sued the United States and Postal Service in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania under the Federal Tort Claims Act. Her husband Michael asserted loss of consortium. The Dolans later acknowledged that only the United States was the proper defendant. Third Circuit judicial reproduction, factual paragraphs beginning “On August 25, 2001” and “On February 7, 2003”; Supreme Court, 483.

On February 7, 2003, the Government moved to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(1). Judge Mary A. McLaughlin granted dismissal on March 19, holding that the postal matter exception, 28 U.S.C. § 2680(b), barred Barbara’s claim and treating Michael’s claim as derivative. On appeal, the Dolans conceded that Michael had not timely exhausted administrative remedies. The appellate dispute therefore concerned Barbara’s claim. Third Circuit reproduction, paragraphs beginning “On March 19, 2003” and “On appeal”; Supreme Court, 483.

The Supreme Court assumed, rather than conclusively determined, that a comparable injury caused by a private delivery party would support damages under applicable state law. Neither appellate judgment decided that the postal employee actually breached a duty, established causation, or owed a particular amount. 485.

## Statutory structure and question

Although the Postal Reorganization Act gives the Service power to sue and be sued, it expressly applies the FTCA to tort claims arising from postal activities. The FTCA grants jurisdiction over specified employee torts and generally makes the United States liable like a private individual in comparable circumstances, subject to exceptions and exclusions such as punitive damages and prejudgment interest. Sovereign immunity remains for an excepted claim. 483–485.

Section 2680(b) preserves immunity for claims arising from loss, miscarriage, or negligent transmission of letters or postal matter. The question was whether negligent transmission included every negligent act in delivery, particularly hazardous placement of mail causing a recipient to fall, or instead addressed failures in the mail’s arrival, timing, destination, or condition. 485–486.

## Third Circuit judgment: broad transmission and affirmed dismissal

Judge Roth wrote for Chief Judge Scirica and Judges Roth and McKee, with no separate writing. The panel reviewed the jurisdictional dismissal plenarily. It emphasized that sovereign-immunity waivers must be expressed unequivocally and construed strictly in the Government’s favor. Dolan argued that transmission covered lost, delayed, or damaged mail, relying on the FTCA’s general waiver, legislative history, and statutory purposes. She challenged Bono’s contrary treatment of a package-placement injury. Third Circuit reproduction, judicial paragraphs beginning “Because the Dolans,” “It is a fundamental principle,” and “At issue here.”

The panel expressly disagreed with the Second Circuit’s Raila decision. If negligent transmission was ambiguous, the immunity canon favored the Government; the breadth of the statute’s reference to any claim arising out of negligent transmission reinforced that conclusion. The panel read the legislative history’s concern with mishaps in ordinary postal operations to encompass actual delivery, not merely sorting and transporting mail. Its only judicial footnote cited Robinson’s similar interpretation. Third Circuit reproduction, paragraphs beginning “While we note,” “Construing 2680(b),” and “Moreover,” and n.1.

Mail placement on the porch was an ordinary delivery operation. Because employees did not monitor how recipients retrieved mail, related accidents could be unavoidable. The panel defined transmission as the conveyance process beginning when the Service received the item and ending when it delivered it. This reading, it reasoned, protected an important governmental activity from damage suits and limited excessive or fraudulent claims. It therefore affirmed the district court. Third Circuit reproduction, final paragraphs beginning “We thus agree,” “We hold therefore,” and “For the foregoing reasons.” This 2004 judgment was subsequently reversed by the Supreme Court; it is presented as the prior stage, not the ultimate controlling result.

## Supreme Court holding: hazardous placement outside the exception

The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve the conflict with Raila. It held that the exception did not bar Barbara’s claim and that her case could proceed under the FTCA’s general waiver. 485, 492.

Kennedy acknowledged that transmission, considered alone, could include delivery and thus the creation of a porch hazard. But a dictionary’s outer possibilities did not determine the meaning of a statutory phrase. The Court considered the whole text, purpose, context, and precedent. It interpreted negligent transmission as not extending beyond negligence causing mail to be lost or to arrive late, damaged, or at the wrong address. 486.

The neighboring words loss and miscarriage helped define that scope. Mail is lost when destroyed or misplaced and miscarried when sent to the wrong address. Those terms describe failures in the postal obligation to deliver correctly and on time. The Court considered it unlikely that the final phrase silently embraced the much broader category of all injuries caused by employees during delivery. This was an application of associated-word reasoning, not a holding that the delivery process excludes the final act of placement. 486–487.

## Precedent, comparison, and practical consequences

Kosak supplied what the Court regarded as decisive additional support. Although Kosak concerned the different customs exception, its comparison of that general provision with the specific postal exception explained that Congress intended the FTCA to allow suits over postal employees’ negligent driving. Both parties accepted that treatment of vehicle accidents. Postal trucks delivering mail were themselves transmitting it, so the exception could not immunize every negligent act performed during transmission. 487–488.

The Government tried to distinguish accidents only circumstantially related to mail from injuries caused by mail itself. The Court found no textual basis for that distinction. It would produce unstable results depending on whether a tripping hazard was an empty or full mail sack, or whether a pedestrian was struck by a truck’s mirror or a dangling parcel. Those examples illustrated the interpretive problem; they were not additional facts in Dolan. 488–489.

The Court’s rule concerned both direct and consequential harms from postal delivery failures. It gave examples such as personal or financial loss from missing or late medicines or foreclosure notices and injury resulting from negligent handling that shattered shipped china. Consequently, the holding did not make every personal injury fall outside the exception merely because a person rather than the mailed property was hurt. 489.

The Government stressed the scale of postal operations, but Congress had drafted other exceptions in much broader terms covering entire classes of activities. It could have used equally comprehensive language for postal torts. The chosen specification of three types of harm instead supported immunity for a subset of postal wrongdoing. 489–490.

Registration and insurance also made losses within the exception at least partly avoidable or compensable. The Court contrasted those mechanisms with discretionary administrative settlements for other injuries, which did not supply the same automatic remedy. Ordinary safeguards against frivolous litigation would have to suffice for eligible slip-and-fall claims, as for private delivery businesses and postal vehicle claims. 490–491.

## Immunity canon and Thomas’s dissent

The majority declined to apply the general rule of construing waivers narrowly in the Government’s favor as a shortcut to a broad exception. In the FTCA setting, overly generous interpretations of exceptions could defeat the sweeping waiver Congress had enacted. The task was to identify the circumstances within the particular exception’s language and reason, neither enlarging nor shrinking them by a default preference. 491–492.

Thomas would have affirmed. He understood the ordinary meaning of transmission to encompass sending or conveying mail through delivery, and found no statutory indication that Congress intended a different meaning. His first footnote identified other postal statutes using transmit and transmission in that ordinary sense. 492–494 & n.1.

He challenged the majority’s boundary between an injury caused by mail placement and an injury consequential to damage to the mail. For example, cutting oneself on the contents of a negligently broken glassware parcel could be barred while tripping on an undamaged heap of mail could proceed. In his view, the broad arising-out-of language covered both. Noscitur a sociis did not require every broad term in a series to be narrowed to match its most restrictive neighbors. 494–496.

Thomas also read Kosak as preserving the particular vehicle-accident category rather than establishing a general distinction based on the consequences of postal negligence. His second note disputed the adequacy and reach of insurance, especially for recipients and sentimental value, and pointed to discretionary settlement authority for claims like Dolan’s. 496–497 & n.2.

Finally, even if the majority’s interpretation were equally plausible, he would resolve uncertainty in favor of immunity. Exceptions conditioned the scope of the waiver just as much as its affirmative language. His third note distinguished cases narrowly construing exceptions where Congress had clearly allowed the substantive claim and only the form of proceeding was disputed. He would have left the dismissal intact. 497–499 & n.3.

## Exact disposition and limits

The Supreme Court reversed the Third Circuit’s judgment and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion. 492. It did not restore Michael’s unexhausted consortium claim, award Barbara damages, or resolve every FTCA exception.
