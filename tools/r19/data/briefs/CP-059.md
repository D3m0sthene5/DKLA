# Bushel v. Miller
1 Strange 128–129, 93 Eng. Rep. 428 (K.B. 1718) | Civil Procedure | Historical trover and conversion

## Goods obstructing a porter's chest

Porters on the Custom-House quay used a hut to store small parcels when the ship was not yet ready to receive them. The porters entitled to use the hut had their own boxes or cupboards. Miller had one of these chests. Bushel, another porter, placed goods belonging to a third person, identified only as A., so that Miller could not reach his chest without moving the goods. Miller moved them about a yard toward the door, left without returning them to their former position, and the goods were lost. The report does not identify who took them or establish that Miller himself appropriated or sold them. 93 Eng. Rep. 428; 1 Strange 128–129.

Bushel paid A. the value of the lost goods and brought trover against Miller. The legal questions arose at trial before the Chief Justice. The short report records two rulings, rather than an appellate court's review of an earlier judgment. It reports no damages figure, verdict wording, or formal final judgment. Although the assigned casebook identifies the court as King's Bench and the year as 1718, neither a precise hearing date nor the Chief Justice's personal name appears in this report. The date and judicial name attached to the following, separately captioned case should not be imported into Bushel. Id.

## A sufficient property interest, but no conversion

The first ruling favored Bushel on his entitlement to sue. By compensating A. for the goods, he had acquired a sufficient property interest to maintain trover. The court therefore did not reject the action simply because the goods originally belonged to someone else. That ruling concerns the interest needed for this particular action on these facts; the report does not explain a general theory of assignment or determine every possible consequence of payment to an owner. 93 Eng. Rep. 428, first numbered ruling.

The second ruling defeated the asserted conversion. Bushel had himself acted wrongfully, in the limited respect that he placed the goods where they obstructed Miller's access to his own chest. Miller had a right to remove that obstruction. Consequently, moving the goods was not wrongful on these facts. The Chief Justice then considered the distinct omission: Miller had not put them back before leaving. He acknowledged that an action in trespass might raise a doubtful question about that omission, but was clear that it did not amount to conversion. Id., second numbered ruling; 1 Strange 129.

The analysis thus separates three matters that could otherwise be confused: Bushel's property interest, Miller's privilege to remove an obstruction, and the legal character of Miller's failure to restore the goods. Establishing the first did not establish the third. Nor did the goods' later disappearance make Miller's earlier authorized movement a conversion. The report does not suggest that every interference with goods is privileged; its reasoning depends on the obstruction of Miller's own chest and on the particular conduct described. Id.

## What the court decided and left open

The reported result is that Bushel could not establish conversion through this removal and failure to replace the goods, despite having a sufficient property interest for trover. No appellate affirmance, monetary award, or separately reported final judgment should be added. The possible trespass question remained open: the Chief Justice's acknowledgment of doubt is not a holding that Miller was liable in trespass. There is one reported judicial speaker and no concurrence, dissent, vote tally, or case-specific judicial footnote. 93 Eng. Rep. 428.

For the assigned historical forms-of-action discussion, the case illustrates why loss following an interference did not by itself answer whether the interference was conversion. A plaintiff needed to establish the wrong appropriate to the action chosen. The accompanying casebook questions about other lost or damaged goods are editorial comparisons, not additional rulings in Bushel. This brief describes the 1718 report and does not certify present conversion law.
