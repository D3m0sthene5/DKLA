Parties
Semtek was the plaintiff asserting state-law business claims, the appellant from the Maryland dismissal, and
petitioner in the Supreme Court. Lockheed Martin was the defendant and respondent. Semtek sought to pursue
its claims in Maryland after a California federal court had dismissed them under California's limitations law.
Lockheed argued that the earlier federal judgment barred the second action altogether. The controversy before
the Supreme Court concerned the later effect of the California judgment, not whether Lockheed had committed
the alleged business torts. 531 U.S. at 499–500.
Material Facts
Semtek sued in California state court for inducement of breach of contract and other business torts. Lockheed
removed the action to the Central District of California based on diversity and obtained dismissal under
California's two-year limitations period. The federal court adopted language proposed by Lockheed declaring
the claims dismissed in their entirety on the merits and with prejudice. The grounds for dismissal nevertheless
concerned time limits, not a trial or finding that the alleged underlying conduct was lawful. Id. at 499–500.
Semtek brought the same causes of action in a Maryland state court, where the applicable three-year limitations
period had not expired. The distinction between states mattered because a limitations dismissal traditionally
could bar the local remedy without extinguishing the substantive claim in another jurisdiction with a longer
period. Whether California's own preclusion law produced that result was disputed, and the Supreme Court
expressly declined to decide its content. Id. at 500, 504, 509.
Lockheed relied on the federal judgment's express wording and Rule 41(b), arguing that an adjudication upon
the merits necessarily precluded the claim in every other court. Semtek maintained that the federal diversity
judgment should receive the same effect as a comparable California state judgment. Those positions presented
separate questions about Rule 41's meaning, the source of preclusion law for federal judgments, and the content
of the appropriate federal rule. Id. at 500–506.
Procedural History
The Ninth Circuit affirmed the California limitations dismissal. Semtek initially did not challenge the order's
on-the-merits designation. During the Maryland litigation, Lockheed unsuccessfully asked the California
federal court to enjoin the second action and removed the Maryland case on federal-question grounds. The
Maryland federal court remanded because the federal issue appeared only as a defense. Diversity removal was
unavailable because Lockheed was a Maryland citizen. Id. at 499–500.
The Maryland state trial court dismissed on claim-preclusion grounds. Semtek then unsuccessfully sought
amendment of the California order in the district court and Ninth Circuit and appealed the Maryland judgment.
The Maryland Court of Special Appeals affirmed, reasoning that federal law gave the federal merits dismissal
preclusive effect irrespective of California's treatment of its own limitations judgments. Maryland's highest
court declined review. Id. at 500.

The Supreme Court reviewed the Maryland judgment, reversed, and remanded. It held that federal common
law governs but ordinarily incorporates the preclusion law applied by courts of the state where the diversity
court sat. The Court did not reopen the California case, revise its limitations holding, or decide that California
law definitely allowed the Maryland action. Id. at 508–509.
Issue
Does Rule 41(b), or a federal court's express description of a limitations dismissal as on the merits and with
prejudice, require other courts to treat the underlying state-law claim as extinguished? What law determines the
claim-preclusive effect in Maryland of the California federal diversity judgment, and should that federal rule
incorporate California's preclusion law? Id. at 499–501, 506–509.
Rule and Holding
Federal common law governs the claim-preclusive effect of a federal judgment dismissing a diversity action. In
the circumstances presented, the federal rule incorporates the law that would be applied by state courts in the
state where the federal diversity court sits. That reference to state law yields when state law is incompatible
with a relevant federal interest, but no such conflict justified giving this state limitations bar broader effect than
California courts would give it. Id. at 507–509.
Rule 41(b)'s adjudication-upon-the-merits language does not itself prescribe the judgment's claim-preclusive
effect in every other court. It primarily distinguishes a dismissal that bars refiling the same claim in the
rendering court from a dismissal without prejudice. That consequence is necessary but not sufficient for claim
preclusion elsewhere. Lockheed's contrary inference from the dismissal label therefore failed. Id. at 501–506.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Maryland Court of Special Appeals and remanded. Maryland had to assess the
prior federal judgment under the federal rule incorporating California's preclusion law, whose content the
Supreme Court left undecided. The disposition removed the automatic federal-preclusion rationale; it did not
award Semtek damages or guarantee that the second lawsuit would survive every applicable defense. Id. at 509.
Reasoning
The Court first declined to treat an older diversity-preclusion decision, Dupasseur, as conclusive by itself. That
case arose under the Conformity Act, which required federal courts to use state procedure in relevant cases.
The procedural landscape had since changed. Although the Court ultimately adopted an outcome consistent
with reference to state law, it supplied a contemporary federal-common-law explanation rather than assuming
the earlier statute still controlled. Id. at 500–501, 508.
Lockheed's Rule 41 argument depended on equating every judgment called on the merits with a judgment
extinguishing the claim for all purposes. The Court explained that the phrase had acquired several meanings.
Its original sense described an actual decision on the substance of a claim, but courts also used it for dismissals
such as limitations rulings that did not adjudicate substantive liability. The label alone therefore could not
resolve the later judgment-effect question. Id. at 501–503.
Rule 41(b) functions as a default rule: specified dismissals operate upon the merits unless the court says
otherwise. The Court found it implausible that such a default instruction, within rules governing a court's own
procedure, silently established a comprehensive law controlling other courts' treatment of federal judgments.
The complexity and external reach of claim preclusion required a more careful inquiry than whether the first
order contained the expected phrase. Id. at 503.

A universal-preclusion reading also raised Rules Enabling Act concerns. If state law preserved a right to sue in
another jurisdiction, a procedural rule that extinguished that right merely because the first action reached
federal court might abridge a substantive right. The Court used that concern to support a limiting interpretation.
It did not hold Rule 41 invalid, and it expressly reserved a narrower possible Enabling Act question about
dismissals barring return to the same court when state procedure would allow it. Id. at 503–504, 506 n.2.
The proposed interpretation would also conflict with Erie policies. An out-of-state defendant facing a stale
state-law claim could remove to federal court to obtain a limitations dismissal with nationwide extinguishing
effect, even if a state-court dismissal would leave an action elsewhere available. Federal and state forums
would then generate materially different consequences for the same state right, encouraging forum shopping
and unequal administration. Id. at 504–505.
Rule 41(a)'s treatment of dismissal without prejudice supplied a more coherent reading. Its primary meaning
concerns the ability to return to the same court with the same claim. The opposite designation accordingly bars
refiling there. The express order in Semtek had that force in the Central District of California, while the
different question of preclusion in Maryland still required the governing judgment law. The Court also
observed that its federal-question preclusion precedents had not relied on Rule 41(b) as the source of their
rules. Id. at 505–506.
Neither Article IV's Full Faith and Credit Clause nor § 1738 directly supplied that governing rule because their
text concerns state judgments, rather than the federal diversity judgment at issue. The Court nevertheless had
long prescribed the effect state courts must give federal judgments. It identified federal common law as the
source of that authority, with the Supreme Court having the last word on the required effect of a federal
judgment. Id. at 506–508.
Federal source did not require uniform nationwide content. Because the underlying claims arose under state
substantive law, the more pertinent uniformity was between state and federal adjudication within the rendering
state. Incorporating that state's preclusion law ordinarily gives equivalent judgments equivalent consequences
regardless of whether a party filed in or removed to federal court. The Court therefore chose California's rules
as the federal rule's incorporated content, not Maryland's rules simply because Maryland heard the second
action. Id. at 508–509.
The Court preserved a federal-interest exception. As an example, it suggested that a state rule denying
preclusive effect to a dismissal for willful disobedience of discovery orders might conflict with federal courts'
ability to protect their processes. That was a hypothetical justification for departing from incorporated state
law, not a finding of misconduct by Semtek. The actual dismissal implemented California's limitations policy,
and the Court found no federal interest in making that policy more destructive of claims elsewhere than
California itself intended. Id. at 509.
Separate Opinions
There were no separate opinions. Justice Scalia's opinion was unanimous. The Court agreed on the
federal-common-law source, the ordinary incorporation of the rendering state's law, the limited meaning of
Rule 41(b), and reversal of the Maryland judgment. It deliberately left California law's actual preclusive effect
for further proceedings. Id. at 498, 509.

Notes
Semtek requires two distinct choices: identify the law that controls the judgment's effect, and then identify the
rule that law selects. The answer here was federal common law, incorporating California preclusion law unless
incompatible with federal interests. Saying simply that either federal law or state law governs loses the
two-level structure central to the opinion. Id. at 507–509.
Compare Allen, where the first judgment was a state judgment and § 1738 supplied the statutory recognition
obligation. Semtek's first judgment was federal, so the Court used federal common law. The identity of the
rendering court, its jurisdictional basis, and the nature of the underlying claim all matter before evaluating a
second court's treatment of the judgment. Semtek, 531 U.S. at 506–509; Allen, 449 U.S. at 96.
The casebook's introductory shorthand describes breach-of-contract allegations; the full opinion specifies
inducement of breach and business torts. This brief follows the full opinion's description without inventing
details of the underlying commercial transaction that the Court did not provide. It also preserves the distinction
between the final California limitations dismissal and the separate Maryland preclusion error. 531 U.S. at
499–500, 509.
