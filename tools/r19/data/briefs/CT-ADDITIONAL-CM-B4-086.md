# Detroit Bank and Trust Company v. Chicago Flame Hardening Company, Inc.
541 F. Supp. 1278 (N.D. Ind. 1982) | Contracts

## Judgment and procedural posture
Judge Lee ordered judgment for Chicago Flame Hardening Company because Roxanne Scott had not accepted, adopted, or acted on the promised widow's benefits before the parties rescinded them. Applying Indiana law, the court rejected an automatic vesting rule for an adult donee beneficiary and held that the contracting parties need not have expressly reserved a power to rescind where her rights had not yet become irrevocable. This was a decision with findings after a bench trial, not summary judgment based on assumed allegations. There were no separate writings. (1280, 1282–1287.)

Judge McNagny had heard the evidence on December 22–23, 1980 but became incapacitated and died before deciding the case. The parties jointly agreed that a successor judge could decide it from the transcript, admitted exhibits, briefs, and final argument, expressly waiving objections under Rule 63, due process, or other grounds. Lee considered that record and entered findings and conclusions under Rule 52(a), in the memorandum dated June 24, 1982. The parties' agreed procedure was material to why a successor judge could resolve factual questions without hearing a new trial. (1278–1280, caption and introduction.)

## Promise of benefits and the later rescission
Marvin Scott, his brother Gainor, and brother-in-law John Keeler founded Chicago Flame in 1956; it was incorporated in 1962. Marvin also owned a successful Detroit company and helped establish the Indiana business as an investment and livelihood for his relatives. On July 29, 1964, the three shareholders approved a resolution promising benefits to each shareholder's surviving wife, if living when he died. The graduated fifteen-year payments totaled $150,300: $1,250 a month for five years, then $835 for five, then $420 for five, terminating upon the widow's earlier death. The wife was to sign undertakings to make herself available for consulting, supply knowledge acquired from her husband, and refrain from competition. The resolution did not name the current wives or expressly reserve an amendment or rescission power. (1280–1281, findings 2–7 & nn. 1–2.)

Roxanne learned of the possible benefits within a reasonable time but later testified that she had forgotten the arrangement. Keeler died in July 1967; his widow signed the required agreement and received benefits, apart from a voluntary eighteen-month postponement during financial difficulties. Roxanne knew those payments were being made. On February 15, 1971, Marvin and the other shareholders approved a second resolution rescinding benefits for Marvin's surviving spouse in order to sustain the company's future finances. Marvin died on October 31, 1971. The rescission did not eliminate the other widows' benefits, which the company continued to honor. (1281–1282, findings 8–15, 20–21 & n. 5.)

After Marvin's death, Roxanne moved to Florida, suffered emotional illness, and was placed under guardianship in March 1972. She later returned to Michigan and experienced another episode requiring hospitalization in 1975 and an estate guardianship that remained in effect. The bank acted as guardian of her estate, though the findings described her as mentally competent at the decision date and under continuing medical care. She had not requested benefits before suit on September 19, 1977, made no expenditure, and neither changed her position nor acted in reliance on the original resolution. The court found she learned of her husband's relinquishment after his death and before suit, even if she had not seen the document itself. (1282, findings 15–19.)

## The governing vesting rule
The guardian argued that the original Restatement's donee-beneficiary rule made the promise irrevocable without an express reserved power; acceptance should be presumed from its beneficial nature; Roxanne's health prevented an earlier assertion; and her rights had vested. Indiana law governed the agreement made in Indiana by an Indiana corporation. (1282–1283.)

The court contrasted the original rule with Indiana's approach under Fanning and earlier decisions: contracting parties may rescind or vary a third-party contract before the beneficiary accepts, adopts, or acts on it. The court therefore did not require an express reservation of rescission power. Note 9 explains its rejection of the old donee/creditor distinction, while notes 10–11 set out the Indiana authority and the court's treatment of beneficiary classifications. The parties stipulated that the resolution was not ultra vires, so invalid corporate authority did not supply the result. (1282–1284 & nn. 9–12.)

The opinion's discussion attributes the modern approach to the Second Restatement, printing a section-142 citation at 1283 and later expressly quoting section 311 comment h at 1287. This brief preserves the opinion's operative analysis rather than presenting its earlier section number as an independently verified modern Restatement locator. The assigned excerpt likewise presents the historical contrast but does not establish current law in every jurisdiction.

## Knowledge, presumed acceptance, and incapacity
The guardian's authorities presuming acceptance involved infant beneficiaries and were distinguishable from a competent adult capable of asserting her rights. The court rejected an attempt to use irrevocable-gift principles to supply the acceptance missing from this contractual claim. Mere knowledge that a beneficial promise existed did not conclusively establish contractual adoption. Notes 14–17 identify the gift, trust, and minor-beneficiary authorities and explain the absence of a supporting adult-acceptance case. (1284 & nn. 14–17.)

Filing suit might manifest acceptance, but the September 1977 suit could not be treated as though filed before the February 1971 rescission. Even the evidence of health decline before Marvin's death concerned a period after rescission. The court left open whether total incapacity before rescission might present an equitable basis for presumed acceptance; that was not the sequence proved here. Subsequent guardianship and lack of prior notice of rescission did not restore a promise already effectively withdrawn. (1284–1285 & nn. 18–21.)

## Comparator cases and the decisive factual finding
Fanning concerned certificates of deposit creating a survivorship account. It recognized the purchaser's lifetime rescission power but involved no attempted rescission, so its recognition of beneficiary ownership after death did not decide this dispute. Bannon concerned inheritance-tax treatment of a widow's employment-related annuity, likewise without attempted rescission. Salesky involved an expressly reserved power and a wife who participated in the original corporate vote, facts absent here. The court declined to turn those cases into a rule that Roxanne's knowledge alone made her benefit irrevocable. (1285–1286 & n. 22.)

Acceptance could take the form of an overt act or adoption expressing intent. Section 311 comment h recognized that invited assent can terminate the contracting parties' variation power and that reliance may sometimes be difficult to prove. The court did not demand a particular expenditure in every case. Here, however, Roxanne's testimony denied long-range plans or dependence on the resolution, and the remainder of the record supplied no corroborating adoption or reliance. The court expressly rested on the entire record, not solely one answer on cross-examination. It found no acceptance, adoption, or action before rescission and therefore no preserved right to the promised payments. (1286–1287 & n. 23.)

## Exact relief and source scope
The order directs entry of judgment for Chicago Flame, consistent with the findings and conclusions. It awards no widow's stipend or damages to the guardian. This was a trial-level judgment on the rescission dispute, not an appellate affirmance, and it did not invalidate the continuing benefits of the other widows. The earlier attempt to add laches was denied at the start of trial; lack of timely acceptance before rescission, rather than an adjudicated laches defense, supplied the decision. (1282 n. 6; 1287.)
