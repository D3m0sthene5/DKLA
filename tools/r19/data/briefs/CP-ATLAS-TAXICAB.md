# Black & White Taxicab & Transfer Co. v. Brown & Yellow Taxicab & Transfer Co.

**Civil Procedure — 276 U.S. 518 (1928). Supreme Court of the United States, April 9, 1928. Justice Butler wrote for the Court; Justice Holmes dissented, joined by Justices Brandeis and Stone. The Court affirmed, 6–3.**

## Facts and the deliberate choice of a federal forum

Two competing companies provided taxicab and baggage-transfer services in Bowling Green, Kentucky. Brown & Yellow had an arrangement with the Louisville & Nashville Railroad that gave it advantages at the station: it could enter trains, the depot, and surrounding railroad premises to solicit passengers and baggage business, and it received a designated area in which to park its taxicabs while awaiting trains. In exchange, it promised services and monthly payments. The contract lasted one year, renewed for successive yearly periods, and could be terminated on thirty days’ notice. The exclusive privilege concerned solicitation and use of railroad property; passengers were still free to hire another carrier. 276 U.S. at 522–523, 528–529.

The original Brown & Yellow company was incorporated in Kentucky, as were its competitor, Black & White, and the railroad. Kentucky’s highest court had held comparable exclusive arrangements invalid. The participants therefore arranged a real change in Brown & Yellow’s corporate identity to obtain a federal forum applying a different understanding of common law. In September 1925, its shareholders organized a Tennessee corporation of the same name, transferred the Kentucky company’s business and property to it, and dissolved the Kentucky company. The Tennessee successor entered the contract at issue. The railroad cooperated in this plan to create diversity of citizenship. These facts were established in the record, rather than merely inferred from an accusation of forum shopping. Id. at 523–524.

Brown & Yellow’s complaint alleged that the railroad permitted competing operators to solicit business and park on its property in violation of the agreement. It alleged that Black & White used the designated premises and also parked on an adjoining street in a manner obstructing Brown & Yellow’s taxicabs. Black & White answered that the Tennessee incorporation fraudulently created federal jurisdiction and evaded Kentucky law; it also challenged the contract as monopolistic, contrary to Kentucky public policy, outside the railroad’s charter powers, and prohibited by the Kentucky Constitution. Id. at 523.

## Procedure and issues

Brown & Yellow sued both Black & White and the railroad in the federal district court for the Western District of Kentucky. The district court found jurisdiction, held the contract valid, found interference substantially as alleged, and enjoined Black & White from continuing it. The railroad declined to join the appeal. Black & White alone appealed, and the Sixth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to decide the jurisdictional and contractual objections. Id. at 522–525.

The questions were connected but distinct. First, did the deliberate creation of an out-of-state corporation require dismissal for improper or collusive manufacture of jurisdiction? Second, did the railroad lack power to grant the privilege, or did Kentucky’s constitutional restriction on common-carrier preferences prohibit it? Third, could the federal court enforce a contract that Kentucky courts treated as contrary to common-law public policy?

## Actual incorporation sustained diversity jurisdiction

Section 37 of the Judicial Code required dismissal when a suit did not involve a real and substantial controversy within federal jurisdiction, or when parties were improperly or collusively made or joined to create a federally cognizable case. The Court found neither defect. There was an actual dispute over a valuable privilege. The Tennessee company was the real owner of the transferred business and contract, and the transfer and dissolution were actual, rather than fictitious or merely colorable. Id. at 524–525.

For Butler, the motive for those genuine transactions did not invalidate them. Brown & Yellow’s incorporation and ownership were not themselves successfully impeached; cooperation with the railroad to secure federal adjudication therefore was not improper collusion within the statute. The distinction was between a genuine succession undertaken to obtain a favorable forum and a sham transaction that only appeared to change the parties. The Court upheld the former on this record. It did not hold that every attempted manufacture of diversity was permissible, and it distinguished decisions rejecting other arrangements. Id.

## Charter power and the common-carrier restriction

Black & White invoked a Kentucky decision condemning a similar grant as beyond the railroad’s powers. The Court answered that the cited decision had not construed any particular charter term; it had reached a general-law conclusion. Another Kentucky decision recognized the railroad’s implied powers to maintain amenities such as hotels, restaurants, and parks. The station arrangement fell within that broader understanding of permissible uses of railroad property. Id. at 525–526.

Kentucky Constitution § 214 prohibited exclusive or preferential arrangements for handling freight or conducting business as a common carrier. The Court held that the taxicab arrangement did not concern the railroad’s business in that capacity. A railroad did not have an obligation to carry passengers or baggage onward from its station, nor did independent transfer businesses have a right to use its premises for solicitation and parking. Granting permission to one business therefore did not violate the constitutional provision as applied here. Id. at 526.

## General common law controlled the contract’s validity

The decisive substantive step was the Court’s classification of the public-policy question as one of general law. Kentucky decisions had condemned comparable privileges because they discouraged competition, discriminated among transfer operators, and created a practical monopoly. But they had not rested that conclusion on a governing statute or constitutional provision. Under the then-prevailing approach associated with *Swift v. Tyson*, a federal court could exercise independent judgment about general common law instead of adopting the state court’s answer. Id. at 526–531.

The Court considered its own precedents, decisions of numerous state courts, and English authorities to conclude that arrangements of this kind were generally valid. It acknowledged contrary decisions, including those in Kentucky, Indiana, and Mississippi, rather than pretending that all jurisdictions agreed. Its premise was that the federal court could choose the rule it regarded as the better expression of general common law. Id. at 527–528.

Butler also explained why he rejected the monopoly and public-policy objections on their merits. The railroad owned the station grounds and could use them consistently with its carrier duties. Passengers remained free to select other transportation. The record showed no lack of service, unreasonable charge, or public inconvenience. Exclusive station privileges could instead promote orderly solicitation, convenience, and safety. A competitive advantage alone did not establish public injury. Allowing competing operators to use railroad property without consent would also disregard the owner’s property rights; the state could not require a public hack stand there without compensation. Id. at 528–529.

The Rules of Decision Act objection did not change the result. The Court noted uncertainty about whether Black & White’s brief actually advanced it, but addressed it assuming it was presented. The privilege involved no question of land title, governing state statute or constitution, or ancient fixed local usage. For a general-law question, federal courts could consult the same sources as state courts and reach an independent conclusion. The Court invoked *Swift* and an extensive line of later applications, including the authorities collected in its asterisk footnote at 531. Id. at 529–531.

## Holmes’s dissent: law needs an authoritative source

Holmes, joined by Brandeis and Stone, would have reversed. He accepted that the lower court had substantial precedent for exercising independent judgment, but argued that the doctrine rested on a fundamental mistake: treating common law as an external body of principles that courts merely discovered. In his view, rules enforced within a state derived their legal authority from that state. The state’s highest court spoke with authority just as its legislature did; its common-law decisions were not less binding merely because they took judicial rather than statutory form. Id. at 532–535.

This challenged the majority’s source-of-law premise, not simply its assessment of whether exclusive taxi privileges were sensible. A federal court’s disagreement with Kentucky’s policy could not create power to substitute another rule where state law governed. Holmes also referred to historical research questioning *Swift*’s construction of the Rules of Decision Act. Nevertheless, his stated proposal was to leave *Swift* itself undisturbed and prevent its assumed domain from expanding into new fields. It would overstate this dissent to say that he expressly demanded overruling *Swift* in this case. Id. at 535.

Holmes offered a narrower ground as well. The dispute concerned permissible use of land in Kentucky by a Kentucky-chartered railroad, and Kentucky had maintained its policy for more than thirty-five years. Even precedents allowing independent judgment on general law recognized binding state rules of property and settled state public policy. He regarded this arrangement as sufficiently local to fall within those limits. Id. at 535–536.

## Judgment and the assigned later treatment

The Supreme Court **affirmed the decree**. Brown & Yellow kept the injunction protecting its exclusive contractual privileges against Black & White’s interference. The Court did not order a new trial or invalidate the contract. The railroad’s refusal to appeal remained a separate procedural fact; the Supreme Court reviewed Black & White’s challenge. Id. at 522, 531.

The assigned casebook primarily presents Taxicab through the later *Erie* opinion: a developed account of the reincorporation and result at printed 447, followed by Holmes’s source-of-law reasoning quoted at 449–450. *Erie* uses the earlier case to show how a corporation could obtain different substantive rights by selecting a federal forum; the editorial question at printed 453 asks whether that problem could have been addressed without overruling *Swift*.
