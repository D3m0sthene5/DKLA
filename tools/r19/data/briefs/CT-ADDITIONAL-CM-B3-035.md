# Fineman v. Faulkner
174 N.C. 13, 93 S.E. 384 (1917) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and disposition

Chief Justice Clark wrote the North Carolina Supreme Court’s September 12, 1917 opinion affirming a judgment for the seller of an Edison talking machine against the purchaser’s estate administrator. The parties had agreed on the material facts. The question was whether the seller’s knowledge that the purchaser engaged in prostitution made the ordinary sale and purchase-money note unenforceable on public-policy grounds. The court rejected that defense and affirmed; the complete report states no dollar amount for the unpaid balance or damages and contains no separate judicial writing or footnote. (174 N.C. at 13–16.)

## Agreed facts and the defense

The purchaser, Mamie Faulkner, conducted prostitution at her home in Princeville across the river from Tarboro. On September 18, 1916, Fineman, a retail dealer in pianos and talking machines, sold her an Edison machine and records, delivered them to that home, and obtained the purchase-money writing. He knew her occupation by general reputation. But the stipulation also established that he had no interest in her business and provided no aid in its conduct beyond whatever incidental assistance the mere sale might furnish. The terms were those of his ordinary transactions, and the instrument was of a generally used kind without a slot attachment. After her death, administrator J. B. Faulkner resisted enforcement of the note on the ground that the transaction was contrary to public policy. (Id. at 13.)

## Rule and rationale

The court adopted, with a modification, the seller’s counsel’s distinction between an unlawful sale, an article usable only for an unlawful purpose, and the ordinary lawful sale of an article having legitimate uses. The first two situations could prevent price recovery. In the third, knowledge of a purchaser’s unlawful occupation did not by itself defeat the seller’s claim where he had not sold to aid that business or participated in an unlawful use. The test focused on the claimant’s own participation and the legality of the transaction, rather than treating the buyer’s occupation as a universal disability to incur ordinary commercial obligations. (Id. at 13–14.)

Allowing this defense would either encourage people engaged in illegal occupations by freeing them from paying for ordinary goods, or effectively bar them from obtaining such goods altogether. Neither was the intended legal result. The court illustrated the point with ordinary provisions or clothing sold to someone known to distill or retail liquor unlawfully. The seller’s ability to establish the lawful debt without relying on his own participation in an illegal transaction supplied the relevant distinction. Here the agreed facts negated the participation on which the administrator’s defense depended. (Id. at 14.)

The court compared a prior land sale where the vendor knew the buyer intended an immoral use after purchase. Lack of the vendor’s participation and control over that subsequent use did not excuse payment. Fineman’s position was stronger in one respect: knowing the purchaser’s occupation did not establish that he knew this machine would be put to an immoral use, even assuming it eventually was. The opinion also cited authority allowing recovery for ordinary furniture, instruments, or lawfully sold liquor furnished to premises associated with prostitution when the supplier did not participate in operating the business. These analogies did not authorize an agreement deliberately made to advance an unlawful purpose. (Id. at 14–15.)

## Why the contrary authorities did not control

The administrator relied on cases refusing to enforce gambling notes, usurious agreements, and transactions supporting the Confederate government or army. Those involved an illegal consideration, an unlawful transaction, or the seller’s participation in the prohibited purpose. A case denying compelled telephone service to premises used for prostitution likewise involved a service almost necessarily aiding the illegal business; it did not concern a buyer avoiding payment for an instrument already purchased. Another authority involved a seller conducting business in violation of a statute, making the sale itself illegal despite the ordinary nature of the building materials sold. None established that general knowledge of a buyer’s occupation invalidated this transaction. (Id. at 15–16.)

The note’s security in the machine did not alter the analysis. The administrator had not even offered to return the goods while refusing to pay. The court treated the sale as legitimate, supported by valuable consideration, and outside the seller’s control once the machine was transferred. On the agreed facts, the estate could not use the purchaser’s occupation to defeat the seller’s lawful claim. The judgment was affirmed. The opinion did not decide the different case of a seller intentionally financing, supplying, or otherwise participating in an unlawful undertaking. (Id. at 16.)
