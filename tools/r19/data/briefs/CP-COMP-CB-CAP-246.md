# Morley v. Gaisford

**2 H. Bl. 441, 126 Eng. Rep. 639 (C.P. 1795) · Court of Common Pleas · Civil Procedure**

An action on the case was the proper form for a master's liability arising from his servant's negligent collision with the plaintiff's carriage. The court discharged a post-verdict rule seeking to arrest judgment because the plaintiff supposedly should have brought trespass. The report begins with the date April 27, 1795; its final resolution occurred a few days after the initial application, without a separately specified date. The assigned account is on CivPro physical page 303, printed page 571.

Morley's declaration consisted of one count. He alleged possession of a chaise and its horse, while Gaisford possessed a cart and horse driven by his servant. The servant allegedly managed the cart so negligently and unskillfully that it struck the chaise with great force, dragged it a considerable distance, and damaged the chaise, wheel, and shaft. The declaration alleged an expenditure of £30 on repairs. That sum appears in the pleaded account of loss; the report does not identify it as the jury's damages award. The report also supplies no personal names, exact collision date, or independently described account of the trial evidence. **126 Eng. Rep. 639–640; 2 H. Bl. 441–443.**

A verdict was returned for the plaintiff. Serjeant Cockell then moved in arrest of judgment, asserting that trespass rather than case was required because the injury was direct rather than consequential. In the old system, selecting an unsuitable form could prevent judgment despite a favorable verdict. The question therefore concerned the legal compatibility of this declaration with the chosen action, rather than a renewed factual inquiry into who caused the collision. **640–641.**

Cockell argued that a lawful initial act could still produce a trespass. He illustrated that proposition with someone raising a stick in self-defense and accidentally striking another person. The cart collision, he argued, supplied an even stronger case because the relevant act was unlawful. He relied on *Day v. Edwards* and *Savignac v. Roome*, characterizing their reasoning as a distinction between direct and consequential injury rather than between willful and negligent conduct. Thus the force and immediacy of the impact were central to his attempt to defeat the plaintiff's chosen pleading form. **641.**

The court initially inclined to refuse the rule. Its reason focused on the identity of the defendant: it was difficult to regard a master as a trespasser for a servant's act that had not been done at the master's command. Although the cart directly hit the chaise, that immediate contact did not resolve whether the master himself was answerable in trespass. Out of respect for the King's Bench decisions cited, the court nevertheless granted a rule to show cause so that it could consider the point further. **641.**

A few days later, Cockell acknowledged that the rule could not be supported. The court agreed, expressing the clear view that case, rather than trespass, was the proper form. It **discharged the rule**. That left the plaintiff's verdict undisturbed by this arrest-of-judgment objection. The report does not separately recite an amount of judgment, an appellate affirmance, or a fresh trial order, and none should be supplied from inference. **641.**

The assigned account accurately emphasizes the master-servant distinction. Its surrounding discussion contrasts this reasoning with later collision decisions debating immediate injury and negligent conduct. Those historical comparisons explain why pleading form was unstable, but *Morley* itself should not be expanded into a comprehensive rule that all negligent direct injuries must be pleaded in case. The actual controversy involved a defendant whose servant drove the cart, and the court's stated concern was whether the master could be treated as the direct trespasser without a command. **Assigned physical 303, printed 571; actual 641.**

The recovered report contains substantial later editorial annotations. The bracketed note beginning at 639 and continuing through 640 discusses subsequent ship, carriage, and coach cases and proposes five distinctions involving negligence, servants, willful direct injury, consequential injury, and possession of chattels. Another bracketed note at 641 cites later authority concerning a servant's willful act. Their references to decisions after 1795 make clear that they are annotations, not words spoken by the *Morley* court. All were read, but the explanatory scheme in those notes is not attributed to the judgment. The upper portion of 639 and an unbracketed footnote continuing a previous attorney-lien case also belong to that earlier case. **639–641, notes (a) and (a)¹.**

The report attributes the reasoning collectively to the court. It reports no separate opinion, named judicial author, or numerical vote.
