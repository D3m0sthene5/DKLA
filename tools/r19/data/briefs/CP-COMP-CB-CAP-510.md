# Yamaha Motor Corp., U.S.A. v. Calhoun

**516 U.S. 199 (1996) — Supreme Court of the United States — January 9, 1996.** Justice Ginsburg wrote for a unanimous Court. The assigned Civil Procedure account is Note 3, printed page 1238, physical PDF pages 625–626; it emphasizes the scope of a certified interlocutory appeal. The complete decision also resolves the maritime-remedies question that made the scope issue consequential.

## Facts and the competing claims

Twelve-year-old Natalie Calhoun died on July 6, 1989, while vacationing with family friends at a Puerto Rico resort. She rented a Yamaha “WaveJammer” jet ski, struck a vessel anchored off the hotel's frontage, and was killed. Yamaha Motor Company, Ltd., manufactured the craft; Yamaha Motor Corporation, U.S.A., distributed it. Her parents, Lucien and Robin Calhoun, sued individually and as administrators of her estate in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania. They alleged defective design or manufacture and asserted negligence, strict liability, and implied-warranty theories. Those were allegations, not liability findings by the Supreme Court. 516 U.S. at 201–203.

The Calhouns invoked Pennsylvania's wrongful-death and survival statutes and requested lost future earnings, loss of society, lost support and services, funeral expenses, and punitive damages. Federal jurisdiction rested on both diversity and admiralty. The parents were Pennsylvania citizens; the American Yamaha company was incorporated and headquartered in California, and the Japanese Yamaha company was incorporated and headquartered in Japan. Id. at 202–203 & n.1.

Yamaha's partial-summary-judgment motion argued that the federal maritime wrongful-death action recognized in *Moragne v. States Marine Lines, Inc.* displaced state remedies altogether. Yamaha maintained that the federal action allowed only funeral expenses here. The District Court accepted the displacement premise but concluded that loss of society and loss of support and services were compensable under federal maritime law. Thus both sides had reasons to seek immediate review before final resolution of liability and damages. Id. at 203.

## Procedure and the appellate-jurisdiction issue

At the parties' requests, the District Court certified an interlocutory order under the historical version of 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b). Its questions asked whether the maritime cause of action permitted damages for loss of society, lost future earnings, and punitive damages. The Third Circuit permitted an appeal but addressed an earlier question: did federal maritime law actually displace the state wrongful-death and survival remedies? It held that state remedies remained applicable, without answering the certified questions about the contents of an exclusively federal remedy. Id. at 203–204.

The Supreme Court asked the parties to address the Third Circuit's authority to reach that antecedent issue. Section 1292(b) required the district judge to identify a controlling legal question, substantial grounds for disagreement, and the possibility that immediate review would materially advance termination of the litigation. But its operative authorization allowed an appeal from the **order**, not merely from the district judge's selected question. The appellate court therefore could decide an issue fairly included within the certified order. It could not use that appeal to review other orders entered in the case. Id. at 204–205.

Here the displacement issue was fairly included because it concerned the premise of the very order that limited the available damages to a federal maritime remedy. Reviewing whether that premise was sound did not require an appeal from some different ruling. Certification consequently opens review of the certified order's included issues while retaining an order-based boundary. It does not give the court unrestricted jurisdiction over the entire lawsuit. Id. at 205.

## The maritime-remedies issue and governing framework

On the merits, the question was whether *Moragne* eliminated state wrongful-death remedies for a nonseafarer's death in territorial waters. The Court held that it did not. Admiralty jurisdiction brings substantive maritime law into the case, but it does not automatically displace state law. The Court used “nonseafarers” to mean persons outside the Jones Act's seamen coverage and the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act's longshore-worker coverage. It used wrongful-death remedies as shorthand including survival statutes. Id. at 205 n.2, 206 & n.3.

The historical problem began with *The Harrisburg*, which refused to recognize a general maritime wrongful-death cause of action. Admiralty courts softened that result by applying state wrongful-death statutes to territorial-water deaths. Congress separately provided remedies through the Death on the High Seas Act and the Jones Act. State supplementation therefore had a longstanding place in the system, rather than being an exceptional innovation made for these plaintiffs. Id. at 206–208 & nn.4–5.

The subsequent development of unseaworthiness created a different difficulty. That doctrine became an absolute, nondelegable shipowner duty and a major means of recovery for maritime workers, while some state wrongful-death statutes recognized only negligence. As *Moragne* explained, a worker could recover for a nonfatal unseaworthiness injury but not for death; a fatality outside territorial waters could produce recovery when a comparable territorial-water death did not; and seamen could be denied remedies available to longshore workers. *Moragne* overruled *The Harrisburg* and supplied a federal action for death caused by violation of maritime duties. Id. at 208–213 & nn.6, 9–10.

## Why uniformity did not require displacement here

Yamaha argued that the new federal cause of action occupied the field, providing one uniform remedial scheme for all territorial-water deaths. The Court acknowledged that maritime policies sometimes require state law to yield. But *Moragne*'s uniformity concern was access to distinctive maritime liability principles, especially unseaworthiness, rather than limiting generous state damages for nonseafarers. Its purpose was to extend relief and remove remedial gaps. It did not express hostility to concurrent state wrongful-death statutes; the state negligence claim in *Moragne* itself remained in place. Id. at 209–215 & nn.8, 11–12.

Congressional remedial design supplied another distinction. When Congress specifies a comprehensive maritime recovery regime, courts ordinarily may not enlarge its prescribed damages through state law. Here, however, Congress had not prescribed wrongful-death remedies for nonseafarers in territorial waters. The Death on the High Seas Act expressly protected state death statutes from displacement by that Act, and the Court considered the congressional objective consistent with preserving state remedies in this setting. Permissible state regulation nevertheless remains subject to federal maritime principles; the decision did not attempt a general synthesis of every state/federal maritime conflict. Id. at 210 n.8, 215–216 & n.13.

## Holding, exact judgment, and reservations

The Court **affirmed the Third Circuit's judgment**. Section 1292(b) permitted consideration of the antecedent issue fairly included in the certified order, and state law properly governed the remedies available for Natalie's death. No justice dissented or wrote separately. Id. at 205, 216.

Several limits matter. The Court assumed without deciding that *Moragne*'s action extended to nonseafarers and included a survival action. It did not decide whether Pennsylvania or Puerto Rico supplied the applicable state remedies; the Third Circuit had left that question for the District Court. Nor did it decide whether federal or state law supplied the **liability standards**, as opposed to the remedial rules. It did not award the requested damages or find Yamaha liable. Id. at 210 n.7, 216 n.14.
