# Van Cauwenberghe v. Biard

**486 U.S. 517 (1988) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 13, 1988. Civil Procedure.** Justice Marshall delivered a unanimous opinion. Neither denial of an extradited defendant’s claimed immunity from civil service nor denial of forum non conveniens dismissal qualified as an immediately appealable collateral order.

## Facts and procedural history

The dispute began with a real-estate loan. Van Cauwenberghe, a Brussels broker, encouraged Biard, also a Brussels resident, to discuss an investment with Alan Blair in the United States. At a Los Angeles meeting, Blair described Three B Investment Associates’ renovation of a Kansas City-area townhouse complex. At Van Cauwenberghe’s urging, Biard lent the partnership $1 million for three years at twenty percent interest, secured by a mortgage on the complex. The partnership had a purchase contract and deposit, but did not yet own the property. It eventually defaulted, and the mortgage proved worthless because title had never been acquired. Biard claimed that he had been misled about ownership. 486 U.S. at 519.

Federal prosecutors subsequently indicted Van Cauwenberghe, Blair, and another person for fraud-related offenses. Van Cauwenberghe was arrested during a trip to Geneva under the United States–Switzerland extradition treaty and delivered to Los Angeles after Swiss legal proceedings. A jury convicted him of wire fraud and causing interstate transportation of a fraud victim. In January 1986, he received a one-year-and-one-day term satisfied by pretrial confinement, restitution of $34,501.26, and probation prohibiting departure until restitution was paid. The probation order was later modified, and he returned to Belgium after providing security. Id. at 519–520 & n. 1.

A week before the criminal trial, Biard filed a separate federal civil action asserting RICO, fraud, and related state claims arising from the loan. About two weeks after sentencing, Van Cauwenberghe was served while arriving for a probation appointment. He moved to dismiss on two grounds: extradition made him immune from civil process, and forum non conveniens warranted dismissal. The district court summarily denied both motions; the Ninth Circuit dismissed his appeal for lack of jurisdiction. Id. at 520–521.

The civil litigation continued while the appeal was pending. Footnote 2 reports a later summary judgment awarding $1.8 million in RICO treble damages and $75,000 in fees. That subsequent judgment was procedural background, not the judgment reviewed on its merits in this Supreme Court decision. Id. at 521 n. 2.

## Issues and holdings

Could either interlocutory denial be appealed under § 1291’s collateral-order doctrine? No. The claimed service immunity could be effectively vindicated after final judgment; its essential protection was against a binding judgment, rather than undergoing the burdens of a civil trial. Forum non conveniens determinations generally were not completely separate from the merits. The two grounds therefore failed different collateral-order requirements. Id. at 522–530.

The Court **assumed without deciding** that Van Cauwenberghe presented a substantial service-immunity claim. It did not determine whether extradition actually gave him that immunity, whether service was valid, or whether the forum non conveniens motion should succeed. Id. at 524–525.

## Collateral-order framework

Section 1291 ordinarily permits appeal after a decision ends the merits litigation and leaves execution of judgment. The final-judgment rule protects trial-court responsibility and limits the expense and harassment of repeated appeals. Cohen’s small collateral category requires conclusiveness, an important issue completely separate from the merits, and effective unreviewability after final judgment. All three must be satisfied. Van Cauwenberghe acknowledged that the denied motions had not ended the underlying litigation. Id. at 521–522 & n. 3.

## Claimed immunity from civil process

Van Cauwenberghe relied on the extradition principle of specialty, under which extradition constrains prosecution for offenses other than those authorizing surrender. He argued that it more broadly protected him from judicial interference, including civil suit, while his presence was compelled by extradition. He analogized his claim to qualified immunity, which can protect against trial itself and thus be irretrievably lost if review waits. Id. at 522–524.

The Court carefully distinguished the nature of the asserted right from a litigant’s general preference to win dismissal before trial. If every potentially successful dismissal argument established a right to avoid trial sufficient for immediate appeal, finality would have little force. The Court therefore examined whether freedom from trial burdens was an essential feature of this particular claimed protection. Id. at 524–525.

Specialty chiefly governs treaty obligations between states and prevents abuse of the surrendering state’s extradition process. A private civil trial did not significantly implicate those obligations before enforcement of a binding judgment. Unlike a criminal prosecution, it did not impose pretrial detention, bail, or even a requirement that the defendant personally attend trial. Neither the treaty nor the identified federal statutes expressly protected an extradited person against civil suit. Id. at 525–526 & n. 4.

Van Cauwenberghe also conceded that proper service abroad before extradition, or service during unrelated U.S. business, could require him to defend the same complaint in the same forum. His argument concerned taking advantage of forced presence at this time and in this manner. Footnote 5 thus confirmed the service-specific character of the claim. The Court characterized it as a right not to be subjected to a binding judgment without valid personal jurisdiction, a protection that later review could vindicate. Id. at 526–527 & n. 5.

## Forum non conveniens

The forum motion failed the separateness requirement. Evaluating access to evidence, witnesses, practical trial needs, local interests, and applicable law requires examination of the underlying allegations and defenses. A court must understand what proof is relevant, where the allegedly culpable conduct occurred, and how the controversy relates to the chosen forum. Those questions generally overlap the merits rather than forming an independent collateral dispute. Id. at 527–528 & n. 6.

The Court acknowledged that some forum motions can be resolved without extensive inquiry and that immediate review sometimes saves time and money. It nevertheless classified categories of orders, not individual injustices. Van Cauwenberghe argued that the summary denial without findings was facially reversible error; footnote 7 declined to make asserted obvious error an exception to nonappealability. The Court did not thereby approve the adequacy of the district court’s explanation on the merits. Id. at 529 & n. 7.

Section 1292(b) provided a separate possibility for interlocutory review when the district court certified the necessary controlling legal question and the appellate court accepted it. That discretionary route reinforced the refusal to create an appeal as of right for all denied forum non conveniens motions. Id. at 529–530.

## Judgment and assigned scope

The Supreme Court **affirmed the Ninth Circuit’s dismissal for lack of appellate jurisdiction**. Its holding covered both attempted grounds of immediate appeal, rather than upholding the substantive civil judgment or criminal conviction. Id. at 530.
