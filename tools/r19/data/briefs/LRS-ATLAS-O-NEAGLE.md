# In re Neagle
135 U.S. 1 (1890) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and posture

On April 14, 1890, the Supreme Court affirmed a federal Circuit Court judgment discharging Deputy U.S. Marshal David Neagle from state custody for killing David S. Terry while protecting Justice Stephen Field. Justice Miller wrote the Court's opinion; Justice Lamar dissented, joined by Chief Justice Fuller. Field did not participate, making the decision six to two among the eight participating Justices. The docket caption was Thomas Cunningham, Sheriff of San Joaquin County, California, v. David Neagle. Cunningham appealed the grant of habeas corpus; Neagle was the successful petitioner below and appellee, not a defendant acquitted by a state jury. The Court held that he acted under federal authority, did no more than necessary and proper to protect Field, and could not be held for that act under California law. (135 U.S. 1, caption note; 3–7, 75–76, 99.)

The decision combined constitutional protective authority with statutes governing marshals and federal habeas relief. It did not simply announce that a presidential directive immunized any federal employee from state criminal law. The inquiry required lawful federal duty, its connection to the conduct, and a justified response to the danger actually perceived. A defense available to a private citizen under state law alone would not have supplied federal habeas jurisdiction. (Id. 53–59, 67–76.)

## Litigation that produced the threat

William Sharon, a Nevada citizen, sued Sarah Althea Hill in the federal Circuit Court in California on October 3, 1883, seeking cancellation of an alleged marriage declaration as a forgery. Judges Sawyer and Deady heard the matter in September 1885 and decreed the instrument fraudulent and void on January 15, 1886. Because Sharon died between hearing and decision, the decree was entered nunc pro tunc as of the hearing. Hill did not surrender the document and continued asserting it in state proceedings. In March 1888, Sharon's son Frederick, as executor, sought revival of the decree against Hill and her new husband, David Terry. This was California federal litigation, notwithstanding Sharon's Nevada citizenship. (Id. 42–43.)

Before decision on revival, Hill harassed Judge Sawyer on a train, pulled his hair, and threatened consequences if he decided against her; Terry proposed taking him into the bay and drowning him. On September 3, 1888, Field delivered the court's decision granting revival. Hill interrupted, accused him of having been bought, and refused to sit down or leave. When Marshal Franks attempted her removal, Terry struck him hard enough to knock out a tooth and reached for a bowie knife. After officers restrained him, he drew the knife outside or at the courtroom exit and struggled before it was taken away. Neagle played the principal role in disarming him. Hill's satchel contained a revolver. The court imposed one month's imprisonment on Hill and six months on Terry for contempt. The contempt and revival judgments were separately reviewed; federal criminal charges arising from the disruption also remained pending when Terry died. Those stages were background, not the judgment under appeal in Neagle. (Id. 43–46.)

Threats continued during and after imprisonment. Terry described plans to horsewhip Field and kill him if he resisted; Hill repeatedly threatened Field and Sawyer. The Court treated testimony, public statements, and the surrounding history as showing a settled plan to provoke an encounter ending in Field's death. The threats were sufficiently public that federal officials sought protective arrangements before Field returned to California in 1889. (Id. 46–47.)

## Federal arrangements and the shooting

Attorney General W. H. H. Miller's April 27 letter directed Franks to exercise unusual caution protecting the judges and preserving the court's independence. Franks described precautions already taken. District Attorney Carey then pointed out danger outside the courthouse and suggested discreet protective deputies. The Attorney General's May 27 letter authorized special deputies at five dollars daily, payable as extraordinary marshal expenses subject to the stated approval procedure. The Court reproduced the correspondence in full and found it sufficient to justify Franks's protective arrangements, although it prescribed no detailed operational method. Franks appointed Neagle and instructed him to accompany Field in court and while traveling between courts. (Id. 47–52, 67–68.)

After holding court in Los Angeles, Field and Neagle traveled back toward San Francisco, where court was still in session. Terry and Hill boarded at Fresno. Neagle sought local peace-officer assistance at Lathrop, where the train stopped for breakfast, but no effective help became available. He urged Field to breakfast in the car; Field instead entered the station dining room with him. The shooting occurred there, not in a train dining car. Hill recognized Field and went back for her satchel and revolver. Before she returned, Terry approached Field from behind, struck both sides of his face, and appeared ready to strike again. (Id. 52–53, 56.)

Neagle rose with his revolver and loudly identified himself as an officer, telling Terry to stop. Terry turned toward him and thrust his hand into his clothing. Knowing the earlier knife incident and threats, Neagle believed he was reaching for a bowie knife and fired twice. Terry died within minutes. Hill returned, appealed to those present for vengeance, and urged examination of Terry's body for weapons; her own satchel again contained a revolver. The Court's justification rested on Neagle's well-founded belief that prompt intervention was necessary to prevent Field's death. It did not make discovery of an actual drawn knife a prerequisite to protection. (Id. 53–54, 75–76.)

## Habeas proceedings and arguments

A Stockton magistrate issued a murder warrant naming both Field and Neagle on August 14, 1889; the official reproduction records Neagle's commitment for examination on August 15. A report note explains that the governor and California Attorney General urged dismissal of the unsupported charge against Field, which was dismissed. On August 16, fellow deputy A. L. Farrish applied for federal habeas relief on Neagle's behalf. Sheriff Cunningham produced him under the writ and relied on the state warrant. Judges Sawyer and Sabin received a substantial documentary and testimonial record; the discharge order states that Cunningham offered no testimony. They found the petitioner's allegations true and his detention contrary to federal law. (Id. 3–7 & report note at 4.)

The sheriff's lawyers contested both the facts and the source of federal authority. They maintained that Terry had not intended fatal injury, that ordinary state self-defense could be assessed in California's courts, and that “law of the United States” in Revised Statutes § 753 meant congressional legislation and its necessary incidents. Marshal statutes concerned attendance on courts and execution of process, not a judge's traveling bodyguard. Federal supremacy, they argued, did not create jurisdiction where Congress had supplied no relevant duty; earlier officer-protection cases involved express enactments or judicial process. They also raised technical objections to Farrish's signing and verifying the application and to amendment of the traverse after evidence. The Court's published opinion resolved the substantive federal-duty and habeas questions without separately developing those pleading objections. (Id. 7–11, 31–40.)

The United States and Neagle answered that a government must be able to preserve its institutions and protect judges performing federally imposed duties. They relied on the Take Care Clause, implied constitutional and statutory authority, marshal powers corresponding to California sheriffs', the Attorney General's directions, and statutes permitting a federal court to determine the federal justification and discharge a prisoner before state trial. Their broad claims of self-executing governmental power were arguments; the Court's actual reasoning must be identified in its own opinion. (Id. 12–31; Court's discussion at 58–75.)

## Court's reasoning: official duty and protective authority

Under § 753, federal habeas extended to custody for an act performed pursuant to federal law and to custody violating the Constitution or federal law. Section 761 required the judge to determine facts summarily by hearing testimony and argument and dispose of the prisoner as law and justice required. The 1885 amendment to the appeal provisions allowed Supreme Court review. Miller distinguished an appeal, which brought up facts as well as law, from a writ of error restricted to legal questions; the Court accordingly reexamined the record rather than treating the Circuit Court's conclusions as beyond review. (Id. 40–42.)

Field was carrying out a federal duty at the time of the attack. Revised Statutes §§ 606 and 610 allotted Supreme Court Justices to circuits and required periodic attendance on Circuit Courts. Necessary travel between places of holding court belonged to that duty, just as chambers work could be official work outside the courtroom. The Court used earlier cases involving judicial work at chambers and enrolling officers returning from a mission to reject the idea that official protection existed only at the instant of formal courtroom activity. Field's breakfast stop during his required return journey did not convert the occasion into a merely private activity. (Id. 54–58.)

The Court rejected an interpretation of “law” confined to a special congressional enactment naming this particular protective service. An obligation properly inferred from the Constitution, or a duty derived from the general statutory responsibilities of a marshal, could qualify. Subsequent punishment of an assassin was an inadequate substitute for prevention. Quoting Ex parte Siebold and Tennessee v. Davis, Miller explained that the federal government operates throughout the States, must be able to enforce its authorized functions physically, and cannot leave its lawful officers vulnerable to state interference that paralyzes those functions. This did not eliminate concurrent state authority; where both could operate consistently, one did not exclude the other. (Id. 58–62.)

The executive branch furnished the practical means of protection. Marshals belonged to that branch and were supervised through the Department of Justice. The President's obligation to see the laws faithfully executed embraced rights and duties arising from the Constitution and the protections implicit in the government's nature, rather than only the express terms of individual statutes and treaties. The Court illustrated implied protective authority with the Koszta diplomatic incident, protection of mail carriers, timber protection in Wells v. Nickles, and government litigation against fraudulently obtained land patents in San Jacinto. These analogies supported protection of a threatened judge executing federal duties; they were not independent grants of unlimited domestic presidential policymaking. (Id. 63–68.)

There was also affirmative statutory support. Section 788 gave marshals and deputies the same powers in executing federal law that state sheriffs possessed in executing state law. California's sheriff had duties to preserve peace, prevent breaches, and make arrests, and California law justified necessary defense against attempted murder or serious injury. An attack on Field in performance of federal duty breached the peace of the United States. With the assigned protective duty and the immediate threat, Neagle had authority and responsibility to act, including lethal force where necessary. The section transferred appropriate peace-officer powers for federal functions; it did not make him a general enforcer of all California law. (Id. 68–69.)

## Court's reasoning: discharge before state trial

The Court reviewed the expansion of habeas protection from the 1789 statute, through the 1833 response to nullification, the 1842 response to the McLeod international controversy, and the 1867 provision addressing detention contrary to federal law. It discussed officer-discharge cases arising under the Fugitive Slave Act, preserving their historical context, and the principle that federal justification could be determined before a state prosecution ran its course. (Id. 70–74.)

California's objection that a jury must try the murder charge did not defeat the statutory proceeding. A jury trial is a protection of the accused, not an entitlement of the government to try every accusation. If a federal court properly established that an officer performed an authorized duty and did no more than necessary and proper, the lawful act could not constitute a state crime. The Circuit Court was competent to decide those facts, and no additional prosecution was required simply because Congress had not created an alternative federal criminal trial for the killing. The Court affirmed discharge, not removal for a later trial, and expressly concluded that Neagle need not answer in California's courts for this transaction. (Id. 74–76.)

## Lamar and Fuller's dissent

The dissent did not rest on a belief that Neagle was guilty. It accepted that protecting Field, even by killing Terry, could be justified for an ordinary bystander on the facts. The dispute was whether that justification derived from federal law and therefore permitted federal habeas to prevent state adjudication. The dissent accepted supremacy within federal jurisdiction, legitimate implied powers, and the availability of habeas for an act genuinely authorized by federal law; it also rejected any automatic objection merely because no later federal trial would occur. But § 753, in its view, did not reach this case. (Id. 76–81.)

For Lamar, implied governmental powers still had to be exercised through constitutionally designated institutions and procedures. The Necessary and Proper Clause entrusted Congress with prescribing means to implement the government's powers. The President's oath and Take Care responsibility did not allow him to create the missing rule of law. Congress had expressly provided protection in election legislation and could do so here. The dissent distinguished Koszta as foreign relations under express constitutional diplomatic authority and San Jacinto as a necessary implication of statutes governing government litigation. It invoked the absence of federal common-law criminal jurisdiction and Congress's control over inferior courts to resist the broader claim of self-executing executive or judicial authority. (Id. 81–94.)

The marshal statutes did not cure the omission. Section 787 required attendance on courts sitting in the district; a judge was not personally the court everywhere he traveled. Section 788 supplied the measure of powers when executing an existing federal duty, but could not create a new duty by importing state police responsibilities. Earlier habeas examples involved congressional enactments or federal process. The dissent also questioned how the United States, lacking a statute criminalizing this hypothetical murder within state territory, could nevertheless free its alleged perpetrator on the asserted implied authority. It would reverse and remand Neagle to Cunningham's custody, trusting California's courts to adjudicate a defense it regarded as potentially conclusive. Broader constitutional limits on Congress's ability to displace state criminal jurisdiction were noted but reserved. (Id. 94–99.)
