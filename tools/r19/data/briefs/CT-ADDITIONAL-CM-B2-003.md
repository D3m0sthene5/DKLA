# Markov v. ABC Transfer & Storage Co.

76 Wn.2d 388, 457 P.2d 535 (1969) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B2-003

## The warehouse relationship and the need for continuity

A fifteen-member partnership, Auburn Industrial Center, bought an industrial property from the federal government in 1962. It leased two warehouse buildings to ABC Transfer & Storage for three years beginning September 15, 1962, at $9,600 monthly plus utilities. The lease ended September 14, 1965 and contained no renewal provision. ABC's principal customer, Scott Paper, used the facility as an important distribution point. Continuous arrivals and departures made warehouse availability central to retaining that account. 76 Wn.2d at 389–391.

The parties repeatedly modified the space and rent. In 1963 they reduced the occupied bays and total rent while raising the square-foot rate; in October 1964 they increased the space again. Another provision required the partnership to absorb $5 railroad charges for each car entering its warehouses. Nevertheless, bills continued going to ABC, eventually reaching $21,000, more than the unpaid rent claimed in the lawsuit. The continuing failure to pay those charges figured in the trial court's assessment of the parties' intentions. These surrounding dealings supplied context for the renewal negotiations rather than an existing contractual right to renewal. Id. at 389–391.

ABC's president, Francis Bury, began renewal negotiations with partner Sidney Eland in February 1965. Scott Paper was simultaneously considering a new three-year warehousing arrangement with ABC, dependent on ABC's ability to remain. It sent traffic representatives to discuss the lease. The trial court found that Eland assured Scott Paper's representative, with Bury present, that Scott could have anything it wanted; the parties understood this to assure a three-year renewal. Bury reasonably relied on that assurance. In June, another partner accepted Bury's proposal for five bays at $7,312.50 monthly through September 1968, and ABC paid the increased rent in June and July. The appellate opinion treats these matters as findings supported by the record, not merely allegations in a complaint. Id. at 391–392.

## The undisclosed sale and resulting loss

While making renewal assurances, the partnership secretly negotiated with an agent for an undisclosed prospective purchaser. On July 1, 1965 it granted an option to sell the warehouse facility for $4 million. The purchaser was Boeing, which later exercised the option and demanded possession. ABC continued operating in the belief that its lease would be renewed. It learned of the sale through the purchaser's agent in an August 25 letter requiring vacation by September 15. Footnote 1 reproduces the finding that the partnership disclosed neither the negotiations, the option, nor its exercise while knowingly allowing ABC to continue on its renewal understanding. Id. at 392–393 & n. 1.

Other comparable storage space had been available. Had ABC received timely notice, it could have moved Scott Paper's goods gradually by routing outgoing stock from the old warehouse and incoming stock to a new one. The trial court estimated that an orderly transfer during July, August, and early September would have cost no more than $1,000, plus $18,281.25 in new-premises rent for two and one-half months. Instead, the misleading assurances caused ABC to incur $45,598.99 in expenses and lose Scott Paper's business, producing $91,127.88 in lost profits. The report separately states the resulting counterclaim judgment as $114,934.92; the component figures should not simply be added and relabeled the award. Id. at 393–394.

## Procedure and questions on appeal

The partnership sued for unpaid rent. ABC denied liability and counterclaimed for the deceptive renewal representations. After judgment on that counterclaim, the partnership appealed with fourteen assignments of error, ten directed at important factual findings. The determinative questions were whether the record supported actionable fraud and misrepresentation under the required proof standard, and whether resulting damages were established. The partnership argued that the supposed promises were unenforceable predictions and that fraud had not been proved, much less by clear, cogent, and convincing evidence. Id. at 389, 394–395.

Other assignments concerned attorney fees under the lease, refusal to reopen, refusal to find waiver against an alleged tortfeasor, and Boeing's dismissal. The Supreme Court rejected those assignments without a developed discussion. That rejection preserves the judgment but supplies no extensive separate rule about Boeing's knowledge or responsibility. Id. at 394, 397.

## Holding and reasoning

Fraud required proof of an existing factual representation, materiality, falsity, the speaker's knowledge of falsity or ignorance of truth, intended action by the recipient, the recipient's ignorance, actual and rightful reliance, and consequent damage. Each element had to be established by clear, cogent, and convincing evidence, exceeding a mere preponderance; fraud was not presumed. Id. at 395.

A prediction of future events, without an undertaking to bring them about, would not suffice. But the partnership's assurances concerned its own future conduct, made under circumstances that transformed them into commitments. The court characterized the supported promise as a consideration-supported undertaking to renew for three years and negotiate rent in good faith. It also explained that a promise intended to deceive, made without intent to perform, could support deceit; a related rule covered a promise made without care whether it would be kept when the promisor knew or should know it would induce detrimental action or inaction. The opinion therefore did more than impose liability for the bare failure of negotiations. Id. at 395–396.

The evidence corroborated the promise and reliance: testimony, repeated lease changes, negotiated increases during the secret sale discussions, and the owner's concrete economic motives. Continued occupation by a nationally known customer helped the sale effort; if the sale failed, the owner would retain its tenant and rental income. Those benefits explained why assurances could be made while keeping the alternate transaction hidden. The court found substantial support for the findings and presumed the trial court had applied the required heightened fraud standard. Id. at 396–397.

## Exact disposition and reading scope

The court **affirmed the trial court's judgment**, including the stated $114,934.92 counterclaim recovery. Hale wrote; Chief Justice Hunter, Hill, Rosellini, and Armstrong, sitting pro tempore, concurred. The reported department's decision was unanimous, with no separate opinion. Id. at 394, 397.
