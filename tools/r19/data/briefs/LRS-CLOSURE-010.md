# Pereira v. Sessions
585 U.S. 198 (2018) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, question, and result

On June 21, 2018, the Supreme Court decided No. 17-459 by an 8–1 vote. Justice Sotomayor wrote for Roberts, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer, Kagan, and Gorsuch. A document styled as a notice to appear that does not specify the time or place of removal proceedings is not the statutory notice that triggers the stop-time rule for cancellation of removal. The Court reversed the First Circuit and remanded; it did not itself grant cancellation, settle the consequences of every possible notice defect, or announce a constitutional due-process holding. Kennedy concurred separately; Alito dissented. (201–202, 207–209 & n. 5, 219–221.)

## Facts and litigation

Wescley Fonseca Pereira, a Brazilian citizen, entered the United States in 2000 at age 19 on a temporary visitor visa and remained after its expiration. By the litigation he was married, worked as a handyman, and had two U.S.-citizen daughters. After a 2006 arrest in Massachusetts for operating a vehicle under the influence of alcohol, DHS personally served him on May 31 with a document charging him as removable for overstaying his visa. It described the proceedings, rights, and failure-to-appear consequences and directed him to an immigration judge in Boston, but supplied no hearing date or time. (205–206.)

DHS filed that document with the immigration court on August 9, 2007. The court then mailed a separate notice setting a hearing for October 31 at 9:30 a.m. It used Pereira's street address rather than the post-office box he had provided to DHS; the notice was returned undeliverable. He did not attend, was ordered removed in absentia, and remained unaware of the order. After a 2013 arrest for driving without headlights and renewed DHS detention, the court reopened proceedings because he had not received the 2007 hearing notice. He then sought cancellation of removal, asserting more than ten years of continuous physical presence. (206.)

The immigration judge held that the 2006 document had stopped the clock, making Pereira ineligible. The BIA dismissed his appeal under Matter of Camarillo, which treated the statute's reference to a notice under § 1229(a) as identifying a document without importing substantive notice requirements. The First Circuit denied review, finding ambiguity and deferring under Chevron to the BIA's permissible interpretation. The Supreme Court took the case to resolve a circuit conflict but narrowed the question from all listed notice requirements to the time-and-place defect actually presented. The initial Boston location did not cure the missing date and time. (205, 206–209 & nn. 4–5.)

## Statutory framework

The 1996 immigration legislation allowed the Attorney General to cancel removal of certain nonpermanent residents who met specified conditions, including at least ten years of continuous physical presence immediately before their application. Eligibility alone did not compel discretionary relief. The stop-time rule in § 1229b(d)(1)(A) ended the relevant period when the noncitizen was served a notice to appear under § 1229(a). A separate offense-based stop-time provision was not at issue. (201–202 & nn. 2–3.)

Section 1229(a)(1) described a written notice, termed a notice to appear, specifying the nature and authority of the proceedings, allegations and charges, counsel-related information, address obligations and their consequences, and the time and place of proceedings and consequences of nonappearance. Paragraph (2) required written notice of a changed or postponed hearing's new time or place, subject to its address-related exception. Other provisions governed in absentia removal, rescission, and a ten-year bar on some discretionary relief following specified oral notice. Those related provisions supplied context; the dispositive question remained what stopped the cancellation clock. (202–204.)

The Attorney General's 1997 regulation required hearing information only where practicable. The government's oral-argument representation was that almost all notices over the preceding three years omitted time and date. That administrative practice did not displace the statutory requirement. (204–205.)

## Majority reasoning: text, context, and notice's function

Congress expressly referred to § 1229(a), directing the reader to its definition of a notice to appear. Paragraph (1)'s identification of a written notice specifying hearing time and place meant that those particulars were essential to the stop-time trigger. Although the cross-reference covered all of subsection (a), only paragraph (1) used and described the term. Paragraph (2)'s provision for a new time or place presupposed previously supplied hearing information and reinforced that reading. (208–210.)

Section 1229(b)(1) also generally required at least ten days between notice service and the first hearing to afford an opportunity to secure counsel. A document with no hearing details followed years later by actual scheduling shortly before the hearing would undermine meaningful preparation. Identical statutory words ordinarily carry the same meaning; the notice needed for this opportunity and for the stop-time rule therefore included hearing particulars. Providing an accurate scheduled hearing did not prevent a later lawful change, and the majority rejected the dissent's premise that the government necessarily must guess. (210–211 & n. 6.)

Common sense supported the statutory reading: notice of where and when to appear performs an essential function, alongside the document's charging function. The government conceded that a blank sheet would not stop the clock because charges were necessary. Neither it nor Alito supplied a text-based basis for making charging information essential but hearing information dispensable. (211–212 & n. 7.)

## Rejected competing readings

The majority rejected the claim that § 1229(a)(1) merely named the document without defining it. Its language was definitional, as comparable statutory formulations confirmed. An incomplete notice was not automatically sufficient just because ordinary speech might still call it a notice: unlike a missing signature on an appeal notice, omission of when to attend deprived this statutory notice of an essential feature. The Court's reservation about other defects was judicial restraint, not acceptance of the government's construction. (208 n. 5, 213–214 & n. 8.)

The word “under” can vary by context, but here it connected the stop-time trigger to the substantive criteria and meant in accordance with them. Alternative readings such as authorized by could not defeat that link. The government's reliance on the different phrases used in the in absentia provisions was likewise unpersuasive: those phrases could refer to compliant notice despite differences in wording. The oral-notice rule's added consequences said nothing to the contrary about the written notice needed to stop time. (214–217.)

Administrative difficulties did not justify ignoring clear text. Congress expressly allowed rescheduling with new notice; DHS and the immigration courts had previously coordinated scheduling, and the majority did not assume they were incapable of doing so again. Notice of a changed hearing reduced, rather than required, confusion. The stop-time rule's stated purpose of preventing noncitizens from exploiting administrative delay was consistent with requiring a compliant notice: proper service would stop accrual regardless of later delay. Alito's comparison to the retroactive treatment of older orders to show cause did not control the different notice to appear created under the new regime. (214 n. 9, 217–219.)

Because the statutory answer was clear, the majority did not proceed to Chevron deference. It applied the statute and reversed the First Circuit, remanding for consistent proceedings. Its holding concerned missing time or place information; note 5 expressly left other omissions for another day. (208–209 & n. 5, 219.)

## Separate writings

Kennedy joined the opinion in full but criticized reflexive deference. Earlier appellate decisions had treated notice as perfected only when the statutory information was received; after Camarillo, most courts deferred to the BIA, sometimes with little independent analysis of ambiguity or reasonableness. He regarded that approach as an abdication of the judiciary's interpretive role and suggested reconsidering Chevron's premises and implementation in an appropriate case, particularly when agencies interpret their own authority. This was a separate invitation, not a majority overruling of Chevron in 2018. (219–221.)

Alito would defer to the BIA under Chevron. He accepted that the statute required notices to contain time and place, but distinguished that requirement from whether a defective document still stopped time. In his view, “under” reasonably meant authorized by, and the reference to all of § 1229(a), rather than paragraph (1), suggested the document category rather than perfect compliance. The extension of stop-time treatment to older orders to show cause, which did not necessarily contain hearing details, reinforced the agency's interpretation. He considered the majority's construction plausible but not uniquely permissible. (221–227 & nn. 1–3.)

He also distinguished a document's required contents from its identity and legal effect: ordinary usage and the treatment of imperfect notices of appeal showed that a defective document might remain effective. He regarded the majority's reservation concerning other omissions as inconsistent with its definitional theory. His note 5 acknowledged that an omission could remove the document's essential character, but denied that hearing details necessarily did so for stop-time purposes. Paragraph (2)'s assumption of compliant notice and the counsel interval did not settle the separate stop-time consequence. (229–234 & nn. 4–5.)

Finally, Alito emphasized that DHS issued the notices while Justice Department immigration courts controlled calendars. Requiring estimated dates could cause confusion and repeated changes without advancing the stop-time policy. He viewed the document reasonably as primarily a charging instrument and criticized the majority's confidence in future scheduling coordination. Note 6 argued that the agency could permissibly choose among those competing functional accounts. He concluded that Chevron remained binding and required accepting the agency's reasonable choice. (227–229, 234–236 & n. 6.)
