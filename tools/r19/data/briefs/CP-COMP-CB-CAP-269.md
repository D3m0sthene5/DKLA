# Johnson v. City of Shelby, Mississippi

**574 U.S. 10 (2014). Supreme Court of the United States, November 10, 2014. Civil Procedure.** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, physical PDF page 339, printed page 645, Note 11's Johnson account and quotation before Skinner.

## The claim and the lower courts' ruling

The plaintiffs had worked as police officers for Shelby, Mississippi. They alleged that the city's board of aldermen fired them because they exposed criminal activities by one alderman, rather than because their work was deficient. They sought compensatory relief from the city for violations of their Fourteenth Amendment due process rights. These were their allegations; the Supreme Court did not find that the aldermen had committed crimes or conclusively determine an unconstitutional dismissal. (10.)

The district court entered summary judgment against the officers because their complaint did not expressly invoke 42 U.S.C. § 1983. The Fifth Circuit affirmed. Its stated concern was that naming § 1983 provided meaningful notice of the governing liability rules, including the absence of respondeat superior liability and consequences for qualified immunity. The Supreme Court reviewed this particular basis for rejecting the complaint, rather than trying the underlying constitutional claim. Although the textbook summarizes the result as reversal of a dismissal, the official opinion identifies the lower disposition as summary judgment. (10–11.)

## The issue and holding

**A complaint adequately stating the relevant facts is not subject to threshold rejection merely because it fails to cite § 1983 as its legal vehicle.** Rule 8(a)(2) requires a short and plain statement showing entitlement to relief, and the federal rules discourage making litigation turn on technical defects in the statement of a legal theory. The Court summarily granted certiorari, **reversed the Fifth Circuit's judgment, and remanded**. It instructed that the officers should have an opportunity to add the citation on remand. It did not award compensation or hold that their constitutional claim had been proved. (11–12.)

## Why an omitted citation did not justify judgment

The Court separated identifying the events giving rise to a claim from elaborating the precise legal theory. A party must supply enough factual substance to show a plausible claim, but need not attach the correct statutory label to those facts to preserve the suit. In this case, the officers had stated the events simply and directly, giving the city the factual basis of the claim. The rejected pleading was therefore not deficient in the factual way addressed by Twombly and Iqbal. Johnson did not dispense with plausibility; it held that a plausible factual statement does not become insufficient merely because the pleader's legal presentation is imperfect. (11–12.)

The Court also relied on decisions rejecting special heightened pleading requirements in municipal civil-rights and employment-discrimination actions. It found no rule requiring a damages plaintiff alleging constitutional violations to expressly name § 1983. That conclusion addresses what the complaint must say; it does not abolish the substantive limits on municipal liability under that statute. (11.)

The Fifth Circuit's qualified-immunity explanation did not fit this lawsuit. The officers sued **the city only**, rather than individual municipal officers. A municipality cannot invoke its agents' good faith as a defense to § 1983 liability. The Supreme Court consequently found the lower court's immunity rationale confused the defendant actually before it with defendants who might be present in another civil-rights case. It did not decide an individual officer's qualified-immunity defense, because no such officer was sued. (11–12.)

## Amendment and the decision's limits

The opportunity to insert a § 1983 citation served clarification and avoided further insistence on a precisely worded theory of the pleadings. The Court invoked Rule 15(a)(2)'s direction to freely allow amendment when justice requires. It did not say that the officers needed to plead an entirely different factual transaction, nor condition its rejection of the lower courts' legal-label requirement on a new substantive allegation. (12.)

The result is a pleading decision tied to the specific ground on which judgment was entered. It leaves the officers to litigate the substantive constitutional and municipal-liability questions in further proceedings. A useful comparison is between an insufficient factual basis and an inaccurately labeled but sufficiently stated claim: Johnson protects the latter without resolving whether its claimant ultimately wins.
