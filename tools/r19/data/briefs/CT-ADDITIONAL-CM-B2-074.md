# Ballard v. Chavez
117 N.M. 1, 868 P.2d 646 (N.M. 1994) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

The New Mexico Supreme Court decided this appeal on January 12, 1994. Justice Frost wrote, with Chief Justice Ransom and Justice Montgomery concurring; the reported panel was unanimous and supplied no separate writing. The court reversed and set aside an order dismissing a contractor's reformation suit for failure to state a claim. It expressly left the merits and factual evaluation to the district court. This was a ruling that reformation was potentially available, not a decree reforming the contract or awarding damages. (868 P.2d at 646, 648.)

## Facts and procedure

James, Wayne, and Glenn Ballard, operating as Ballard and Sons, successfully bid to build Phase I of Loving High School. According to their amended complaint, on December 26, 1988, they signed a construction contract with the Loving Municipal School District and simultaneously executed a change order deducting the cost of paving certain parking areas. Neither side realized that the same paving cost had already been deducted from the construction price in the contract itself. The alleged double deduction cost the contractors $93,840, of which the district had paid $20,000. They sought equitable reformation to correct the writing. These were allegations accepted for the dismissal appeal, not facts established at a trial. (Id. at 646–647.)

The district court treated the suit as an impermissible attempt to change a bid price after bids had been opened. It relied on Procurement Code § 13-1-106 and on Gardner-Zemke's treatment of a contractor who had failed to read contract documents. The Supreme Court reviewed the amended complaint under the then-stated Rule 1-012(B)(6) test: accept well-pleaded facts and ask whether the claimant could prevail under any provable state of facts within the claim. (Id.)

## Questions and governing rules

The questions were whether the statutory prohibition on post-opening bid modification barred correction of a mutually mistaken executed contract, and whether the contractor's failure to discover the duplicated deduction necessarily defeated reformation. The court answered both questions no. Bids are essentially offers; executed contracts are agreements. A restriction directed to modification of bids does not itself govern modification or reformation of a subsequent contract. (Id. at 647.)

Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 155, a court may reform a writing that fails to express the parties' agreement because both parties were mistaken about its contents or effect. An omitted or incorrectly written agreed term, or an included term never agreed upon, can qualify. Section 157 further provides that failure to know or discover the facts does not preclude reformation unless the failure amounts to a lack of good faith and reasonable standards of fair dealing. The ordinary duty to read therefore is not an absolute defense to reformation for mutual mistake. (Id. at 647–648.)

## Reasoning and the district's defenses

The allegedly mistaken instruments were already executed when the mistake was discovered. The change order amended the contract and became part of it. Ballard consequently sought correction of those writings rather than permission to revise its bid. Section 13-1-106 addressed bid correction or withdrawal, including limited withdrawal of a materially mistaken nonresponsive low bid after opening; its prohibition on modification of bid prices did not dispose of this distinct contract claim. (Id. at 647 & n. 1.)

The pleaded double subtraction could be a failure of the writings to express the parties' actual agreement. The court recognized the duty to read but applied the qualified fault rule governing reformation. It did not find that these contractors had acted in bad faith, nor did it hold that every careless mistake is reformable. It held that neglect to discover the discrepancy was not the categorical bar used by the district court. (Id. at 647–648.)

Gardner-Zemke was different. There a contractor sought adjustment under a contractual changed-subsurface-conditions provision; the court refused adjustment because the contractor had not read material bearing on what conditions it could reasonably expect. That case did not involve a mutually mistaken writing. Its treatment of awareness of site conditions could not replace the rules governing the reformation claim pleaded here. (Id. at 648.)

The school district also invoked § 37-1-23, which granted governmental entities immunity from actions not based on a valid written contract. Footnote 2 rejected that argument because both the contract and the change order that Ballard sought to reform were written. The court did not announce a general immunity exception for oral public contracts. (Id. at 647 n. 2.)

## Disposition and assigned significance

The court reversed the dismissal and expressly declined to decide the merits. No final recovery, factual finding of mutual mistake, or payment of the unpaid alleged loss was ordered. The casebook's “Reformation of Bids” problem asks whether the remedy depends on discovery before construction begins. The opinion's controlling distinction concerns discovery after contract formation and correction of an executed writing; it does not make commencement of construction the decisive statutory dividing line or separately decide that hypothetical timing question. (Id. at 647–648; C&M printed 545, PDF 238.)
