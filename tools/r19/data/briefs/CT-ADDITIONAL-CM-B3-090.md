# Patterson v. Meyerhofer
204 N.Y. 96, 97 N.E. 472 (1912) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

The New York Court of Appeals decided this case on January 9, 1912, by a four-to-three vote. Judge Willard Bartlett wrote for the majority, joined by Chief Judge Cullen and Judges Vann and Werner. Judge Chase dissented, joined by Judges Hiscock and Collin. The court reversed the Appellate Division and Special Term judgments dismissing Benjamin Patterson’s complaint and granted a new trial, with costs to abide the event. The majority held that Patterson’s $620 contract-damages claim could succeed on the existing record despite failure of his proposed trust theory; it did not itself enter a final $620 money judgment. (204 N.Y. at 96–97, 100–102, 107.)

## Transaction, interference, and the relief sought

Patterson agreed in writing to sell Anna Meyerhofer four parcels with houses for $23,000, payable partly in cash and partly by taking title subject to mortgages. Meyerhofer knew when contracting that he did not own the property and expected to acquire it at a foreclosure sale. Before the sale she announced that she would not perform the contract and would buy for herself without recognizing it as binding. Patterson attended able, ready, and willing to purchase and bid, but she outbid him whenever he bid. According to the majority’s account, she acquired the four parcels for $5,595 apiece: $22,380 altogether, $155 below the agreed resale price for each parcel and $620 below the total contract price. She also acquired a fifth house. (Id. at 98–99.)

Patterson alleged an earlier oral arrangement under which he would acquire all five houses, convey the four covered by the written agreement to Meyerhofer, and retain the fifth. He demanded conveyance of the fifth house, a declaration that Meyerhofer held the premises in trust subject to his contract, a lien, and $620 damages reflecting the difference between her acquisition cost for the four houses and the price she had agreed to pay him. The asserted oral arrangement was an allegation; the majority emphasized that there was no finding establishing it. (Id. at 99–100.)

Special Term dismissed the complaint after trial. It reasoned that an ordinary vendor-purchaser agreement created no relation of confidence, so each party could pursue her or his own interest subject only to the agreement’s stipulations. Meyerhofer therefore could bid as a stranger and keep the property without paying Patterson the difference. The Appellate Division affirmed. Patterson argued that the written sale was only part of a broader agreement and that Meyerhofer’s acquisition made her a trustee. She defended the finding that no oral agreement existed, denied any relationship of trust, and argued that known inability to perform permitted rescission and direct purchase. (Id. at 97–100.)

## Majority holding and reasoning

The majority substantially agreed that the transactions established no trust. It found no proved, enforceable oral agreement concerning the fifth house. Meyerhofer also had not entered possession under Patterson’s title before improving that title; she acquired the houses in declared defiance of his rights. The cases treating a vendee’s purchase of an outstanding superior title as held for a vendor involved purchasers already in possession under completed sale arrangements, a relationship likened to landlord and tenant. They did not fit this transaction. (Id. at 99–100.)

Failure of that equitable theory did not eliminate the pleaded contract claim. Patterson expressly sought $620. On the majority’s view of the found facts, that amount represented the profit denied him when Meyerhofer prevented the intermediate purchase and resale contemplated by their written agreement. A plaintiff should not be dismissed simply because the complaint additionally requested excessive equitable relief. (Id. at 100.)

The contract carried an implied undertaking against intentional prevention of the other party’s performance. A party responsible for causing or sanctioning nonperformance cannot use that failure to recover damages or defend against the contract. Meyerhofer knew Patterson needed to acquire the houses at the identified foreclosure sale before he could convey them to her. Her agreement to buy from him therefore implied that she would not prevent that acquisition by deliberately bidding against him and taking the houses herself. The absence of an express prohibition against bidding did not remove this undertaking. (Id. at 100–101.)

The majority presumed that, without her interference, Patterson could have acquired the four houses for the price she paid and then received $620 more under their contract. Although the houses ultimately reached the buyer, the agreement contemplated that they would first pass through Patterson, preserving his contractual profit. Requiring payment of that difference would give him what the contract promised and charge Meyerhofer no more than she had agreed to pay. The majority left the fifth house outside the remedy and expressly allowed the possibility that she could present a better defense at the new trial. (Id. at 101–102.)

## Chase’s dissent: factual causation and procedural limits

Chase supplied a substantially fuller chronology and challenged both the necessity of Patterson’s nonperformance and the availability of relief in the action actually litigated. In his account, Shorrock owned five adjoining, equally valuable houses encumbered by mortgages. An initial proposed agreement expressly described the foreclosure and special arrangements but was rejected as too complex. The executed January 13, 1909 agreement concerned only four houses for $23,000 and set March 16 for delivery; it omitted those special terms. After postponements proposed by the trust company’s attorney, Meyerhofer protested without security for performance and declared the agreement void before the March 29 sale. These particulars belong to the dissent’s account and should not silently replace the majority’s framing. (Id. at 102–103.)

The dissent described the purchase of all five houses for approximately $27,975 and Meyerhofer’s openly announced willingness to bid up to $28,000. Patterson was acting for Shorrock and had acquired the third mortgage; excess bidding money would return through a surplus proceeding. Chase therefore thought another $25 bid could have secured the entire property and enabled performance, with that additional amount returning to Shorrock. Patterson testified that he was prepared to bid whatever was necessary and intended to bid when she reached $28,000, yet stopped without an explained reason. Chase regarded this as unnecessary acquiescence, undermining the majority’s prevention account. (Id. at 103–106.)

Chase nevertheless left open whether Patterson could bring an action at law on the written contract. His decisive objection was that Patterson had chosen and consistently maintained an equitable action asserting a trust, with damages only incidental to that relief. Patterson had not requested transfer to Trial Term when his trust theory failed or argued on appeal for relief as an action at law. Chase viewed the distinction as still necessary despite both systems being administered by one court, including the proper jury-trial route for legal issues. Because the equitable grounds were unproved and the plaintiff had not pursued the alternative legal route, the dissent would affirm dismissal rather than convert the case on appeal. (Id. at 105–107.)
