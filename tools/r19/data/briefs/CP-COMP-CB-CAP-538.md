# Vanover v. NCO Financial Services, Inc.

**857 F.3d 833 (11th Cir. 2017).** May 17, 2017, No. 15-15294. Byron, a district judge sitting by designation, wrote for a panel with Tjoflat and Hull; no separate opinion. Assigned source: CivPro.pdf physical page 641, printed pages 1269–1270, Note 8.

## Facts and procedural history

Karen Vanover alleged that NCO repeatedly telephoned her to collect medical debts despite lack of permission and instructions to stop. The appeals court recounted allegations at the pleading stage; it did not find that NCO actually violated the collection statutes. Vanover I, filed April 23, 2014 in Florida federal court, asserted Telephone Consumer Protection Act claims concerning cellular calls during April 2013–April 2014. NCO moved for summary judgment May 13, 2015, disputing both lack of consent and use of an automatic dialing system. 836–837.

On May 20, 2015, Vanover filed Vanover II in Florida state court, adding Fair Debt Collection Practices Act and Florida Consumer Collection Practices Act theories as well as TCPA claims. NCO removed it to federal court. Its amended complaint expanded the alleged calling period backward, included landline and third-party communications, and concerned the same medical collection efforts. The account gives differing periods for different versions of the allegations; these are not all one identical complaint. After NCO renewed its motion to dismiss for claim-splitting, Vanover sought to add Expert Global Solutions and Transworld Systems in a proposed second amended complaint. The district court denied joinder and dismissed Vanover II **with prejudice**. 837–838.

## Questions and standard of review

Could Vanover require or obtain permission to join the additional defendants? Could the later action be dismissed for improperly splitting claims while the earlier action was pending?

The court reviewed joinder and this dismissal for abuse of discretion. Although a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal ordinarily receives de novo review, the claim-splitting decision here chiefly concerned management of duplicative litigation. Applying an incorrect legal standard, following improper procedures, or making clearly erroneous findings can constitute abuse. 837–838.

## Joinder analysis

Under Rule 19(a), Vanover failed to show that she could not obtain complete monetary relief from NCO without the additional companies. A bare parent-subsidiary relationship and unsupported assertions that the parent directed NCO did not establish the necessary liability or control. The record likewise lacked allegations explaining why Transworld’s relationship to NCO prevented full relief against NCO. Joinder would not have destroyed subject-matter jurisdiction, so infeasibility under Rule 19(b) was not the operative problem. 838–839.

Rule 20(a) generally encourages joinder where the claims arise from a shared transaction or series and present a common question. Nevertheless, the district court could consider practical fairness and docket management. These additions were proposed to defeat the claim-splitting motion and exposed NCO to further duplicative litigation and possible inconsistent outcomes. Vanover already knew of Transworld’s alleged role from August 2012 phone screenshots and had not alleged recent discovery of the parent relationship. She had nine months to amend the first complaint. NCO had no obligation to seek consolidation to repair her litigation choices. Denying permissive joinder fell within the court’s discretion. 839–840.

## Claim-splitting rule and application

Addressing an issue of first impression in the circuit, the court adopted the Tenth Circuit’s approach: ask whether the first action, **assuming it were final**, would preclude the second. Actual finality is unnecessary because this doctrine manages pending duplicative cases. Ordinary claim preclusion’s protection of final judgments is related, but distinct. 840–842.

The test examines the same parties or their privies and the same transaction or series of transactions. A shared nucleus of operative facts is evaluated pragmatically through time, origin, motivation, convenient trial unit, and party expectations. Here the actual parties were identical after joinder was denied. The cases challenged the same collection efforts on the same medical accounts. Extending the historical period backward, adding other telephones and third-party contacts, or labeling the conduct under additional statutes did not create an independent transaction. The relevant collection conduct preceded the first complaint and formed a convenient trial unit. 841–843.

**Holding and disposition:** the district court did not abuse its discretion in refusing joinder or dismissing the amended complaint in Vanover II. **Affirmed.** The appeal did not decide whether the collection allegations were true, determine damages, or resolve NCO’s pending summary-judgment merits arguments in Vanover I. 843.

## Notes, textbook comparison, and source limits

All three numbered judicial notes were read: note 1 permits a pleading-stage defense when the necessary basis appears in the complaint and judicially noticeable material; note 2 refuses appellate review of Vanover’s discovery objection because she did not raise it below; note 3 explains the pending-first-action setting. The starred note identifies Byron’s designation. 835 n.*, 836 n.1, 840 nn.2–3.

The textbook’s Note 8 accurately states the two-part inquiry and lack of a final-judgment requirement, but omits the FDCPA/FCCPA theories and joinder appeal. Its “one year later” is approximate; the actual filing dates are April 23, 2014 and May 20, 2015. The dismissal concerned the removed second case, not termination of the first federal case. The report’s caption says Services while its narrative calls NCO Systems; the caption is retained with that variant recognized. Its historical statutory descriptions are not independently updated legal advice.
