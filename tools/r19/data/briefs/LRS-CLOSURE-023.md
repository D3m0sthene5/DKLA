# Citizens to Preserve Overton Park, Inc. v. Volpe
357 F. Supp. 846 (W.D. Tenn. 1973) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, motion, and this stage's disposition

Chief Judge Bailey Brown issued this memorandum decision on April 19, 1973, in No. C-70-17 in the Western District of Tennessee. Tennessee's highway commissioner sought another remand after Secretary Volpe refused to approve Interstate 40 through Overton Park. The park-protection plaintiffs and Secretary defended that refusal. The court held the Secretary had not made the necessary determinations and directed preparation of a remand order requiring a decision within 45 days. This is the district court's post-remand decision, subsequently reversed by the Sixth Circuit; it is not the Supreme Court's 1971 decision or the final appellate rule. (848–852.)

## Earlier approval, judicial remand, and the new administrative record

The action originally sought to prevent release of federal construction funds for the park route. The district court granted defendants summary judgment in 1970, the Sixth Circuit affirmed, and the Supreme Court reversed for plenary review of the Secretary's November 1969 approval. After an evidentiary hearing, the district court's January 5, 1972 decision found that Volpe had not actually made the required route determination and alternatively had relied on an incorrect view of the governing law. It remanded for a lawful route decision while leaving the proposed design's approval standing. No party appealed that remand. (848–849.)

Volpe requested a complete analysis in the context of an environmental impact statement and ordered additional route and design hearings, held in Memphis on September 14–15, 1972. On January 12, 1973, the Acting Federal Highway Administrator submitted a 457-page final environmental impact statement and recommended a finding that no feasible and prudent alternative existed and that the proposed design minimized harm. The record also included the earlier administrative materials, the six-week district-court trial, and the additional hearings. The Secretary did not claim inability to decide because the record was incomplete. (849; 851 n. 3.)

On January 18 Volpe nevertheless refused approval. In light of the Supreme Court's park-protection standard and intervening environmental legislation and noise requirements, he could not find that there were no feasible and prudent alternatives or that the environmental and noise requirements were satisfied. He suggested possible use of the I-240 circumferential with arterial improvements, the L & N Railroad corridor, public transportation, or combinations. He preferred a tunnel design's lesser harm to the park, but expressly did not endorse any particular alternative, exclude unmentioned alternatives, or reject the no-build alternative. (849–850.)

## The parties' competing accounts of the required finding

Tennessee argued that this refusal did not satisfy either the earlier remand or the statutes Volpe invoked. The Secretary and park-protection plaintiffs argued that inability to find an absence of feasible and prudent alternatives was enough to prevent approval; they did not believe he had to affirmatively identify an acceptable alternative. Plaintiffs also characterized the distinction between those formulations as semantic. (850–851.)

The district court treated the federal-highway statutes as a cooperative scheme: states proposed programs and project plans; the Secretary reviewed and approved them for federal funding. Section 4(f), with its counterpart in the highway statute, did not shift highway planning to the federal department. It did, in the court's view, require the Secretary to determine whether a feasible and prudent alternative existed when the proposed route used parkland. Approval required finding no such alternative; rejection on that ground required an affirmative finding that one existed. With a complete record, the Secretary had to decide rather than stop at inability to make the approval finding, and APA § 706(1) allowed the court to compel that decision. (850–852.)

## Reviewability and the court's added requirement

The court went further: the Secretary had to designate the feasible and prudent alternative so that Tennessee could obtain effective review. It acknowledged that the Supreme Court did not require formal findings and said the Secretary therefore need not explain why the designated route was feasible and prudent. But a court could not review an unidentified alternative. The court also concluded that disapproval was reviewable under APA § 701, paralleling review of the earlier approval. In note 2 it observed that an affirmative finding favoring the L & N corridor would have had evidentiary support under the earlier district decision; it did not itself select that route. (851 n. 2; 852 & n. 4.)

For the same reason, the court required an affirmative finding that the proposed route or design violated NEPA, the Federal-Aid Highway Act, or federal noise standards and identification of the respects in which it did so. The existing design approval need not be reconsidered, but because there had never been a valid route decision the successor Secretary could reconsider design in his discretion. (852.)

## Judgment, later reversal, and assigned scope

The memorandum required preparation of a remand order with a 45-day decision deadline, based on the long delay and departmental familiarity with the record. It did not directly approve the highway, select an alternative route, or award damages. Its affirmative-alternative requirement was later rejected in Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Brinegar, 494 F.2d 1212 (6th Cir. 1974), which is briefed as a separate judgment. (852; later decision 1215–1216.)
