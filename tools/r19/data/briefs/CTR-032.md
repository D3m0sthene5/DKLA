Dorton v. Collins & Aikman Corp.
Citation
Dorton v. Collins & Aikman Corp., 453 F.2d 1161 (6th Cir. 1972). United States Court of Appeals for the
Sixth Circuit, decided January 6, 1972. Judge Celebrezze wrote the opinion. The case concerns whether an
arbitration clause in a seller's acknowledgments became part of carpet sales under UCC § 2-207. Full judicial
opinion.
Parties
Frank E. Dorton and his partners, doing business as The Carpet Mart in Kingsport, Tennessee, were the
plaintiffs and appellees. They bought carpets for retail sale and claimed that the seller misrepresented the
fiber content. Collins & Aikman Corporation, a Delaware corporation with its principal place of business in
New York and a carpet plant in Dalton, Georgia, was the defendant and appellant. It sought to enforce
arbitration language in its printed sales forms. Both sides qualified as merchants for the transactions at issue.
Material Facts
During 1968 through 1970, the parties conducted more than fifty-five carpet transactions. Orders were
telephoned to Collins & Aikman's Georgia order department, sometimes by a Carpet Mart partner and
sometimes through the seller's visiting salesman. The parties disputed whether those communications
themselves produced oral sales agreements. One buyer affidavit described employees or the salesman
agreeing to sell the requested carpet during the ordering process. The seller's position and the wording of its
forms suggested that written acknowledgment supplied acceptance.
After checking the price and the buyer's credit, Collins & Aikman sent a printed acknowledgment. The forms
had several titles and variations. Their front sides stated that the transaction was subject to terms on both
sides, including arbitration. They purported to bind the buyer through various acts, including signing,
furnishing shipping directions, accepting delivery, or in some versions retaining the form for ten days
without objection. The reverse contained a broad clause requiring arbitration in New York City of disputes
connected with the contract.
The seller mailed the acknowledgment on the ordering day or the following day. Except for possible postal
delay, it arrived before the carpets. The Carpet Mart accepted delivery and paid without objecting to the
printed terms. In May 1970, a customer complaint led it to discover that some carpet supposedly
manufactured entirely from Kodel polyester allegedly contained a cheaper, inferior fiber. The parties'
agreement to buy and sell carpet was evident; the dispute concerned whether they had also agreed to the
seller's arbitration mechanism. 453 F.2d at 1163-65.
Procedural History
The Carpet Mart sued in Tennessee state court for fraud, deceit, and misrepresentation, seeking $450,000 in
compensatory and punitive damages. Collins & Aikman removed the action to federal district court on
diversity grounds and moved under § 3 of the Federal Arbitration Act to stay the litigation pending
arbitration. The district court concluded that no binding arbitration agreement existed. It applied § 2-207(3),
treating the transactions as contracts established by conduct, and refused to include the seller's additional
arbitration clause.

Collins & Aikman appealed the denial of the stay. Although the order was interlocutory, the Sixth Circuit
held it appealable under the doctrine then governing stays pending arbitration and 28 U.S.C. § 1292. It noted
that Georgia and Tennessee had both enacted the UCC before these transactions. The appeal required the
court to review the district court's use of § 2-207, not to determine whether the alleged fiber substitution or
fraud actually occurred.
The Sixth Circuit concluded that the existing findings did not permit a final decision on whether arbitration
was a contractual term. It remanded for findings about formation during the oral dealings, whether
arbitration had already been included, and, if arbitration was an added term, whether it materially altered the
relevant offer or agreement. Id. at 1163-64, 1169-71.
Issue
Did the seller's acknowledgments make acceptance expressly conditional on the buyer's assent to additional
terms, so that the writings failed to form a contract and § 2-207(3) governed? If they instead were effective
acceptances or confirmations under § 2-207(1), did the arbitration clause enter the contracts under subsection
(2)? If it did, did the buyer's fraud claims nevertheless permit the court action to proceed without a stay?
Rule and Holding
An acceptance is expressly conditional within § 2-207(1) only when it clearly conveys that the offeree will
not proceed without the offeror's assent to the additional or different terms. The seller's subject-to language
and provisions treating various acts or silence as acceptance did not satisfy that requirement. The district
court therefore could not bypass subsections (1) and (2) by applying subsection (3) on that basis.
If arbitration was additional to the oral offers or prior agreements, it had to be evaluated under subsection
(2). Because the parties were merchants, the material-alteration inquiry was central. A material addition
would require express agreement to become binding. The appellate court did not decide whether this
arbitration clause materially altered these transactions; it required factual findings on remand. If the clause
was part of the contracts, its breadth covered the asserted fraud claims and required a stay. Id. at 1168-71.
Judgment
The court remanded to the district court for further findings consistent with its opinion. It did not finally
order arbitration and did not finally hold that arbitration was excluded. The disposition required the trial
court to use the correct formation and terms analysis before deciding whether the action should be stayed.
Reasoning
Section 2-207 responds to the commercial practice of exchanging forms whose standard terms seldom
match. Under the traditional mirror-image approach, a response adding terms could become a counteroffer,
and subsequent performance could give the party sending the final form a substantial advantage. The Code
permits a contract to exist despite differences in boilerplate and separately determines which added terms
enter it. Treating every qualified acknowledgment as a counteroffer would undermine that statutory design.
The first factual question was whether the oral dealings already completed the contracts. If they did, the
acknowledgments were confirmations of existing agreements. If they did not, the forms could be acceptances
of oral offers. Section 2-207 applies in either situation; the dispute need not involve two competing written
forms. The distinction nevertheless matters because it identifies the agreement against which the alleged
addition must be compared. It also prevents a court from treating a later confirmation as if no prior contract
existed.

The second question was whether arbitration was actually new. The district court had apparently assumed
the oral orders contained no arbitration term. The Sixth Circuit required a finding. If the parties already
agreed to arbitration, the printed clause would not need to enter as an additional term under subsection (2). If
they did not, its inclusion depended on the statutory treatment of additions or on a later actual agreement.
The conditional-acceptance proviso required more than the seller's assertion that its terms governed. It
required an express condition tied to the buyer's assent. The forms supplied several supposed ways to bind
the buyer, including mere retention without objection. That language did not distinctly establish that the
seller would refuse to proceed unless assured of assent to the added terms. Calling an acknowledgment
subject to its reverse-side conditions therefore did not have the same effect as clearly conditioning
acceptance on the offeror's agreement to them.
Once the acknowledgments were treated as acceptances or confirmations, subsection (2) governed an
additional arbitration clause. The court left materiality to factual determination. If the addition materially
altered the transaction, the buyer would not become bound without expressly agreeing. If it did not
materially alter it, the merchant rule could incorporate it under the circumstances presented. The repeated
transactions and receipt of forms supplied relevant context; they did not eliminate the need for the correct
legal inquiry.
Finally, the court distinguished a challenge to making an arbitration agreement from a claim that the overall
contract was induced by fraud. Under Prima Paint, a broad arbitration clause, if actually agreed upon, could
cover the alleged fraud in carpet composition. The buyer attacked the underlying sale rather than asserting
fraud specifically in procuring the arbitration clause. Thus, the court first had to decide whether the
arbitration agreement existed. Only after an affirmative answer would the broad clause require a stay. Id. at
1166-71.
Separate Opinions
The reported decision contains Judge Celebrezze's opinion without a separate concurrence or dissent. Judge
Brooks participated in the decision but died before it was filed.
Notes
The holding concerns the meaning of expressly conditional acceptance and the need for proper findings.
Whether arbitration materially altered the particular bargains remained unresolved. A brief that says the
Sixth Circuit conclusively rejected arbitration would misstate the disposition. Likewise, the court's statement
that a stay would be required if the clause was included is conditional on the formation issue being resolved
for the seller.
Dorton also separates two provisions that are easy to conflate. The § 2-207(1) proviso asks what condition
the responding party expressly communicated. Section 2-207(2)(b) asks what effect an added term would
have on the bargain. A substantial alteration does not, merely because it is substantial, turn an otherwise
effective acceptance into an expressly conditional counteroffer. C. Itoh presents the contrasting situation in
which the seller's language did satisfy the proviso.
