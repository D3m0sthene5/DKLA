# Calcutt v. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation
598 U.S. 623 (2023) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and administrative-law rule

On May 22, 2023, the Supreme Court granted certiorari limited to the first question presented, reversed the Sixth Circuit, and required remand to the FDIC for reconsideration. Its unsigned per curiam opinion records no dissent or separate writing. Once the appellate court identified legal errors in the FDIC’s reasoning, it could not sustain discretionary sanctions by supplying its own corrected analysis. Under Chenery and the ordinary remand rule, the agency must first exercise its delegated judgment on legally proper grounds. This did not finally exonerate Calcutt or prohibit sanctions after a lawful reconsideration. (Slip 1–2, 6–7.)

## Lending relationship and enforcement chronology

Harry Calcutt was Northwestern Bank’s CEO from 2000 to 2013. The Michigan community bank developed its largest lending relationship with nineteen family-owned Nielson businesses in real estate and oil. When they stopped paying on September 1, 2009, they owed $38 million. The parties arranged a multistep Bedrock Transaction to bring the loans current. That stabilized the relationship for a year, but payments stopped again in September 2010. Another temporary agreement followed, and the businesses stopped paying again in January 2011, remaining in default. (Slip 2–3.)

The FDIC investigated the officers beginning April 13, 2012, and issued removal, prohibition, and civil-penalty notices on August 20, 2013. It alleged that the Bedrock Transaction violated internal loan policy, that the bank’s directors were misled or misinformed, that Calcutt answered FDIC inquiries inaccurately, and that the transaction was misreported in financial statements. Those allegations were the enforcement basis; the Supreme Court’s remand decision was not an independent evidentiary finding on each one. (Slip 3.)

An administrative law judge began a seven-day evidentiary hearing on October 29, 2019; twelve witnesses, including Calcutt, testified. On April 3, 2020, the ALJ recommended exclusion from the banking industry and a $125,000 civil penalty. Calcutt appealed to the FDIC Board, which adopted those sanctions. The agency’s final decision also removed him from office and prohibited further banking activities. (Slip 1, 3–4.)

## Statutory elements and the Board’s findings

Under 12 U.S.C. § 1818(e), the FDIC had to find misconduct, a harmful effect or personal benefit by reason of that misconduct, and the specified degree of culpability. Relevant misconduct included unsafe or unsound practices or breach of fiduciary duty. Culpability required personal dishonesty or willful or continuing disregard of the bank’s safety or soundness. (Slip 2.)

The Board found unsafe or unsound practices and rejected any requirement that Calcutt proximately cause the harm. It identified three harmful effects: a $30,000 charge-off on a Bedrock loan, $6.4 million in losses on other Nielson loans, and investigative, auditing, and legal expenses. It also found persistent concealment of the common nature and problems of the loan portfolio, false answers during examinations, concealed documents, and false testimony about the directors’ knowledge. These were Board findings made under its legal approach; the Supreme Court recounts them to explain why the appellate court’s substituted analysis mattered. (Slip 3–4.)

## The Sixth Circuit’s errors-corrected affirmance

Calcutt challenged the Board’s rejection of proximate cause and its treatment of the alleged harms. The Sixth Circuit agreed that the statutory phrase “by reason of” required proximate cause. It found that Calcutt had proximately caused the $30,000 charge-off through his extensive involvement in the transaction. But it attributed only part of the $6.4 million losses to him because some loss probably would have occurred regardless of his conduct. It also excluded the investigation, audit, and legal expenses as part of the bank’s normal business. (Slip 4–5.)

Despite that changed legal standard and narrower set of harms, the appellate court affirmed the sanctions by a 2–1 vote, relying on substantial evidence. The Board itself had never applied proximate cause or considered whether the same sanctions were warranted on the revised basis. The Supreme Court identifies that substitution as the defect. It did not issue a separate merits ruling establishing every part of the Sixth Circuit’s causation analysis as Supreme Court statutory doctrine. (Slip 5–6.)

## Why remand was required

A reviewing court ordinarily evaluates agency action on the agency’s stated grounds. If those grounds are legally inadequate, the court cannot affirm by making the discretionary judgment Congress assigned to the agency. It must ordinarily remand for further explanation or investigation rather than conduct its own inquiry and decide the matter itself. Both Calcutt and the Solicitor General agreed that the Sixth Circuit should have followed that course here. (Slip 1, 6.)

The Sixth Circuit relied on Wyman-Gordon’s description of remand as an idle and useless formality where there is no uncertainty about the outcome. The Supreme Court recognized the narrow exception and pointed to a case in which the agency was legally required to take a particular action despite giving an incorrect rationale. Calcutt’s case was different. Whether to sanction him, and the type and severity of sanctions, were discretionary, contextual judgments involving multiple culpability considerations. The corrected harmful-effects analysis left room for the Board to exercise that judgment differently. Declaring the same outcome inevitable would deny it the flexibility Congress conferred. (Slip 6–7.)

## Judgment, assignment, and limits

The Supreme Court reversed the Sixth Circuit and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion, directing that court to return the matter to the FDIC for fresh consideration. Certiorari was limited to the first question; the opinion did not finally determine Calcutt’s liability, grant all relief he sought, or forbid the FDIC from reaching the same result through a lawful exercise of discretion. (Slip 1–2, 7.)
