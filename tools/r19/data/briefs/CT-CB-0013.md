# Sullivan v. O’Connor

363 Mass. 579, 296 N.E.2d 183 (1973)

## Citation, court, and assigned scope
The Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts decided the case on May 9, 1973. Justice Kaplan wrote the opinion. The participating court comprised Chief Justice Tauro and Justices Reardon, Quirico, Kaplan, and Wilkins. The casebook prints pages 13–18. The full opinion adds the policy discussion concerning medical promises and the complete footnotes, and makes especially clear the limits imposed by the plaintiff's appellate waiver. Pinpoints below use Massachusetts Reports pages.

## Parties and their objectives
Alice Sullivan, the patient, sued physician James H. O'Connor for breach of a promise concerning the result of cosmetic surgery and, separately, for negligent treatment. After the jury found contractual liability but no negligence, the dispute on appeal concerned recoverable damages. O'Connor sought to confine compensation to Sullivan's out-of-pocket expenses. Sullivan had objected that the instructions did not grant full promised-result compensation, but she offered to abandon those objections if the verdict in her favor stood. (363 Mass. at 579–81.)

## Material facts
Sullivan was a professional entertainer, a fact known to O'Connor. The jury could find that he undertook to improve her appearance by making her relatively long, prominent nose shorter and more pleasing in relation to her other features. The contemplated treatment consisted of two operations. In fact, he performed three, and the result worsened her appearance. The opinion describes a concave upper line, a bulbous lower portion, flattening and broadening, and an asymmetrical tip. Further surgery evidently could not improve that configuration. (Id. at 580.)

Sullivan incurred stipulated physician and hospital expenses totaling $622.65. Although appearance could matter to her profession, she did not prove lost employment or earnings attributable to the changed nose. The trial judge accordingly excluded lost earnings from the jury's damages calculation. Her professional circumstances remained relevant to the appraisal of disfigurement and associated distress; the court did not treat them as proof of an unestablished wage loss. (Id. at 580–81; 588 n.7.)

The additional third operation was important because the parties' contemplated two-operation course already entailed some pain and distress. The suffering of an extra operation could be characterized as a consequence beyond what successful performance would have required. Suffering during the first two operations raised a different baseline question: it was a cost of the contemplated treatment under an expectation measure, but arguably wasted reliance under a restoration measure. The litigation's actual posture ultimately made it unnecessary to decide that harder question. (Id. at 583–89.)

## Procedural history and review posture
The case was tried to a jury on the patient's demand. The judge first submitted liability questions; the jury found for Sullivan on the contract count and for O'Connor on the negligence count. The subsequent damages instructions allowed out-of-pocket expenses, worsening of appearance and associated mental distress, and pain and suffering from the third operation. They excluded pain from the first two operations and unproved lost earnings. The jury's contract award was $13,500. (Id. at 579–81; casebook p. 13.)

O'Connor excepted to the refusal to restrict recovery to expenditures and to the inclusion of disfigurement and third-operation suffering. His objection concerning a directed verdict on liability was not pressed on appeal and, the court said, could not succeed. Sullivan conditionally waived her own exceptions challenging the failure to award the full difference between the promised appearance and actual appearance, together with other objections. This waiver is integral to understanding what the appellate court decided. (363 Mass. at 581 & n.1; 589.)

## Issues
Can damages for breach of a medical result promise include physical deterioration, mental distress, and the pain of an additional operation even though the physician was not negligent? Is the permissible award limited to a refund of expenditures? Must this case choose between expectation damages, which protect the promised improvement, and reliance damages, which restore the patient's preagreement position? A related discussion addresses whether result promises should be unenforceable as a matter of medical policy. (Id. at 581–89.)

## Rules and holdings
Massachusetts recognizes actions on a physician's promise of a specified result, while requiring clear proof that such a promise was made. The jury's rejection of negligence does not defeat a distinct contractual undertaking. Physical and psychological injury are not categorically excluded from contract damages; their recoverability depends on the subject matter, the contemplated performance, and the relationship between the breach and the claimed injury. (Id. at 582–84, 587–88.)

The challenged award could include expenditures, the worsening of Sullivan's condition, and suffering and distress associated with the third operation. Those items were recoverable under either an expectation or a reliance approach in this case. Because Sullivan waived the claims that would distinguish the two measures, the court did not have to select one as the exclusive governing formula. It spoke favorably of reliance compensation in this setting but did not hold that every medical contract must use reliance and exclude expectation. (Id. at 585–89.)

## Actual judgment
The court overruled the defendant's exceptions and treated the plaintiff's exceptions as waived. The $13,500 verdict consequently stood. It ordered no new trial and did not enlarge the award to include the full promised improvement or the pain of the first two operations. A description such as “the court awarded reliance damages instead of expectation damages” would omit the express reservation that both theories sustained the items actually before it. (Id. at 589.)

## Reasoning
### Medical uncertainty requires scrutiny of the promise, rather than categorical immunity
Kaplan described reasons to be cautious. Medical results are uncertain, patients can reinterpret optimistic reassurance as a guarantee after disappointment, and expansive contract liability could encourage defensive practices. Conversely, refusing to enforce genuine promises could permit practitioners to induce patients through commitments they never intend to honor. Massachusetts takes an intermediate position: the action is available, but the jury should scrutinize whether an actual undertaking, rather than an opinion or therapeutic assurance, was proved. (Id. at 582–83.)

The court suggested that instructions could point jurors to features such as the difficulty or complexity of the proposed operation when evaluating the likelihood of a promise. That evidentiary caution does not rewrite an established result promise into a duty merely to exercise care. The absence of negligence addresses the quality of the treatment, while the contract count addresses the content of the undertaking. One can fail to deliver a promised outcome without having performed negligently. (Id. at 583–84.)

### The three remedial interests answer different questions
Restitution returns the benefit conferred on the physician, most obviously the physician's fee. Reliance seeks to reverse detrimental changes incurred because the agreement was undertaken, potentially including expenditures to hospitals or other third parties and a worsened bodily condition. Expectation compares the actual result with the result promised. Calling a reimbursement of all expenditures “restitution” can therefore be inaccurate when some money went to others and did not enrich the physician. The court explained the distinction rather than treating the three labels as interchangeable. (Id. at 583–85.)

An expectation measure based on Hawkins would capture both failure to improve and deterioration. It ordinarily would exclude the pain that the patient would have endured even if the promised result had been achieved, while allowing additional suffering attributable to the breach. The court also noted that unpaid fees would have to be accounted for to avoid placing the patient in a better position than performance would have produced. These observations concern how to construct the comparison, not a refusal to recognize personal injury simply because the suit sounds in contract. (Id. at 583–84.)

### Reliance offered an attractive approach, but the opinion preserved the actual decisional limit
Kaplan reasoned that a fee-only recovery might provide too little protection for a real undertaking, whereas the full value of the imagined promised appearance might be difficult to estimate and potentially disproportionate to the fee. Those concerns supported serious consideration of restoring the patient's preagreement position. The opinion also discussed the different strength of expectation's justifications in commercial and noncommercial settings. It did not declare those policy observations sufficient to bar all expectation awards in medical cases. (Id. at 585–86.)

The concluding paragraphs control the scope. Sullivan no longer sought the part of the expectation interest consisting of improvement above the preoperative condition, and she waived a possible reliance claim for suffering from the first two operations. The remaining challenged items—the deterioration and the extra operation—were compensable on either view. A doctrinal preference developed in the discussion should therefore be separated from the narrower proposition necessary to preserve this verdict. (Id. at 588–89.)

### Foreseeability and the contract's subject matter explain the treatment of distress
Mental anguish over an ordinary merchandise transaction typically is not the anticipated consequence for which the seller assumes responsibility. An agreement to operate on a person's body is different in the relevant respect: bodily change and the distress attending that change are central to what the parties contemplate. The court rejected sweeping statements that pain belongs exclusively to malpractice law. It allowed the jury to account for the subject matter of this undertaking and Sullivan's known professional circumstances. (Id. at 587–89 & n.7.)

The opinion's footnotes also warn against double recovery and mechanical formulas. A claimant who receives the full expected benefit should not add reliance costs that would have been incurred to earn that benefit. Conversely, a reliance claim can make sense when a promised benefit is difficult to value. The discussion does not authorize combining the most generous elements of every measure without checking what counterfactual position the combined award creates. (Id. at 586–87 n.5; 588 n.6.)

## Separate opinions
No separate opinion appears in the report. The opinion identifies the participating justices and states the disposition without a recorded dissent. Its discussions of dissenting opinions in other cases are authorities or arguments considered by Kaplan, not separate opinions in Sullivan.

## Use in class and limits of the decision
To compare this case with Hawkins, identify which suffering would occur with successful performance and which suffering exists because performance failed. Then ask whether the plaintiff seeks a promised improvement, restoration of the prior condition, or the return of a benefit transferred to the defendant. The dispute over the first two operations illustrates how the answer changes with the chosen remedial interest, but the waiver prevents attributing a conclusive resolution of that dispute to this judgment.

The source comparison adds the complete medical-policy discussion, the unpressed directed-verdict objection, the footnotes concerning overlapping remedies, and the final limitation on choosing a damages theory. The casebook's shortened policy passage and the opinion's sympathy for reliance should not eclipse that limitation. The brief reports the historical decision; it does not purport to establish contemporary medical-liability statutes or later Massachusetts doctrine.
