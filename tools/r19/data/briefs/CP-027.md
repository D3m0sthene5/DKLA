# State ex rel. Sivnksty v. Duffield
137 W. Va. 112, 71 S.E.2d 113 (1952) | Civil Procedure

## Accident, confinement, and the disputed service

Mike Sivnksty, a nonresident of Gilmer County, West Virginia, entered the county on June 30, 1951, intending to spend several days there over the Fourth of July holiday. His automobile struck two children walking on State Route 33 within Glenville. A town officer arrested him on a reckless-driving charge. Unable to obtain bond, Sivnksty remained in the county jail until July 2. The court’s procedural account identifies the injured children as Johnny Bob and Alma Jean DeVaughn; Johnny Bob, through his next friend, brought the civil personal-injury action involved here. 71 S.E.2d at 113–14.

Service occurred at a particularly important moment. Sivnksty was taken before the mayor at ten in the morning on July 2, but his counsel was absent, so he was returned to jail pending an afternoon trial. While still in the jailer’s custody, he received a summons commencing Johnny Bob’s damages action. Thus, he had entered the county voluntarily, but his presence when served was involuntary, and the criminal charge had not yet been tried. The civil action arose from the same accident as the reckless-driving prosecution. Id. at 113.

That afternoon he was convicted and fined fifty dollars plus costs. An appeal to the circuit court produced another guilty verdict; the state supreme court later refused a writ of error in the criminal proceeding. Those criminal results do not change the relevant time for this dispute: the challenged civil service preceded the initial conviction. Counsel stipulated that Sivnksty left Gilmer County immediately after obtaining release on appeal bond on July 2. Id. at 113–14.

## Procedural posture and the claimed immunity

In the civil action Sivnksty appeared specially and filed a plea in abatement. He argued that the circuit court lacked jurisdiction because he was a nonresident confined in jail when served. The circuit court sustained a demurrer to that plea and placed the damages action on its trial docket. Sivnksty then sought a writ of prohibition from the Supreme Court of Appeals against the trial judge and the civil plaintiff, asking that further proceedings be stopped. The decision concerns immunity from service, rather than negligence, the children’s damages, or the validity of the criminal conviction. Id. at 114.

Sivnksty’s argument distinguished his original travel from his later detention. Planning a voluntary holiday visit could not, he contended, make compulsory confinement voluntary after his arrest. The issue was whether that confinement while awaiting a criminal trial insulated him from service in the related civil suit. The majority treated the circumstances as presenting a question not previously decided in the jurisdiction. Id.

## Governing purpose and the majority’s distinctions

President Riley explained that immunity from process principally protects judicial administration. Historically, it prevented outside litigation from interfering with the pending court proceeding; protection later extended beyond court officials to litigants, witnesses, and others. A related policy encourages nonresidents to appear in response to criminal proceedings without fearing that doing so will expose them to additional civil litigation away from home. Id. at 114–15.

The court recognized substantial immunity in appropriate circumstances. In *Whited v. Phillips*, a nonresident voluntarily came to answer an indictment; immunity protected his attendance. In *Morris v. Calhoun*, a person appeared in another county pursuant to a public-safety officer’s citation. In *Lang v. Shaw*, a person returned to the county to answer a criminal charge after release on recognizance. These examples connect the person’s presence to responding to judicial or equivalent criminal process. By contrast, *State ex rel. Godby v. Chambers* refused immunity to a person already serving a misdemeanor sentence: confinement did not itself constitute the kind of criminal process that justified the privilege. Id. at 115.

For the majority, Sivnksty’s original reason for entering Gilmer County was decisive. He had not come in response to an existing charge or process, and no criminal proceeding was pending against him when he entered. The later arrest did not transform his originally voluntary presence into attendance that needed immunity to encourage or facilitate it. His confinement alone therefore did not supply the privilege. The court cited authorities permitting civil service on prisoners, before or after conviction, particularly when they were voluntarily within the jurisdiction at arrest. It also cited *Crusco v. Strunk Steel Co.* as rejecting automatic immunity merely because someone had become a criminal defendant. Id.

The court accordingly denied the writ. The practical result was that the challenged civil action could proceed despite the pleaded immunity objection. It did not adjudicate Sivnksty’s liability for the collision or award Johnny Bob compensation. Nor does the reasoning establish that everyone appearing to answer criminal process lacks immunity; the majority expressly recognized the contrary examples. Its operative distinction was between presence brought about by answering process and voluntary entry followed by arrest and confinement. Id. at 115.

## Lovins’s dissent

Judge Lovins would have granted prohibition. He accepted the judicial-administration rationale but gave greater weight to protecting people compelled to defend themselves in unfamiliar courts from expense, distraction, and additional litigation. For him, the relevant circumstance was Sivnksty’s presence at service: regardless of why he originally traveled, he was now detained and could not leave. Id. at 115–18.

Lovins emphasized language in *Morris* treating the distinction between voluntary and involuntary presence as immaterial while the need to answer the criminal charge continued. He read the West Virginia decisions as protecting the litigation attendance itself. He also noted that some authorities invoked by the majority arose under statutory schemes, whereas West Virginia’s privilege was governed by its common law. The conflicting authorities did not compel the majority’s restrictive result. Id. at 116–17.

The dissent distinguished *Godby* on timing. That defendant was already convicted and serving a sentence; Sivnksty was still awaiting trial when the civil summons arrived. Service could harass him while he prepared his criminal defense and force civil litigation far from his residence, where he could receive little benefit from his local reputation. Lovins also feared abuse: a traveler involved in an accident could be arrested on a real or supposed charge and, while confined, immediately exposed to a local damages action. These concerns supported immunity even without finding that the civil plaintiff had engineered the arrest. Id. at 117–18.
