# Schneir v. State
43 So. 3d 135 (Fla. 3d DCA 2010), no. 3D09-555, decided August 25, 2010 | Contracts

## Facts and procedure

Brian Gerald Schneir participated in an organized-fraud scheme that stole gasoline stocks from Miami-Dade County’s International Airport. He entered a cooperation agreement involving a guilty plea to a first-degree felony under section 817.034(4)(a)(1), dismissal of other charges, and a two-year sentence in exchange for truthful testimony against his codefendants. A breach exposed him to the thirty-year maximum, while leaving the prosecution free to recommend a lesser sentence. Schneir repeatedly lied under oath during his pretrial deposition. The opinion describes that material breach as undisputed. He nevertheless later testified for the State at trial and claimed that a mutually agreed modification restored his right to the two-year sentence. The sentencing court imposed twelve years, and he appealed. (Actual opinion, opening factual paragraph and n. 1.)

The codefendants vigorously attacked his trial testimony, including his admission that he had committed deposition perjury, but the jury found them guilty. Their convictions did not establish that Schneir had complied with the original agreement or that the State had agreed to disregard the earlier breach. His appeal challenged his own twelve-year sentence, rather than the codefendants’ convictions. (Opening paragraph; n. 1.)

## Claimed modification and mutual assent

Applying contract principles to the cooperation agreement, the appellate court first found no agreement on the claimed modification. The prosecution’s alleged statement offering one final chance if Schneir testified did not objectively promise a two-year sentence. His unilateral expectation of that result could not create a contractual undertaking. The court drew on the distinction between outward expressions of agreement and an individual’s uncommunicated intention. It treated the supposed modification as no contract at all, rather than finding an enforceable agreement with ambiguous wording. (First numbered analytical paragraph, citing Gendzier v. Bielecki.)

The footnotes reinforced this conclusion. The State had recommended, and Schneir had received, twelve years rather than the potential thirty years, so the court considered the asserted last-chance promise satisfied in any event. It also doubted that he had actually expected an assured two-year sentence. In the reproduced trial cross-examination, Schneir acknowledged his plea agreement, understood that lying worsened his position, and expressed fear that he could receive thirty years because of his lies. This was record evidence undermining his asserted expectation, not an additional judicial finding that trial testimony was false. (Notes 2–3, the latter reproducing supplemental-record transcript pages 2101–2102.)

## Consideration and the preexisting duty

The second ground assumed, without finding, an understanding that the State would forgive the breach. A modification or novation, like an original contract, required lawful consideration. The only performance allegedly required of Schneir under the new arrangement was truthful trial testimony. The original agreement already obligated him to provide it, and the court also described truthful testimony as a duty imposed on any citizen. Promising the same performance therefore added no lawful consideration for an undertaking to restore the two-year benefit. The analysis separately addresses contractual and otherwise existing duties; it does not depend on a duty owed only to a particular third party. (Second analytical paragraph, citing New Kirk Construction, Stitt, Slattery, Davidpur, and Blair.)

The conclusion was limited to the arrangement alleged here. The court did not rule that all renegotiated cooperation agreements are unenforceable or decide a case in which the defendant promised materially different lawful performance. It rejected the claimed agreement both for want of assent and, alternatively, for want of new consideration. No new promise of performance was identified merely because Schneir finally did what he already had to do. (First and second analytical paragraphs.)

## Holding and exact disposition

The Third District Court of Appeal affirmed the twelve-year sentence. It summarily found no merit in Schneir’s other arguments without developing them. Senior Judge Schwartz wrote for a panel including Judges Cortiñas and Rothenberg. The complete reproduction contains three footnotes and no separate writing or dissent. The result does not impose a new sentence, reinstate the initial two-year term, or award contract damages. (Final two disposition paragraphs; panel heading and author signature.)
