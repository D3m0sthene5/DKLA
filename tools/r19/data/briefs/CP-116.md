## Citation

Justice Ginsburg filed a concurrence. Assigned excerpt: Civil Procedure casebook pp. 1120–1124, beginning at repository PDF p. 568. Full opinion: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-530/pdf/USREPORTS-530-133.pdf.

## Parties

Roger Reeves was the employee-plaintiff, the prevailing party before the jury, and the Supreme Court petitioner. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., his former employer, was the defendant, Fifth Circuit appellant, and Supreme Court respondent. Reeves alleged discharge because of age under the Age Discrimination in Employment Act. Sanderson asserted that it fired him for inaccurate attendance records and related shortcomings. The appeal concerned whether the evidence permitted the jury’s finding of intentional discrimination and how a court should review that evidence under Rule 50. 530 U.S. at 137–140, 149–154.

## Material Facts

Reeves was fifty-seven when fired in 1995 and had worked for Sanderson for approximately forty years. The company manufactured toilet seats and covers. Reeves supervised the regular production line in the Hinge Room; Joe Oswalt, in his mid-thirties, supervised a special line, and Russell Caldwell, forty-five, managed the department. The supervisors maintained attendance information used in tracking employees’ work and pay. An audit of those records supplied the company’s stated reason for discharging Reeves and Caldwell while retaining Oswalt. Id. at 137–138.

Powe Chesnut, the director of manufacturing and husband of company president Sandra Sanderson, participated in the audit and recommended discharge along with other officials. The president made the formal termination decision. Reeves offered evidence that Chesnut exercised substantial practical authority within the company and was the real force behind his firing. After Reeves’s dismissal, three employees in their thirties successively assumed his duties. These facts supported the initial discrimination showing and the argument that the formal decisionmaker’s stated neutrality did not resolve whose motivation drove the decision. Id. at 137–142, 151–152.

Reeves disputed both the accuracy of the audit’s conclusions and his responsibility for the asserted deficiencies. He explained that the automated time clock could fail to record work and that he entered a 7 a.m. arrival when he personally saw an employee working at that time. Some supposed errors occurred while Reeves was hospitalized. Evidence also indicated that Caldwell, rather than Reeves, bore responsibility for disciplining late employees. The employer had never calculated the amount of overpayments allegedly attributable to Reeves’s errors, and there had been no union grievance or employee complaint about his recordkeeping. This evidence allowed the jury to question whether the announced reason was genuine. Id. at 143–145.

There was additional evidence of age-related hostility. Reeves testified that Chesnut referred to his age disparagingly and said he was too old to do his job. Oswalt corroborated a marked difference between Chesnut’s treatment of the two supervisors. Although their recorded productivity was nearly identical, the employer conducted an efficiency study of Reeves’s line alone and placed him on probation. Chesnut’s role in that episode and the termination supported an inference connecting the hostility to the decision. The Court treated this evidence through the jury-favorable Rule 50 standard rather than independently deciding which witnesses were truthful. Id. at 151–153.

## Procedural History

Reeves sued in federal district court in Mississippi in 1996. The case proceeded to a jury. Sanderson moved for judgment as a matter of law, arguing that the proof could not establish intentional age discrimination. The district court denied the motion. The jury awarded $35,000 in compensatory damages and found the violation willful; the court entered judgment including an equal amount of liquidated damages and separately awarded front pay. It denied the employer’s renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law and alternative request for a new trial. Id. at 138–139.

The Fifth Circuit reversed. It acknowledged that a reasonable jury could reject Sanderson’s explanation as pretextual but treated that rejection, combined with the initial discrimination showing, as insufficient without additional evidence of discriminatory motivation. It discounted the age-related remarks because they were not made in the immediate context of discharge and stressed that other decisionmakers had not been shown to harbor age bias. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to address that evidentiary approach and then considered whether Sanderson nevertheless deserved judgment under the correct Rule 50 standard. Id. at 139–140, 145–146, 149–154.

## Issue

May a jury infer intentional discrimination from a plaintiff’s prima facie case combined with evidence that the employer’s asserted legitimate reason is false, or must the plaintiff always provide an additional, independent category of discrimination evidence? In reviewing the verdict under Rule 50, what evidence must the court consider, what evidence must it credit, and could this particular verdict survive under those principles? Id. at 140, 146–154.

## Rule and Holding

A prima facie case combined with sufficient evidence that the employer’s stated justification is false may permit a reasonable factfinder to infer intentional discrimination. The inference is permissible rather than mandatory. There is no categorical requirement of additional independent evidence once those showings are made, but judgment as a matter of law can still be appropriate when the entire record would not permit a rational discrimination finding. The plaintiff retains the ultimate burden of persuasion throughout. Id. at 142–149.

Under Rule 50, the court reviews the whole record and draws reasonable inferences for the nonmoving party, without weighing evidence or resolving credibility. It must disregard evidence favorable to the movant that the jury was not required to believe, while crediting the nonmovant’s evidence and uncontradicted, unimpeached evidence for the movant, at least when supplied by disinterested witnesses. Applied here, that standard required sustaining the jury’s determination: Reeves had supplied a prima facie case, evidence of a false explanation, and additional evidence connecting age-based hostility to his dismissal. Id. at 149–154.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court reversed the Fifth Circuit’s judgment. It resolved the sufficiency question itself rather than directing another round of appellate consideration under a corrected standard. The district court had properly submitted the case to the jury, and the Fifth Circuit had erred in overturning the verdict. The Court did not require a new trial or direct the factfinder to infer discrimination whenever an employer’s explanation is disbelieved. Its result sustained this verdict on this record. Id. at 153–154.

## Reasoning

The Court first located the dispute within the McDonnell Douglas burden-of-production framework. Reeves established the initial showing through his protected age, qualification, discharge, and replacement by younger workers. Sanderson then produced evidence of a legitimate reason: deficient recordkeeping. Producing that explanation satisfied an evidentiary burden; it did not require the judge to believe the explanation. Once the employer supplied it, the presumption created by the prima facie case disappeared, but the underlying evidence remained available to the jury. The Court assumed the framework applied to this ADEA case because the parties did not contest that point; it did not separately decide its general applicability to every age-discrimination claim. Id. at 141–143.

The remaining question was intentional discrimination. Reeves always bore the burden of persuading the factfinder of that ultimate proposition. He could seek to do so by showing that Sanderson’s explanation was not credible. The Court distinguished rejection of the employer’s explanation from a compulsory judgment for the employee. A factfinder could reject an explanation yet conclude that the actual reason was some other nondiscriminatory consideration. Maintaining the plaintiff’s burden therefore did not require treating proof of falsity as legally irrelevant. Id. at 143, 146–148.

A false explanation is circumstantial evidence. An employer ordinarily knows why it acted; evidence that it offered an untrue reason can support the inference that it is concealing an unlawful one. Together with the circumstances establishing the prima facie case, the elimination of the asserted justification can make discrimination a reasonable explanation.

The Fifth Circuit had mistaken the rule that disbelief does not compel liability for a rule that disbelief cannot help establish liability. That mistake caused it to discard important parts of Reeves’s evidence when deciding whether the verdict was supportable. Id. at 146–148.

The Court expressly preserved limits on the inference. A plaintiff might establish the two initial showings while other evidence conclusively revealed a different nondiscriminatory reason. Or the proof of falsity might be weak while abundant and uncontroverted independent evidence indicated that discrimination had not occurred. Relevant considerations include the strength of the prima facie showing, the probative value of the falsity evidence, and other employer evidence that a reviewing court may properly credit. Thus, the rule authorizes a case-specific sufficiency inquiry and rejects both automatic liability and automatic judgment for the employer. Id. at 148–149.

Rule 50 required clarification because courts had described its evidentiary scope differently. Some formulations referred only to evidence favoring the nonmovant; others referred to the whole record. The Court explained that these formulations could be reconciled by separating the evidence a judge must review from the evidence the judge may treat as true. All the evidence is reviewed. Credibility, disputed inferences, and weight remain for the jury. This approach also fits the parallel standards governing summary judgment and judgment as a matter of law. Id. at 149–151.

The whole-record instruction did not license the appellate court to choose Sanderson’s witnesses over Reeves’s. The court had to credit evidence favoring Reeves and disregard employer-favorable evidence that the jury was entitled to reject. Uncontradicted and unimpeached evidence from disinterested witnesses could receive credence, but the Court did not convert interested managers’ accounts into conclusive proof merely because they described the formal decision-making process. That distinction preserved meaningful sufficiency review without allowing a judge to retry credibility. Id. at 150–151.

Reeves’s record satisfied that standard. His evidence called into question whether he maintained inaccurate records, whether he was responsible for discipline, and whether inaccuracies justified the employer’s charge of intentional falsification. The alleged discrepancies and the company’s asserted reason were not interchangeable: the employer had to contend with evidence distinguishing an inaccurate record from an intentionally falsified one. A jury could also consider the absence of the supposed practical consequences of the recordkeeping problems. Id. at 143–145, 152.

The age-related evidence further supported the verdict. Chesnut’s remarks, differential treatment, selective efficiency study, and practical authority supplied a basis for finding that his motivation affected the dismissal.

The Fifth Circuit discounted the remarks because of their timing and relied on the apparent neutrality of other participants. Those observations could be argued to a jury, but they did not eliminate the reasonable inference that Chesnut drove the decision. Similarly, the presence of other managers over fifty and criticism of other supervisors did not conclusively disprove discrimination against Reeves. Id. at 151–153.

Finally, the jury had been instructed that Reeves must prove both that the stated reasons were not the real reasons and that age discrimination was the real reason. Its verdict therefore did not rest on an instruction making pretext alone automatically establish liability. Given the full body of supporting evidence, the Supreme Court concluded that a reasonable jury could find intentional discrimination and that the Fifth Circuit had substituted its own assessment of evidentiary weight. Id. at 153–154.

## Separate Opinions

Justice Ginsburg joined the Court’s opinion in full and wrote separately about its reservation concerning cases requiring more than a prima facie showing and evidence of a false explanation. She anticipated that such cases would be uncommon. In her view, a dishonest explanation ordinarily supports a rational inference of concealed discrimination unless evidence the court must credit conclusively demonstrates that discrimination could not have been the true motive. She welcomed further elaboration in an appropriate case but agreed that the Fifth Circuit’s categorical demand for extra evidence was erroneous. Her expectation about how frequently exceptions would occur was a concurring observation, not an additional categorical rule adopted by the Court. Id. at 154–155.

## Notes

The assigned excerpt concentrates on circumstantial proof and Rule 50. The full opinion supplies the detailed workplace facts, amounts and procedural sequence, the explanation that the ADEA framework was assumed, and Ginsburg’s omitted concurrence. Those details show how the general evidentiary rule operated in an actual post-verdict review. Casebook pp. 1120–1124; 530 U.S. at 137–155.

Distinguish the burden of producing a nondiscriminatory explanation, the employee’s continuing burden of persuasion, and the court’s threshold sufficiency inquiry. An employer can satisfy its production burden even when the jury later finds its explanation false. A permissible discrimination inference allows the jury to decide; it neither compels a plaintiff’s verdict nor bars Rule 50 in every case. The parallel with Celotex concerns the judge’s role and sufficiency standard, while the specific allocation of employment-discrimination burdens supplies the substantive setting of this decision. Id. at 142–151.
