# Kanzmeier v. McCoppin
398 N.W.2d 826 (Iowa 1987) | Contracts

## Holding and procedural result
The Iowa Supreme Court upheld a finding that a livestock seller breached an oral cattle-sale contract made through the buyer's agents, but rejected the trial court's calculation of cover damages. It vacated the Court of Appeals decision, affirmed liability, reversed the damages award, and remanded for a new damages determination on the existing record. Schultz wrote the January 14, 1987 decision for the listed panel of McGiverin, Larson, Schultz, Carter, and Neuman; no dissent or concurrence is reported. Neither the trial court's $19,458.07 award nor the buyer's $37,717.20 lost-profit demand became the final judgment on damages. (826, 828, 831–833.)

## Negotiation, agency, and breach
On December 2, 1983, two livestock order buyers approached Charles McCoppin about his cattle. They discussed local sale-barn prices and an offer to sell 360 head for $60 per hundredweight. That evening one order buyer called cattle feeder Bill Kanzmeier, a longstanding client who had previously expressed interest in this kind of cattle. Kanzmeier authorized the purchase and agreed to a fifty-cent-per-hundredweight commission. He proposed December 8 pickup, when space would be available at his operation. The order buyer then accepted McCoppin's offer and arranged delivery. (828.)

McCoppin subsequently learned that some cattle had sold at a local barn for $62 per hundredweight. He asserted that the order buyer had omitted this higher price during their discussion. On December 5 he said the cattle were not ready; the next day he said he did not want to sell. When Kanzmeier visited the farm on December 7, McCoppin had already sold the cattle that morning to a third party for $62. The bench trial found a breached oral contract and awarded Kanzmeier damages. The Court of Appeals reversed on the ground that no contract existed, and the Supreme Court granted further review. (828.)

The Supreme Court rejected an attempt to recast the transaction as one in which Kanzmeier was merely a third-party beneficiary. That theory had not been the trial court's ruling or the parties' trial position. The actual question was agency: whether the order buyers acted for Kanzmeier, with his consent and subject to his control. Consent and control could be proved by conduct and circumstances, and existence of agency was ordinarily factual. The substantial-evidence standard governed review of these findings in an action at law. (829–830.)

Kanzmeier authorized the purchase and directed delivery; the order buyers expected his commission rather than a resale profit on their own cattle. There was also evidence identifying Kanzmeier to McCoppin as the purchaser, despite a factual dispute on that point. These circumstances substantially supported the trial court's agency and contract findings. The court therefore did not require Kanzmeier to establish an independently enforceable third-party-beneficiary promise. (830.)

## Appeal timing and preservation
Kanzmeier argued that McCoppin's appeal was untimely or, alternatively, that its scope should be limited to matters raised in a posttrial motion. The motion was labeled one to reconsider, terminology not itself authorized by the rules. Its substance nevertheless satisfied rule 179(b) governing enlargement or amendment of findings, so the appeal period ran from the ruling on that proper motion. The court urged use of the correct terminology without allowing the caption to defeat the motion's substance. (828–829.)

A proper rule 179(b) motion extended the time to appeal all issues actually resolved in the single final judgment, rather than only the issues mentioned in the motion. The court distinguished a precedent involving separate appealable merits and attorney-fee judgments. It did not excuse failure to obtain a trial ruling on an unresolved issue: a motion remained necessary to preserve a matter the trial court had not decided. Here the appeal was timely and was not confined to its posttrial-motion grounds. (829.)

## Alleged price concealment and materiality
The court assumed without deciding that the order buyer concealed the highest reported sale-barn price. McCoppin characterized the omission as negligent, rather than fraudulent. Under the cited Restatement rule, a misrepresentation could make assent voidable if it was either fraudulent or material, so the absence of an allegation of intentional fraud did not end the inquiry. Materiality meant likely inducement of a reasonable person's assent, or a known likelihood of inducing the particular recipient. (830–831.)

The trial court found that the higher-priced cattle were not comparable to McCoppin's cattle, and substantial evidence supported that finding. Information about their price therefore would not have induced assent to this transaction. The immateriality finding defeated this avoidance theory even on the assumed concealment. The decision does not announce a general permission to conceal market prices or establish that the order buyer actually withheld the information; it rests on the finding that the omitted comparison was not material here. (830–831.)

## Why the replacement cattle were not proper cover
Kanzmeier claimed $37,717.20 in lost profits based on feeding, weight gain, and an anticipated sale. Alternatively, the trial court calculated $19,458.07 from the difference between the contract price and the per-hundredweight prices of 358 replacement cattle, multiplied by the replacements' weights. Those cattle came from four purchases between December 21, 1983, and February 6, 1984. They averaged approximately 135 pounds less per head than the contracted cattle. (831.)

Iowa's UCC cover provision required a good-faith purchase, without unreasonable delay, of reasonable substitutes. Cover did not require identical goods, but the substitutes had to serve the intended commercial use. Reasonableness was ordinarily factual, although a finding still required substantial evidence. If cover failed, the market-price remedy measured the difference between the contract price and market price when the buyer learned of the breach, together with authorized incidental and consequential damages. The objective was the position full performance would have produced. (831–832.)

Kanzmeier bought roughly 2,000 cattle a year, but this bargain served a specific need. He wanted large, already corn-fed steers requiring no feeding break-in period, obtained as one local group to reduce disease risk, and ready for sale in the early-April market. The smaller replacements would reach market condition approximately two months later. He admitted that none was comparable to the contracted cattle. The record supplied no basis to separate a price difference caused by their different weight and commercial characteristics from a difference caused by rising cattle prices. (832–833.)

The court therefore held the purported cover unreasonable as a matter of law on this record. The problem was not a categorical rule that replacement goods must be identical, nor solely the elapsed time between breach and purchase. These particular replacements were not commercially comparable for the contemplated transaction, and the evidence could not sustain the trial court's calculation. (832–833.)

## Lost profits, mitigation, and the limited remand
The trial court's description of lost profits as speculative was an observation, rather than the necessary finding about whether a loss existed. The Supreme Court distinguished uncertainty about the existence of damage from uncertainty about its amount. If the existence of loss was speculative, recovery could be denied. If loss was proved, difficulty fixing its precise amount did not preclude an award supported by a reasonable basis of approximation. (833.)

If the trial court found lost profits, it also had to address mitigation. Under the consequential-damages provision, Kanzmeier bore the burden of showing that the loss could not reasonably have been prevented by cover or otherwise. McCoppin had introduced evidence that comparable cattle could have been purchased promptly in December. That evidence required consideration; the Supreme Court did not itself calculate the avoidable portion or find that all claimed profits were recoverable. (833.)

The remand required a determination of damages on the existing record, not a new trial. The court preserved the contract and breach findings while directing application of the market-price remedy and proper incidental or consequential damages, with findings sufficient to resolve lost profits and mitigation. The exact result was vacation of the Court of Appeals decision, affirmance of liability, and reversal and remand of damages. (833.)
