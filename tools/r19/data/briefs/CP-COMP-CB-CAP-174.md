# Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group, L.P.

**541 U.S. 567 (2004) · Supreme Court of the United States · May 17, 2004**  
**Civil Procedure — time-of-filing diversity and changes in a partnership’s membership**

The withdrawal of Mexican partners before trial changed Atlas’s citizenship but did not cure the absence of diversity when Atlas filed suit. The same partnership remained the plaintiff. Justice Scalia’s five-Justice majority distinguished that change from dismissal of a nondiverse litigant, the recognized cure applied in *Caterpillar* and *Newman-Green*. Justice Ginsburg dissented for four Justices. CivPro assigns the account in PDF page 217, printed page 391, immediately after its *Caterpillar* comparison and before the collateral-attack section.

## A favorable verdict exposed an initial jurisdictional defect

Atlas, a limited partnership organized under Texas law, sued Grupo Dataflux, a Mexican corporation, in federal district court in November 1997. It sought more than $1.3 million for breach of contract and quantum meruit and alleged diversity between a Texas citizen and a Mexican citizen. After nearly three years of pretrial litigation, the parties consented to a jury trial before a magistrate judge. The jury returned a $750,000 verdict for Atlas on October 27, 2000. **568–569.**

Before entry of judgment, Dataflux moved to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. Atlas’s citizenship included that of every partner. At filing it had two Mexican partners, so it shared Mexican citizenship with Dataflux, notwithstanding its additional Texas and Delaware citizenship. The requisite statutory diversity was absent. The magistrate judge dismissed. **569 & n.1.**

Atlas did not contest the initial defect on appeal. Instead, it relied on a September 2000 transaction, completed before trial, in which the Mexican partners left the partnership. The transaction was unrelated to the suit, as Ginsburg’s dissent explained. Her fuller account also identified a withdrawing general partner, a limited liability company with Mexican members, that at least arguably spoiled diversity; the Court had not then decided the LLC-citizenship question. These were internal membership changes, not judicial dismissal of Atlas from the lawsuit. **569–570; dissent 583, 585–586 & n.1.**

The Fifth Circuit relied on *Caterpillar* to create a limited exception: where the defect was not raised until after a verdict or dispositive ruling, and it had disappeared before that resolution, the result should survive. It reversed and directed entry of judgment for Atlas. **570.**

## The majority preserved the time-of-filing rule

Scalia began with the longstanding rule that diversity is determined by the facts at commencement. The rule applies whether the defect is detected early, after trial, or on appeal. Courts had adhered to it even after considerable litigation and expenditures, rather than permitting a continuing party’s changed circumstances to create jurisdiction retrospectively. **570–572 & nn.2–3.**

There was a recognized exception for dropping a dispensable nondiverse **party**. Rule 21 and decisions such as *Newman-Green* allowed that cure, including by appellate courts. *Caterpillar* likewise involved removal followed by dismissal of the nondiverse defendant before trial. The remaining parties then satisfied diversity. Its strong language about finality and economy addressed whether an earlier statutory removal violation required undoing a judgment after jurisdiction had been cured; it did not announce that efficiency itself cured every kind of diversity defect. **572–574 & n.4.**

Here, Atlas remained the same artificial entity and the same plaintiff throughout. Its internal composition changed, and consequently its citizenship changed. That was the condition of a continuing party, not a change in the parties to the action. The majority declined to create an exception treating the former as the latter. **574–575 & n.5.**

## Citizenship attribution did not make partners the original plaintiffs

The dissent argued that because diversity analysis looks through a partnership to its members, withdrawal of the citizenship spoilers resembled elimination of nondiverse litigants. Scalia answered that looking to members determines the citizenship of the entity that is a party; it does not make each member a party. Under *Carden*, Atlas was a single artificial entity whose citizenship included all its partners. No court could dismiss members from the partnership under the procedural power to drop litigants. **577–580 & nn.6–8.**

Footnote 9 supplied a further procedural distinction: the two Mexican individuals were later added personally for Dataflux’s counterclaim under Rule 13(h), which concerned persons not already parties to the original action. Their addition did not turn their earlier membership in Atlas into individual plaintiff status. **580 n.9.**

The majority did not premise its result on resolving the dissent’s constitutional-minimal-diversity theory. It questioned that theory and discussed complications posed by an entity’s multiple citizenships, but the statutory time-of-filing defect remained dispositive regardless. **577–579 & n.6.**

## Why the timing of the objection could not save the action

The Fifth Circuit’s distinction between discovering the same defect before and after a verdict lacked a sound jurisdictional basis. If the membership change cured the defect, timing of discovery would not matter. If it did not, a verdict could not supply jurisdiction. Treating Dataflux’s silence as forfeiture would conflict with the principle that litigation conduct cannot expand subject-matter jurisdiction. **575–576.**

Scalia also rejected a broad efficiency exception. Uncertain exceptions would invite further jurisdictional litigation and arguments about how much work sufficed to justify retention. A fixed rule reduced those disputes. Atlas could file a new action with the changed citizenship; it had actually filed and then voluntarily dismissed a second action. The majority anticipated that prior discovery and the verdict could facilitate settlement or a quicker retrial, but those were practical predictions, not findings of an eventual settlement. **576–577, 580–582.**

## Ginsburg’s dissent

Joined by Justices Stevens, Souter, and Breyer, Ginsburg viewed the time-of-filing rule as a judicial policy rule rather than an immutable constitutional or statutory command. She accepted its protection of jurisdiction already established, but emphasized decisions permitting later corrections of initially defective jurisdiction. In her view, the partnership’s membership change trimmed away the spoilers while leaving an already-present minimally diverse core, making it sufficiently similar to *Caterpillar* and *Newman-Green*. **582–592 & nn.1–8.**

She supported the Fifth Circuit’s limited completed-adjudication exception because it identified cases with indisputable sunk judicial costs. She denied that this relied on forfeiture: the relevant issue was when a court-made rule should permit a recognized cure. She also stressed that Dataflux had admitted jurisdiction in its answer and objected only after losing, while the partnership transaction was unrelated to the litigation and no manipulation was charged. **593–597 & n.9.**

For the dissent, relitigation would likely waste resources despite the majority’s optimism about settlement. Potential state saving statutes made refiling likely available; her discussion did not finally adjudicate which State’s limitations law governed. Unlike the premature removal in *Caterpillar*, Atlas had not filed to beat an expiring time bar. Ginsburg would affirm the Fifth Circuit and preserve the adjudication. **594–599 & nn.10–12.**

## Judgment and limits

The Supreme Court **reversed the Fifth Circuit**, **582**. Scalia was joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices O’Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas; the four dissenters were Ginsburg, Stevens, Souter, and Breyer. **568.** The majority treated dismissal for lack of jurisdiction as the available disposition for this action; it did not determine the substantive contract claims or bar a properly supported new action.
