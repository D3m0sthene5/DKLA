# Hakimoglu v. Trump Taj Mahal Associates
876 F. Supp. 625 (D.N.J. 1994) | Contracts

## Court and distinct procedural stage

Judge Simandle decided this district-court opinion on December 23, 1994. It concerns reconsideration of the dismissal of a gambler’s affirmative tort claims, the casino’s separate motion for partial summary judgment on unpaid credit markers, and certification of the dismissal for immediate appeal. It is not an appellate remand or a final adjudication that the gambler lacked contractual capacity. The complete published opinion incorporates pertinent excerpts from the court’s unpublished March 31, 1994 decision; it does not reproduce that earlier decision in full. There are no separate writings. (627–628, 637–641.)

## Facts, pleadings, and competing positions

Hakimoglu alleged that the defendants enticed him to gamble at the Taj Mahal on numerous occasions in 1992 and 1993, provided free alcohol in his hotel room, and continually supplied complimentary four-to-five-ounce gin martinis while he gambled. According to the complaint, they continued serving him after he became visibly and substantially intoxicated, allowed him to continue gambling, and permitted additional credit markers. He alleged losses exceeding $2 million. The court accepted these allegations for purposes of the motion to dismiss; it did not find that the casino had in fact caused those losses or supplied alcohol in the alleged manner. Although the complaint pleaded negligence, intentional and malicious conduct, and unjust enrichment, Hakimoglu consolidated those theories into a common-law dram-shop theory. Footnotes 2 and 5 specifically delimit the issue: he did not present a general negligence claim, an affirmative contract claim, or a claim for an implied private remedy under casino regulations. (627–629 & n.2; 631 & n.5.)

Trump Taj Mahal Associates counterclaimed for $700,000 advanced through five counterchecks executed during gambling on April 25, 1993. The checks were deposited on May 12 and returned unpaid on May 20 with the designation “account closed.” The casino also sought $17,750 in baccarat commissions, or vigorish, but excluded that amount from its partial-summary-judgment motion. Against the credit claim, Hakimoglu asserted that intoxication deprived him of capacity to enter the credit agreement. That defense concerned enforceability of a contractual debt, rather than a tort right to recover previously lost gambling money. (627–628; 637 & n.16.)

On March 31 the court had held that it possessed subject-matter jurisdiction but predicted that New Jersey would not recognize Hakimoglu’s proposed dram-shop action for gambling losses. Subsequently, the Third Circuit’s September 6 decision in Greate Bay Hotel & Casino v. Tose, 34 F.3d 1227, held that the Casino Control Commission lacked exclusive primary jurisdiction over such disputes. A footnote said that a reasonable argument could be made for a common-law duty to prevent a known intoxicated patron from gambling, while expressly declining to decide whether that cause of action existed. The district court invited supplemental briefing and argument on whether this intervening decision warranted reconsideration. (628, 634–636.)

## Governing distinction: affirmative tort recovery and contractual incapacity

Sitting in diversity, the district court had to predict New Jersey law rather than create its preferred casino policy. It considered state decisions and the Casino Control Act’s extensive regulatory framework. Miller v. Zoby rejected an implied private money-damages remedy for violations of casino credit regulations absent legislative intent. Earlier federal decisions had reasoned more favorably toward gamblers’ proposed claims, but the court distinguished those predictions and identified contrary reservations and subsequent developments. It did not hold that casino regulation automatically displaces every common-law action. (629–634 & nn.3–6.)

New Jersey dram-shop law had protected persons injured by negligent alcohol service, including intoxicated patrons, but the state Supreme Court’s comparative-fault analysis also emphasized the drinker’s responsibility for voluntary consumption. The district court found no state authority extending that doctrine to the financial losses at issue. Gambling losses were materially different from drunken-driving injuries, barroom falls, and fights. Neither the Legislature nor the state Supreme Court had supplied the expansion requested here. (631–634.)

At the same time, a casino seeking to enforce a credit marker remains subject to ordinary contract defenses. Incapacity caused by intoxication, duress, and unconscionability had not been abrogated by the Casino Control Act. If a patron lacked capacity when obtaining credit, the casino could not collect that contractual debt merely because casino gambling was regulated. The court’s discussion of a supposed contract underlying individual gambling games in footnote 7 does not erase this distinction: it questioned mutuality in the regulated game itself and noted that Hakimoglu had not asserted an affirmative contract theory for recouping losses. The counterclaim, by contrast, sought enforcement of signed credit obligations. (633–634 & nn.7–8; 637.)

## Why reconsideration of the tort dismissal failed

Reconsideration required more than disagreement or repetition. Although new facts or persuasive intervening authority could justify revision, Greate Bay had decided jurisdiction, expressly reserving the existence of a substantive cause of action. Judicial power to hear a claim and a legally sufficient claim are separate questions, reflected in the different Rule 12(b)(1) and 12(b)(6) defenses. A jurisdictional ruling therefore did not require recognition of the requested tort remedy. The “reasonable argument” remark justified careful consideration, not a conclusion that the law had changed. (634–636.)

The court gave several reasons for declining to predict expanded liability. A high-stakes patron initially chooses to gamble, unlike someone who does not choose a subsequent automobile accident. Baccarat turns on chance rather than a skill necessarily impaired by drinking. Causation is difficult because sober patrons may lose and intoxicated patrons may win; footnote 14 explains the Third Circuit’s related concern. A claim brought years later also lacks the objective accident and contemporaneous blood-alcohol evidence available in many personal-injury cases. Casino surveillance footage may be recycled when no incident is reported within thirty days. Finally, regulation already deters misconduct, and the inability to enforce credit extended to a person lacking capacity supplies an additional constraint. The court noted regulatory silence concerning compulsory refunds of gambling losses but expressly declined in footnote 15 to assess whether that policy was enlightened. These are the court’s stated reasons, not findings that intoxication can never influence a gambler’s conduct. (636–637 & nn.11–15.)

Reconsideration was denied, leaving the dismissal of the affirmative dram-shop claims in place. The result did not eliminate the separate contractual defense to the casino’s debt claim. (637.)

## Why the casino did not obtain summary judgment on the markers

The casino argued that equitable estoppel barred the intoxication defense. Hakimoglu had consulted counsel about alcohol-related gambling losses and sent a letter threatening litigation against Caesars shortly before returning to the Taj Mahal. The court regarded the theory as potentially significant: someone who knowingly gambles after identifying this supposed basis for litigation might keep winnings but claim legal relief after losing. Equitable estoppel can apply to contracts and is not confined to tort law; footnote 17 distinguishes the tort doctrine of assumption of risk. Nevertheless, estoppel requires justified and reasonable detrimental reliance by an innocent party, not simply substantial reliance. (638–639 & n.17.)

The evidentiary record did not establish those elements as a matter of law. The casino supplied the letter and deposition material but no affidavits or certifications establishing its knowledge of Hakimoglu’s drinking and gambling problems, or sufficient evidence of his practice in obtaining markers and executing counterchecks. Hakimoglu testified that he had warned his casino host about excessive service and intoxication. The court cautioned in footnote 18 that the single supplied deposition page did not identify when this warning occurred, so its relevance remained uncertain. The casino’s knowledge and the transaction’s surrounding circumstances presented genuine factual issues. The court did not decide that estoppel could never succeed or that the claimed warning had been proved. (639 & n.18.)

The casino alternatively contended that gambling enjoyment could not be restored, making the markers binding. Footnote 19 rejected that characterization of consideration. For purposes of the motion, the credit furnished $700,000 worth of chips; because the checks were dishonored, Hakimoglu retained his own $700,000. If the incapacity defense failed, the casino could obtain judgment for that sum. If the defense succeeded, it would be entitled to neither the money nor reimbursement for the already-consumed atmosphere. That conditional analysis did not award either side the money or decide actual capacity. The motion for partial summary judgment was denied without prejudice. (639–640 & n.19.)

## Certification, exact disposition, and capacity standard

The dismissal finally resolved all of Hakimoglu’s affirmative claims, while the counterclaim remained pending. Under Rule 54(b), the court found no just reason to delay entry of a final judgment on those separate claims. The tort claims covered gambling losses on multiple occasions; the contractual counterclaim concerned one credit transaction. Their intoxication standards also differed. Visible and obvious intoxication under the proposed tort theory was not enough by itself to establish contractual incapacity. For the latter defense, the court quoted Seminara’s requirement of such excessive and complete intoxication that the person could not realize and appreciate the nature and consequences of the act. (640.)

The court granted certification, noting the novel recurring state-law issue and absence of cognizable prejudice to the casino. It permitted counterclaim litigation, including possible further summary-judgment practice, to continue. It expressly reserved the timing of trial and whether a brief stay pending appeal should issue. Thus the exact result was denial of reconsideration, denial without prejudice of the casino’s partial-summary-judgment motion, and grant of Rule 54(b) certification of the dismissal. There was no remand, completed jury trial, finding of incapacity, or $700,000 recovery in this opinion. (637, 640–641.)
