# Dave Gustafson & Co. v. State
83 S.D. 160, 156 N.W.2d 185 (1968) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Presiding Judge Hanson wrote for the South Dakota Supreme Court, with all judges concurring. A highway contractor sued the State and its Highway Commission to recover $14,070 withheld for late completion. The sole appellate question was whether the contractual assessment was valid liquidated damages or an unenforceable penalty. The court affirmed the trial court's determination that it was liquidated damages. The preserved report contains no separate opinion or numbered judicial footnote. 83 S.D. at 162, 167.

## Facts and contract
On October 5, 1963, Gustafson agreed to construct subbase, base, and bituminous surfacing for a new highway between Wessington Springs and Woonsocket. The work exceeded $530,000. The new road replaced a portion of State Highway 34, although the old road remained open during construction and afterward. Gustafson completed the project sixty-seven working days late without requesting or receiving an extension. Under the applicable contractual bracket, the State withheld $210 for each day, totaling $14,070. When asked by interrogatory what damage, loss, or expense the delay caused, the State answered that it was unknown. The court specifically distinguished an unknown amount from proof of no damage. Id. at 162.

The agreement declared time important and identified increased engineering, inspection, supervision, and administration costs, together with public inconvenience and deprivation of full use. Section 8.9 authorized deductions for each weather working day after the deadline or approved extension and characterized the amount as agreed compensation rather than a penalty. Section 8.10 graduated daily damages by the project's size. Projects above $500,000 and up to $1 million carried the $210 rate; smaller and larger projects had different rates. The label mattered less than the provision's substance and relation to anticipated harm. Id. at 162–163, 165–167.

## Governing law and issue
South Dakota's statutes voided contractual penalties but permitted agreement on presumed damages where fixing actual damage would be impracticable or extremely difficult. Without a valid stipulation, ordinary actual damages had to be alleged and proved. With valid liquidated damages, the agreed sum replaced the factual damage measure, even if actual loss proved larger or smaller. The court treated actual-loss evidence as immaterial to the amount recoverable under an enforceable clause. Id. at 163–164.

The court compared those statutes with the first Restatement's requirements of a reasonable forecast of compensation and harm incapable or very difficult of accurate estimation. Whether a particular clause met that standard was a legal question. The controlling approach examined, at formation, difficulty of estimating harm, a reasonable effort to fix fair compensation, and a stipulated sum reasonably related to probable damages. Id. at 164–166.

## Arguments and reasoning
The contractor's challenge presented circumstances that could suggest little measurable harm: the public could continue using the old road, and the State did not identify a known actual-loss figure. Nevertheless, the court concluded that the consequences of delayed highway construction included public inconvenience, postponed use, and added administration costs not susceptible to precise measurement. Availability of the older route did not transform delay of the promised new road into a readily calculable loss. Id. at 162, 166–167.

The daily schedule also represented a reasonable effort to approximate compensation. It scaled upward with total work, permitting an inference that a larger undertaking produced greater inconvenience and costs. Each extra day increased the stipulated amount, relating the assessment to the extent of delay. The court found the amount reasonably related to probable loss and not legally disproportionate to the harm that could reasonably have been anticipated. It did not require the State to prove $210 in actual expense on every one of the sixty-seven days. Id. at 167.

The court clarified its earlier Fitzgerald decision, which had found a paving-contract delay was not attributable to the contractor and then also declared a daily-delay forfeiture invalid because municipal damages were readily measurable. Hanson characterized the latter discussion as unnecessary dictum and unrealistic about losses to municipalities, travelers, and adjoining owners. The court expressly overruled that portion to prevent future reliance upon it. It did not overrule Fitzgerald's finding that the contractor had not caused the delay, or all earlier decisions refusing penalties where actual loss was readily ascertainable. Id. at 165–166.

The opinion contrasted disfavor of forfeitures with the usefulness of fair liquidated-damages clauses, especially in government work. Such provisions resolve difficult measurement problems in advance. Their capacity to encourage timely performance did not alone make this graduated compensatory schedule an unenforceable punishment. Id. at 166–167.

## Holding and significance
The $14,070 withholding was enforceable because delay damages were difficult to measure and the graduated per-day amount fairly approximated anticipated harm. The case illustrates a formation-focused evaluation of a stipulated remedy and a distinction between inability to quantify actual damage and proof that a contractual provision is penal. Its treatment of actual-loss evidence should be read in the context of the statutes and legal test the 1968 court applied.
