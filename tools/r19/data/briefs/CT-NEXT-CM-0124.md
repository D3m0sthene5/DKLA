# Stewart v. Newbury
220 N.Y. 379, 115 N.E. 984 (1917) | Decided March 27, 1917 | Contracts

## Building agreement and disputed payment practice
Alexander Stewart, a contractor from Tuxedo, agreed to perform work on a new concrete foundry building for partners trading as Newbury Manufacturing Company in Monroe, New York. His July 18, 1911 letter offered excavation at sixty-five cents per cubic yard, concrete labor and forms at $2.05 per cubic yard, reinforcing labor at $4 per ton, and labor for frames, sash, doors, and hardware for $112. It also offered cost plus ten percent as an alternative. The defendants’ July 22 acceptance referred to the concrete work and asked for an early start. The correspondence specified no time or manner of payment. 220 N.Y. at 380–82.

Stewart testified that between those letters he told Newbury by telephone that he expected the usual payments and received an affirmative reply. According to Stewart, the custom was to pay eighty-five percent every thirty days or at month’s end, withholding fifteen percent until completion. The defendants denied the telephone agreement. Thus a monthly progress-payment arrangement was disputed evidence, not an established contract term. Id. at 382.

Stewart began in July and worked through September 29, reaching the first floor. He billed $896.35, the defendants refused payment, and construction by Stewart stopped. Stewart claimed the owners prevented further work because they asserted departures from the specifications. The owners claimed he voluntarily abandoned after their refusal to pay. Their October 5 letter treated his silence about completing the job as abandonment, contested the bill’s items and amount, and denied that anything was due before completion. Stewart’s next-day response argued that waiting until completion was neither expressed nor reasonably implied; it did not mention the telephone promise on which he later testified. Id. at 382–83.

## Verdict, appeal, and preserved instructional error
Stewart sought the billed amount as agreed compensation and another $95.68 for breach. The jury awarded the bill but none of the additional damages. The Appellate Division affirmed, and the owners appealed by permission on exceptions to the charge. Id. at 380, 383.

The trial judge instructed that, even without an agreement for monthly payments, the defendants owed payment at reasonable periods in light of the work’s character, quantity, and value. A supplemental instruction gave the same right when neither an agreement nor a mutually understood custom existed. The owners requested an instruction that, on that hypothesized absence of payment terms, nothing was payable until completion. The court refused it. The charge therefore allowed the jury to find that a refusal of reasonable interim payment excused abandonment even if it rejected Stewart’s alleged oral agreement and custom. Id. at 383–84.

## Default rule for an entire work contract
The Court of Appeals held that this instruction misstated the law. The work agreement was an entire contract. Where parties contract for work and make no agreement about payment, the contractor must substantially perform before demanding payment. The existence of measurable unit prices did not itself produce a duty to pay installments at judicially chosen reasonable intervals. Id. at 384–85.

The qualification matters: the court was correcting the rule applicable if no payment arrangement existed. It did not find that the telephone agreement necessarily occurred or necessarily failed, and it did not prohibit the parties from bargaining for progress payments or contracting against a shared custom. The rule required substantial performance, not an assertion that every insignificant defect forever bars compensation. On the charge given, however, the jury could award interim compensation and excuse cessation without deciding any valid basis for an interim payment duty.

## Prevention theory and why a new trial was necessary
The alternative theory was that the owners themselves breached by refusing to let Stewart continue because of supposed specification departures. Some evidence supported that account. But a general verdict did not reveal which theory the jury accepted. A potentially sound prevention theory could not make the erroneous payment theory harmless. The bill-only award, without the requested breach damages representing lost profits, suggested the jury might have proceeded on the erroneous premise that nonpayment justified Stewart’s abandonment. Id. at 385.

The appellate court accordingly required another trial rather than deciding that Stewart could never recover. The new trial could address the disputed agreement, the parties’ conduct, and any actual prevention of completion under proper legal instructions. Treating the opinion as a final finding that the owners committed no breach would erase the very alternative the court preserved.

## Judgment and participation
The Court of Appeals reversed and ordered a new trial, with costs to abide the event. Crane wrote; Hiscock, Collin, Cardozo, Pound, and Andrews concurred. Cuddeback was absent. The complete report contains no separate opinion or judicial footnote. Id. at 385.
