# Carr v. Mahaska County Bankers Association

222 Iowa 411, 269 N.W. 494 (Iowa Oct. 27, 1936) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B1-067

The Iowa Supreme Court affirmed a $1,000 reward judgment, with interest and costs, against the sole appealing bank, Taintor Savings Bank. Removing a reward poster without publicizing a withdrawal did not effectively revoke this offer aimed at deterring future bank robberies. The public could reasonably understand the offer as continuing, even without continuous display. Justice Richards wrote, with Parsons, Hamilton, Mitchell, Anderson, Donegan, Albert, and Kintzinger concurring. The complete report contains no dissent or concurrence in a separate writing. (222 Iowa at 411–417.)

## The reward offer and the bank's private acts

The Mahaska County Bankers Association was an unincorporated voluntary association without the legal existence needed to sue or be sued. Its poster offered $1,000 for capture or information leading to the arrest and conviction of robbers or burglars of any member bank in the county. It represented that the displaying bank belonged to the association and subscribed to its constitution and bylaws. It also described local armed vigilance committees and cooperation with public authorities and the State Bankers Association. The record did not establish the poster's exact origin; the court inferred that it had existed for some years before 1930 and had been displayed at various county banks. (Id. at 413.)

Taintor's cashier admitted that the bank joined the association and displayed an identical poster. Taintor conceded that displaying it made the bank an offeror because it represented that a principal made the offer when the association was not a legally suable entity. The case therefore did not rest on proof that an incorporated association remained responsible while the bank was merely its agent. The bank's own representation supplied the liability theory accepted for this appeal. (Id. at 413–414, 416–417.)

The cashier testified that he removed the poster in December 1931 or January 1932 and threw it in the wastebasket. At the same time he refused an association sight-draft for dues, and no further dues were paid. He said he never attended association meetings over ten years. But no bank resolution withdrawing from the association existed, and no directors' or stockholders' minutes recorded such a proposed withdrawal. The bank relied on the removal and cessation of dues to show that its offer no longer existed. (Id. at 414.)

On January 10, 1935, the Farmers Savings Bank in Leighton was robbed. Carr and the other plaintiffs discovered and furnished information leading to the offenders' arrest and conviction. They relied on the asserted reward offer and sued the association and several alleged member banks. After jury waiver, a bench trial produced a $1,000 judgment with interest and costs against three banks. Taintor alone appealed. The appeal consequently concerned Taintor's obligation, not a new appellate adjudication of every defendant's membership or liability. (Id. at 411–412.)

## The bank's own evidence cured its first motion's asserted deficiency

Taintor challenged denial of a motion for directed verdict and dismissal made first at the close of the plaintiffs' evidence and then renewed after all testimony. The motion asserted lack of evidence that the association existed when the robbery occurred, that Taintor was then a member, or that Taintor displayed a reward notice then or at any time. The bank argued that an error at the first stage required reversal even if later evidence established the missing elements. (Id. at 412.)

The court rejected that procedural position. Taintor did not stand on its initial motion; it proceeded to introduce its own evidence. If competent evidence established the plaintiffs' case when the motion was renewed at the end of the trial, a deficiency at the earlier stage did not require sending a now adequately supported case back for retrial. The review therefore addressed the renewed motion on the full record, including the cashier's admissions about posting the notice and joining the association. The plaintiffs had not challenged the use of the directed-verdict motion in this bench trial, so the court did not decide a separate objection to its form. (Id. at 412–413.)

Although the revocation theory was not strictly within the motion's stated grounds, both sides treated it as presented, and the court did likewise. That permitted decision of the actual poster-removal defense without incorrectly characterizing it as an independent pleading rule governing every appeal. (Id. at 414.)

## Like publicity was needed for effective public revocation

A public reward offer ordinarily may be revoked before acceptance by performance. But revocation must be effective against the public to whom the offer was made. The court discussed Shuey's published reward and later published revocation, where the claimant's personal ignorance of the withdrawal did not defeat it. Sullivan supplied the rule that revocation must use the original manner of publication or another method giving publicity comparable to the offer. Thus a public offer need not always be revoked by actual individual notice, but private conduct that conveys nothing publicly is insufficient. (Id. at 414–415.)

The offer's purpose also matters to how the public reasonably understands it. The court distinguished rewards addressing an existing temporary evil or particular past crime from those looking to future events. Its discussion of Smith v. Lancaster City described an arson reward as a temporary response whose purpose could be fulfilled. Its discussion of Sullivan also rejected an offeror's secret restriction when the language and circumstances expressed a broader purpose. These were explanations of the authorities, not holdings that Taintor's offer could never expire. (Id. at 415–416.)

This notice did not seek perpetrators of a crime already committed. It contemplated any future robbery of a county bank. Deterring that future conduct was its paramount incentive. The trial court could therefore find that the public reasonably assumed continuity from the offer's nature; continuity did not additionally depend on the poster remaining constantly visible. The absence of uninterrupted posting was the only outward circumstance on which Taintor could rely. The public could not be charged with knowledge that the cashier discarded the paper or refused a dues draft. Those private acts supplied no communicated withdrawal. (Id. at 416.)

The record accordingly supported a finding that Taintor did not revoke in the manner in which the offer was made or by another method providing like publicity. The court suggested posting a revocation notice in the bank as a possible method, without holding that this was the only permissible means or deciding the sufficiency of a notice actually posted. It affirmed on the facts presented: removal alone had not negated the public's justified understanding that the offer remained in force when the robbery occurred. (Id. at 416.)

## What the affirmance did and did not decide

Because liability arose from Taintor's representation of a principal lacking legal entity status, the alleged absence of the association or termination of membership at the robbery date did not defeat this claim. The association's constitution, bylaws, and agreements with Taintor were not in evidence, and no liability based on a separate specific undertaking was before the court. It also found no need to decide whether removal was personally the cashier's act or properly the bank's act: even the asserted removal did not amount to an effective public revocation. (Id. at 416–417.)

Most significantly for the casebook's offer-termination discussion, neither at trial nor on appeal did the bank claim that a reasonable acceptance time had expired. The affirmance therefore does not declare that prospective bank rewards remain open perpetually or resolve a properly presented reasonable-time defense. It sustains the judgment on the challenged evidentiary and revocation grounds. The precise disposition was affirmance, with no remand or new trial. (Id. at 417.)
