# Rosewood Care Center, Inc. v. Caterpillar, Inc.
226 Ill. 2d 559, 877 N.E.2d 1091 (2007) | Contracts

## Court and disposition
Justice Burke delivered the Illinois Supreme Court’s opinion, joined by Chief Justice Thomas and Justices Freeman, Fitzgerald, Kilbride, Garman, and Karmeier. The court unanimously affirmed the appellate judgment reversing dismissal of Rosewood’s complaint, but rejected the appellate court’s rationale. Illinois has no rule that a debt must already exist when a guaranty is made before the suretyship provision of the statute of frauds can apply. Nevertheless, factfinding was required to determine whether Caterpillar’s alleged promise was a suretyship and, if so, whether its main purpose was Caterpillar’s own economic advantage. The case returned to the circuit court without an award of the nursing charges or a final finding of Caterpillar’s liability. (562, 572, 575–577.)

## Allegations and medical-care arrangement
The court recounted facts from Rosewood’s complaint and exhibits, not findings after a completed trial. Betty Jo Cook, Caterpillar’s employee, suffered a workplace injury on October 21, 2001, remained hospitalized until January 30, 2002, and filed a workers’ compensation claim. On January 3 Caterpillar asked Rosewood’s management agent to admit her at a fixed managed-care rate; Rosewood declined. On January 10 Dr. Norma Just, the Caterpillar employee responsible for workers’ compensation medical care, allegedly told the agent that Caterpillar would cover 100 percent of Cook’s skilled nursing care, pay Rosewood directly, and impose neither a deductible nor a maximum limit. She asked that bills go to its workers’ compensation division and said Cook was precertified for four weeks. (563.)

Rosewood’s agent faxed a confirmation of admission, four weeks’ treatment, and the need to evaluate further care. Just signed and returned it. Rosewood did not argue that this document itself satisfied the statute’s writing requirement—a qualification important to any suggestion that the dispute was simply about an entirely undocumented relationship. Additional calls authorized transfer; a January 30 call changed the initial precertification to two weeks. On admission Cook assigned insurance benefits and acknowledged responsibility for unpaid services. Caterpillar’s health-care manager subsequently authorized care on eight dates through June 4. She remained at Rosewood until June 13, generating charges of $181,857. Monthly bills went to Caterpillar without objection to billing or notice that treatment was unauthorized, but it ultimately refused payment. (563–564 & n.1.)

Rosewood alleged that Caterpillar previously arranged and paid for care of other injured employees and that it admitted Cook only because of Caterpillar’s promise. Those allegations could support its theories but were not conclusive determinations of the promise’s legal character or business purpose. Cook’s acknowledgment of unpaid charges also made the potential existence of an obligation of another a live question. (564–565, 575–577.)

## Proceedings and scope of the appeal
The amended complaint asserted contract, promissory-estoppel, and quantum-meruit claims against Caterpillar, as well as claims against Cook. Caterpillar moved under section 2–619(a)(7) to dismiss the contract and estoppel counts because the alleged undertaking lacked the writing required by the Frauds Act; it separately sought to strike quantum meruit. The circuit court granted those requests and made its orders immediately appealable under Rule 304(a). The appellate court reversed, reading Williams v. Corbet and Hartley Bros. v. Varner to exclude a promise made before the underlying debt arose. Justice Lytton agreed that precedent constrained the intermediate court while specially concurring to criticize the supposed requirement. The Supreme Court accepted Caterpillar’s petition and affirmed the result on other grounds. (565–566.)

Footnote 2 expressly limits the equitable-claim discussion. The appellate court said that an applicable statute of frauds would bar the estoppel and quantum-meruit claims as well, but allowed them to proceed because it thought the statute inapplicable. Rosewood did not challenge that broader proposition in the Supreme Court. The opinion should therefore not be presented as a separately contested Supreme Court adjudication that equitable theories can never avoid the statute under any circumstances. (566 & n.2.)

## No preexisting-debt requirement
The suretyship provision of 740 ILCS 80/1 requires a signed writing for a special promise to answer for another’s debt, default, or miscarriage. Its text does not qualify “debt” by requiring that it exist before the promise. Reading such a temporal condition into the enactment would conflict with its ordinary language. The policy of requiring more secure evidence of another person’s undertaking likewise applies to promises of future as well as existing debt. Indeed, future debt can expose a promisor to an amount not yet known. Antifraud concerns do not disappear because the promise precedes the underlying transaction. (567–569.)

The older Illinois cases did not compel a different result. Williams considered to whom credit was extended and whether the undertaking was actually a suretyship; it deferred to the jury’s resolution of conflicting testimony. Its reference to a later promise did not establish timing as the sole test. Hartley Bros. simply relied on Williams without analyzing when the debt arose. Neither established a categorical preexisting-debt rule, so the Supreme Court did not face a need to overrule those holdings in order to reject the appellate reading. The operative conclusion was that Illinois recognizes no such rule. (570–572.)

## Main-purpose exception
Even a promise that is a suretyship can fall outside the writing requirement if its main purpose or leading object is to promote the promisor’s own pecuniary or business interests. The court explained the rule through Restatement provisions and Illinois decisions recognizing it since 1873. Where the desired consideration chiefly serves the secondary obligor’s economic benefit, the transaction lacks much of the gratuitous character that makes an ordinary guaranty vulnerable to fabricated or enlarged claims, and the commercial setting can itself furnish evidence of the undertaking. This is different from concluding that no suretyship exists at all. (572–574.)

Caterpillar denied that it promised primarily for its own advantage or received a benefit; alternatively it requested further proceedings. The impetus for the promise was generally a fact question. Potential considerations include the principal obligor’s default or inability to pay, a creditor’s forbearance from a lien in property the promisor uses, the relationship between benefit and promised amount, the principal obligor’s absence from the negotiations, and a broader transaction of which the undertaking is incidental. These are relevant indicators, not a mechanical checklist or findings that all were present here. The allegations alone did not permit a definitive determination of Caterpillar’s main purpose; evidence and factfinding were necessary. That unresolved issue prevented dismissal. (574–576.)

## Original undertaking or suretyship
Rosewood separately argued that Caterpillar contracted directly for its own commitment rather than guaranteed Cook’s obligation. A suretyship entails undertaking another person’s obligation to the obligee; application of the statute also involves the promisee’s knowledge or reason to know of that relationship. The court discussed simultaneous promises and consideration benefiting one or both promisors, rather than making liability depend solely on whether Caterpillar used words of guarantee. Contract existence, terms, conditions, and intent required factual determination. As with main purpose, the complaint did not conclusively establish whether the undertaking was an original obligation or a suretyship. This issue also remained for the circuit court. (576–577.)

## Assigned problem and limits
The casebook asks how to characterize the promise and what evidence would illuminate it. Its suggested statements about not troubling Cook or protecting Rosewood against her nonpayment are questions for analysis, not quoted findings or established testimony from the opinion. The actual court left both the suretyship characterization and main purpose unresolved. It affirmed the reversal of dismissal and remanded, without finding that Caterpillar necessarily owed $181,857.
