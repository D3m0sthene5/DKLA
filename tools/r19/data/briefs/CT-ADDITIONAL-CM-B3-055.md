# Commune de Morsang-sur-Orge
Conseil d’État, Assemblée, 27 October 1995, No. 136727, published in Recueil Lebon | Contracts

## Decision and assigned context
The Conseil d’État, sitting in its Assemblée formation, held that respect for human dignity is part of public order and can justify a municipal prohibition of an attraction even without particular local circumstances. It annulled the Versailles administrative tribunal’s judgment against the commune and rejected the underlying challenges to the mayor’s prohibition. The official reproduction identifies M. Denoix de Saint Marc as president, Mlle Laigneau as rapporteur, and M. Frydman as commissaire du gouvernement. It publishes no numerical voting division or separate opinion. Frydman’s conclusions are identified as having been heard, but their complete text is not reproduced as part of this decision. (Official text, heading; reasoning beginning “Considérant qu’il appartient”; arts. 1–6.)

The assigned account on printed page 803 places this decision beside the later United Nations Human Rights Committee decision in Manuel Wackenheim v. France, Communication No. 854/1999 (2002). They are separate adjudicative stages. This brief concerns the French administrative appeal, No. 136727, not the Committee’s later ruling or the same-day Conseil d’État decision concerning Aix-en-Provence, No. 143578. The official judgment anonymizes the individual as M. X.; the assigned account identifies him as Wackenheim.

## Facts and procedural history
The contested municipal order was issued on October 25, 1991, and prohibited a dwarf-tossing show scheduled that same day at the Embassy Club nightclub in Morsang-sur-Orge. The attraction involved spectators throwing a person with a physical disability who was presented in that capacity. Protective measures had been taken, and the individual participated freely for payment. The Conseil d’État did not resolve the case by finding that his consent was coerced or that the precautions were physically inadequate. Its concern was the character of the attraction itself. (Official text, opening statement of the request and reasoning beginning “Considérant que l’attraction”.)

Fun Production and the individual challenged the prohibition in the Versailles administrative tribunal. On February 25, 1992, that tribunal annulled the mayor’s order and required the commune to pay the applicants 10,000 francs in compensation. It reasoned that, even assuming the show infringed human dignity, a prohibition could not lawfully issue without particular local circumstances. The commune’s appeal, registered on April 24, 1992, asked the Conseil d’État to annul that judgment and award it 10,000 francs for litigation expenses under Article 75-I of the July 10, 1991 law. The company and individual also pursued an incidental appeal seeking increased compensation and made other requests concerning litigation expenses and allegedly abusive appeal. (Official text, opening request; reasoning beginning “Considérant que, pour annuler”; compensation and expenses sections.)

The assigned narrative says that the mayor banned the show in 1995. The official judgment establishes the more precise sequence: the municipal prohibition was in 1991, the first-instance judgment and appeal filing were in 1992, and the Conseil d’État’s decision was delivered in 1995. The brief retains those dates rather than treating the year of appellate adjudication as the date of the mayor’s act.

## Issues and legal authority
The central question was whether the mayor’s general municipal police power under Article L. 131-2 of the Code des communes authorized this prohibition on dignity grounds without particular local circumstances. The provision charged municipal police with protecting public order, safety, security, and public health. The appeal also required the court to address the freedoms of work, commerce, and industry, and objections concerning the prohibition’s asserted alternative legal bases. (Official text, reasoning beginning “Considérant qu’aux termes” through “Considérant que le maire”.)

The court treated human dignity as a component of public order. The official holding therefore concerns public-law authority to prohibit an activity, rather than a damages action between contracting parties or a general adjudication that every voluntarily accepted activity of this kind creates an unenforceable private contract. Its Contracts relevance is the assigned comparison between individual consent and a public-policy limit that the individual cannot overcome simply by volunteering to participate.

## Reasoning
The Conseil d’État first stated that a municipal police authority may take measures to prevent interference with public order and that respect for human dignity falls within that category. An attraction that infringes dignity can be prohibited even where the municipality has not established distinctive local circumstances. That rule directly rejected the legal premise of the tribunal’s decision. (Official text, reasoning beginning “Considérant qu’il appartient” and “Considérant que, pour annuler”.)

The court then applied that principle to the show’s nature. Treating a person with a physical disability as a projectile, while presenting him precisely in that capacity, infringed human dignity by the attraction’s very object. Safety precautions and the participant’s voluntary, remunerated participation did not eliminate that infringement. Thus, the judgment identifies a substantive public-order limit despite consent; it does not rest on incapacity to consent, a finding of physical injury, or an inference that all compensated performances necessarily undermine dignity. (Official text, reasoning beginning “Considérant que l’attraction”.)

Having corrected the tribunal’s ground, the Conseil d’État examined the other arguments advanced by the company and individual through the devolutive effect of the appeal. Freedom of work and freedom of commerce and industry did not preclude a municipal authority from prohibiting even an otherwise lawful activity where prohibition was the only measure capable of preventing or ending the interference with public order. In this case, the attraction’s nature met that standard. The court therefore did not make illegality under some separate criminal rule a prerequisite to municipal intervention. (Official text, reasoning beginning “Considérant qu’il appartient au Conseil d’Etat saisi” and “Considérant que le respect du principe”.)

The mayor’s reliance on Article L. 131-2 independently justified the ban. Consequently, the objection that the decision could not find a legal basis in Article 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights or a November 27, 1991 ministerial circular was ineffective. The court did not need to establish either of those alternative authorities as the operative source of municipal power. Its final merits conclusion followed: the tribunal had wrongly annulled the order and awarded compensation, and the applicants’ request for increased compensation also had to fail. (Official text, reasoning beginning “Considérant que le maire” and “Considérant qu’il résulte”.)

## Disposition and costs
Article 1 annulled the Versailles tribunal’s February 25, 1992 judgment. Article 2 rejected the company’s and individual’s first-instance requests. Article 3 rejected their incidental appeal. Article 4 required Fun Production to pay the commune 10,000 francs under the litigation-expense provision. Article 5 rejected the company’s and individual’s own Article 75-I requests, and Article 6 provided for notification of the decision. In its reasoning, the court deemed the request to fine the commune for an abusive appeal inadmissible. It declined an expense award against the individual, while ordering the award against the company. This is not a new 10,000-franc damages award for the show’s prohibition; it is the specified award of litigation expenses to the successful commune. (Official text, abusive-appeal and Article 75-I sections; arts. 1–6.)

## Contracts significance and limits
The assigned comparison asks whether dignity can restrict a competent person’s freedom to contract. This judgment supplies a concrete example: voluntary participation, remuneration, and physical precautions did not defeat a prohibition grounded in human dignity as public order. The operative legal mechanism, however, was the mayor’s municipal police authority and the administrative appeal’s rejection of the challenge. The later Human Rights Committee proceeding involved a different forum and treaty framework and should be read and briefed as its own stage.
