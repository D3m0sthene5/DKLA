# Ex parte Albert Levitt
302 U.S. 633 (1937) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Motion, asserted defects, and exact action

Levitt, appearing pro se, moved for leave to file an original petition requiring Justice Hugo Black to show cause why he should be permitted to serve as an Associate Justice. Levitt asserted that the President’s appointment and the Senate’s confirmation were void because Black was ineligible under Article I, § 6, cl. 2, and because there was no vacancy to which he could lawfully be appointed. These are the grounds described in the Court’s order; the order does not recount the factual basis of the emoluments objection or adjudicate whether either ground was correct. (633–634.)

On October 11, 1937, the Court’s unsigned per curiam order **denied the motion for leave to file**. It did not remove Black, issue the requested show-cause order, or uphold the appointment on the constitutional merits. No dissent or separate opinion is recorded. The volume’s participation note states that Black took no part in consideration or decision of orders announced October 11 or earlier; it also states that Cardozo was absent due to illness during this period except for the December 10 session. The source therefore should not be described as a nine-Justice merits judgment. (633 participation note; 634.)

## Reason for denying access to judicial adjudication

The motion papers disclosed no interest other than Levitt’s position as a citizen and a member of the Court’s bar. The Court held that insufficient. An individual invoking judicial power to determine the validity of legislative or executive action must show a direct injury already sustained or immediately threatened by the action. A general interest common to the public does not satisfy that requirement. The order cites Tyler, Southern Railway, Newman, Fairchild, and Mellon in support. It gives no additional substantive constitutional analysis. (634.)

The decision thus separates an allegation of structural constitutional invalidity from the moving party’s entitlement to have it adjudicated. The seriousness or specificity of an asserted constitutional prohibition did not itself provide Levitt a personal injury. The narrow order does not announce that an appointment objection can never be heard from a properly injured litigant or that every shared injury is necessarily inadequate.
