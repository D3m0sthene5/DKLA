# Albre Marble and Tile Company, Inc. v. John Bowen Co., Inc.
338 Mass. 394, 155 N.E.2d 437 (1959) | Contracts

## Court, four counts, and divided disposition
The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court decided this case January 15, 1959, after November 6, 1958 argument. Justice Spalding wrote for the four listed justices, Spalding, Williams, Counihan, and Whittemore; there was no separate writing. It overruled the subcontractor's exceptions concerning its two contract-breach counts, sustained the exceptions concerning its two quantum-meruit counts, and remanded those latter counts for further proceedings. It dismissed the separately taken appeal because all issues were available through the exceptions. No damages amount was fixed. (338 Mass. at 394–396, 401.)

The case followed the general contractor's successful motion for immediate judgment under the then-existing Massachusetts summary-judgment statute. The court distinguished deficient evidentiary opposition to an impossibility defense from a legally viable claim for fair value of specifically requested preparatory work. The result was therefore neither total victory for the subcontractor nor rejection of all its expenditures. (Id. at 395–401.)

## Invalid public contract and the subcontract claims
Albre agreed to provide tile and marble labor and materials to Bowen, general contractor for a public Chronic Disease Hospital and Nurses' Home in Boston. Counts 1 and 2 alleged breach of those separate subcontracts; counts 3 and 4 sought the value of work and labor supplied at Bowen's request. The general contract with the Commonwealth had been declared invalid in earlier litigation, making continued subcontract performance impossible. Bowen's answer invoked that impossibility against the breach claims. Against quantum meruit it asserted absence of an architect's certificate and that it need not pay before receiving payment from the Commonwealth, which it had not received apart from pre-invalidation payments. (Id. at 395–396 & n. 1.)

Bowen supported its motion with an affidavit amplifying impossibility. Albre's vice-president countered on information and belief that Bowen deliberately omitted subcontractor performance-bond costs from its bid, procured the general contract by fraud, and fraudulently induced the subcontracts with knowledge of its defects. Those allegations sought to attribute impossibility to Bowen's wrongful conduct. They were not treated as proven fraud. (Id. at 396.)

## Why the contract-breach counts failed at summary judgment
The statute required affidavits based on personal knowledge of admissible facts, with affirmative indication of competence to testify. Information-and-belief allegations did not meet that requirement. A separate statutory route allowed an affidavit specifically and clearly identifying reasonable grounds to expect contradiction at trial when proof could not be furnished through affidavits. Albre did not satisfy that route either. (Id. at 396–397.)

Although the contractor's knowledge and intent were largely within its control, Albre showed no attempt to obtain interrogatory answers or admissions, and supplied only its own officer's affidavit. Vague general assertions of expected proof would undermine the statute's purpose of avoiding trials where no genuine factual issue existed. The judgment against the two breach counts was consequently proper. This procedural holding did not find fraud established, nor did it make every defective public bid a basis for subcontractor breach damages. (Id. at 397.)

## Fair value of requested preparations could still be recovered
Albre had supplied samples, shop drawings, tests, and affidavits under clauses in both contracts requiring those submissions for approval as ordered or specified. No tile or marble had yet been installed, and Albre conceded that no labor or materials were wrought into the structure. Bowen argued that Young v. Chicopee limited recovery after impossibility to installed materials or work, excluding preparation. Albre responded that Bowen's express request and supervision distinguished its work from discretionary preparations. A prior related case had reserved this quantum-meruit issue because no such count had been pleaded there. (Id. at 397–398.)

The court recognized the difficulty of allocating losses after a supervening event. The conventional tendency permitted fair value of actual performance and denied merely preparatory or reliance expenditures, often expressed through benefit or wrought-in tests. Yet a literal surviving-benefit requirement was unworkable: receiving part of what one agreed to purchase could suffice even where later destruction eliminated its lasting utility. Scholarly criticism and English legislation illustrated possible broader approaches, but the court did not adopt a rule compensating every preparatory expenditure whenever faultless impossibility intervened. (Id. at 399–400.)

Its narrower recovery rested on a combination of circumstances. First, Bowen's involvement in causing the invalidity was greater than the subcontractors', as described in earlier decisions. The court distinguished that contribution from culpability sufficient for breach liability. Second, Bowen drafted the clause specifically requesting the submissions; Albre did not control all preparations on its own. Third, the requested drawings, tests, and affidavits could not by their nature be wrought into a building. The house-moving recovery in Angus v. Scully supplied a closer analogy than Young's denied recovery for stored materials lost in a fire not attributable to either party. These considerations justified shifting this bounded loss to Bowen. (Id. at 400–401.)

## Limits of remand and unresolved amounts
Recovery was limited to the fair value of acts conforming to Bowen's specific contractual request after the contracts were executed. Precontract expenditures, such as bid preparation, were excluded. Bowen's contention that payment depended on receipt of Commonwealth funds had already been rejected in related precedent and furnished no defense here. (Id. at 401.)

The court sustained the exceptions only for counts 3 and 4 and remanded those counts for proceedings consistent with these limits. Counts 1 and 2 remained unsuccessful, the separate appeal was dismissed, and no fixed sum or wholesale reimbursement of all reliance costs was ordered. (Id.)
