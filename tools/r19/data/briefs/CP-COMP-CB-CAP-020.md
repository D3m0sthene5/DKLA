# Milliken v. Meyer

**Civil Procedure · CP-COMP-CB-CAP-020**  
**311 U.S. 457 (1940)** · December 23, 1940 · No. 66  
**Douglas, J., delivering the Court's opinion. The official caption is Milliken et al. v. Meyer, Administratrix; no separate writing or numerical vote is reported.**  
**Assigned:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 73, printed pages 88–89, Note 5 concerning domicile and service on an absent resident.

## The controversy behind the jurisdiction question

The dispute concerned a share of profits from Colorado oil properties. Transcontinental Oil Company had contracted in 1922 to pay Meyer four sixty-fourths of the profits. Milliken claimed an interest in that share. A 1924 settlement gave Milliken two sixty-fourths and included an assignment to Transcontinental of his claims against Meyer concerning the property and profits. Milliken subsequently sued in Wyoming, alleging a joint venture and conspiracy to defraud him, seeking cancellation of the settlement and an accounting. The judgment ultimately disputed was a personal judgment for withheld profits, rather than a Wyoming adjudication transferring title to Colorado land. 311 U.S. at 458–460 & nn. 1–2.

Meyer was alleged to be a Wyoming resident but was personally served in Colorado. Wyoming's statutes allowed publication in specified situations involving an absent or concealed resident avoiding creditors or service, and allowed personal service outside Wyoming in cases in which publication was authorized. Meyer did not appear in the Wyoming action, although a deposition concerning his Wyoming legal residence was taken. Transcontinental appeared and answered. Id. at 459 & nn. 3–4.

The Wyoming court found the 1924 contracts valid and dismissed the action against Transcontinental with prejudice. It nevertheless found a joint venture between Milliken and Meyer, concluded that Meyer had withheld one sixty-fourth of the profits due Milliken, and entered a personal judgment for those profits and interest. It also prevented Transcontinental from paying, and Meyer from receiving, more than three sixty-fourths. After the July 11, 1931 judgment, the disputed share was paid to Milliken's assignee rather than Meyer. Id. at 460 & n. 5.

## The Colorado collateral challenge

In 1935, the respondent's side brought the Colorado action seeking recovery of the sums paid away under the Wyoming judgment, an injunction against its enforcement, and a declaration that it was void. The challenge alleged that Meyer had ceased residing in Wyoming before service, that Wyoming lacked jurisdiction over him or his property, and that its judgment violated due process. Milliken asserted continuing Wyoming residence and the judgment's entitlement to full faith and credit. The Supreme Court's case caption identifies the respondent as Meyer's administratrix; the textbook uses the shorter Milliken v. Meyer title. Id. at 457, 460–461 & n. 6.

The Colorado trial court found that Meyer was domiciled in Wyoming when the Wyoming suit began. It also found the substituted-service statute constitutional, the supporting affidavit truthful and substantially compliant, and the judgment within Wyoming's jurisdiction. It dismissed the challenge. The Colorado Supreme Court reversed on a different ground, without deciding the jurisdictional questions: the judgment supposedly contained an irreconcilable inconsistency. In that court's view, a valid assignment of Milliken's claims against Meyer left no claim on which the Wyoming court could award relief against Meyer. Id. at 461–462 & nn. 7–8.

Thus, the case involved two distinct inquiries. Colorado could examine whether Wyoming had authority to issue the judgment. It could not use a disagreement about the logic of Wyoming's merits decision as an equivalent of absent jurisdiction. Milliken sought Supreme Court review of the refusal to recognize the judgment; the Court granted certiorari because the full-faith-and-credit question was substantial. Id. at 458, 461–462.

## Holding: domicile plus an adequate statutory method of notice

The Supreme Court held that Wyoming could render a personal judgment against its domiciliary despite his physical absence, using an appropriate statutory method of service. Domicile supplied the adjudicatory relationship; reasonable notice and an opportunity to be heard supplied the procedural protection. Here, service was more than a hope that published notice would reach Meyer: he actually received personal service in Colorado pursuant to Wyoming's statutory arrangement. The Court found that arrangement adequate under due process. Id. at 462–464.

The Court's explanation tied the duties of citizenship to its benefits. A state protects its domiciliary and permits him to invoke its laws; it may correspondingly require him to answer proceedings even during an absence. Mere travel or residence elsewhere for a time does not by itself dissolve domicile or that relationship. The Court compared this continuing state authority with the national government's authority over absent citizens in Blackmer. The analogy explained continuing allegiance, while the adequacy of notice remained a separate requirement. Id. at 463–464.

The holding is not that receipt of a summons alone makes a state competent to bind any nonresident. Wyoming's basis was Meyer's domicile, established by findings that the Colorado Supreme Court had not impaired. Nor does the decision approve every publication scheme: substituted service must be reasonably calculated to provide actual notice and an opportunity to be heard. The Court evaluated both the source of authority and the method used to notify the person subject to it. Id. at 462–464.

## Holding: jurisdiction review does not permit merits review

A judgment issued in one state remains open to a challenge in another for want of personal or subject-matter jurisdiction. When it appears to be the judgment of a court of general jurisdiction, however, those jurisdictional elements are presumed unless the record or outside evidence disproves them. Once jurisdiction exists, full faith and credit prevents the recognizing court from reopening the merits, the consistency of the reasoning, or the correctness of the legal principles used. Legal error within a competent court's judgment does not make it void for collateral purposes. Id. at 462.

The Colorado Supreme Court's asserted contradiction therefore could not justify refusing recognition. Even assuming a mistake in simultaneously upholding the assignment and awarding relief against Meyer, that was a merits objection. Wyoming possessed jurisdiction on the surviving domicile and service findings, so Colorado could not declare the judgment void because it found the reasoning inconsistent. The Supreme Court did not independently decide that Wyoming's accounting or contract analysis was substantively correct; it explained why that correctness was outside this collateral inquiry. Id.

## Exact judgment and assigned significance

The Supreme Court **reversed the Colorado Supreme Court's judgment**. Its mandate is the single word “Reversed” on page 464; the official report supplies Douglas's Court opinion without a separate concurrence, dissent, or numerical vote tally. The reversal restored the full-faith-and-credit principle applicable to the competent Wyoming judgment, rather than awarding a new damages amount in the Supreme Court.

The assignment concentrates on the passage explaining domicile and reciprocal duties. The complete opinion adds the important recognition distinction: a state may test jurisdiction, but it may not treat perceived inconsistency in another competent state's judgment as permission to retry the merits.
