# California Packing Corp. v. Kelly Storage & Distributing Co.

**228 N.Y. 49, 126 N.E. 269 (1920) — January 27, 1920 — Civil Procedure**

Judge Pound wrote the New York Court of Appeals opinion. Chief Judge Hiscock and Judges Chase, Collin, Cardozo, Crane, and Andrews concurred, producing a unanimous seven-judge decision. The appeal concerned the sufficiency of pleading and attachment papers, rather than a trial determination of the underlying debt.

## Facts, parties, and procedure

California Packing alleged that it drew a draft upon Kelly Storage and Distributing Company and that Kelly accepted it for valuable consideration. The draft was not alleged to be negotiable. California Packing relied on the complaint and accompanying affidavit to obtain a warrant of attachment in its contract action. The reported opinion supplies no transaction amount or detailed description of the actual consideration; those facts should not be invented. (52–53.)

Phoenix and Third National Bank of Lexington, Kentucky, a third party to the underlying dispute, moved under Code of Civil Procedure § 682 to vacate the attachment. Special Term denied the motion. The First Department reversed and vacated the attachment. California Packing appealed by permission on three certified questions: whether the bank could make the motion; whether the consideration allegation stated fact or a legal conclusion; and whether the papers sufficiently showed a cause of action. (50, 52.)

California Packing disputed the bank’s standing and defended the allegation as a factual statement supporting the attachment. The bank maintained that it could challenge the attachment and that the supporting papers lacked adequate proof of the elements of a cause of action. Those positions appear in the report’s counsel submissions; the court’s actual answers, particularly on standing, must be distinguished from California Packing’s arguments. (50–52.)

## Issue and holding

Was alleging acceptance for valuable consideration enough under the Code’s requirement of a plain, concise statement of facts, or did the plaintiff have to plead the particular consideration? The court held the allegation sufficient. It stated an ultimate, principal, issuable fact, allowing proof of the facts comprising the actual consideration. The complaint and affidavit consequently supported issuance of the attachment. (52–54.)

## Reasoning

The First Department had repeatedly treated such an allegation as a mere legal conclusion, while the Third Department regarded a more detailed statement as unnecessary. Pound adopted the latter approach. Section 519 required liberal construction of pleadings toward substantial justice. Established common-law forms had permitted pleading according to legal effect, and precedent accepted value received as an adequate averment on a nonnegotiable note. A pleader could safely rely on a form stabilized by authority rather than risk error by devising a more elaborate account. (52–53.)

Negotiable instruments supplied a useful comparison, not a factual finding that this draft was negotiable. Statute presumed consideration for a negotiable instrument, allowing pleading by copy without a separate consideration averment. Comparable language in an action on a nonnegotiable draft sufficiently alleged the corresponding ultimate fact. Valuable consideration conveyed an exchange for something of value; the allegation was not defective merely because it summarized the underlying transaction. (53.)

The complaint and affidavit showed a contract cause of action on which attachment could rest and liquidated damages. The immediate question was whether the papers conferred jurisdiction to issue the warrant. The court noted that even unliquidated-damages attachment cases had sometimes sustained fairly general papers against another attaching creditor’s challenge where the allegations were not vague and inconclusive. It did not hold that evidence was unnecessary or that every generalized allegation would suffice. Requiring meticulous recital of facts ultimately proved at trial would obscure the real issue without furnishing a useful safeguard against oppression. (53–54.)

## Exact disposition and assigned use

The Court of Appeals reversed the Appellate Division’s order, awarded costs in that court and the Court of Appeals, and affirmed Special Term’s order. It answered both the first and third questions yes: the bank could make the motion, but the attachment papers were sufficient. It answered the second by declaring the allegation factual rather than a legal conclusion. Thus California Packing prevailed on the attachment despite losing its contention that the bank lacked standing. (54.)
