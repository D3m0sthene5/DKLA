Pennoyer v. Neff
Citation
Pennoyer v. Neff, 95 U.S. (5 Otto) 714, 24 L. Ed. 565. Supreme Court of the United States. Justia identifies
the report as 1878; the casebook caption identifies 1877. The opinion text supplied does not state exact
argument or decision dates, so none is inferred here. Justice Field delivered the Court’s opinion; Justice
Hunt dissented. Casebook pages 79-86; the casebook omits Hunt’s dissent. Full judicial opinion.
Parties
Neff claimed ownership and possession of Oregon land under a federal land patent, the government’s
instrument conveying title. He was the plaintiff in this federal action. Pennoyer claimed title through a
sheriff’s sale enforcing an earlier state-court judgment against Neff and sought reversal of the federal
judgment favoring Neff. J. H. Mitchell, the lawyer who obtained the earlier fee judgment, was the plaintiff in
that earlier action, not the opposing party in the Supreme Court case. 95 U.S. at 719-20.
Material Facts
Mitchell sued Neff in an Oregon state circuit court for attorney services. Neff was not an Oregon resident
when the action began or when judgment was entered. He was not personally served and did not appear. The
court entered a default judgment, meaning judgment following his failure to respond, for less than $300
including costs in February 1866. The attempted service was constructive service by newspaper publication
rather than delivery of process to Neff. 95 U.S. at 719-20.
Oregon law allowed publication against an absent nonresident having property in the state and separately
allowed attachment in money actions. Attachment brings identified property under the court’s control before
judgment. The land at issue was not attached or otherwise brought before the court during Mitchell’s suit. It
first became connected with the litigation when the sheriff levied execution after judgment, meaning seized
property to enforce an already-entered judgment. Pennoyer’s asserted title depended on the resulting sale. Id.
at 720.
Neff claimed the land under a federal patent issued in 1866, and the property was alleged to be worth
$15,000. His challenge to Pennoyer’s title therefore depended on whether Mitchell’s earlier personal
judgment could validly authorize the sale. The reported opinion’s account of the parties’ title claims is used
here; the casebook’s later historical narrative is not treated as a judicial finding. Id. at 719-20; casebook pp.
86-87.
Procedural History
Neff brought the present action in the United States Circuit Court for the District of Oregon to recover
possession of the land. The federal court ruled for him because it considered the affidavits supporting
publication and proving publication defective. Pennoyer brought the case to the Supreme Court by writ of
error, the historical procedure for reviewing the judgment. This was a separate attack on the title derived
from Mitchell’s judgment, rather than a direct appeal in Mitchell’s original fee action. 95 U.S. at 715-16,
720-22.
The Supreme Court rejected the federal trial court’s stated reasons. A majority held that defects in the
affidavit supporting the publication order could be raised on direct review, not through this collateral attack,
and that an affidavit by the newspaper’s editor satisfied the statutory proof requirement. The Court

Issue
Could an Oregon court render a binding personal money judgment against a nonresident who neither
appeared nor received personal service within Oregon, using publication alone, and then create authority
over his land by seizing it only after judgment? Did the later sale transfer a valid title under that judgment?
Rule and Holding
The state-court money judgment was invalid and did not authorize the sale. Under the territorial rule the
Court applied, an ordinary personal money judgment against an absent nonresident required personal service
within the state or voluntary appearance, subject to the opinion’s expressly reserved settings such as consent
and status. Publication alone did not create power to determine Neff’s personal liability. 95 U.S. at 722-24,
727, 733-36.
A state could instead proceed against a nonresident’s property within its territory when that property was
brought under the court’s control through attachment or an equivalent proceeding. In that setting,
adjudication of the owner’s obligation was limited to disposition of the property, not a generally enforceable
personal judgment. Seizure after an invalid personal judgment could not supply the missing authority
retroactively: “the validity of every judgment depends upon the jurisdiction of the court before it is
rendered”. Id. at 723-28.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the federal judgment for Neff on the alternative jurisdictional ground.
Pennoyer could not establish valid title through the sheriff’s sale made under Mitchell’s invalid judgment.
95 U.S. at 734, 736.
Reasoning
The Court began with territorial sovereignty. Each state possessed authority over persons and property
within its territory, while another state’s equal sovereignty prevented the first state from directly asserting
authority over persons or property outside its borders. A state could affect out-of-state interests indirectly
when exercising valid authority over someone or something within its own territory, but that was different
from extending its own process beyond its borders. 95 U.S. at 722-24.
That premise explained the distinction between proceedings in personam and in rem. An in personam
judgment determines a person’s own liability; a proceeding in rem acts on property. The Court also used in
rem broadly to include actions between particular parties whose object was to reach identified property. The
casebook calls a recovery limited to the defendant’s property quasi in rem. The important distinction for the
Court was the limited reach of property-based authority, not merely the label on the complaint. Id. at 723-27,
733-34; casebook pp. 87-88.
Publication had a different role in a property proceeding. The Court assumed that property ordinarily
remained in the possession of its owner or an agent, so seizure would prompt the owner to learn of
proceedings affecting it. It regarded publication alone in an unrestricted personal action as an unreliable
means of reaching an absent defendant and a possible instrument for enforcing fictitious or stale demands.
These were the Court’s justifications for the distinction, not findings that Neff actually saw or failed to see
the newspaper notice. Id. at 726-27.

The timing of control over the property was decisive. If jurisdiction depended on the later discovery or
acquisition of assets, a judgment’s validity could change after the court had adjudicated the claim. Even
ownership of property at the beginning would not solve the problem if the property could be disposed of
before execution. The Court required authority before judgment rather than treating the later levy as a cure.
Id. at 727-28.
The Court also relied on the limits of full faith and credit, the constitutional and statutory obligation to
recognize qualifying judgments from other states. Recognition did not prevent inquiry into whether the
rendering court had jurisdiction over the person and subject matter. The full report, in a passage omitted
from the excerpt, further explains that federal courts exercised a different sovereignty and did not have to
give the defective personal judgment greater effect than another state’s courts would. Id. at 729-33.
The opinion connected jurisdiction to the Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause. A valid judicial
proceeding requires a tribunal authorized to decide the subject matter and, for personal liability of the kind at
issue, lawful authority over the defendant through in-state service or voluntary appearance. The Court’s
discussion explains why proper authority is a prerequisite to a binding judgment; it is not a conclusion that
any method producing actual notice automatically supplies that authority. Id. at 733-34.
The Court limited its reasoning. It reserved proceedings determining the status of a state’s own inhabitants,
such as divorce; arrangements requiring a nonresident to appoint an agent for service in connection with
local activities; and prescribed procedures concerning state-created corporations. It emphasized that none of
these features appeared in the simple contract money judgment before it. Those reservations prevent reading
every sentence about territoriality as an exceptionless rule for every type of case. Id. at 734-36.
Separate Opinions
Justice Hunt dissented; his opinion is omitted from the casebook and the following account comes from the
full report. He agreed that a state could subject local property to debts owed by a nonresident but considered
the timing of attachment a matter for legislative regulation, not a constitutional boundary. In his view,
reasonable substituted notice and an opportunity to defend could support sale of local land even without
preliminary seizure. He warned that the majority’s distinction would unsettle titles acquired under
longstanding statutes. He also distinguished enforcing a personal judgment in another state from using it to
reach property where the judgment was entered. 95 U.S. at 736-48 (Hunt, J., dissenting).
Notes
Narrow reading — interpretation. An absent nonresident’s land could not be sold under a personal fee
judgment entered on publication alone when the land had never been attached or otherwise placed within the
court’s authority before judgment. A later execution sale could not convert that invalid personal judgment
into a valid property adjudication. 95 U.S. at 720, 727-28, 734.
Broad reading — interpretation. The opinion organizes adjudicatory power around territorial authority
over a person or identified property and makes that authority a prerequisite to a binding judgment. Its own
reservations for status, consent, and corporate arrangements limit that framework. This is a reading of the
historical opinion, not a statement that its territorial formulation alone supplies every rule of present-day
personal jurisdiction. Id. at 722-24, 733-36.
