# Sheets v. Teddy’s Frosted Foods, Inc.
179 Conn. 471, 427 A.2d 385 (1980) | Contracts

## Facts and procedural posture
Emard Sheets alleged that Teddy’s employed him from November 1973 until November 1977 as quality control director and, later, operations manager. He received raises and bonuses. During his work he noticed vegetables below the quality specified on the company’s labels and meat components below the represented weights. In May 1977 he wrote to the company about these departures and recommended purchasing better raw materials and using conforming components. The company allegedly ignored his recommendations and discharged him on November 3. Although it attributed the discharge to unsatisfactory performance, Sheets alleged that the real reason was retaliation for his efforts to make its products comply with labeling and licensing law. These were allegations accepted favorably to the plaintiff on a motion to strike, not facts proved at trial. 179 Conn. at 472–474 & n.1.

His amended complaint asserted an implied employment contract, violation of public policy, and malicious discharge. The trial court granted the employer’s motion to strike; Sheets declined to plead further, producing a final judgment for the employer. On appeal he did not separately pursue malice. Although contract and tort remained in the case, the court resolved the appeal on the intentional tort theory principally pressed at argument. Id. at 472–474.

## Issue and holding
The issue was whether an employer’s ordinary power to terminate indefinite employment defeated a claim that it discharged a quality control employee for insisting on compliance with the state’s food labeling law. A three-justice majority held that the complaint stated a tort claim for wrongful discharge contrary to a clear mandate of public policy. The court reversed the judgment and remanded for further proceedings; it did not find that retaliation occurred or award damages. Id. at 474–480.

## Governing rule and reasoning
The majority preserved the ordinary at-will rule. It expressly distinguished a general requirement of just cause—which would require the employer to justify every dismissal—from liability when the employee proves a demonstrably improper reason grounded in an important violation of public policy. The legislature’s refusal to enact a general just-cause requirement therefore did not foreclose this narrower tort. A lawful contractual power can be exercised in a tortious manner; the court drew analogies to reliance-based liability and abuse of process. Other courts’ protection of employees who refused perjury, sought workers’ compensation, engaged in union activity, or served on juries likewise showed that termination discretion could have public-policy limits. Id. at 474–477.

The relevant policy here came from Connecticut’s Uniform Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act. Section 19-213 prohibited specified acts involving misbranded food, section 19-222 addressed false or misleading labels, and section 19-215 supplied criminal penalties, including penalties that did not require an intent to defraud. The act expressly sought to protect public health and consumers from merchandising deceit. Given Sheets’s responsibility for quality control and operations, the majority considered it possible that the violations could expose him personally to criminal prosecution. It did not say that he had been charged or that liability had already attached. Id. at 477–479 & n.6.

Responsibility for product quality also distinguished Sheets from the salesman in Geary, whose position did not give him responsibility or expertise to make independent product-safety judgments. The court acknowledged the danger of unwarranted litigation and interference with managerial discretion, but concluded that employees lacking bargaining power to obtain fixed-term contracts deserved some protection when employers punished conduct serving public obligations. The complaint alleged a direct connection between Sheets’s assigned responsibilities, insistence on statutory compliance, and discharge. That sufficed at the pleading stage. Id. at 477–480.

The court left open whether a statutory violation was always necessary to establish a public-policy discharge claim. Its narrower conclusion was that an employee should not have to choose between possible criminal sanctions and continued employment when a relevant statute states the public policy at issue. Id. at 480.

## Dissent and exact disposition
Chief Justice Cotter, joined by Justice Loiselle, dissented. He feared that the new cause of action would give discharged employees a means of coercing employers through litigation’s nuisance value. In his view, the connection to statutory policy was less direct than in cases where the discharge itself violated a statute. Sheets could have reported the alleged violations, even anonymously, to the enforcement authorities, and the prospect of criminal prosecution was remote. The dissent stressed legislative rejection of broad employment protection and favored legislative judgment over an expansive common-law exception. Its first note emphasized the absence of an allegation that Sheets faced criminal liability and the statutory exception under which the consumer-protection commissioner need not report minor violations for prosecution. The other notes discussed unsuccessful protective bills and limits of the out-of-state decisions invoked by the majority. Id. at 480–485 & dissent nn.1–3.

Justice Peters wrote the majority, joined by Justices Bogdanski and Healey. The 3–2 court declared error and remanded for further proceedings. The result reopened Sheets’s tort claim; it did not establish a universal just-cause condition or resolve factual liability. Id. at 480, 485.
