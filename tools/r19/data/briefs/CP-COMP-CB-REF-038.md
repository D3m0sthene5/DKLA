# Brown v. Cinemark USA, Inc.

**876 F.3d 1199 (9th Cir. 2017) · Ninth Circuit · December 7, 2017 · No. 16–15377**

**Civil Procedure — appellate finality after settlement and the limits of Microsoft v. Baker.** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, physical page 438, printed page 847, the Brown account in Note 2 before the Rule 23(d) section.

## Facts and the path to appeal

Silken Brown sued Cinemark USA and Century Theatres in a class complaint alleging wage-and-hour violations. The defendants removed the action to federal court. The district court consolidated it with similar pending litigation, including Mario De La Rosa’s action. The district court dismissed Brown’s individual direct wage-statement claim and denied certification of several proposed classes: meal and rest breaks, reporting pay, off-the-clock work, derivative wage statements, and direct wage statements. The remaining individual claims were set for trial. (Official order 3; report 1200.)

The litigation did not stop at the certification ruling. The defendants moved for summary judgment on the remaining claims, and the district court tentatively proposed granting that motion in part and denying it in part. The parties then stipulated to the tentative ruling and settled all remaining individual claims. Their settlement reserved the right to challenge the denial of certification of the direct wage claim and the dismissal of Brown’s individual direct wage-statement claim. Brown and De La Rosa appealed the reserved issues. (Order 3–4.)

While that appeal was pending, the Supreme Court decided Microsoft Corp. v. Baker. Cinemark and Century Theatres moved to dismiss Brown’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. They contended that settlement of the remaining claims, like the voluntary dismissal in Baker, could not create a final decision permitting review of an interlocutory class ruling. The published Brown order addressed that jurisdictional motion. It did not itself adjudicate the underlying wage claims or the merits of certification. (Order 3–5 and n.1.)

## Issue, holding, and exact disposition

Does Baker deprive a court of appeals of § 1291 jurisdiction when parties resolve remaining individual claims through a mutual settlement for consideration and expressly preserve specified issues for appeal?

**Not in these circumstances.** The Ninth Circuit concluded that the settlement produced a valid final judgment and denied the motion to dismiss. It retained jurisdiction to consider the reserved appeal on the merits. This was a denial of a jurisdictional motion, not an affirmance or reversal of the certification ruling in the published order. (Order 5; report 1201.)

## The distinction from Baker

Baker involved plaintiffs who, after losing class certification and being denied discretionary Rule 23(f) review, voluntarily dismissed their own individual claims to create a final judgment. They then sought review only of the order striking their class allegations. The Supreme Court rejected that tactic because it undermined § 1291’s protection against piecemeal appeals and circumvented Rule 23(f)’s calibrated procedure for discretionary interlocutory review. Brown’s panel accepted that rule; the question was whether the actual litigation and settlement before it presented the same problem. (Order 4.)

The panel found material differences. Brown and De La Rosa continued litigating their individual claims after certification was denied. Some claims resolved in the defendants’ favor, and others were settled. The parties’ agreement was a mutual exchange for consideration, rather than a unilateral abandonment of claims expressly designed to manufacture appellate jurisdiction. It reserved specific claims for appeal, and the record disclosed no sham tactic to obtain a nominal final judgment. Those circumstances supplied a genuine end to the remaining trial-court litigation while leaving a concrete appellate dispute over the reserved matters. (Order 5.)

The reasoning was therefore narrower than a rule that every dismissal accompanied by an appeal reservation is effective. The court considered the course of actual merits litigation, the nature of the exchange, and the absence of the circumvention that drove Baker. An appeal reservation by itself was not identified as a device that could override § 1291 or turn any otherwise interlocutory order into a final judgment. Here the mutual settlement did not implicate Baker’s concerns and qualified as a valid final judgment. (Order 4–5.)
