# Rourke v. Amchem Products, Inc.

**384 Md. 329, 863 A.2d 926 (2004), decided December 14, 2004.** Judge Wilner wrote the majority opinion. Chief Judge Bell dissented, joined by Judge Cathell; the reported seven-judge lineup and two dissenters yield a 5–2 decision. The assigned account is CivPro PDF page 684, printed page 1359, Note 1. Its interstate-preclusion question arose within a dispute over arbitration of an asbestos settlement.

## Settlement and procedural history

Asbestos manufacturers created the Center for Claims Resolution (CCR) to administer and settle claims on their behalf. Their Producer Agreement allocated liability and expenses among members and provided for membership changes. In April 2000, plaintiffs’ law firms negotiated a global settlement with CCR for 882 Maryland personal-injury and wrongful-death claimants. Individual payment depended on meeting qualifying criteria, accepting the settlement amount, and signing a release. The anticipated aggregate was approximately $10.1 million, payable in three installments, subject to adjustment. (333–335.)

The Settlement Agreement expressly made each member liable only for its individual share, rather than jointly liable for all members’ shares. It specified options if a member defaulted, required good-faith efforts to resolve disputes followed by binding arbitration of unresolved disputes arising while implementing the agreement, and selected Maryland law for interpretation and performance. (335–336.)

A problem arose because one law firm wanted to distribute installments proportionately among its clients instead of paying successive groups in full. The required releases acknowledged full payment before some clients would actually receive it. CCR’s claims officer, Michael Rooney, therefore sent an October 31, 2000 letter explaining that the releases did not establish full satisfaction. A claimant not fully paid could pursue a contract remedy for the deficiency, with interest and reasonable collection fees and expenses, including collection “by lawsuit or otherwise.” The letter described that contract remedy as the releasing claimant’s sole legal remedy, subject to an identified exception. (337–338.)

After payment shortfalls and member defaults, the plaintiffs argued that the letter imposed joint and several liability on the members and authorized a judicial collection action. They sued CCR and its twelve then-current members for declaratory and monetary relief. Defendants moved to compel arbitration. The plaintiffs opposed that motion both by construing Rooney’s letter as superseding arbitration and by invoking a Virginia Supreme Court decision construing a similar agreement and letter against arbitration. The Maryland plaintiffs were not parties to the Virginia case; only three of the Maryland defendant members, together with CCR, had been actual parties there. Alleged privity for other defendants was not contested on this appeal, and the court did not decide it. (339–341; majority n. 5.)

The circuit court compelled arbitration. It entered no order on the motion to dismiss CCR. The Court of Special Appeals affirmed, applying Virginia’s mutuality requirement to deny preclusion and construing the letter as leaving arbitration intact. Maryland’s highest court also affirmed. (340–343, 352–356.)

## Interstate issue preclusion

The plaintiffs wanted offensive nonmutual issue preclusion: parties newly suing defendants sought to bind those defendants to an issue lost against different plaintiffs. They argued that Maryland could apply its own preclusion principles, including through the settlement’s Maryland-law clause, rather than Virginia’s rule. The majority held that Maryland must give the Virginia judgment the effect Virginia would give it. Virginia retained mutuality and would not bind these defendants on the request of different plaintiffs. Consequently, the Virginia judgment did not foreclose litigating arbitrability in Maryland. (343–352.)

The majority grounded its result in both full faith and credit and Maryland’s common-law conflicts rule. It read § 1738 and Supreme Court precedent as requiring the rendering State’s preclusion rules, ordinarily giving the judgment neither greater nor lesser preclusive force. Its discussion of *Columbia College*, *Kremer*, *Migra*, and *Marrese* explains why the forum cannot replace the original State’s preclusion policy simply because its own rules might give a prior adjudication broader consequences. (343–348.)

Policy reinforced that analysis. Whether to dispense with mutuality is a State’s choice about fairness, litigation incentives, and the reliance parties may place on its judgments. Offensive preclusion can reward waiting for another plaintiff’s victory, expose defendants to disproportionate consequences from a first small-stakes case, and disregard inconsistent judgments or different procedural opportunities. The rendering State should be able to limit those consequences. Virginia had affirmatively retained mutuality; Maryland could not defeat that choice by enlarging the Virginia judgment’s reach. (348–352.)

The decision did not newly adopt offensive nonmutual preclusion as a general Maryland rule. Maryland had recognized defensive use and discussed the possible fairness of nonmutual preclusion, but the majority said it had not formally embraced offensive use and declined to do so here. The contractual choice of Maryland law did not alter the result: Maryland’s own law directed the court to Virginia’s rules when determining this Virginia judgment’s effect. (349; majority n. 11.)

## Independent construction of the arbitration agreement

Once the Virginia judgment was nonbinding, the Maryland court had to decide arbitrability itself. The broad original clause clearly covered a dispute over whether nondefaulting members owed the defaulting members’ shares. The question was whether the later letter had displaced that clause. That was a question for the court concerning the continued existence of the arbitration obligation, rather than merely an ambiguous question about the scope of a concededly existing clause. Note 12 expressly preserves that distinction. The court was not deciding the underlying joint-liability merits. (352–354; majority n. 12.)

Maryland’s objective interpretation principle required reading the contractual documents harmoniously where possible. A “remedy in contract” could be an arbitration remedy. “Such action” referred back to that remedy and did not necessarily mean a lawsuit. Providing interest and collection expenses likewise did not negate arbitration. The later letter’s purpose was to protect clients whose signed releases preceded full payment, not necessarily to change the dispute-resolution mechanism. (354–355.)

The express reference to collecting a deficiency “by lawsuit or otherwise” did not compel a contrary reading. The original default provisions permitted the settlement to end as to defaulting members, leaving litigation options against them. Such collection lawsuits could coexist with continued arbitration obligations concerning nondefaulting members, the defendants at issue here. This harmonious construction, reinforced by resolving relevant arbitration ambiguities in favor of arbitration, preserved paragraph 12 despite the contrary Virginia interpretation. The court therefore affirmed the order compelling arbitration. It did not finally decide that the nondefaulting members owed all deficient payments. (355–356.)

## Bell’s dissent, joined by Cathell

The dissent viewed issue preclusion as a forum’s tool for controlling repeated litigation, distinct from the validity and enforceability of the earlier judgment between its original parties. Giving a Virginia judgment additional collateral consequences in Maryland would not, on that view, deny it full faith and credit. Maryland should be able to apply its own fairness and resource-allocation policies to parties who had already fully litigated the issue. (357–368.)

Bell emphasized that the participating defendants had received review by Virginia’s highest court and that the record did not show inadequate opportunity to litigate. He challenged the majority’s reliance on federal § 1738 cases and distinguished precedents about a decree’s finality or the judgment’s own effect from a later forum’s collateral-estoppel policy. He found the contrary reasoning in *Finley* and *Hart* persuasive; the majority’s response that more authorities supported its rule did not answer the logic of those decisions. (366–372; dissent nn. 5–6.)

The dissent did not simply declare these plaintiffs entitled to payment. At minimum, Bell would remand to the Court of Special Appeals to review the trial court’s refusal of offensive preclusion on its merits, without treating full faith and credit as conclusively requiring Virginia’s mutuality rule. He was inclined to see a substantial basis for applying preclusion but preserved that minimum remand position. (372.)
