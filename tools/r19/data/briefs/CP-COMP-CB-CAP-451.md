# McDonough Power Equipment, Inc. v. Greenwood

**464 U.S. 548 (1984) — Supreme Court of the United States, January 18, 1984.** Justice Rehnquist delivered the Court’s opinion, joined by Burger, White, Blackmun, Powell, Stevens, and O’Connor. Blackmun also concurred separately, joined by Stevens and O’Connor. Brennan concurred in the judgment, joined by Marshall. All nine agreed to reverse the appellate judgment; seven joined the Court’s standard. Civil Procedure: juror nondisclosure, impartiality, harmless error, and motions for a new trial. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 590, printed 1164–1165.

## Accident, verdict, and undisclosed family injury

Billy Greenwood and his parents sued the manufacturer of a riding lawnmower after Billy’s feet came into contact with its blades. The suit was a diversity action in the District of Kansas. Although McDonough was the only defendant, Kansas law permitted allocation of comparative fault to nondefendants. After a trial extending over three weeks, the jury assessed damages of $375,000 but assigned no fault to McDonough. It allocated fault among the neighbor operating the mower, that neighbor’s father, and Billy’s mother. The district court entered judgment for McDonough. These were the jury’s determinations, not new liability findings made by the Supreme Court. (549–550, n.1.)

During voir dire, plaintiffs’ counsel asked the prospective jurors whether they or immediate family members had suffered severe injuries resulting in disability or prolonged pain and suffering. Ronald Payton did not respond. He became a juror and ultimately the foreman. His son had previously broken a leg in an exploding truck-tire incident. Whether Payton’s silence reflected a dishonest omission or a different understanding of injury severity was central to the later dispute. (550; 552, n.3; 557.)

## Postjudgment inquiry and appellate ruling

Four days after judgment, the Greenwoods requested permission under a local rule to approach jurors, asserting information and belief concerning Payton’s son. The court initially found insufficient cause. The next day the Greenwoods submitted another request with the father’s affidavit describing information obtained from the son’s Navy enlistment application. The court then authorized a brief, polite inquiry confined to the injury, but made no provision for recording it. (550–551.)

The Greenwoods also moved for a new trial on 18 grounds. Their reference to the earlier denial of permission to approach jurors was their only even indirect presentation to the district court of nondisclosure as a possible new-trial ground. The parties then interviewed Payton by telephone, but the district court never received the results and the Greenwoods never directly asserted there that the nondisclosure required a new trial. The judge denied the pending motion, finding the matter fairly tried and the verdict supported by the evidence. (551.)

On appeal, the parties’ briefs supplied recollections of the interview. Plaintiffs’ counsel recalled comments treating accidents as part of life; defense counsel recalled Payton believing the broken leg did not meet the question’s criteria of severity, disability, or prolonged suffering. The Tenth Circuit assumed good faith but found the undisclosed information significant evidence of probable bias and prejudice to the plaintiffs’ exercise of peremptory challenges. It ordered a new trial without first returning the issue to the district court for a hearing. (551–552, n.3.)

Note 2 separately cautions that if plaintiffs or counsel already knew the pertinent information during voir dire, choosing not to inquire further would bar a later challenge on that basis. The record did not establish that timing, so it was a conditional limitation rather than a finding of intentional silence by the Greenwoods. (550, n.2.)

## Holding and majority reasoning

The Supreme Court **reversed**. To obtain a new trial in this nondisclosure situation, the movant must show that the juror failed to answer a material voir-dire question honestly and that a correct answer would have supplied a valid basis for a challenge for cause. Recreating the opportunity for peremptory challenges simply because counsel lacked information was not enough. The concern was impartial adjudication, not perfect informational conditions for jury selection. (553–556.)

Rehnquist placed the issue within harmless-error doctrine. Rule 61 directs courts to disregard errors that do not affect substantial rights, and §2111 similarly governs appellate review. Although Rule 61 itself governs district courts, appellate courts must follow the same policy and are directly subject to §2111. A fair trial does not require an error-free trial; automatically undoing substantial litigation resources for technical imperfection would disregard that distinction. (553–554, n.4.)

Voir dire exposes potential bias and assists both for-cause and peremptory challenges. Truthfulness therefore matters. But the particular question asked jurors to characterize injuries using terms whose meanings might vary among people with different backgrounds. Other jurors responded differently to similar prompts, including a relatively minor bicycle-chain injury and a machinery accident disclosed only after further questioning. The Court considered an average-juror disclosure standard uncertain and insufficiently tied to the impartiality that made an error prejudicial. (554–555.)

The Court’s two-part standard linked the failure of honesty to information that would have justified removing the juror for cause. Different reasons for concealment might exist; only those affecting impartiality truly implicated trial fairness. The opinion did not find Payton dishonest, biased, or conclusively impartial on the incomplete record. Nor did it decide that the Greenwoods could never receive a new trial. (555–556.)

## Why trial-court fact development mattered

New-trial decisions ordinarily belong to the district court’s discretion. The appellate court had decided this factual issue from counsel’s unrecorded recollections rather than a developed district-court record. Note 3 criticizes that unusual procedure and explains that appellate tribunals are poor substitutes for trial courts in resolving factual disputes. The Court addressed the legal standard to correct the approach for possible further proceedings. (551–552, n.3; 556.)

The precise disposition left other appellate contentions to be resolved. If the juror issue remained relevant afterward, the district court could hold a hearing applying the Supreme Court’s principles. Thus reversal of the automatic new-trial award and availability of a later fact-based hearing coexist; the decision was not a final Supreme Court rejection of every ground for relief. (556.)

## Blackmun’s concurrence

Blackmun joined the Court’s opinion but expressly preserved the ordinary avenue of relief for a party denied an impartial jury. He considered honesty a useful initial indicator in most cases, not a reason to foreclose further examination. Regardless of whether an answer was honest or dishonest, a trial court could hold a posttrial hearing permitting proof of actual bias or, in exceptional circumstances, facts supporting an inference of bias. Stevens and O’Connor joined this clarification. (556–557.)

## Brennan’s concurrence in the judgment

Brennan and Marshall agreed that incomplete voir-dire information did not automatically justify a new trial and that the appellate court should not have resolved bias without district-court fact development. They declined to join the majority’s legal standard. Brennan would require an incorrect answer to a material question and bias against the movant under the particular circumstances. Honesty, dishonesty, inadvertence, and intent would be factors rather than an indispensable dishonesty prerequisite. (557–559.)

Brennan distinguished bias conclusively presumed by law from actual bias inferred from surrounding facts, proposing a more-probable-than-not inquiry for actual bias when conclusive presumption did not apply. His sole footnote identified circumstances the district court should consider: Payton’s reported comments, his role as foreman, and the jury’s initial zero-damages verdict before the judge instructed it to reconsider. Those concerns did not amount to a finding that bias existed. No claim of conclusively presumed bias was presented in this case. (558–559, concurrence n.*.)

## Judgment and assigned comparison

The Court **reversed the Tenth Circuit judgment ordering a new trial**. It supplied the governing nondisclosure standard and preserved the possibility of a district-court hearing if the issue remained material after other appellate questions were resolved. The original report contains no dissent: Blackmun joined the majority, while Brennan and Marshall concurred only in the judgment. (556–559.)

The assigned account correctly presents all three writings but compresses the sequence of requests for permission, the missing trial-court record, and the unresolved hearing. It cites Payton’s reported interview comments to 553 n.3; the original report places that note’s continuation and the statements at **552**, following the start on 551. The comments are counsel’s recorded appellate recollections, not a Supreme Court finding based on a transcript. The assigned majority quote at 555–556 and separate-opinion passages at 556 and 557–558 are otherwise consistent with the complete report. This is a historical source comparison, not a later-law certification.
