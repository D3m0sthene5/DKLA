Mooers v. Middlebury College
No. 2:20-cv-00144, 2021 WL 4225659 (D. Vt. Sept. 16, 2021)
Citation, court, and assigned scope
Judge Christina Reiss issued this twenty-page opinion and order in the District of Vermont on
September 16, 2021. The casebook's principal excerpt, printed pages 162–166, presents the
contrasting treatment of contract and unjust-enrichment claims. The full opinion also
addresses educational malpractice, conversion, consumer protection, affirmative defenses,
the documents properly before the court, and leave to amend. Pinpoints below refer to the
opinion's printed pages, which coincide with the twenty-page docket document, rather than
unverified Westlaw star pages.
Parties and requested relief
Henry Mooers, a Middlebury undergraduate, sued the college for himself and a proposed
class after the college shifted to remote instruction during the COVID-19 pandemic. He
requested disgorgement and a prorated return of tuition and mandatory fees for the period
when he no longer received in-person services. He pleaded breach of contract, unjust
enrichment, conversion, and violation of the Vermont Consumer Protection Act. Those were
distinct theories with different requirements, not four labels for a single automatically valid
refund claim. Slip op. at 1–4.
Material allegations
Mooers and the proposed class paid approximately $28,940 in undergraduate tuition and
$218 in student-activity fees for the spring 2020 semester, scheduled from February 10 to
May 19. He alleged that college publications and statements received during application,
enrollment, registration, and payment promised on-campus education and associated
services. He asserted that he chose Middlebury instead of another institution or an
online-only university in reliance on those representations. Id. at 2–3.
The cited materials described immersive learning, an inclusive campus, a residential
community, shared facilities, and education both inside and outside the classroom. The
complaint included Instagram images of students interacting with professors, using
dormitories, and studying on campus. It also quoted descriptions of the arts center, library,
academic facilities, and the president's statements about creating a campus experience.
These statements supplied the alleged content of the promise; the dispute was whether they
communicated an enforceable undertaking to provide in-person services under the
circumstances. Id. at 2–3, 9–12.
Middlebury held no in-person undergraduate classes after March 13, 2020. Classes
continued remotely. Mooers alleged loss of access to libraries, laboratories, study rooms,
face-to-face interaction, extracurricular activities, health and fitness facilities, sporting
events, and commencement. Students requested refunds, but the college did not refund
tuition. The complaint characterized the remote experience as materially different from the
experience for which students paid. These were allegations assessed for plausibility, not
findings after evidence or trial. Id. at 3–4.

At oral argument, Mooers urged separate treatment of activity fees. The court observed that
his amended complaint had not identified those fees as a separate contractual obligation. The

concerning those payments. Id. at 4 n.2.
Procedural history and standard
Middlebury moved to dismiss the original complaint on January 22, 2021. Following a
stipulation permitting amendment, Mooers filed an amended complaint on March 12.
Middlebury moved against that pleading on April 12 under Rule 12(b)(6). The court heard
argument July 20 and issued the present ruling in September. The first motion was denied as
moot because the amended pleading and new motion superseded it. Id. at 1–2 & n.1.
Under the plausibility standard, the court accepted well-pleaded factual allegations and
reasonable favorable inferences, but not conclusory recitations of legal elements. It did not
weigh evidence or decide how likely Mooers was to prevail ultimately. This posture explains
why one alternative theory survived although a specifically pleaded promise had not been
established. Id. at 5.
Issues
Did the educational-malpractice doctrine categorically bar claims arising from the move to
remote instruction? Did the identified publications and images plausibly establish a specific
contractual promise of continued in-person services? Could unjust enrichment proceed while
the scope of the contractual arrangement remained unresolved? Did Middlebury's refund
policy or the student's payment and continued participation foreclose that alternative at the
pleading stage? Finally, did the facts state conversion or consumer deception, and should
further amendment be allowed?
Rules and holdings
The educational-malpractice doctrine did not bar the claims as pleaded because they
principally concerned whether promised services were delivered, not judicial evaluation of
teaching quality or academic judgment. That threshold conclusion did not establish that a
specific enforceable promise existed. Id. at 5–8.
The contract claim failed because the cited materials were informative or aspirational and
did not identify a sufficiently specific, concrete promise that the college would provide the
asserted in-person educational services even during a pandemic. The unjust-enrichment
claim nevertheless survived as a plausible alternative. A factual record had not established a
valid contractual provision governing retention of tuition and fees under the closure
circumstances, and restitution did not itself require the missing specific promise. Id. at 8–16.
Conversion failed because the tuition and fees were not alleged to remain a specifically
identifiable fund over which Mooers had a superior possessory right. The
consumer-protection claim failed because the alleged statements did not plausibly mislead a
reasonable consumer into expecting unchanged on-campus services during a pandemic. The
court did not decide the economic-loss defense to conversion because the claim failed on
another ground. Id. at 16–19.

Actual judgment
Middlebury's motion was granted as to contract, conversion, and consumer
protection—Counts 1, 3, and 4—and denied as to unjust enrichment, Count 2. The earlier
dismissal motion was denied as moot. Mooers received twenty days to file a second amended
complaint because the court could not yet find amendment futile and identified no other
reason to deny it. Id. at 13, 16–20.
The opinion ordered no refund, restitution payment, damages award, or class certification. It
permitted an alternative claim to continue and allowed another pleading. “Student wins
restitution” would therefore materially misstate the actual judgment.
Reasoning
Enforcing an alleged undertaking differs from grading educational quality
Middlebury argued that determining a refund would require valuing online against in-person
instruction and would invade protected academic judgment. The court distinguished the core
claim from its potential remedial consequences. A court could interpret what services were
promised and whether they were supplied without evaluating curricula, teaching methods,
course materials, or individual student achievement. An allegation that a different service
was delivered was not necessarily an educational-malpractice claim. Id. at 5–8.
Some allegations referred to value, and damages might require comparison. That did not
transform the asserted wrong into negligent education. The court treated the source of the
duty and the nature of the alleged breach as controlling the threshold characterization. It
then separately tested whether the pleaded materials actually created the asserted
obligation. Id. at 7–8.
A contractual relationship did not establish every asserted term
Both sides accepted that payment for education occurred within a contractual relationship.
The court nevertheless required identification of the relevant specific undertaking.
Brochures, catalogs, and official statements can supply terms under Vermont law, but a
general mission, aspiration, or description of normal campus life does not necessarily
guarantee continuation of a particular service regardless of extraordinary circumstances. Id.
at 8–10.
The opinion compared cases in which different institutional materials supported different
outcomes. In Patel, for example, pricing distinctions between online and in-person classes
and more concrete catalog and contractual language supported a plausible in-person
commitment. Mooers's cited descriptions and images did not supply the same degree of
specificity. The court did not establish that university materials can never imply contractual
obligations; it found these allegations inadequate. Id. at 10–13.
This distinction also limits the pandemic discussion. The court did not first find an
unambiguous promise and then excuse its breach under a separately analyzed impossibility
or force-majeure defense. It concluded that the pleaded promise had not been identified.
Conflating the existence of a duty with an excuse for nonperformance would obscure the
holding.

The restitution theory required unjust retention rather than a specific promise
Vermont unjust enrichment required a benefit conferred, its acceptance, and circumstances
making retention inequitable. Mooers alleged payments for services and activities he did not
receive. That formulation stated a plausible alternative claim without requiring proof that
Middlebury had promised every asserted service in specific contractual language. Id. at
13–15.
The court recognized the ordinary rule that a valid, enforceable contract governing the
subject matter displaces quasi-contractual recovery. The issue was whether the record
established a contractual allocation covering these circumstances. Middlebury admitted a
contractual relationship but did not identify a term determining what happened to the
payments if the college closed or shifted exclusively online. Rule 8(d)(3) and Vermont law
allowed inconsistent alternative theories while that scope question remained open. Id. at
13–16.
This was not permission to override an unfavorable contract through restitution. The court
expressly allowed the factual record eventually to establish a contractual provision
foreclosing the claim. Nor did it decide how any enrichment should be valued, whether online
education offset it, or whether retention ultimately was unjust. Those questions remained
beyond the pleading determination.
The refund policy did not establish a defense on this motion
The full opinion supplies two reasons for rejecting Middlebury's reliance on its refund policy.
First, the policy was not incorporated into, referenced by, or integral to the amended
complaint, and Middlebury had not requested judicial notice. It therefore was not properly
before the court on the dismissal motion. Second, even considering it, the policy governed a
student's voluntary withdrawal or reduction below a full course load. It did not address a
college closure or exclusive online instruction during a pandemic. Id. at 14.
The two grounds are distinct: one concerns the record a Rule 12(b)(6) court may consider;
the other concerns the policy's scope. Neither is a factual finding that every institutional
refund policy is unenforceable.
Waiver and ratification were not established by the complaint
Middlebury argued that payment for the education and continued participation ratified its
performance and waived objections. The court treated waiver and ratification as affirmative
defenses whose potential existence did not negate the complaint's plausibility. A plaintiff
ordinarily need not anticipate and plead around such defenses. Their assertion therefore did
not justify dismissal of this unjust-enrichment claim on the existing pleading. Id. at 15.
Conversion required an identifiable property interest
The refund demand alleged an obligation to repay part of an amount spent. It did not identify
segregated money or a documentary intangible over which Mooers retained a superior right.
Tuition and fees were presumably commingled with other funds. Under the conversion
authorities applied, a claim for reimbursement was not sufficient to establish appropriation of
the requisite specific property. Id. at 16–17.

Although Middlebury also invoked the economic-loss rule, the court expressly declined to
decide that ground. The conversion dismissal should be attributed to the missing identifiable
property interest, not to a holding that the economic-loss rule necessarily barred every tort
claim in the dispute. Id. at 16–17.
Consumer deception did not follow from disappointed expectations
The VCPA required a material representation, omission, or practice likely to mislead
consumers who interpreted it reasonably. Middlebury arguably operated in commerce, so the
court did not create a categorical immunity for colleges. It instead found no plausible
allegation that the cited statements reasonably promised uninterrupted on-campus services
during the pandemic. Id. at 18–19.
Footnote eight also qualifies Middlebury's argument that the VCPA never applies to contract
claims. A mere breach does not establish consumer fraud, but contractual dealings can
involve independently deceptive conduct. Mooers failed the required pleading showing here;
the holding is not that contract and consumer-protection remedies are mutually exclusive in
all cases. Id. at 18 n.8.
Separate opinions and subsequent casebook note
There is no separate opinion. The casebook separately reports a 2022 ruling on a second
amended complaint: the new tuition allegations remained inadequate, while a more distinct
activity-fee contract claim survived. That is later procedural history supplied by the editors,
not part of the September 2021 holding. The present brief covers the 2021 opinion in full; it
does not represent a comprehensive audit of later merits rulings or appellate history.
Use and limits
Mooers is useful for distinguishing four propositions: a contractual relationship exists; a
particular promise has been adequately alleged; a contract governs allocation of a disputed
loss; and unjust retention has been proved. The first does not establish the second or third,
and survival of an alternative restitution pleading does not establish the fourth. Track the
procedural stage and each count's disposition before drawing a rule about tuition refunds or
restitution.
