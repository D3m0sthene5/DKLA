# Budinich v. Becton Dickinson & Co.

**486 U.S. 196 (1988) — Supreme Court of the United States, May 23, 1988. Civil Procedure.** Justice Scalia delivered a unanimous opinion. A pending request for attorney’s fees attributable to the litigation does not prevent an otherwise final decision on the merits from being final under 28 U.S.C. § 1291.

## Facts and procedural history

Budinich sued in Colorado state court for compensation allegedly owed from employment. Becton Dickinson removed the action to the federal district court on diversity grounds. A jury returned a $5,000 verdict for Budinich, considerably less than he had requested, and judgment was entered on March 26, 1984. He timely moved for a new trial, challenging various district-court rulings, and requested attorney’s fees under Colorado law. That statute directed that a judgment in an action to collect employment compensation include a reasonable fee for the winning party, taxed as costs. The Supreme Court did not decide the substantive correctness of the compensation verdict. 486 U.S. at 197.

On May 14 the district court denied the new-trial motions and found Budinich entitled to fees, but requested further submissions before determining their amount. The court’s final fee order followed on August 1. Budinich filed his notice of appeal on August 29, seeking review of all post-trial orders. Becton Dickinson argued that the merits judgment had become appealable upon denial of the new-trial motions in May, making the August notice too late to challenge the merits. The Tenth Circuit agreed: it dismissed the appeal on all issues except the fee award, which it affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve a circuit conflict over the effect of unresolved fees on appellate finality. Id. at 197–198.

Both sides accepted that the merits appeal was untimely if the merits decision was appealable before determination of the fees. The disputed question was therefore the source and meaning of the finality rule, rather than a factual disagreement about when Budinich filed his notice. Id. at 198.

## Issue and holding

Does an unresolved fee request postpone finality of a federal merits judgment, particularly when the law authorizing fees treats them as part of the judgment? No. Federal law determines appealability under § 1291 even in diversity litigation. A decision resolving the merits is final for that provision whether or not fees attributable to the case remain for adjudication. The Court chose a uniform rule rather than an inquiry into each fee authorization’s characterization of the award. Id. at 198–203.

## Parties’ arguments and the governing law

Budinich first argued that Colorado law controlled and that the state did not regard a claim as final and appealable until fees were fully determined. He also contended that applying § 1291 to diversity litigation would violate the Tenth Amendment. The Court rejected both positions. Although state law ordinarily supplies rules of decision in diversity cases, it does not displace a governing federal statute. Section 1291 supplies federal appellate jurisdiction over final district-court decisions and applies across federal litigation, rather than incorporating different state concepts of finality. Id. at 198–199.

The Court contrasted “final decisions” with statutory language, such as “property,” that might naturally refer to state-law concepts. Congress’s regulation of appeals between federal courts was readily classified as procedural and was necessary and proper to implement its authority to establish those courts. State control over the employment-compensation claim therefore did not entail state control over the federal appellate timetable. Id. at 199.

## Why the outstanding fee request did not postpone finality

The usual definition of a final decision asks whether it ends litigation on the merits, leaving execution of the judgment. An unresolved matter does not defeat that finality if its resolution cannot alter the merits order or moot or revise decisions embodied in it. Prior attorney-fee decisions had characterized fees as collateral to the merits, including White’s holding that a request for civil-rights fees was not a motion to alter or amend the judgment under Rule 59(e). Id. at 199–200.

Scalia acknowledged a limitation in that reasoning: simply calling fees collateral assumes the answer if a party claims that the fees are themselves part of the merits. The Court therefore addressed that premise directly. Generally, litigation fees do not remedy the underlying injury and may be awarded to a defending party. Their traditional treatment as costs likewise supports separation from the merits. But the Court did not make finality depend on persuading a court that every particular fee statute fit this general description. Id. at 200–201.

Budinich’s stronger alternative argument was that statutory or decisional law could make fees part of the merits, notwithstanding their usual collateral character. Some circuit decisions supported that distinction, and the Supreme Court acknowledged that its own Boeing opinion had implicitly recognized such a possibility. Squarely confronting the issue, however, it concluded that the characterization chosen by the underlying law should not determine the § 1291 effect of an unresolved fee request. Id. at 201.

The decisive concern was operational consistency. The statutory purpose of finality concerns the functioning of the judicial system, not just conceptual labels. Whether the authorizing statute deems fees part of the merits does not make a merits appeal more disruptive, more susceptible to settlement, or more likely to interfere with trial-court reconsideration. A characterization-dependent rule would instead require parties to resolve a potentially difficult fee-classification question before knowing when their jurisdictional appeal clock began. A uniform rule supplied clearer, more predictable timing without sacrificing a relevant finality interest. Id. at 201–203.

This holding concerns fees attributable to the litigation in question. It should not be expanded into a statement that every unresolved monetary demand is collateral, or that a court can certify a merits claim as final while leaving substantive relief on that claim undecided. The opinion’s discussion of Wetzel distinguishes the situation in which the merits themselves remain unresolved. Id. at 200.

## Prospective application, judgment, and assigned account

Budinich finally requested prospective application on the theory that the decision changed existing law. The Court did not need to decide whether it represented a change. Under its jurisdictional analysis, an untimely merits appeal gave the circuit no authority to review the merits; the circuit could not acquire that authority by applying the finality rule only prospectively. Id. at 203.

The Supreme Court **affirmed the Tenth Circuit’s judgment**. It upheld the federal finality rule and the resulting untimeliness of the merits appeal. It did not reexamine the jury’s compensation verdict or independently redetermine a reasonable fee. Id.
