# Curtice Brothers Co. v. Catts
72 N.J. Eq. 831, 66 A. 935 (Ch. 1907) | Contracts

## Decision and significance

The New Jersey Court of Chancery advised a decree enforcing a contract for the entire tomato crop of specified land. The canner's need for timely supplies during a six-week packing season made ordinary damages inadequate: the danger was loss of the coordinated factory operation, not simply paying a higher price for replacement tomatoes. The court also rejected the objection that enforcement necessarily compelled the planter's personal services, because an injunction against sale to others and, if necessary, a receiver could preserve the crop. The decree was advised without costs. This was an original equitable proceeding on final hearing, not an appellate affirmance. Vice-Chancellor Leaming delivered the opinion; no separate writing or divided vote appears in the reproduced report. (72 N.J. Eq. at 831–834; 66 A. at 935–936.)

## Facts and procedural posture

Curtice Brothers operated a tomato-canning factory capable of packing about one million cans. Its active packing season lasted approximately six weeks. It had to arrange cans, other equipment, and labor in advance according to the plant's capacity. Using that capacity and an estimate of the average yield per acre, it calculated the acreage necessary to supply the plant and contracted with growers to secure the essential pack. The disputed agreement obligated Catts to sell Curtice the entire product of specified land planted with tomatoes. Curtice filed a bill seeking specific performance against Catts and other defendants. Catts contested the court's power to provide equitable relief. The opinion describes the facts as stipulated and addresses the bill on final hearing, pleadings, and proofs. It supplies no acreage, price, or dated delivery schedule beyond the crop commitment; those details should not be invented. (72 N.J. Eq. at 831, 833–834.)

The vulnerability arose from the combination of the short production season and commitments already made for the factory. Whether Curtice secured all necessary acreage by contract, or contracted for part and expected to buy the balance in the open market, a grower's refusal would leave it dependent upon an uncertain substitute supply. The relevant deficiency concerned tomatoes of the needed quality and quantity available at the needed time. A breach by one grower created the same kind of disruption as breaches by all, differing in degree. (Id. at 833–834.)

## Issues and competing positions

The principal issue was whether an agreement involving ordinary personal property could be specifically enforced when its breach threatened an extraordinary business need. Curtice sought the promised crop, rather than a monetary substitute. Catts challenged equitable jurisdiction and objected that specific performance would require personal services. The opinion does not present a separate contract-formation dispute or a quantified damages award. Its focus is the adequacy of relief and the means of protecting performance. (Id. at 831–834.)

## Rule and reasoning

The court began with the same basic equitable principles for real and personal property. Land-sale agreements ordinarily support a presumption favoring specific performance, whereas agreements concerning chattels ordinarily do not, because their breach usually produces damages that can readily be measured and recovered at law. That practical distinction is not an inherent jurisdictional barrier. When a legal remedy is inadequate, a personal-property agreement may be enforced as freely as a land agreement, subject to equitable discretion. (Id. at 831–832.)

The opinion discussed Story's broader proposal that a buyer of chattels should be allowed to elect damages or specific performance. Although the court considered the development of equitable remedies to be moving in that direction, it declined to adopt an unrestricted election inconsistent with accepted principles. Existing decisions instead supported relief because of a contract's characteristic features or the parties' peculiar circumstances and needs. Curtice had to establish that sort of inadequacy; merely preferring the actual goods was insufficient. (Id. at 832–833; 66 A. at 935–936.)

The coordinated packing operation supplied the necessary circumstance. A higher replacement price could be estimated with some accuracy and would ordinarily be compensated through damages. The irreparable injury here was inability to obtain suitable tomatoes at any price in time to use the equipment and labor arranged for the short season. The agreement therefore served a need different from an ordinary purchase of merchandise in the usual course of business. If equivalent tomatoes were available in the open market without serious disruption of the plant's economic arrangements, the court would hesitate to intervene; the supply contracts and production arrangements demonstrated the importance of assured delivery in the situation before it. (72 N.J. Eq. at 833–834.)

The personal-services objection did not defeat relief. The court could restrain Catts from selling the promised crop elsewhere and appoint a receiver to harvest it if necessary. This explanation identifies possible methods of preserving performance. It does not establish that the court actually appointed a receiver or ordered Catts personally to perform every agricultural task. (Id. at 834.)

## Exact disposition and assigned material

The court advised a decree pursuant to the bill's prayer and stated that no costs would be taxed because of the manner in which the factual stipulation had been made. There was no appellate reversal, remand, or money-damages calculation. (Id. at 834.)

Independent review also read all four original New Jersey Equity reporter scans, pp. 831–834, through the ending before Kelsey v. Dilks.
