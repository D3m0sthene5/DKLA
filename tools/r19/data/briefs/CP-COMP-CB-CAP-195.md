# Nandjou v. Marriott International, Inc.

**985 F.3d 135 (1st Cir. 2021). January 15, 2021; accompanying errata correct closing punctuation.** Judge Barron wrote for Judges Thompson and Barron. Judge Torruella heard argument and participated in the conference but did not participate in issuance; the remaining panelists acted under 28 U.S.C. § 46(d). Citations below use the preserved court opinion’s numbered slip pages. Assigned account: CivPro PDF 237, printed 431, Note 4.

## The family’s trip and the litigation

Chimene Nandjou, her husband William, and their three children lived in Lynn, Massachusetts. They received numerous Marriott mailings, including at least three promoting a Residence Inn in Montreal and describing its pool. After reviewing the advertisements, William booked a stay. On April 20, 2016, he took the children to the pool. Holding their two-year-old son Menelik, he began drowning. The surviving children, ages eight and four, tried unsuccessfully to rescue them. Nobody else was present when the drownings occurred. An Arizona guest later arrived and summoned help; emergency personnel began arriving approximately twenty minutes later. William died that day, and Menelik was declared brain dead two days later. (Slip 3–5.)

Nandjou sued the Canadian hotel owner, Reluxicorp, and two American Marriott entities in Massachusetts state court in September 2018. She asserted wrongful death, vicarious liability, the surviving children’s emotional distress, and Menelik’s conscious pain and suffering, seeking punitive as well as other damages. The defendants removed on diversity grounds. After initially denying their dismissal motion, the district court reconsidered, upheld personal jurisdiction, but dismissed for forum non conveniens subject to continued submission to Canadian jurisdiction. Nandjou already had a parallel Canadian action. (Slip 5–7.)

The First Circuit reversed the forum non conveniens dismissal and affirmed the rejection of the personal-jurisdiction challenge. It decided neither negligent operation of the pool nor damages. The complaint and limited-discovery facts described by the opinion supplied the record for these threshold questions. (Slip 3–4, 38.)

## A correct standard can still be incorrectly applied

A defendant seeking to displace the plaintiff’s home forum bears a heavy burden. The court first asks whether an adequate alternative exists, then balances public and private interests. A balance that is equal or only marginally favors the foreign forum does not suffice. Appellate review is for abuse of discretion: failure to consider a material factor, substantial reliance on an improper factor, or clearly erroneous weighing of proper factors. Massachusetts remained a home forum for this purpose even though Nandjou had moved to Georgia. (Slip 7–12.)

The panel rejected her argument that the district court had simply forgotten the presumption favoring her chosen forum. Although one sentence referred to interests favoring Canada without saying “strongly,” the opinion read as a whole articulated the demanding standard correctly. The error lay in its application. Canadian availability and adequacy were undisputed, as was the neutral weight of public interests: both jurisdictions had legitimate interests in the dispute. Private convenience was therefore decisive. (Slip 12–15.)

## Why counting Canadian witnesses distorted convenience

The district court overlooked a distinctive part of the vicarious-liability claims: proof of the relationship between the American Marriott entities and their Canadian franchisee. Evidence about whether Marriott held out the hotel as owned, operated, and controlled by Marriott would involve witnesses who were not predominantly Canadian. That claim could not be evaluated solely through pool-operation evidence. The panel nevertheless recognized that sufficiently strong grounds for dismissing the other interdependent claims might justify dismissal of the whole suit. It proceeded to examine those grounds rather than treating the vicarious-liability point as automatically conclusive. (Slip 15–17.)

For the remaining claims, the district court emphasized approximately twenty-five Canadian third-party witnesses, including responders, physicians, police, and inspectors. But the two surviving children were the only witnesses to the moment of drowning. Their significance extended beyond emotional-distress damages: only they could describe the pool conditions before anyone arrived to help. Requiring them to return to the country where their father and brother died also imposed an emotional burden relevant to convenience. Three American third-party guests, including the Arizona rescuer and two Massachusetts residents, could supply important immediate-aftermath evidence. The Arizona witness did not make every Canadian witness redundant, but her testimony could not simply be discounted. (Slip 17–21.)

The district court also undervalued American damages witnesses. Liability testimony is not inherently more important than damages testimony. The relevant distinction in earlier precedent concerned the need for live testimony as compared with evidence readily presented through depositions and translation. Here, witnesses describing William and the consequences of his death might critically require live presentation; the district court had not found the same necessity for all the Canadian witnesses. The defendants had not explained why each of their estimated twenty-five witnesses needed to appear live. Accounting for materiality, live-testimony needs, and the children’s role showed that the heavy burden was unmet. (Slip 21–24.)

## The separate personal-jurisdiction issue

The defendants offered lack of personal jurisdiction as an alternative basis to preserve dismissal. The parties accepted the prima facie method: properly documented proffers were credited and construed favorably to Nandjou. The panel reviewed that assessment de novo. Because the defendants developed no distinct Massachusetts long-arm restriction, the appeal focused on federal due process. Nandjou relied on specific jurisdiction, requiring relatedness, purposeful availment, and reasonableness; the Marriott entities developed only a relatedness challenge. (Slip 24–28.)

They argued that the mailings, at most, were a but-for cause of the family’s presence in Canada, not a proximate cause of drowning. The panel’s precedent preferred proximate causation but did not impose it rigidly in every jurisdictional setting. Marriott deliberately cultivated this family’s business through direct mail sent to their Massachusetts home, promoting this particular hotel and its pool, and the record supported inducement of the reservation. That was more specific than a universally accessible internet advertisement. Those contacts sufficiently related to the resulting claims. The court did not find that the advertisements legally caused the deaths on the merits. (Slip 28–32.)

Reluxicorp challenged attribution of Marriott’s mailings because it did not control the precise marketing methods. The panel did not need to resolve every actual-agency question. On the accepted agency-by-estoppel principles, Reluxicorp’s branding, its contract authorizing marketing that included direct mail, and its failure to correct the impression of a Marriott-owned hotel supported attribution. The family’s failure to recognize separate corporate entities reinforced, rather than defeated, the reasonable belief that Marriott marketed by or for the hotel. This was a prima facie jurisdictional conclusion, not a final determination of vicarious tort liability. (Slip 32–38.)

The panel **affirmed denial of dismissal for lack of personal jurisdiction and reversed the forum non conveniens dismissal**, with each party bearing its own costs. (Slip 38, as corrected by errata.) The case illustrates why deference to the trial court permits reversal when a formally correct framework misweighs the actual claims and critical witnesses.
