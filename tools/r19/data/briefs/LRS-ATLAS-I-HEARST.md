# NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.

**322 U.S. 111 (1944) — Supreme Court of the United States, April 24, 1944.** Justice Rutledge delivered the Court’s opinion. Justice Reed concurred in the result with a brief stated reason; Justice Roberts dissented. The judgment was 8–1. The opinion covers four companion cases, Nos. 336–339, involving four newspapers, including two Hearst cases, Stockholders Publishing, and Times-Mirror. Assigned accounts appear in LRS November-a, physical 149–150 and 163, printed 929–930 and 942, and November-b, physical 14–15, printed 958–959.

## Workers, distribution arrangements, and the bargaining dispute

Four Los Angeles daily newspapers refused to bargain with a union representing regular street vendors of their papers. The publishers said the vendors were independent contractors rather than employees under the National Labor Relations Act. The affected workers, conventionally called newsboys, were generally mature men who worked regularly over substantial periods, depended on sales for their livelihoods, and often supported families. Their situation differed from schoolchildren, temporary sellers, and casual vendors. Only street sellers were involved, not every independent dealer, newsstand, or home-delivery carrier distributing the newspapers. (113–116.)

District managers coordinated delivery and sales within geographical districts. Checkmen or main-spot vendors sometimes redistributed papers and collected receipts, receiving small salaries for those tasks while spending most of their time selling. Ordinary vendors earned the difference between the price they paid and the retail price, but publishers effectively controlled that margin and could influence total earnings through territories and paper allocations. Vendors generally received papers on credit and could return unsold copies, although the record included exceptions and they bore losses on papers not returned. Their supposed freedom to choose the number of copies was not absolute: allocation often depended on managers and could be imposed unilaterally. (116–118 and notes 10–15.)

Managers assigned selling locations, could transfer vendors, prescribed hours to a significant extent, supervised performance, and applied sanctions ranging from reprimands to dismissal. Publishers furnished equipment and advertising materials and gave instructions about displays and sales methods. The Board treated these workers as an integral part of newspaper distribution. At the same time, the publishers identified features suggesting independent enterprise: vendors might sell competitors’ products, buy and sell locations, hire relief workers, bear some losses, and lack the usual payroll or insurance classifications. The dispute required appraisal of that entire relationship, not denial that such contrary features existed. (118–120 and notes 16–17.)

## Administrative and judicial proceedings

The Los Angeles Newsboys union petitioned for investigation and certification. Following a consolidated hearing, the NLRB found the regular full-time vendors employees, selected bargaining units, and ordered elections. The union won majorities and was certified. When publishers refused to bargain, subsequent unfair-labor-practice proceedings produced cease-and-desist and bargaining orders. Although the representation proceedings were consolidated, the Board did not apply evidence about one publisher indiscriminately to the others. (114 and notes 3–6.)

The Ninth Circuit, over one judge’s dissent, set the orders aside. It independently examined the relationships using what it regarded as controlling common-law employment standards and concluded that the vendors were not employees. In the Supreme Court, publishers also contested the units and the sufficiency of the supporting record. They abandoned an earlier argument that the controversies failed to affect commerce. (114–120 and note 18.)

## Holdings

The Court rejected treating employment classifications developed for other legal purposes as exclusively controlling the NLRA’s employee definition. The national statute’s history, context, purposes, and the economic relationship mattered. Where a broad statutory term required initial application by the agency administering the Act, the Board’s determination would be accepted if supported by the record and reasonably grounded in law. Those requirements were met for the designated vendors. (120–132.)

The Board also acted within its discretion in drawing the bargaining units. Its distinctions based on regular full-time tenure and the circumstances of suburban organization had a rational basis on this record. The Court reversed the judgments and remanded all four causes for further proceedings consistent with the opinion. (132–135.)

## Why common-law labels did not settle coverage

The publishers’ position assumed a simple, uniform common-law employee test. The Court found instead that classifications varied across jurisdictions and contexts, including tort liability and social legislation. Depending on local rules would produce different NLRA coverage for otherwise similar workers, contrary to a national scheme addressing a national labor problem. Extracting a supposed general essence of those local rules would still elevate technical distinctions developed for different ends into controlling limits on the statute. (120–125 and notes 19–23.)

The Court did not conclude that every service provider was an employee or that established legal classifications had no relevance. It identified an intermediate region between obvious employment and obvious independent entrepreneurship. There, the statute’s concern with industrial unrest, bargaining inequality, and effective collective organization supplied the relevant perspective. Workers formally regarded as contractors for another purpose could face the same dependence and inability to bargain individually that the Act sought to address. Their economic situation could accordingly bring them within the Act’s protections. (124–129.)

The statute’s broad definitions and legislative history reinforced that conclusion. The Court considered relationships extending beyond a narrow master-servant concept and cited existing collective organization among several groups with atypical employment forms. Those references did not make every member of each occupation an employee in this case; they supported the conclusion that technical labels alone could not answer the statutory question. (127–129 and notes 25–33.)

## What belonged to courts and what belonged to the Board

The Court stated that statutory interpretation remained a judicial responsibility, with appropriate weight given to administrative judgment. But it distinguished initial agency application of a broad statutory term to a particular factual relationship. The Board’s accumulated experience with workers, industries, bargaining needs, and labor disputes placed that application within its ordinary administrative work. Courts should not replace supported agency factual inferences with their own. The Board’s ultimate classification here had both evidentiary support and a reasonable legal basis. (130–132.)

Regular work, livelihood dependence, publisher influence over prices and markets, supervision, and supplied equipment collectively supported employee status. This was not merely a conclusion that the agency had used the word employee. The Court examined the Board’s findings and their relation to the statute before accepting the classification. The opinion thus combined a judicial account of the statute’s purpose with a limited reviewing role for this specific administrative application. (131–132.)

## Bargaining units and separate positions

The units generally included regular full-time street vendors and, for some papers, checkmen, while excluding bootjackers and temporary, casual, or part-time sellers. Different papers’ units used different practical definitions, such as regular sales of specified editions or established spots for four hours daily, five days weekly. The Board could emphasize stable tenure rather than identical functions. It did not need mathematical precision where peripheral or hypothetical deviations did not defeat an otherwise appropriate unit. (132–133 and notes 37–41.)

Certain suburban sellers were excluded because organization had not extended to them. The record showed neither deliberate rejection of applicants nor suburban interest in collective bargaining, and no competing union sought representation. Congress had given the Board flexibility to shape units that made bargaining practicable; the Court found no plausible reason to deny an organized group protection until geographically separated workers chose to participate. This upheld the Board’s particular exercise of discretion, not an unlimited rule permitting arbitrary exclusions. (133–135 and notes 42–45.)

Reed concurred in the result, identifying the coverage test announced by the Board in its Stockholders Publishing proceedings as the proper one. His short statement did not join every formulation of the Court’s reasoning. Roberts would have affirmed the Ninth Circuit. He considered employee a term with an established, generally understood meaning and regarded its interpretation as judicial. He rejected the suggestion that the alternative required mechanically applying each state’s local law and denied that Congress had authorized the Board to redefine employment simply to advance its own view of statutory policy. He also understood the Act’s language about employees of different employers as protecting cross-employer union organization, not permitting designation of nonemployees as employees. (135–137.)

## Assigned significance and source limits

The assignment quotes this decision in historical accounts of deference and later opposing explanations in Loper Bright. Those later passages emphasize different aspects of the 1944 reasoning. The complete Hearst opinion preserves both its judicial interpretation of the statute and the warrant-in-the-record/reasonable-basis standard for the Board’s specific application. This brief describes the Act and judgment addressed in 1944 and does not assume that they settle employment classification under later statutes or amendments.
