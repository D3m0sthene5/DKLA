Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes
Citation
564 U.S. 338, 131 S. Ct. 2541, 180 L. Ed. 2d 374 (Supreme Court of the United States 2011). Argued March
29, 2011; decided June 20, 2011. Justice Scalia wrote the Court’s opinion; Parts I and III were unanimous,
and Part II was joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy, Thomas, and Alito.
Casebook pp. 810–822; source PDF pp. 420–426. Full judicial opinion.
Parties
Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., the employer and defendant, challenged certification of a nationwide class of
approximately 1.5 million current and former female employees. Betty Dukes, Christine Kwapnoski, and
Edith Arana were the named representatives remaining in the class before the Supreme Court. They alleged
sex discrimination in pay and promotions under Title VII. Opinion, Part I.B and n.1; CB 810–812.
Material Facts
The company described in the record operated about 3,400 stores and delegated substantial pay and
promotion discretion to local managers within some general limits. The plaintiffs alleged that a uniform
corporate culture allowed gender stereotypes to affect that discretion throughout the company. Their theories
included disparate impact from discretionary practices and disparate treatment through the company’s
asserted awareness and failure to constrain those practices. They did not allege an express corporate policy
forbidding women’s advancement. Opinion, Parts I.A–B; CB 810–812.
The named employees described different experiences. Dukes alleged discriminatory pay and discipline;
Kwapnoski described sex-specific mistreatment and appearance-related remarks; Arana attributed denial of
management opportunities to sex discrimination. For class certification, plaintiffs submitted statistical
comparisons, about 120 anecdotal accounts, and sociologist William Bielby’s analysis of the company’s
vulnerability to bias. Wal-Mart disputed both the conclusions and suitability of the evidence. These were
allegations and competing evidentiary positions, not final findings of liability for every employee. Opinion,
Parts I.B–C; CB 811–812.
Procedural History
The district court certified the class under Rule 23(a) and Rule 23(b)(2). The en banc Ninth Circuit
substantially affirmed, finding a common question about corporate policies and permitting backpay claims
because they did not predominate over prospective relief. It also approved possible use of sample claims and
extrapolation to determine classwide relief. The Supreme Court reviewed commonality and the use of Rule
23(b)(2) for backpay. Opinion, Parts I.C–D; CB 812–813; sampling detail in full opinion.
Issue
Did the evidence establish a common contention capable of resolving a central issue across the nationwide
class under Rule 23(a)(2)? Could individualized backpay claims be certified under Rule 23(b)(2) alongside
requests for injunctive and declaratory relief?

Rule and Holding
By five votes, the Court held that commonality requires a common contention whose determination can
resolve an issue central to every member’s claim on a classwide basis. Alleging violation of the same statute
is insufficient. The proponent must affirmatively establish Rule 23’s requirements through a rigorous
analysis, which may overlap with the merits. On this record, plaintiffs failed to show the common
discriminatory policy or common exercise of discretion needed to unite their claims. Opinion, Parts II.A–C,
slip op. 8–20; CB 813–818.
All nine Justices agreed that the individualized backpay claims could not proceed under Rule 23(b)(2). That
provision concerns an indivisible injunction or corresponding declaration benefiting the class as a whole. It
cannot carry these individualized monetary awards, and the Court reserved whether genuinely incidental
monetary relief could ever be included. Part III, establishing this second holding, is omitted from the
assigned principal excerpt. Opinion, Part III, slip op. 20–27 (full opinion).
Judgment
The Court reversed the Ninth Circuit’s certification judgment. The decision rejected the nationwide class as
certified; it did not adjudicate whether any particular employee had experienced unlawful discrimination.
The majority did not decide whether the typicality and adequate-representation requirements were separately
satisfied. Opinion, conclusion and n.5; CB 818.
Reasoning
The majority required a connection among the reasons for the challenged decisions. A common biased test
or significant proof of a general discriminatory policy might supply that connection. Here the employer
formally prohibited sex discrimination, and plaintiffs did not show a companywide method of exercising the
delegated discretion. The majority recognized that discretionary employment systems can violate Title VII;
its conclusion concerned whether this evidence supported adjudicating all the proposed claims together.
Opinion, Parts II.A–C; CB 814–817.
Bielby’s testimony established susceptibility to bias but did not estimate how frequently stereotypes actually
affected employment decisions. The majority therefore found it inadequate to establish the asserted uniform
policy, even assuming it was admissible. It questioned the district court’s refusal to apply Daubert at
certification but did not resolve that evidentiary issue because the testimony was insufficient even if
considered. Opinion, Part II.B; CB 815–816; Daubert discussion in full opinion.
The majority found national and regional statistics insufficient to establish a shared cause of discriminatory
decisions at individual stores. Even common disparities would not necessarily identify the same challenged
practice in each location. It also considered the anecdotal accounts too limited and geographically uneven to
prove the claimed companywide policy. These conclusions concerned the inference needed for this
nationwide class, not a finding that every anecdote was false. Opinion, Part II.C; CB 816–818.
Both sides accepted that a single qualifying common question could suffice. The majority maintained that
differences among employees mattered because they defeated the proposed common answer; it denied
importing Rule 23(b)(3)’s predominance requirement. Justice Ginsburg disputed that distinction and
regarded the majority’s inquiry as an unduly demanding assessment of differences at the threshold stage.
Opinion, Part II.C; Ginsburg opinion, Part II.A; CB 818, 821–822.

In Part III of the full opinion, the Court explained that individualized monetary claims require the
protections associated with Rule 23(b)(3), including predominance, superiority, notice, and an opportunity
to opt out. Merely characterizing backpay as equitable, or combining it with a larger request for an
injunction, did not eliminate those requirements. Former employees’ lack of a prospective-remedy claim
further complicated use of Rule 23(b)(2). Opinion, Parts III.A–B, slip op. 20–26 (full opinion).
Title VII entitled Wal-Mart to present individual defenses to backpay claims, even after a showing of a
pattern or practice. The proposed sampling method could not deprive the company of that substantive right.
The Rules Enabling Act therefore barred certification premised on replacing the necessary individual
defense proceedings with extrapolation from a sample. Opinion, Part III.C, slip op. 26–27 (full opinion).
Separate Opinions
Justice Ginsburg, joined by Justices Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan, agreed with the Rule 23(b)(2) holding
but dissented from Part II. She viewed the common discretionary compensation and promotion system,
reinforced by corporate culture and supporting evidence, as presenting a disputed issue that could advance
all claims. She stressed the district court’s findings and the difference between the commonality threshold
and Rule 23(b)(3)’s more demanding requirements. Ginsburg opinion, Parts I–II; CB 818–822.
Ginsburg would have allowed the district court to consider Rule 23(b)(3) on remand rather than foreclosing
certification at Rule 23(a). She did not conclude that the proposed class necessarily satisfied predominance
or superiority. Her opinion also disputed the majority’s treatment of the statistical evidence and its
understanding of the relationship between discretion and common practices. Ginsburg opinion, introduction,
Parts I.B–C, II, and n.5; CB 819–822.
Notes
Keep the two holdings and their vote alignments separate. Commonality asks whether a material contention
can generate a common answer; predominance asks whether common issues outweigh individual ones. The
disagreement concerns how much evidence and cohesion the first inquiry requires. Class size alone is not the
stated legal test. Opinion, Part II; Ginsburg opinion, Part II; CB 813–822.
The excerpt centers on Rule 23(a)(2). The additional Part III analysis is included from the full opinion so the
brief accurately explains the disposition and the Justices’ partial agreement. Do not convert the reservation
about incidental monetary relief into a categorical holding prohibiting every monetary component of every
Rule 23(b)(2) class. Opinion, Part III.C; CB 810–822.
