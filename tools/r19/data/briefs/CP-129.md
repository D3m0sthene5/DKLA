Parties
Richard Will and the other petitioners were federal customs agents sued individually by Susan Hallock.
Hallock and her computer-software business had also pursued a separate Federal Tort Claims Act action
against the United States. The distinction matters because the agents invoked the judgment in the suit against
the government as a statutory bar to the individual-capacity constitutional action. The Supreme Court reviewed
the agents’ attempt to take an immediate appeal from the refusal to apply that bar. It did not adjudicate their
alleged responsibility for the destruction of the computers or data. Opinion, Part I.
Material Facts
According to the complaint, Susan Hallock owned a software business operated from home with her husband
Richard. Someone stole information associated with Richard’s credit card and used it to pay for access to a
child-pornography website. Customs agents investigating that site traced the payment to his card, obtained a
warrant, and seized the Hallocks’ computer equipment, software, and disk drives. No criminal charges
followed. When the equipment was returned, several drives were damaged and the stored data, including
business records and trade secrets, had been lost. The alleged loss forced the business to close. The Court
recounted these as allegations underlying the procedural dispute. Part I.
Hallock and the company alleged negligence in an action against the United States under the FTCA. The
district court did not address the tort merits because it held that the agents’ activities fell within the exception to
the statutory waiver of sovereign immunity for detention of goods. While that suit remained pending, Hallock
filed a separate Bivens action against the agents, alleging deprivation of property in violation of the Fifth
Amendment’s Due Process Clause. Although the casebook’s introductory bracketed summary calls the
individual action a negligence suit, the full opinion specifically identifies it as a constitutional Bivens action.
Part I; casebook p. 1224.
Once the FTCA suit was dismissed, the agents invoked 28 U.S.C. § 2676. The provision makes a judgment in
the specified FTCA action a bar to an action by the claimant against the government employee arising from the
same subject matter. Whether this particular dismissal triggered that provision was disputed. The appealability
question arose because the agents sought appellate review of the district court’s rejection of their defense
before the constitutional case had reached final judgment. Part I.
Procedural History
The FTCA suit was filed in July 2002 in the Northern District of New York and dismissed on the
sovereign-immunity exception. The agents then moved for judgment in Hallock’s separate action, relying on
the FTCA judgment bar. The district court denied the motion because it considered the earlier dismissal
procedural and therefore insufficient to activate the bar. Part I.
The agents appealed immediately. The Second Circuit first held that it had jurisdiction under the
collateral-order doctrine and then affirmed the refusal to apply the bar. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to

Issue
Is an order refusing to apply the FTCA’s judgment bar immediately appealable under the collateral-order
doctrine because the agents assert a right to avoid further trial? More specifically, does that defense protect an
interest sufficiently important and incapable of effective later review to justify an appeal before final judgment
under § 1291? Parts II(A)–(C).
Rule and Holding
A collateral order must conclusively determine the disputed question, resolve an important issue completely
separate from the merits, and be effectively unreviewable after final judgment. These are stringent
requirements that preserve the narrow scope of the doctrine. An assertion of a right to avoid trial does not by
itself satisfy the final requirement: the loss threatened by waiting must implicate a sufficiently substantial
interest, not simply the expenditure of time and money on litigation. Part II.
An order rejecting the FTCA judgment-bar defense does not qualify for immediate collateral-order appeal. The
bar’s interest in avoiding duplicative litigation resembles ordinary claim preclusion. It does not protect the
distinctive governmental or constitutional interests that justify immediate review of certain immunity and
double-jeopardy rulings. The Court therefore held that the Second Circuit lacked jurisdiction over the agents’
interlocutory appeal, without deciding whether the district court correctly interpreted the judgment bar. Parts
II(B)–(C).
Judgment
The Supreme Court vacated the Second Circuit’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss the
appeal for lack of jurisdiction. It did not affirm the appellate court’s interpretation of § 2676, order dismissal of
Hallock’s individual action, or find the agents liable. The challenged defense could not support this immediate
appeal of right; the underlying action remained to proceed subject to whatever properly available defenses and
later review applied. Part II(C).
Reasoning
The Court described the collateral-order doctrine as a practical construction of the final-decision requirement.
It covers a small class of conclusive determinations sufficiently separate from the merits and important enough
to be treated as final for appellate purposes. The narrowness is integral to the doctrine. If every adverse ruling
could generate an immediate appeal, the costs and harassment of successive appellate proceedings could
obstruct otherwise valid claims and undermine efficient adjudication. Part II.
Some established examples appear to support the agents’ argument. Orders denying absolute immunity,
qualified immunity, a state’s Eleventh Amendment immunity, or a criminal defendant’s double-jeopardy
defense can qualify for immediate review. In each setting, the asserted protection includes avoiding at least
some burden of undergoing the proceeding itself. Once trial occurs, an appellate court cannot erase that
historical fact. The agents argued that their judgment-bar defense should receive comparable treatment. Part
II(A).
The Court rejected the inference that every defense describable as a right not to be tried is therefore
immediately appealable. Successful limitations, jurisdictional, claim-preclusion, and pleading defenses also
allow a defendant to avoid trial. So does a valid settlement. If the irreversibility of incurred litigation costs were

The distinguishing question concerns the interest lost through delayed review. Absolute presidential immunity
protects separation-of-powers interests. Qualified immunity serves governmental effectiveness and officials’
ability to act reasonably when governing law is unclear. State sovereign immunity protects a state’s dignitary
interests. Double-jeopardy protection responds to the government’s powerful ability to subject an individual
repeatedly to prosecution. These public or constitutional interests extend beyond ordinary private convenience
and expense. Part II(B).
The customs agents’ reliance on government efficiency did not make their judgment-bar defense equivalent to
qualified immunity. Any lawsuit against government employees can consume time and distract from public
duties. Qualified immunity, as discussed by the Court, serves the more particular purpose of preventing
liability and trial burdens from deterring reasonable initiative in the face of unclear law. The judgment bar
arises for a different reason: a prior FTCA case has reached judgment. Its availability does not turn on the legal
clarity or reasonableness of the official’s original conduct. Part II(C).
The timing of the defenses illustrates that difference. Qualified immunity can be asserted when the official is
served in the individual action. The judgment bar cannot operate until a relevant FTCA judgment exists.
Hallock could have pursued the individual action alone, in which event the bar would never have arisen. Nor
could the agents invoke it while both actions were merely pending simultaneously. Thus, the bar did not
embody a general immunity from being sued over the challenged conduct. Part II(C).
Claim preclusion was the closer analogy. Both defenses depend on an earlier judgment and seek to prevent
duplicative litigation over overlapping entitlements or obligations. The Court acknowledged that the statutory
bar may be broader than traditional res judicata in some respects; it did not hold the two rules identical. Their
relevant similarity was functional: neither ordinarily carries the kind of public value requiring an immediate
appeal whenever the defense is rejected. The possibility of correcting an erroneous rejection after final
judgment is ordinarily sufficient under the appellate system. Part II(C).
The Court also addressed the apparent complication that double jeopardy likewise depends on an earlier
proceeding. That timing similarity did not control. The special protection against repeated use of prosecutorial
power explained its treatment under the collateral-order doctrine. A formal characteristic shared with an
established exception does not suffice when the underlying interests differ. Part II(C) & footnote.
The jurisdictional conclusion prevented resolution of the judgment-bar merits. The Second Circuit’s affirmance
of the district court could not remain a merits ruling merely because the result might later prove correct.
Appellate authority had to exist before the court could decide the statutory question. Vacatur and dismissal of
the appeal preserved that distinction between the availability of an interlocutory forum and the substantive
strength of the defense. Parts I, II(C).
Separate Opinions
No separate opinion is reported. Justice Souter’s opinion was unanimous. The agreement extended to the
collateral-order ruling; it should not be treated as a unanimous construction of when § 2676 applies to an FTCA
dismissal, because the Court expressly disposed of the case on appellate-jurisdiction grounds. Parts I–II.

lawsuits’ distinct legal bases, the procedural sovereign-immunity dismissal, the comparison with claim
preclusion, and the footnote explaining why double jeopardy remains different despite requiring an earlier
proceeding. The introductory narrative is drawn from the full opinion where it is more precise than the

Keep three propositions separate: the agents may have a potentially dispositive defense; the district court may
have erred in rejecting it; and an immediate appeal may nevertheless be unavailable. Will decides the third
proposition. In an appellate-jurisdiction problem, identify the asserted lost interest and explain why final
review cannot adequately protect it; repeating that a party has a right to avoid trial simply restates the claim
that the Court found insufficient. Parts II(A)–(C).
