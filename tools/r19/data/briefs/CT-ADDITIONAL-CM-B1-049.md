# Foley v. Yacht Management Group, Inc.
2009 WL 2020776 (N.D. Ill. July 9, 2009), No. 08 C 7254, Document 23 | Contracts

## Actual decision
Judge Virginia M. Kendall granted defendant Yale Turner’s motion to dismiss because the Northern District of Illinois lacked personal jurisdiction over him and consequently considered venue improper under the then-applicable section 1391(a). Turner had framed his pro se motion as one for improper venue, but its substance concerned personal jurisdiction, which the court addressed first. The complaint remained viable against the other defendants, Samir Jaber and Yacht Management Group. This memorandum did not adjudicate whether an enforceable boat-sale contract existed, whether an auction agent’s mistake justified rescission, or whether the remaining defendants breached a contract. Those are distinct from the contract questions posed in the assigned problem. (Actual memorandum 1, 8.)

## Allegations and transaction
Foley, an Illinois resident, alleged that Massachusetts resident Turner, Massachusetts resident Jaber, and Yacht Management Group breached a contract for a yacht auctioned on eBay. Jaber was the corporation’s president and treasurer, and the corporation’s principal place of business was Massachusetts. Turner had engaged Jaber and the corporation to sell his 1987, 55-foot Hatteras Convertible, Material Girls, through a brokerage service. On July 14, 2008, Foley traveled to Massachusetts, met Turner, and inspected the vessel. They hypothetically discussed a route to Chicago if Foley bought it, but did not negotiate its purchase. The court drew these facts from the complaint and Foley’s response, treating them as the plaintiff’s allegations for the jurisdictional motion, rather than findings following a contract trial. (1–2.)

Defendants listed the yacht in a reserve-price eBay auction on November 13. Turner called and emailed Foley in Chicago that day to inform him of the listing and invite a bid. Foley was the highest bidder when the auction ended November 21, but did not meet the undisclosed reserve, so no sale followed. On or about November 28 defendants listed the vessel again, this time with a $100,000 opening bid and no reserve. When that auction ended December 4, Foley’s $135,100 bid was highest and eBay emailed confirmation that he had won. Under the stated sale terms, a $2,000 deposit was due within twenty-four hours; Foley paid through PayPal, but defendants rejected and returned it. On December 6 he faxed and mailed a letter expressing his intention to complete the sale. Turner called him that day, but the sale was never completed. (2–3.)

The assigned account rounds the winning bid to $135,000, describes two unsuccessful reserve auctions before the no-reserve auction, identifies Yale and Donna Turner as owners, and attributes the no-reserve designation to an agent’s error. This actual memorandum describes one unsuccessful reserve auction followed by the November 28 no-reserve listing; its exact winning-bid allegation is $135,100. It does not decide or make findings about the agent-error account. Those differences are preserved as differences between the textbook problem and the judicial account, rather than silently combined into factual findings. (Assigned PDF 258, printed 213; actual 1–3.)

## Jurisdictional framework
The court placed the burden on Foley to make a prima facie jurisdictional showing. It could consider written submissions and resolved factual disputes in his favor. In a diversity case, it looked to whether an Illinois court could exercise jurisdiction. Illinois’s long-arm catch-all extended to constitutional limits; relying on then-existing Seventh Circuit precedent concerning the state and federal limits, the court treated the analysis as a federal due-process inquiry. Foley did not assert general jurisdiction, so the decision considered only specific jurisdiction arising from or relating to Turner’s forum contacts. (3–4.)

The governing question was whether Turner had purposefully availed himself of Illinois, deliberately engaging in significant activity or creating continuing obligations there, such that requiring him to defend this particular suit was consistent with fair play and substantial justice. Mere foreseeability that an out-of-state purchaser might respond to an internet auction did not itself answer that question. The court noted that the Seventh Circuit had not yet decided this particular eBay-auction issue and examined district-court decisions from other circuits. (4–5.)

## Why the auction and messages were insufficient
The court adopted the reasoning that an ordinary eBay auction does not, without further forum ties, purposefully direct a seller’s conduct toward the eventual high bidder’s state. The seller cannot choose the winning bidder’s identity or location. The resulting forum connection was therefore random, fortuitous, and attenuated rather than targeted. This was a conclusion about the ordinary auction mechanism and the contacts in the record, not a categorical immunity for all online sellers or all transactions involving eBay. (5–6.)

In its first note, spanning pages 5–6, the court discussed the Zippo sliding scale for a defendant’s internet activity. Cases applying that framework generally concerned websites owned or controlled by the defendant. eBay’s interactive platform did not itself establish the seller’s forum-directed conduct. The court therefore found Zippo unhelpful for this public auction and used traditional purposeful-availment analysis. It did not hold that website interactivity is universally irrelevant. (5–6 & note 1.)

Foley emphasized that Turner had gone beyond a public listing: he had called and emailed a person he knew lived in Illinois. The court considered those communications insufficient. They still left the identity of the actual auction winner outside Turner’s control. It contrasted McGuire, where a seller stopped an auction and chose to sell directly to the forum buyer. Turner allowed this auction to run to completion instead. The court’s second note supplies a further, transaction-specific distinction: the invitation messages concerned the first, November 13 reserve auction, while the claim arose from the later no-reserve auction. Foley did not assert, and the record did not show, a comparable invitation to that later auction. (7 & note 2.)

## Result and assigned significance
The court granted Turner’s motion alone. Its statement that the complaint remained viable against Jaber and Yacht Management Group was not a finding that the plaintiff had proved a contract against them or that jurisdiction over them had been litigated and sustained. Nor did the opinion impose liability on the seller at the winning bid. The assigned no-reserve-auction and mistake questions can be examined as pedagogical problems, but this cited judicial stage supplies a forum ruling rather than their merits resolution. (8.)
