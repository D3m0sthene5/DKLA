# United States v. Seacoast Gas Co.
204 F.2d 709 (5th Cir. 1953) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

The Fifth Circuit decided this appeal on May 29, 1953; the caption records denial of rehearing on July 20 without a separate rehearing opinion. Chief Judge Hutcheson wrote for a panel also comprising Judges Borah and Russell. The government appealed a judgment entered after a bench trial holding that Seacoast’s retraction cured its anticipatory breach before the substitute gas contract was signed. The appellate court reversed and remanded with directions to enter judgment for the government for the loss the breach caused. The report does not fix a dollar amount, contain a separate opinion, or publish a numerical voting breakdown. (204 F.2d at 709–712.)

## Contract, threatened termination, and replacement procurement

Seacoast undertook to supply gas to a federal housing project from April 15, 1947, to June 15, 1948. The government’s claim described an October 7 letter declaring that Seacoast would cancel as of November 15 because of an asserted government breach. The government immediately disputed any right to stop performance and announced that it would solicit alternative bids if the refusal persisted. Seacoast admitted in its pleadings and testimony that the facts were substantially as the government claimed. The case ultimately concerned the timeliness of retraction, not an adjudication sustaining Seacoast’s asserted justification for cancellation. (Id. at 709–710.)

By November 6, bids had been received, with Trion the low bidder. Zell, president of both Seacoast and Trion, attended the bid opening and refused a request to withdraw Seacoast’s notice. Regional counsel sent Seacoast a registered letter addressed to Zell saying that unless the government received a written withdrawal and assurance of continued performance within three days, it would accept Trion’s bid and hold Seacoast and its performance bond responsible for breach. Note 1 reproduces that entire notice, identifying the Newport News, Virginia project and its liquefied petroleum gas requirements for November 15 through June 15. (Id. at 710–711 & n.1.)

Seacoast did not respond within that period. Zell again refused to retract when asked on November 10, and the government accepted Trion’s bid and proceeded toward execution. On November 13, Zell finally told regional counsel that Seacoast admitted it had no right to cancel and rescinded its notice. The new contract was signed on November 17. The uncontradicted evidence explained the intervening delay by Trion’s failure to furnish its bond sooner, rather than a governmental intention to prolong Seacoast’s opportunity to retract. (Id. at 710–712.)

## Defense and the district court’s error

Seacoast argued that it withdrew the notice and promised continued performance before the government signed the substitute contract. On its view, the government had not substantially changed position or suffered damage before then, so the attempted withdrawal healed the breach. The district court accepted that approach: because the November 13 assurance preceded the later substitute agreement, the government could not recover. The government argued on appeal that its previous declaration and award already made the repudiation final. (Id. at 710.)

The parties agreed that an anticipatory breach occurred and that, absent timely withdrawal, Seacoast was liable for the government’s loss from the higher-priced replacement arrangement. They also agreed in general that a repudiator’s power to retract ends after the other party acts or relies upon the repudiation. Their disagreement lay in identifying the relevant action. Seacoast treated November 17 as the acceptance date; the appellate court held that this confused acceptance of Trion’s bid with signing the resulting contract. Acceptance had occurred November 10. (Id. at 711.)

## Holding and reasoning

Definite action treating the anticipatory breach as final closed the opportunity to retract. Such action could consist of filing suit or a firm declaration that the breach would be accepted if not withdrawn within a stated period. Here the government obtained bids, gave the three-day declaration, and then awarded the contract to the lowest bidder after Seacoast again refused to withdraw. Formal signing of the replacement agreement was not the event that first made the repudiation final. The court held that the opportunity ended with the three-day period, not the later signature. (Id. at 711–712.)

Seacoast’s claim that it did not receive the registered notice was immaterial in this record. The court gave two reasons: the government need not have provided a notice or grace period at all, and Zell personally repeated his refusal to regional counsel on November 10. This reasoning should not be expanded into a rule that every unreceived communication conclusively ends retraction irrespective of context. The opinion relied on the government’s definite treatment of the breach and Zell’s actual conduct. (Id. at 711.)

The corporate connection reinforced the result’s equity, but was not a substitute for the contract analysis. Zell was president and practically sole owner of both companies and had organized Trion specifically to participate in this bidding. He refused to withdraw Seacoast’s repudiation at the bid opening and award, then attempted withdrawal only a day or two before the new contract was signed. Treating a delayed bond as extending the old supplier’s power to undo the government’s declaration and award would misidentify the reason for the signing delay. The court therefore directed entry of judgment for the loss caused by Seacoast’s breach rather than affirming the defense judgment or ordering merely an unrestricted retrial. (Id. at 712.)
