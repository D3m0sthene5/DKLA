CT-CB-0026 | Morris v. Sparrow
Morris v. Sparrow
225 Ark. 1019, 287 S.W.2d 583 (1956)
Citation, court, and assigned scope
The Supreme Court of Arkansas decided the case on February 20, 1956; rehearing was
denied March 26. Justice Robinson wrote the opinion. The assigned excerpt appears at
printed pages 26–27. The full reported decision additionally discusses statutory authority for
specific performance and the alleged accord and satisfaction. Pinpoints below use the South
Western Reporter.
Parties and their objectives
Archie Sparrow, a cowboy and experienced horse trainer, sought delivery of a particular
horse, Keno, as promised compensation for ranch work. C. L. “Chip” Morris owned the ranch
and horse. Morris argued that Sparrow had not satisfied a condition concerning the quality of
his work, that money rather than delivery should be the remedy for personal property, and
that a final wage check had settled the obligations between them. Sparrow sought the horse
itself rather than merely its market price or a separate charge for training it. (287 S.W.2d at
584–86.)
Material facts
The parties met in Florida through rodeo activities. They first planned to travel to Morris's
Arkansas ranch and then to Canada, but changed the arrangement: Morris would go to
Canada while Sparrow stayed at the ranch to perform the necessary work. They agreed on
sixteen weeks of work for $400 in cash. Sparrow maintained that the compensation also
included the brown horse Keno. Morris acknowledged a possible transfer but said it
depended on satisfactory ranch work and asserted that Sparrow's work was inadequate. (Id.
at 584.)
Keno was essentially unbroken when Sparrow arrived. In his spare time Sparrow trained the
horse so that, with a little further training, it would be a first-class roping horse. That work
gave the particular animal a distinctive value to Sparrow. The court used this value to explain
why an award of the horse's market price would not supply the promised performance in an
adequate form. It did not find that all horses are categorically irreplaceable for every buyer.
(Id. at 584–85.)
After the sixteen weeks, the parties agreed that $167 remained owing on the cash
compensation. At a bank, Morris gave Sparrow a check for exactly that amount with a
notation that labor had been paid in full. Sparrow cashed it while continuing to claim the
horse. During the same encounter Morris was willing to turn over Keno on a new condition
that Sparrow not sell or dispose of him; Sparrow refused that restriction. The parties
therefore had agreed about the outstanding money without reaching the claimed
comprehensive settlement of the horse dispute. (Id. at 585.)

Procedural history and review posture
Sparrow brought a chancery action for specific performance. The chancellor ordered Morris
to deliver the horse. Morris appealed the decree. The Supreme Court considered the
availability of equitable relief, the factual contention about satisfactory performance, and
whether acceptance of the final check barred Sparrow's claim. It deferred to the chancellor's
superior opportunity to evaluate the witnesses and found no basis for saying the finding for
Sparrow was against the preponderance of the evidence. (Id. at 584–85.)
The appellate court was reviewing an existing decree after testimony, not deciding whether
allegations in a complaint might be true. Its treatment of the disputed condition therefore
rests on the trial court's factual resolution. Conversely, the check issue involved the
undisputed amount of the cash balance and the surrounding circumstances of its payment.
These distinct postures help explain why the court did not need to revalue the entire
employment bargain. (Id. at 585–86.)
Issues
May equity require delivery of a specific item of personal property when that item has
acquired a peculiar value through the promised recipient's training? Did the evidence require
rejection of Sparrow's contractual entitlement because his work was unsatisfactory? Finally,
did cashing the accurately calculated final wage check, labeled paid in full, conclusively
discharge the separate obligation concerning Keno? (Id. at 584–86.)
Rules and holdings
Although specific performance ordinarily is not the remedy for an agreement to sell personal
property, equity may grant it when special circumstances make damages inadequate.
Sparrow's training of this particular horse created the requisite peculiar and unique value.
The court also relied on an Arkansas statute authorizing a court of equity, when appropriate,
to order specific delivery of specific or ascertained goods. (Id. at 585.)
The chancellor's finding for Sparrow on the asserted condition of satisfactory work would
stand. Acceptance of the check for the undisputed cash balance did not establish accord and
satisfaction of the horse claim. Words such as paid in full must be evaluated in their setting;
they do not invariably extinguish every separate obligation between the parties. (Id. at
585–86.)
Actual judgment
The court affirmed the decree requiring delivery of Keno. It did not substitute a damages
award, order a new trial, or award a separate fee for the training. The affirmed decree gave
Sparrow the particular performance he had sought under the compensation agreement. (Id.
at 586.)

Reasoning
The recipient's work made this animal a distinctive object of the bargain
The court accepted the ordinary preference for damages in chattel transactions but found
special circumstances here. Sparrow had turned a green horse into a trained roping prospect
through his own time and skill. A market-value payment would not necessarily reproduce the
particular relationship between that labor, that animal, and the promised compensation. The
court accordingly recognized a right to receive Keno rather than forcing Sparrow to accept
the price a general market might assign him. (Id. at 584–85.)
The holding should not be reduced to a demand that the object have no ascertainable price.
Many individually valuable objects can be appraised; the question is whether money supplies
an adequate remedy for this promisee's loss of the particular performance. Nor does the case
order Sparrow to continue a personal-service relationship. His work had been done. The
decree required transfer of property from the party who had promised it as compensation.
(Id. at 585; analytical distinction concerning the decree's operation.)
Statutory authority reinforced equitable discretion
Arkansas Statutes § 68-1468 authorized specific performance of a seller's broken
undertaking to deliver specific or ascertained goods when a court of equity thought the
remedy appropriate. The court quoted that provision as an additional basis for relief. It did
not make specific performance automatic whenever the goods were identified. The
surrounding equitable inquiry—why ordinary damages were insufficient—remained
necessary to the reasoning. This statutory discussion is absent from the short casebook
excerpt but present in the full opinion. (Id. at 585.)
The asserted performance condition was resolved through the evidence
Morris's objection depended on his claim that delivery was conditional on a good job and that
Sparrow failed that condition. Both parties had testified before the chancellor. The Supreme
Court recognized the chancellor's advantage in assessing them and sustained the finding in
Sparrow's favor. The opinion does not announce a general doctrine that an employer's
satisfaction clause is always invalid, nor does it supply a detailed objective or subjective
satisfaction test. Its decision on this record concerns the factual foundation of Sparrow's
entitlement. (Id.)
The check settled an undisputed cash item rather than the disputed right to the
horse
Accord and satisfaction would require treating acceptance of the check as acceptance of a
proposed settlement. Several facts defeated the claim that the notation conclusively
accomplished that result. The check paid precisely the cash balance both sides
acknowledged. Sparrow never abandoned his entitlement to Keno. Morris himself was still
discussing delivery of the horse at the bank, albeit with a restriction Sparrow would not
accept. There consequently was no clear basis for reading the check as an agreed
replacement for the whole compensation package. (Id. at 585–86.)
The court recognized that a different setting—a disputed money amount and knowing
acceptance of a payment tendered in full settlement—might have a different consequence. It

Separate opinions
No separate concurrence or dissent appears in the reported opinion. The later denial of
rehearing is recorded in the report but does not supply a second merits opinion or a different
judgment.
Use in class and source qualifications
Identify the promised item, the facts that make substitution inadequate, and the precise
performance the decree orders. Then distinguish entitlement from remedy: uniqueness would
not entitle Sparrow to a horse never promised, and a promise alone would not automatically
establish the inadequacy of damages. Both the underlying agreement and the reason for
equitable relief mattered here.
The case also illustrates how a full-opinion brief can differ materially from an excerpt-only
summary. The assigned pages emphasize unique property and specific performance. The full
report adds the statute and the separate attempted discharge through the final check.
Including those issues explains why affirmance disposed of more than the availability of
equitable relief. No contemporary negotiable-instruments rule is substituted for the court's
1956 analysis.
