# Cabaness v. Thomas
2010 UT 23, 232 P.3d 486 (2010), amended opinion | Contracts

## Court, version, and result
The Utah Supreme Court’s amended opinion bears April 23, 2010. Justice Parrish wrote for herself, Chief Justice Durham, Associate Chief Justice Durrant, and Justice Nehring; Justice Wilkins dissented. The court affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded. The relevant employee-manual provisions created a contract as a matter of law, governmental immunity did not bar that legitimate contract claim, and emotional-distress damages remained potentially recoverable upon the required proof. Thomas’s intentional-infliction claim also survived summary judgment. Dismissal of that tort claim against Michaelis and of the wrongful-constructive-termination claim remained effective. The court did not award damages or establish breach on the disputed evidence. The amendment flag identifies changes to paragraphs 2, 17, 23, 47, 71, and 76 and notes 3, 7, and 9. (Amended opinion 1; ¶¶60–61, 70–76, 81–84.)

## Employment, promises, and alleged mistreatment
Kipp Cabaness worked for Bountiful Light & Power from March 1978 until January 2004 and was a line-working foreman from 1984 onward. His immediate supervisor, superintendent of operations Brent Thomas, lacked firing authority; director Clifford Michaelis possessed it. The city’s personnel manual applied to regular employees, a category that undisputedly included Cabaness. Its disclaimer excluded contractual obligations concerning salary, salary ranges, movement within those ranges, and employee benefits. Other provisions promised not to tolerate harassing, intimidating, offensive, or hostile workplace conduct, prohibited threats and violence, and barred harassment or retaliation against employees using the manual’s procedures in good faith. (¶¶4–5, 58–59.)

On summary-judgment review, the court viewed facts and inferences favorably to Cabaness. Employees described years of Thomas’s profanity, humiliation, unnecessary changes to work arrangements, threats of dismissal, and disregard of electrical-safety concerns. Examples included refusing a safety ground for work on a high-voltage line, cutting safety meetings short, requiring avoidable work in rain, and mocking personal matters. In July 2003, he ordered another employee to use a jackhammer near live electrical equipment despite warnings. The operation ended before anyone was injured. Cabaness was on medical leave during that event and learned of it later. These were allegations and supporting testimony used to test whether trial was required, not findings of final liability. (¶¶3, 6–11.)

Cabaness was diagnosed with workplace-related depression in 1997 and again in 2003. After a medically supported absence in summer 2003, Thomas publicly singled him out and threatened dismissal despite knowing of his depression. Employees had repeatedly complained to Michaelis; a 1997 response could reasonably have been understood as threatening termination for further complaints, although Michaelis disputed that intended message. A later investigation interviewed fifteen employees and resulted in a warning to Thomas that his intimidation had to stop. Cabaness resigned in January 2004; nearly all members of Thomas’s crew left between summer 2003 and spring 2004, primarily because of the alleged conduct. (¶¶12–14 & n.2, 43–44.)

## Procedure and evidentiary rulings
Cabaness sued Thomas and Michaelis for intentional infliction of emotional distress and the municipal employer for breach of the manual’s obligations and wrongful termination. The district court granted defendants summary judgment, denied his cross-motion on contractual formation, and rejected his postjudgment motion accompanied by two city employees’ affidavits concerning intent to make the manual binding. The Supreme Court reviewed summary-judgment legal questions for correctness and exclusion of evidence for abuse of discretion. It refused to strike his appellate brief: despite occasional emotional bias, its factual assertions and good-faith legal arguments were adequately supported by record citations and authorities. (¶¶15–21, 47–51.)

The postjudgment city-employee affidavits remained excluded. Newly discovered evidence required due diligence; the employer’s answer had already denied contractual formation, and the employee should have gathered evidence on intent before judgment. That conclusion did not foreclose a contract finding based on the manual itself. Separately, the court held that Knighton’s affidavit contained admissible personal observations of Thomas’s treatment of Cabaness, even though some statements were conclusory and the affidavit alone did not establish the tort. It left the admissibility of the Hawks report unresolved because the outcome did not depend on it. The safety committee’s factual findings fell within the public-records hearsay exception. (¶¶9 n.1, 22 n.3, 31–34, 48–51.)

## Continuing emotional-distress tort and the presence limit
The defendants sought to limit evidence to acts within four years before filing. The court adopted a continuing-conduct rule: when intentional infliction is alleged through an ongoing pattern, limitations run when the last injury occurs or the tortious conduct ceases, and recovery may concern the entire course of that conduct. It rejected a requirement that uncertainty about the exact initial onset of severe distress first be shown. Here the period began January 2, 2004, when resignation ended exposure; the March 2004 suit was timely. The recovery rule was expressly specific to this type of continuing emotional-distress tort, not automatically transferable to continuing nuisance or trespass. (¶¶23–28 & nn.4–6.)

Conduct outside the plaintiff’s presence could contribute to this tort only under particularly compelling circumstances, including an intention to injure the absent plaintiff. Although the jackhammer episode distressed Cabaness when he learned of it, he supplied no direct evidence that Thomas intended it to injure him. Excluding it from this tort theory was therefore proper. The amended opinion expressly preserved use of the episode, and other outside-presence conduct, for the different contract claim. (¶¶29–30 & n.7.)

## Different results for the two supervisors
The court declined to expand intentional-infliction liability on the argument that Michaelis failed to curb another person’s conduct. Cabaness had not persuasively supported such an extension; dismissal against Michaelis stood. The court did not deny that other jurisdictions might recognize a broader duty, but refused to adopt it on this presentation. (¶¶35–37.)

Thomas’s alleged course of conduct warranted trial. Ordinary insults or unfairness alone were insufficient, but their persistent combination with threats, deliberately stressful work arrangements, safety disregard, and renewed humiliation after known medical problems could reasonably be considered outrageous and intolerable. Reasonable minds could differ, so the district court could not resolve that element against Cabaness as a matter of law. This ruling authorized jury consideration; it did not itself find every tort element proved. (¶¶36, 38–45.)

## Why the manual created an enforceable contract
Public employment ordinarily arose under statute, but government could voluntarily assume additional contractual duties. An employment manual could supply sufficiently definite communicated promises under unilateral-contract principles, with employee service furnishing acceptance and consideration. Although formation commonly presented factual questions, the parties relied on the manual’s language and stipulated on cross-motions that no material formation facts were disputed. The court could therefore decide that issue as a matter of law without the excluded postjudgment affidavits. (¶¶54–57, 60 & nn.8–9.)

The disclaimer was limited, unlike a blanket disclaimer of every contractual obligation. The affirmative promises not to tolerate specified harassment and workplace hostility, considered together with that limitation, objectively manifested additional obligations. Some manual language remained policy or expectation; the court did not transform every provision into an enforceable promise. It held that the relevant protective provisions created a contract and carried an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. (¶¶58–61.)

## Governmental immunity
The governing pre-July-2004 immunity statute waived immunity for any contractual obligations. The employer argued that an emotional-anguish exception in the separate negligence-waiver provision restored immunity. The court rejected importing that exception into the independent contract waiver. Prior decisions involving other waivers arose from negligence, and legislative amendments expressly incorporated the exceptions into certain other provisions while leaving the contract waiver unqualified. The manual claim genuinely sounded in contract, rather than merely relabeling a tort to avoid immunity. (¶¶63–70 & nn.10–12.)

The opinion also corrected a prior appellate case’s immunity rationale while agreeing with its result: where no relevant waiver existed, general governmental-function immunity, rather than an imported negligence exception, supplied the ground. The governing statute’s age mattered; the court stated in note 12 that the 2008 renumbered legislation would produce a very similar analysis and the same outcome. Those are the court’s historical statutory observations, not a current-law certification. (¶¶67–70 & nn.11–12.)

## Emotional damages remained a factual question
Ordinary commercial disappointment did not normally support emotional damages. Drawing on its insurance-contract decision in Beck and other personal-contract authorities, the court nevertheless held that such damages could be available when both foreseeable from breach and explicitly within the parties’ contemplation at formation. The inquiry depended on the agreement’s nature and language and the parties’ reasonable expectations. It did not rest on a tort finding against either supervisor. (¶¶71–75.)

The particular promises protecting employees against harassment, intimidation, and a hostile environment were directed toward mental concerns. Their unusual character and specific wording made it possible that emotional damages were contemplated. That possibility defeated summary judgment, but the amended opinion remanded whether the parties actually contemplated them and whether recovery was consequently appropriate. Contract formation was resolved as law; breach and the necessary damages showing were not. The textbook’s shorthand statement that damages were available must be read with that conditional posture. (¶¶71, 76, 82.)

## Constructive termination and dissent
The protective manual provisions did not establish a restriction on termination procedures or require cause for termination. Neither side invoked a statutory exception to at-will employment, and Cabaness admitted he had not pleaded a clear and substantial public-policy violation. A hostile environment causing resignation might support consequential damages under the surviving contract theory, but did not establish the required exception for his separate wrongful-constructive-termination claim. Dismissal of that claim was affirmed. Note 13 expressed doubt that constructive departure would ever fit the specific contractual-procedures exception; it did not decide all future constructive-termination claims categorically. (¶¶77–81 & n.13.)

Justice Wilkins would affirm every district-court ruling. His brief dissent rejected extending Beck beyond insurance contracts and considered these manual provisions insufficient to create a contract. He supplied no longer separate analysis of the other issues. (¶84.)
