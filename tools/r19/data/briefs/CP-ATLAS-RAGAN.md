# Ragan v. Merchants Transfer & Warehouse Co.

**Course:** Civil Procedure

## Citation

Ragan v. Merchants Transfer & Warehouse Co., 337 U.S. 530–534 (1949). Supreme Court of the United States. Argued April 20, 1949; decided June 20, 1949. Justice Douglas wrote the Court's opinion, which Justice Reed announced. Justice Rutledge dissented through a writing published with Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 557–561. Assigned account: Civil Procedure casebook pp. 460–461, repository PDF p. 251, within note 4's trio of Erie decisions.

## Parties and material facts

Ragan sought damages from Merchants Transfer & Warehouse for injuries sustained in a highway accident on October 1, 1943. He invoked diversity jurisdiction in the federal District Court in Kansas and filed his complaint on September 4, 1945. Effective service occurred on December 28, 1945, after the two-year Kansas tort limitations period. An earlier summons issued September 7 and was subsequently served but quashed. Filing, issuance, and effective service therefore occurred at different times. 337 U.S. at 531 & n.2.

Kansas law specified when an action commenced for limitations purposes by reference to service. Its quoted provision included a sixty-day allowance for an attempt accompanied by faithful, proper, and diligent efforts to obtain service. The Court did not announce that all attempted service lacked legal effect; it accepted that this particular case would be barred in a Kansas court. Id. at 531–532 & nn.3–4.

## Procedural history

The defendant pleaded limitations and sought summary judgment. The District Court struck the defense and denied the motion, and Ragan obtained a verdict at trial. The Tenth Circuit reversed, holding after examining Kansas decisions that timely service was an integral part of the state limitations scheme and that summary judgment should have been granted. The Supreme Court granted certiorari. Id. at 530–532.

## Issue

Did timely filing under Federal Rule 3 toll the Kansas limitations period for this state-created tort claim in diversity, although Kansas law required service and the claim would be untimely in state court? Id. at 532–534.

## Rule and holding

The state limitations rule governed the life of the state-created cause of action. Proper commencement of an action in federal court under Rule 3 did not itself give that claim a longer life than Kansas law allowed. The Court accepted the Tenth Circuit's determination that the state service requirement was integral to limitations and held the action barred. Id.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court affirmed the Tenth Circuit's reversal of Ragan's trial recovery. The disposition sustained the limitations defense; it did not affirm the plaintiff's verdict or make an independent finding about the accident's merits. Id. at 534.

## Reasoning

Erie and York sought to prevent diversity litigants from obtaining materially different state-law rights merely by choosing a federal forum. It was conceded that Ragan could not recover in a Kansas court. Allowing him to recover in federal court would create the advantage those decisions rejected. Id. at 532.

The Court acknowledged that the Federal Rules determined how an action began in federal court and that Ragan properly followed that procedure. It nevertheless distinguished commencing the federal proceeding from preserving the state claim. The claim carried the same limitations burden and defenses in either forum. Local law determined when it accrued and ended. Id. at 533.

Ragan relied on Bomar v. Keyes, which treated filing as tolling. The Court distinguished that case because it involved rights under a federal statute. Ragan concerned a locally created claim. Nor did it matter that the state limitation expired after federal filing rather than before it: either way, extending the claim's life solely in federal court would add to the state-created right. Id. at 533–534 & n.5.

Finally, the Court declined to disturb the Tenth Circuit's careful reading of Kansas law. Its treatment of the service provision rested on that local-law interpretation, not on a newly invented national service-based limitations rule. Id. at 534 & n.6.

## Dissent and notes

Rutledge's shared dissent addressed Cohen, Woods, and Ragan together. He accepted Erie as a sound administrative principle for substantive state law but doubted its constitutional foundation and rejected the later assumption that federal doors must close whenever state doors closed. In his view, courts had extended Erie into incidents of federal litigation that belonged within congressional control. He recognized that procedure and substance could overlap and urged attention to the policies involved rather than a mechanical open-or-closed-courthouse test. He would reverse in Ragan. Cohen, 337 U.S. at 557–561 & dissent n.1.

The assigned note asks whether federal or state law determines commencement in a suit enforcing federal law. Ragan's explicit distinction of Bomar supplies the relevant contrast; its holding should not be converted into a rule that state service law always governs federal causes of action. Casebook pp. 460–461; 337 U.S. at 533.

The complete source includes all six Court footnotes and Rutledge's cross-referenced dissent, including its footnote. This brief describes the historical decision and does not present its treatment of Rule 3 as a substitute for later Rules Enabling Act or Erie decisions.
