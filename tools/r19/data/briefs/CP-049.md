Citation
Walker v. Armco Steel Corp., 446 U.S. 740 (1980). Supreme Court of the United States; argued January 8,
1980; decided June 2, 1980. Justice Marshall delivered the unanimous opinion. Assigned casebook pp.
480–484. Full opinion: Full judicial opinion . This account follows the assigned excerpt and uses the full
opinion’s footnotes for the express reservations about federal claims and the validity question. The Oklahoma
provisions discussed are those applied in this litigation.
Parties
Walker was a carpenter, the injured plaintiff, and the Supreme Court petitioner. Armco Steel manufactured the
nail that Walker alleged had injured him and was the defendant and respondent. Walker resided in Oklahoma;
the opinion describes Armco as an out-of-state corporation with its principal place of business elsewhere and
treats diversity jurisdiction as established. Armco sought dismissal because the state limitations requirements
had not been met. 446 U.S. at 741–743.
Material Facts
Walker alleged that, on August 22, 1975, he was driving a Sheffield nail into a cement wall in Oklahoma City
when the nail’s head shattered and struck his right eye, causing permanent injury. He attributed the defect to
negligent manufacture and design. These were allegations relevant to the underlying claim, not findings that
the Supreme Court made about Armco’s product or conduct. 446 U.S. at 741–742.
Walker filed in the Western District of Oklahoma on August 19, 1977, within the applicable two-year period.
The summons issued that day, but Armco’s authorized agent was not served until December 1. Oklahoma
treated an action as commenced for limitations purposes upon service. A timely filing could relate back to its
filing date if service followed within sixty days, but Walker missed that additional period as well. The federal
complaint thus was timely filed while the state requirements for stopping the limitations period were
unsatisfied. 446 U.S. at 742–743.
Federal Rule 3 provided that filing a complaint commences a civil action. Walker argued that this federal
commencement event also tolled the Oklahoma limitation. He expressly conceded that his action would be
barred in state court. Unlike York, there was no unresolved question about whether the state rule, if applicable,
would defeat this particular claim. 446 U.S. at 743, 748–749.
Procedural History
Armco moved to dismiss on January 5, 1978. The district court held that Oklahoma’s service requirement was
integral to its limitations scheme and dismissed under Ragan v. Merchants Transfer & Warehouse Co. It
rejected Walker’s contention that Hanna had implicitly overruled Ragan. 446 U.S. at 743.
The Tenth Circuit affirmed. It described the state statute and Rule 3 as directly conflicting, but considered itself
bound by Ragan because the Oklahoma provision was indistinguishable from the Kansas provision in that
earlier case. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve disagreement among the circuits and affirmed,
while explaining that the rules’ properly understood scopes did not directly collide. 446 U.S. at 744, 749–752.

Issue
Does Rule 3’s identification of filing as the commencement of a federal action displace a state rule making
service necessary to toll a limitations period for a state-law claim in diversity? Did Hanna’s enforcement of an
applicable Federal Rule undermine Ragan’s contrary result on state limitations tolling? 446 U.S. at 741,
748–750.
Rule and Holding
Rule 3 did not govern tolling of this state limitations period. In a diversity action it established the starting
point for federal procedural time requirements, while Oklahoma’s service provision controlled the separate
question of when a state-created claim ceased to be vulnerable to the limitations defense. The provisions could
operate alongside each other. 446 U.S. at 750–752.
Because no Federal Rule controlled the tolling issue, Erie and Ragan required application of the state
provision. Allowing a concededly time-barred state claim to proceed solely because diversity permitted a
federal filing would produce inequitable administration of state law. Hanna had distinguished Ragan rather
than overruled it. 446 U.S. at 749–753.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the Tenth Circuit. The district court’s dismissal as time-barred remained in place.
The decision resolved the limitations defense; it did not decide whether the nail was defective, whether Armco
was negligent, or what damages Walker otherwise could have recovered. 446 U.S. at 741–743, 753.
Reasoning
The Court began by comparing the case with Ragan. Both involved a complaint filed within a state limitations
period, service after both that period and a sixty-day extension, and a concession that the state courts would
reject the claim. The Oklahoma provision descended from the same statutory approach considered in Ragan.
Walker therefore needed more than a general invocation of Hanna to escape an otherwise directly applicable
precedent. 446 U.S. at 745–749.
Hanna’s Federal-Rule analysis begins only after the Rule has been construed to cover the issue. In Hanna, the
Federal Rule affirmatively permitted the very manner of service rejected by the state provision. Here, the word
commencement did not answer every legal question associated with starting a lawsuit. Rule 3 identified an
event for the operation of federal procedural rules; it did not say that filing tolls every state statute of
limitations. The Court found no indication that the Rule was designed to displace state tolling policy. 446 U.S.
at 747–751.
That construction did not rest on a general instruction to narrow Federal Rules whenever state law differs. The
Court expressly required their plain meaning to control. If a Rule’s proper meaning directly covers a disputed
issue, Hanna’s validity inquiry applies. Walker instead determined that this Rule did not cover the particular
limitations question at all. The distinction is between interpreting scope and balancing away a Rule that
unquestionably governs. 446 U.S. at 749–750 & n.9.
Oklahoma’s service requirement also had a specific connection to the substantive limitation. The state sought
to give defendants repose after a defined time and to prevent litigation of old claims when assembling evidence
would become unfairly difficult. Notice through service allowed a defendant to know that liability remained in
dispute and that evidence should be gathered. Treating service as part of the limitations scheme therefore
implemented those policies rather than imposing an unrelated technical step. 446 U.S. at 751–752 & n.12.

The Court explained that Oklahoma itself distinguished ordinary commencement from commencement for
limitations purposes. A separate state provision dealt with filing and issuance of summons, while the service
provision controlled the limitations question. The existence of two state commencement rules showed why
Rule 3’s federal commencement function could coexist with the state tolling requirement. One word could
identify different operative events for different legal purposes without creating a contradiction. 446 U.S. at 752
n.13.
Once the supposed federal conflict disappeared, the ordinary Erie reasons for following state law applied. Rule
3 could not prolong a state cause of action that the state limitation had ended. Even if Walker did not select
federal court to evade state law, permitting the suit would give him an advantage unavailable to an otherwise
identical state-court plaintiff. The Court relied on inequitable administration without requiring proof of actual
forum-shopping intent. 446 U.S. at 752–753 & n.15.
Stare decisis reinforced the result. Hanna had addressed Ragan and maintained the distinction between a
directly applicable Federal Rule and a Rule whose scope did not reach the issue. Walker effectively sought
reconsideration of both decisions’ treatment of that distinction. The Court found no adequate reason to do so.
Its affirmance therefore preserved Hanna’s methodology while rejecting Walker’s broad reading of Rule 3. 446
U.S. at 748–750.
Separate Opinions
Justice Marshall wrote for a unanimous Court. No separate opinion was filed. The Supreme Court’s reasoning
should nevertheless be distinguished from the Tenth Circuit’s: the court of appeals thought there was a conflict
but felt bound by Ragan, whereas the Supreme Court construed the provisions as governing separate questions.
446 U.S. at 744, 750–752.
Notes
The holding is limited to the state-law cause of action in diversity. The Court expressly left open Rule 3’s
tolling effect for a federal cause of action, whether the applicable limitation was supplied by federal law or
borrowed from state law. It also found it unnecessary to decide whether a hypothetical Rule 3 covering the
tolling issue would exceed the Enabling Act or Congress’s constitutional power. Those reserved questions
cannot be presented as holdings invalidating the Federal Rule. 446 U.S. at 751 n.11, 752 n.14.
Walker and Hanna require a carefully framed question. Whether delivery to a household resident is a
permissible method of service is different from whether service must occur by a state-law limitations deadline.
Federal compliance on the first question does not necessarily establish timeliness on the second. The
distinction follows from the purposes and scopes the Court assigned the provisions, rather than from a rule that
everything involving service is always procedural or always substantive. 446 U.S. at 749–753.
