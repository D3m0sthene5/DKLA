# Paternity of Cheryl
434 Mass. 23, 746 N.E.2d 488 (2001) | Contracts

## Holding and result
The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court vacated an order permitting a man to seek prospective relief from a paternity judgment upon confirmation that he was not the child's biological father. His challenge, first brought five and one-half years after voluntarily acknowledging paternity, was not made within a reasonable time under the domestic-relations version of Rule 60(b). Conclusive genetic evidence did not automatically undo legal paternity. Chief Justice Marshall wrote the April 24, 2001 opinion for the four-member court comprising Marshall, Greaney, Cowin, and Sosman; there was no separate writing. The case was remanded to the Probate and Family Court for proceedings consistent with denial of this relief. (23–25, 30–34, 39.)

## Acknowledgment, family relationship, and delayed challenge
Cheryl was born in August 1993. The Department of Revenue's child-support enforcement division sued on behalf of the mother and the public-welfare department to establish paternity, support, health insurance, and reimbursement of prior public assistance. The parents apparently had never married or lived together. The department offered genetic testing before judgment and did not require advance payment; reimbursement was sought only if testing identified the man as the biological father. On December 16, 1993, the unrepresented father instead voluntarily signed an acknowledgment recognizing its effect as a judgment and its support obligations, and agreed to pay $56.50 weekly. The mother also affirmed his paternity, and judgment entered that day. The record did not explain why he declined testing. (25–26 & nn. 3–5.)

For years he acted as Cheryl's father, regularly paid support, sought and obtained expanded visitation, and fostered a substantial relationship. His parents and siblings were known to her as grandparents, aunt, and uncles. The court carefully noted the sparse record, the absence of an evidentiary hearing and formal findings, and the largely unsworn basis for several asserted facts. Those qualifications matter particularly to the allegations against the mother. (26–27 & nn. 6–8.)

After the department obtained an increase to $90 weekly in May 1999, he moved five days later for genetic testing and amendment of the judgment if warranted. His own motion described reasons to doubt biological paternity long before then: reports from two friends in 1995, an alleged statement by the mother, perceived physical differences, and a 1996 medical report of low sperm count. The judge denied testing in July 1999, and a separate effort to reduce support was dismissed. In November the father privately took Cheryl for testing without the mother's knowledge. The report excluded him as biological father. A January 2000 motion then sought amendment or vacation and reimbursement of all support paid since 1993. Neither the mother nor department challenged the private test's scientific results, although the department sought to strike references to a test obtained after the judge had denied the requested testing order. (27–28 & nn. 8–9.)

## The order under review and the parties' theories
Following a nonevidentiary hearing, the Probate Court ordered further genetic testing and stated that, if nonpaternity were established, the father would qualify for prospective relief under Rule 60(b)(5), but not reimbursement for past support. It placed his interest in ending support above the child's interest in retaining the relationship and characterized continued enforcement as prolonging an apparent fraud. The order was stayed pending the mother's direct appeal. The actual case therefore involved an existing paternity judgment and a conditional prospective-relief order, rather than an ordinary contract action arising from a divorce settlement. (24, 28–29 & nn. 10–11.)

The mother argued that claims of mistake, newly discovered evidence, or fraud belonged under Rule 60(b)(1)–(3), with their one-year limits. Even if prospective relief under subsection (5) were available, she argued that the delay was unreasonable. She additionally invoked the amended paternity statute and other defenses. The father asserted scientific certainty made future enforcement inequitable under subsection (5), or justified the catchall subsection (6); he argued that time began running only when the 1999 tests revealed the supposed deceit. Although his counsel originally argued subsection (6), the trial judge's order relied solely on (5), as footnote 11 explains. (29–30.)

## Reasonable time and the child's interests
The court reviewed discretionary Rule 60 relief for abuse of discretion. Subsection (5), like subsection (6), required action within a reasonable time, assessed under the circumstances. Paternity judgments carried a compelling interest in finality and in children's stable emotional and financial support. The opinion considered the child's best interests and the established parent-child relationship important beyond the genetic connection itself. These were reasons for enforcing the procedural timeliness requirement here, not a declaration that genetics can never affect legal paternity. (30–32 & nn. 13–15.)

The father had declined available testing before acknowledging paternity and then failed to challenge at the earliest reasonable opportunity despite his own account of increasing doubts. During that delay he enjoyed the relationship while Cheryl relied on him as father. The record did not establish that his original acknowledgment was conditioned exclusively on biological paternity; the court declined to assume that every acknowledgment has that sole purpose. The private test did not erase the years of unexplained inaction. Consequently Cheryl's interests outweighed his interest in reopening this judgment. The earlier decision relied on below had merely assumed that prospective relief might be available and had not resolved that question in comparable circumstances. (32–34 & n. 16.)

## Catchall relief and fraud upon the court
Subsection (6) supplied no alternative. It too required reasonable timing, and the father's asserted mistake, new evidence, fraud, and inequitable prospective enforcement fell within the other subsections rather than presenting an independent catchall ground. The court did not decide the department's broader argument that subsection (5) is categorically unavailable whenever a claim could be framed under (1)–(3); footnote 18 reserves that proposition. It likewise did not resolve the mother's res-judicata defense on the merits. (34–35 & nn. 17–18.)

The father also invoked the provision preserving power to set aside a judgment for fraud upon the court. The doctrine required clear and convincing proof of an unconscionable scheme corrupting the judicial process, with examples such as bribing judicial participants or misconduct involving officers of the court. Mere nondisclosure of a pertinent fact, without more, did not meet that demanding standard. Even assuming the mother knew he was not the biological father, her silence would not establish this kind of fraud. More fundamentally, the record did not establish the assumed knowledge or deception: the mother's affidavit affirmed good faith, and the court expressly rejected the lower judge's unsupported inference that she knew someone else was the biological parent. Thus alleged maternal fraud must not be reported as a proven finding. (35–36, 38 & n. 22.)

## Qualifications, reserved questions, and exact disposition
The court recognized that it could not force continuing affection or prevent disclosure of genetic parentage, but could protect the child's financial security and legal rights. It acknowledged the father's substantial interests and the State's role in requiring a public-assistance applicant to identify a father. A different result might follow where testing was unavailable before acknowledgment or where a man promptly challenged upon receiving contrary information. Footnote 21 urged the department to require testing before acknowledgments in the described State-initiated circumstances. (36–38 & nn. 19–21.)

The court did not resolve retroactive application of the 1998 statutory one-year limitation, the mother's equitable-estoppel and unclean-hands theories, or whether the father's motions qualified as statutory challenges. Its testing discussion advised consultation with the child's legal custodian or a court order in most circumstances, but did not rest the outcome on unclean hands. The decision vacated the Probate Court's prospective-relief order and remanded, rather than vacating the original paternity judgment, ordering reimbursement, or freeing the father from support. (38–39 & nn. 23–24.)
