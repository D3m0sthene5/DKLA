# Richardson Lumber Co. v. Hoey
219 Mich. 643, 189 N.W. 923 (1922) | Contracts

## Court, date, and posture
The Michigan Supreme Court decided the case on October 2, 1922; the report records denial of rehearing on November 2. Justice McDonald wrote, and Chief Justice Fellows and Justices Wiest, Clark, Bird, Sharpe, Moore, and Steere concurred. The sellers had won an assumpsit action in the Alpena circuit court for the price of cedar ties destroyed by fire. The buyers brought error, challenging both the passing of title and the treatment of their concealment and mistake defenses. The Supreme Court reversed and granted a new trial with costs to the buyers. It did not finally rescind the sale or determine that the alleged mutual mistake had been proved. (643–645, 648–650.)

## Agreement, loss, and competing accounts
On September 16, 1916, Richardson Lumber offered to sell Western Cedar & Lumber all its cedar ties on the Boyce property at Essexville. The estimated quantity was 11,500, the price was forty-two cents per tie on the ground, and the buyers could reject none. Cash payment would follow counting. Hoey accepted for Western Cedar. The entire identified pile was sold; counting would establish the amount payable, rather than select a subset of ties or require the seller to finish preparing them. (644, 646–648.)

The buyers additionally claimed an oral understanding that title would remain with Richardson until the ties were counted and paid for. According to their account, Richardson proposed an immediate check for 11,500 ties and removal whenever convenient; Hoey instead proposed counting and payment through the Bay City office. Richardson then said the ties remained its property until payment, and Hoey agreed that he would not touch them before counting and paying. This alleged further agreement was disputed and was submitted to the jury. (644–646.)

The buyers began loading and removing ties on September 19. They had removed 1,721 when fire destroyed the uncounted remainder. They tendered payment for those 1,721 at the contractual unit price as full settlement; Richardson refused and sued for the whole quantity. The seller obtained judgment for its full claim. The report supplies neither a separately verified total quantity nor an exact dollar judgment, so the estimated quantity is not converted into a definitive damages award here. (645.)

The buyers advanced three defenses: title had not passed as to undelivered ties; Richardson knew and concealed that the ties were situated in imminent danger of fire; and the parties were mutually mistaken about that danger, or the buyers were mistaken while Richardson knew the truth. Some testimony supported the claim that an underground fire was already burning close to the pile when the contract was made and later consumed it. The existence, timing, and consequences of that fire remained matters for the factfinder. (645, 648–649.)

## Title passed under the written sale unless the further agreement was proved
Intention controlled the passing of title. Under the Uniform Sales Act, an unconditional sale of specific goods already in a deliverable state passed property to the buyer when the agreement was made, even if payment or delivery was postponed. By contrast, where the seller still had to put goods into a deliverable state, title awaited that work. These ties fell within the first rule: the entire identified pile was sold, with nothing left for the seller to do to prepare it for loading and delivery. Counting to calculate the price did not prevent passage of title. (646–648.)

The court reached the same conclusion under its earlier decisions. Measurement can be necessary to identify goods where only part of a larger mass is sold; it is unnecessary to identify the subject matter when the whole mass is sold at an agreed unit price. The remaining arithmetic did not require a further meeting of minds. Accordingly, the trial judge correctly instructed that the written contract passed title when made unless the jury found the subsequent agreement retaining title in the seller. The appellate court did not itself find that such an oral reservation existed. (647–648.)

## Existing danger could support mistake despite passage of title
The court also approved the instructions on fraudulent concealment and rejected numerous evidentiary and party-liability assignments as without merit. Its basis for reversal was the refusal to submit mutual mistake to the jury. The circuit judge had regarded the alleged mistake as insufficient because it did not concern the identity or existence of the ties themselves. That restriction was too narrow. A collateral fact materially inducing the sale could support relief when both parties were mistaken about it. (645, 648–650.)

The decisive distinction was temporal. A fire originating after the sale would be a later casualty and would not affect the buyers' liability under the sale as construed. But an underground fire already threatening the pile at formation was a present fact. If the parties contracted believing that the ties were safe and could be removed, while an existing fire made destruction before removal through reasonable diligence imminent, their mistake could go to the entire consideration. The buyers were entitled to have the supporting evidence considered, even though the ties still physically existed when sold. (648–649.)

The court's rule remained conditional. If both parties believed there was no imminent fire danger, but such danger actually threatened destruction before the buyers could reasonably remove the ties, the buyers could rescind. If discovery left no time or opportunity to rescind before the threatened destruction occurred, they would be liable only for the ties actually delivered. The court did not excuse payment for the 1,721 ties removed, declare that any dangerous location automatically defeats a sale, or resolve the disputed factual prerequisites itself. (649–650.)

## Disposition and assigned problem
The circuit-court judgment was reversed, a new trial was granted, and costs were awarded to the defendants. The title instruction and concealment instructions survived review; the mistake theory required jury consideration. (648–650.)

The assigned problem at printed page 1160 simplifies this litigation to a seller and buyer, states that the buyer immediately assumes the risk of loss, and asks whether the nearby underground fire presents mistake or erroneous prediction. The actual contract contains no express risk-of-loss sentence reproduced in the opinion. The actual court addressed title through the written sale, the statute, and the disputed oral reservation, then separately recognized a potentially material mistake about a danger already existing at formation. The assignment's immediate-risk premise is therefore part of its adapted problem, while the conditional new-trial holding above describes the judicial decision.
