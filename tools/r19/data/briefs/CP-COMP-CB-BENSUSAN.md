# Bensusan Restaurant Corp. v. King
937 F. Supp. 295 (S.D.N.Y. 1996) | Civil Procedure

## Court, question, and disposition

District Judge Stein decided No. 96 Civ. 3992 (SHS) on September 9, 1996. The owner of New York's Blue Note jazz club sued the operator of a Missouri club with the same name, alleging trademark infringement, dilution, and unfair competition. The Missouri operator's website was accessible in New York, but the court found neither an adequate New York long-arm basis nor constitutionally purposeful contacts. It granted the Rule 12(b)(2) motion and dismissed the complaint. It did not resolve the parties' competing trademark rights or award damages. This brief addresses that district-court decision, not a later appellate stage. Stein wrote the sole opinion, with three footnotes and no appendix. (297–301.)

## Two clubs and a locally fulfilled ticket transaction

Bensusan Restaurant Corporation was a New York corporation that created the Blue Note jazz club in New York City, operated other clubs internationally, and owned the federally registered mark. Richard King lived in Columbia, Missouri, where he owned a small club also called the Blue Note. (297.)

In April 1996 King placed a website on a Missouri server to promote his club. The site allegedly displayed a logo substantially similar to Bensusan's. Anyone with Internet access could view its club information, event calendar, and ticketing instructions without an access code. The instructions listed Columbia ticket outlets and a telephone number for charging tickets; tickets ordered by telephone had to be collected at the Missouri box office on the night of the show. King did not mail or transmit tickets to New York customers. This local fulfillment feature was central to the court's assessment of where an alleged infringing sale would occur. (297, 299.)

Initially, the site distinguished the Missouri club from the famous New York club, recommended visiting the latter when in New York, and linked to Bensusan's website. After Bensusan objected, King removed the visit recommendation and hyperlink. The opinion did not turn that reference or link into a finding that King commercially targeted New York. Note 2 explains the hyperlink technology, while note 1 describes the contemporary Internet. Those technical descriptions are part of the 1996 setting. (297–298 & nn. 1–2.)

## The plaintiff's favorable pleading standard still required a statutory basis

No discovery or evidentiary hearing had occurred. Bensusan needed only a prima facie showing of jurisdiction and could rely on factual allegations. The court read its complaint and affidavits favorably and resolved doubts for the plaintiff, even where King disputed jurisdictional facts. It could consider materials outside the pleadings without converting the jurisdiction motion into summary judgment. (298.)

That favorable treatment did not eliminate the need to satisfy New York's long-arm statute. Bensusan invoked § 302(a)(2), covering an in-state tortious act, and § 302(a)(3)(ii), concerning an out-of-state tort causing in-state injury when the specified foreseeability and interstate-or-international-revenue requirements were met. The court examined each route separately. (298–300.)

## Access to information was not an in-state infringing offer

For § 302(a)(2), the court applied authority locating trademark infringement where a product is passed off to a deceived purchaser. An infringing offering for sale in New York could suffice even without a completed sale. The question was therefore whether King's website and telephone number constituted such an offer in New York. (299.)

The court concluded that they did not. A New York user had to find and access the site, telephone Missouri, and collect a ticket in Missouri. Even if confusion existed concerning the two clubs, the resulting transaction described by these allegations would occur in Missouri. Merely learning about an allegedly infringing product was different from a defendant making an effort to advertise, promote, sell, or otherwise target it in New York. There was no allegation of infringing goods shipped into the state or other infringing conduct King caused there. Section 302(a)(2) therefore supplied no jurisdiction. The court did not find that Internet information could never help establish an in-state tort; it assessed this website and transaction. (299.)

## The out-of-state-tort provision failed in several respects

Section 302(a)(3)(ii) required substantial revenue from interstate or international commerce, not merely involvement in interstate activity. Bensusan relied on King's presentation of nationally known bands but did not allege the required substantial revenue. King's affidavit stated that ninety-nine percent of patronage and revenue came from local Columbia residents, primarily university students; most nonlocal patrons had a present or former connection to the area. The distinction was between participation in commerce and deriving substantial revenue from it. (299–300.)

Knowledge that Bensusan operated in New York also did not by itself establish the required reasonable expectation of forum consequences. The court required a discernible effort to serve the New York market, directly or indirectly. Nor did Bensusan's conclusory allegation of loss in New York establish the relevant injury: financial repercussions merely because an injured business was domiciled there were not enough under the authorities the court applied. Thus multiple components of the statutory route were missing. (300.)

Bensusan also argued that King could foresee New Yorkers accessing the site and should have restricted access geographically. The court did not resolve the technical feasibility of restricting access. Foreseeability and failure to avert a forum consequence were insufficient without the required jurisdictional conduct. The decision therefore did not make website operators' failure to block a state equivalent to purposeful solicitation there. (300.)

## An independent constitutional ground

The statutory failure was not the opinion's only conclusion. The court added that jurisdiction would violate due process even if the long-arm statute were satisfied. King had not purposefully sought New York's benefits: he had simply made a site available to anyone able to find it. Worldwide accessibility and predictable exposure were different from directing conduct toward the particular forum. The opinion analogized creation of a site to placing a product into the stream of commerce, citing the purposeful-direction reasoning of an Asahi plurality. It found no allegation that King encouraged New Yorkers to access the site, conducted business there, or otherwise had a forum presence. (300–301.)

The court distinguished CompuServe's different Internet relationship. There, a Texas user subscribed to an Ohio service, made a separate agreement to sell software through it, advertised through it, and repeatedly sent software to Ohio. Those facts showed reaching out to and maintaining contacts with Ohio, unlike King's site. Note 3 preserves CompuServe's express reservation about suit everywhere the software was purchased or used. Bensusan did not transform the Internet into a zone beyond personal-jurisdiction law; it required identifiable contacts rather than mere accessibility. (301 & n. 3.)
