# Selmer Company v. Blakeslee-Midwest Company
704 F.2d 924 (7th Cir. 1983) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural setting

On March 29, 1983, the Seventh Circuit affirmed summary judgment for Blakeslee-Midwest, Westinghouse Electric, and Federal Insurance. Judge Posner wrote for Chief Judge Cummings and Judge Cudahy; no separate opinion appears. In this diversity case applying Wisconsin contract law, a financially distressed subcontractor could not reopen its settlement merely because it urgently needed money and the general contractor refused to increase its offer. The court also rejected the subcontractor's specific retainage theory against the undisputed payment evidence. This was a ruling that the asserted economic-duress ground presented no triable issue, rather than a finding after trial that the contractor had performed its original obligations. (926–929.)

## Construction, extra work, and settlement

The facts assumed true for the appeal were that Selmer agreed to erect prestressed concrete materials for $210,000. General contractor Blakeslee-Midwest breached its obligations, including supplying materials late. Selmer could have terminated without penalty, but instead orally agreed to finish if Blakeslee-Midwest paid the additional costs attributable to its defaults. After completion Selmer demanded $120,000. Blakeslee-Midwest offered $67,000 and would not increase that offer. Selmer accepted while in desperate financial circumstances. (926.)

Two and a half years later, Selmer sued, asserting that its actual additional costs were $150,000 and that the earlier $120,000 demand had itself been a compromise proposal. It sought the $150,000 less the $67,000 received, together with consequential and punitive damages. Although it described the action as tort, the court regarded the substance as enforcement of the alleged oral reimbursement promise. The label did not affect the decisive issue: Selmer conceded that a valid settlement barred its action. Liability of the other defendants was derivative and required no separate analysis. The district court entered summary judgment on the settlement, and Selmer appealed claiming economic duress. (926.)

## Why financial pressure did not invalidate this settlement

Posner framed duress around the character of the inducement that law should discourage, rather than the intensity of the promisee's desire to escape pressure. A victim may desperately want to make a promise when faced with a threat; describing that choice as involuntary does not identify why enforcement should be refused. Selmer characterized the offer as a demand to abandon $53,000 or receive nothing. The same statement could be described as an offer of $67,000 for a release. Treating every unyielding settlement offer as an unlawful threat would allow a dissatisfied party to reopen ordinary compromises. (926–927.)

The court nevertheless rejected an absolute rule that threatening breach can never be duress. It used Alaska Packers to explain why opportunistic modification can undermine contractual security when a party cannot obtain adequate legal relief or a practical substitute. There the employer's remote, short fishing season left it unable to replace workers who demanded more money without a valid reason. Although the older judgment formally rested on lack of fresh consideration, Posner understood its concern as coercion through breach. Permitting avoidance in such circumstances protects, rather than weakens, confidence in contracts. (927.)

Capps supplied the contrasting settlement example. In that case, as the opinion described it, a defendant acknowledged owing a $157,000 commission but extracted a release for $5,000 from a claimant facing immediate repossession and foreclosure. The absence of a genuine debt dispute, the acknowledged obligation, and a payment under three percent of the debt supported duress. The court saw no reason Wisconsin would reject that analysis. Selmer's situation lacked those features: Blakeslee-Midwest did not acknowledge a $120,000 debt, and its offer exceeded half the demand. The compromise was not unreasonable on its face. (928.)

Financial need alone could not justify a trial on avoidance. Otherwise precisely those people who most needed immediate cash, and could least afford litigation, would lose the ability to make a binding settlement. The court recognized that a different analysis may apply when the opposing party causes the financial distress. But Selmer's allegation that added expenses created its difficulty did not overcome its freedom to leave the project when the original contract was breached. It voluntarily continued in return for promised additional payment; unlike the employer in Alaska Packers, circumstances did not prevent it from walking away. The court did not know whether Selmer's inability to withstand the subsequent payment dispute resulted from inadequate capital, excessive borrowing, or something else, and declined to attribute that necessity to Blakeslee-Midwest on this record. (928–929.)

## Retainage theory and exact outcome

Selmer also asserted duress of goods: the contractor allegedly withheld $21,000 of retainage to force acceptance. The contract permitted withholding ten percent of advance payments as completion security, with the retained amount payable upon completion. Unjustifiably holding an otherwise due retainage payment solely to exploit known desperate circumstances might constitute duress. The court therefore examined the allegation rather than declaring retainage coercion legally impossible. (929.)

Selmer's precise claim was that the $67,000 offer included the $21,000 retainage. If it had previously received ninety percent of the $210,000 price, that account would produce total payments of $256,000. The opinion states instead that Selmer received $280,000 and that the uncontradicted evidence showed payment of the full contract price plus the $67,000 extras. It treated those payments as discrediting the allegation that the settlement withheld the retainage. The report does not reconcile the $280,000 figure with the $277,000 sum of $210,000 and $67,000; this brief preserves that discrepancy rather than silently correcting the evidence. (929.)

The alternative hypothetical—offering the retainage plus $67,000 only if Selmer released all further claims—was expressly distinguished from what Selmer actually alleged. Its subsequent subcontractor-lien waiver also indicated payment in full and supplied additional evidence against duress; the waiver was signed after receipt of the supposedly coerced $67,000. The court affirmed summary judgment. It awarded neither further reimbursement nor consequential or punitive damages. (929.)
