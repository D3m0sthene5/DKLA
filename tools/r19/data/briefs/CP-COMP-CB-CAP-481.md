# Lunn v. Commonwealth

**Citation:** 477 Mass. 517 (2017). **Court and date:** Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, July 24, 2017; argued April 4; No. SJC-12276. **Opinion:** By the Court, with Gants, Lenk, Hines, Gaziano, Lowy, Budd, and Cypher participating; no separate writings. The Sheriff of Suffolk County intervened. **Course:** Civil Procedure — civil arrest and the source of state officers' detention authority. **Assigned:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 605, printed pages 1195–1196, Note 1 under Civil Arrest.

## Material facts and posture

Lunn was arraigned in the Boston Municipal Court in October 2016 on an unarmed-robbery charge. A federal immigration officer had issued a civil immigration detainer the day before. Initial bail was $1,500, which Lunn did not post. The detainer requested that state custodians keep him for up to forty-eight hours after he otherwise became entitled to release, allowing federal authorities to assume custody. The detainer did not itself explain his custody before dismissal: he was held in lieu of bail and, for a period, served a sentence in a separate case. (519–520 & nn.4–6.)

The criminal charge was reduced with his consent to larceny from a person. Bail was later reduced to $750; although financially able to post it, Lunn declined because he believed the detainer would prevent release anyway. At the second scheduled trial date, February 6, 2017, the Commonwealth remained unready, and the judge dismissed for lack of prosecution. With no Massachusetts charge pending, counsel requested release. The judge declined to act; the amended docket accurately recorded that response, rather than a denial. Court officers nevertheless kept Lunn in a holding cell until federal officials took custody several hours later. The record did not specify the precise duration. (520–521 & nn.6–8.)

The next morning counsel filed a supervisory petition under G. L. c. 211, § 3, seeking release and alleging absence of detention authority and constitutional violations. Lunn was already in federal custody, making the requested state release moot. The single justice reserved and reported the important, recurring, time-sensitive issues likely to evade review. Thus the full court addressed state authority despite mootness. It did not order immediate release from federal custody. (521.)

## Issue and holding

**Could Massachusetts court officers arrest and hold an otherwise releasable person solely on a federal civil immigration detainer? No.** Neither Massachusetts common law nor its statutes authorized that arrest. The court declined to create inherent or implicit authority and rejected construing 8 U.S.C. § 1357(g)(10) as an independent affirmative grant of arrest power absent state authority. (518–519, 528–537.)

The detainer was a civil request concerning possible removal, not a criminal warrant, criminal detainer, or charge. Removal proceedings remained civil even when a crime supplied a ground for removal. The record did not establish that Lunn had committed an immigration crime. His earlier removal order had not been executed because his country of origin declined travel documents, according to the United States' representation. The opinion also noted his later release from federal detention. These facts should not be converted into a finding that his presence constituted a criminal offense. (521–526 & nn.10–17.)

## Why voluntariness did not supply authority

Federal detainers expressly requested cooperation and imposed no mandatory state obligation. The United States conceded voluntariness. The federal act did not authorize commanding state detention, and the court discussed anti-commandeering concerns. A regulation's use of “shall” in its timing provision did not make compliance compulsory: the government conceded that it specified a maximum detention period. (526–527 & n.18.)

Voluntariness answered whether federal officials could require cooperation; it did not answer whether state officers had lawful power to undertake it. Holding Lunn after the state basis for custody disappeared constituted a new arrest under Massachusetts law. This was custody to await federal collection, with no investigatory purpose, rather than a brief investigative stop. The government acknowledged the arrest characterization. (527–528.)

## State common law and statutory limits

Court officers on courthouse premises had the arrest powers of Massachusetts police officers. Common law authorized warrantless arrests for felonies on probable cause and, in specified circumstances, misdemeanors involving an ongoing breach of the peace in the officer's presence. It supplied no general power to arrest for civil matters or specifically for civil immigration matters. These common-law rules were not a statement that statutes could never authorize additional arrests. (528–531 & nn.19–20.)

The court examined statutes authorizing varied arrests and noncriminal detention, including protective custody, emergency hospitalization, civil contempt, and material-witness detention. None authorized this detention solely on a federal civil immigration detainer. Enumerated authority for particular civil custody did not create a general civil-arrest power. (531–532.)

The United States urged inherent authority to cooperate. The court assumed without deciding that such a theory remained viable under federal law after Arizona v. United States, but refused to adopt it as Massachusetts law. State-law silence was insufficient to create an undefined arrest power without protections available to other arrestees. Otherwise an officer could have greater common-law authority to arrest for a federal civil matter than for a state criminal misdemeanor. The court left any carefully defined new authority to the legislature. It expressed no view on the constitutionality or preemption of a hypothetical future statute. (532–534 & nn.23–25.)

## Federal cooperation provision and express reservations

Section 1357(g) allowed written agreements under which trained state officers performed federal immigration functions, subject to statutory conditions. Subsection (g)(10) permitted specified communication and cooperation without such an agreement. Read in context, it preserved cooperation that state law authorized and state officers chose to provide; it did not affirmatively confer power to make arrests otherwise unauthorized by state law. The government's cited cases principally concerned unilateral action versus federal cooperation and preemption, rather than this distinct state-authority question. (534–537 & n.27.)

The case involved no written 287(g) agreement, and the court expressly reserved detention under such an agreement. It also reserved authority when an officer receives probable cause of a federal criminal offense. Revised detainer forms and accompanying federal administrative warrants did not transform civil removal into a criminal process or supply state arrest authority. Those warrants were issued administratively to federal officials, without judicial authorization. The court did not resolve whether an arrest, if authorized, would comply with the federal or Massachusetts constitutions. Notification and transfer requests in the detainer were outside the issue decided. (519 n.2, 524 n.16, 524–526 n.17, 531 nn.21–22, 535 n.26.)

## Exact disposition and textbook comparison

**Remanded to the county court for a judgment dismissing Lunn's case as moot and declaring the absence of Massachusetts authority to hold an otherwise releasable individual solely on a federal civil immigration detainer.** No damages, criminal acquittal, or broad injunction against federal immigration enforcement was ordered. (537.)

The textbook accurately uses Lunn to illustrate the need for state authority for civil arrest. Its executive-order history and broader discussion of debt imprisonment are editorial context.
