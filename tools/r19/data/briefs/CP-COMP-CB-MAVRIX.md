# Mavrix Photo, Inc. v. Brand Technologies, Inc.

**647 F.3d 1218 (9th Cir. 2011), decided August 8, 2011.** Civil Procedure — nationwide commercial websites, purposeful direction, and specific versus general jurisdiction. Judge William A. Fletcher wrote for Judges Wardlaw, Fletcher, and District Judge Barbara M. Lynn, sitting by designation; there was no separate opinion. Assigned: CivPro PDF page 126, printed page 203, Note 4. Checked source locators below use the printed labels on the complete twenty-two-page court opinion, rather than inventing a conversion to reporter pagination.

## The photographs and the two businesses

Mavrix was a celebrity-photo agency incorporated in Florida and principally based in Miami. It paid photographers for candid pictures and licensed the images to celebrity publications such as People and Us Weekly. Its business also had California connections: many photographed celebrities lived or worked in Southern California; Mavrix maintained a Los Angeles office, used local photographers, had a California registered agent, and paid California franchise fees. Court opinion pp. 10337–10339.

Brand Technologies was an Ohio corporation principally based in Toledo. It operated celebrity-gossip.net, a popular entertainment website with photos, videos, and short stories. Visitors could comment, vote in polls, receive a newsletter, join a membership club, and submit tips and photographs. The website attracted a national audience. Brand's marketing materials claimed more than twelve million unique U.S. visitors and seventy million U.S. page views per month, but the record contained no precise count of California visitors. The distinction between evidence of a substantial audience and an exact audience census remained important. Id. at 10338.

Brand earned advertising revenue. Some third-party ads promoted California jobs, hotels, or vacations, and the site linked to a ticket seller offering events including California events. Brand also had relationships with California companies supplying advertising, wireless hosting, design or maintenance, and shared news links. Yet Brand had no California office, real property, or staff, was not licensed there, and paid no California taxes. These relationships supported an argument about market exploitation but did not automatically establish a California corporate home. Id. at 10338–10339.

In 2008, a Mavrix photographer took thirty-five photographs of singer Stacy Ferguson, known as Fergie, and actor Josh Duhamel vacationing in the Bahamas. Mavrix registered copyright and posted the photographs on its own website. It alleged that Brand soon reposted them without authorization, destroying their market value. Mavrix sued Brand and CEO Brad Mandell in the Central District of California for copyright infringement, seeking an injunction and actual and statutory damages. Brand moved to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction. The district court denied jurisdictional discovery and dismissed; Mavrix appealed. These were allegations of infringement, not a final appellate finding of liability. Id. at 10339.

## The showing required at this stage

The court reviewed the jurisdictional dismissal de novo. Because the motion rested on written materials rather than an evidentiary hearing, Mavrix needed a prima facie showing. It could not rest on bare allegations contradicted by affidavits, but uncontroverted allegations were accepted and factual disputes were resolved in its favor. The court was deciding whether the suit could proceed, not weighing all evidence as at trial. Id. at 10339–10340.

No applicable federal statute independently authorized personal jurisdiction. Rule 4(k)(1)(A) led to California law, whose long-arm statute was coextensive with federal due process. The court therefore separately examined general and specific jurisdiction under constitutional minimum-contacts principles. Id. at 10340.

## General jurisdiction failed

General jurisdiction permits adjudication of claims unrelated to the forum contacts and consequently requires a much more substantial affiliation. Applying its contemporaneous precedents and the Supreme Court's recent Goodyear decision, the court asked whether Brand's contacts approximated an in-state presence and rendered it essentially at home. It contrasted the exceptional temporary corporate headquarters in Perkins with cases in which substantial purchases, business relationships, or some sales did not suffice. Id. at 10340–10343.

Brand's advertisements were less probative for this purpose because third parties were marketing their own businesses through its website. The ticket feature also supplied little support: regardless of the disputed identity of the ticket seller, the only ticket sale shown was to Mavrix's counsel. The design contractors worked in Canada and incorporated a California firm only after building the website; that later incorporation was a fortuitous circumstance. Other California relationships represented doing business with California residents without necessarily conducting substantial corporate operations within California. Id. at 10343–10345.

The court also rejected reliance on Zippo's website-interactivity scale as a substitute for general-jurisdiction analysis. A highly interactive site may have many functions without cultivating the depth and quantity of contacts required for all-purpose jurisdiction. Comments, newsletters, polls, and uploads were standard website features; allowing them to establish general jurisdiction wherever accessible would expose most large media companies to suit nationwide on unrelated claims. Considering all the contacts together, Brand was not subject to general jurisdiction in California. Id. at 10345–10347.

## Specific jurisdiction succeeded on a different basis

Specific jurisdiction required purposeful direction or availment, a claim arising out of or relating to forum activity, and reasonableness. Mavrix bore the first two burdens; a sufficient showing shifted the burden to Brand to demonstrate a compelling case of unreasonableness. Only the first requirement was disputed here. The court treated the copyright claim as arising from publication on the website accessible to forum users, and Brand did not press an argument that jurisdiction was unreasonable under the governing factors. Id. at 10347–10348.

For this tort-like copyright claim, the court applied purposeful direction through Calder's effects framework: an intentional act, express aiming at the forum, and harm the defendant knew was likely to occur there. It distinguished that analysis from the purposeful-availment inquiry in the recent McIntyre product-liability plurality. Brand's deliberate reposting satisfied the intentional-act requirement; the harder issue was express aiming. Id. at 10348–10350.

Accessibility alone did not answer that issue. Even a passive website could support jurisdiction if accompanied by conduct targeting the forum, while mere passive publication would not. The court considered interactivity, the geographic scope of commercial ambitions, and deliberate targeting of a known forum plaintiff as possible evidence. Here, the most important feature was Brand's use of the allegedly infringing photos as part of commercial exploitation of California's audience. Id. at 10349–10350.

The court drew an analogy to Keeton: Hustler's deliberate, regular magazine distribution in a state supported jurisdiction over a claim concerning the publication's contents, although those sales did not support general jurisdiction. Brand likewise cultivated a national audience for profit, including a substantial California audience. Its advertisements directed to Californians supported the inference that it actually or constructively knew about and commercially exploited those users. In this context, whether Brand or the advertiser selected the California targeting did not defeat the inference about Brand's audience and advertising business. Id. at 10350–10352.

Brand had not advertised its own website in California's local media. Nevertheless, its concentration on the California-centered celebrity and entertainment industries, combined with the size and commercial importance of that market, supported the inference that it anticipated, wanted, and obtained substantial California viewership. Those viewers were part of its profitable business model, rather than isolated visitors arriving entirely by chance. The record supported that inference despite lacking an exact California traffic count. Id. at 10352–10353.

The court carefully limited the analogy. A local newspaper, an individual, or an unpaid blogger posting material accessible nationally would not necessarily have the same commercial ambitions or predictable national consumption. Universal Internet accessibility alone did not establish express aiming at every state. The combination of national scope, commercial cultivation, and an audience in the particular forum supplied the connection here. Id. at 10353–10354.

## California injury and the actual judgment

Express aiming still required jurisdictionally significant forum harm. Mavrix's Florida headquarters did not establish that all economic injury occurred only in Florida. A corporation can suffer relevant losses in multiple places. The alleged unauthorized republication destroyed the photographs' market value, a substantial part of which arose from California readers who otherwise would buy publications to see them. The court therefore found foreseeable, jurisdictionally significant California economic injury from the alleged infringement. Id. at 10354–10355.

Mavrix had established a prima facie case of purposeful direction sufficient to survive the motion. The court held that Brand was subject to **specific jurisdiction, but not general jurisdiction**, in California. It **reversed the dismissal and remanded for further proceedings**. It did not award copyright damages, order a permanent injunction, or decide that the copyright allegations were ultimately true. Id. at 10355.

The assigned account describes the result in terms of purposeful availment and highlights third-party advertising and foreseeable injury. The full opinion more precisely uses purposeful direction for the copyright tort and adds the commercial-audience and California-market-value analysis. The textbook's question about consistency with McIntyre remains an editorial question; the court expressly addressed that plurality through the distinction between intentional-tort direction and product-liability availment. This brief states the 2011 decision on its historical terms rather than treating its analysis as a verified current rule for every online publisher.
