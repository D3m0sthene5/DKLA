# Contemporary Mission, Inc. v. Famous Music Corporation
557 F.2d 918 (2d Cir. 1977) | Contracts

## Court and disposition
The Second Circuit decided the appeal and cross-appeal on May 18, 1977. Judge Meskill wrote the majority opinion with Judge Mansfield; Judge Van Graafeiland concurred in part and dissented in part. The court affirmed the judgment against Famous in all respects except the ruling concerning lost royalties, and remanded for a Federal Rule of Evidence 403 determination. That determination would decide whether Contemporary received the new damages trial requested on its cross-appeal. The court neither awarded additional projected royalties nor unconditionally ordered their evidentiary admission or a new trial. (918, 920, 928.)

## Agreements, promotion, and sale of the record division
Contemporary was a nonprofit corporation composed of Roman Catholic priests who wrote and produced music. It owned the rights to Father John O’Reilly’s rock opera Virgin. Famous’s president, Tony Martell, had helped distribute Tommy and Jesus Christ Superstar and hoped Virgin would similarly succeed. On August 16, 1972, Famous agreed to pay royalties in exchange for the master recording and exclusive manufacturing and sales rights. Additional obligations included appointing a personal promotional overseer, spending at least $50,000 on promotion in the first year, and releasing at least four singles during the first two years. Assignment was allowed only in specified transactions, conditioned on delivery of an agreement binding the assignee to Famous’s obligations. (920–921 & n.3.)

A May 8, 1973 Crunch agreement covered other recordings. It gave Famous exclusive United States distribution rights under a new label, required Contemporary to deliver specified numbers of albums and singles, and required Famous to use reasonable promotional efforts. A default became material only after written notice specifying the failure and an uncured thirty-day period. It restricted assignment by Contemporary but contained no corresponding restriction on Famous. Paragraph 12 denied either party authority to create obligations in the other’s name. (921 & n.4.)

The music had not fulfilled the parties’ initial hopes, but their relationship remained amicable until Famous sold its record division to ABC on July 31, 1974. Martell told O’Reilly to look to ABC for performance; an ABC lawyer said that company would have no relationship with Contemporary. Records were removed from retail outlets and sent to ABC. Contemporary’s August notice communications objected to the transaction and resulting removal of its records. Suit followed. (921, 925.)

## Jury findings and awards
After trial before Judge Richard Owen in the Southern District of New York, the jury found inadequate Virgin promotion causing damage, damage from assignment of that agreement, ABC’s refusal to perform the Crunch agreement, and resulting damage. It specifically found that Famous had not failed to use the required reasonable efforts under Crunch before the assignment. It also found an oral reimbursement agreement. Awards were $68,773 under Virgin, $104,751 under Crunch, $21,000 unallocated among the agreements, and $16,500 under the oral agreement. The reimbursement issue was not relevant to this appeal; dismissal of claims against Gulf + Western and Paramount was likewise not appealed. (920–922 & nn.2, 5–6.)

The judge had instructed that assignment of Virgin without obtaining ABC’s written undertaking breached its nonassignment provision as a matter of law. Famous did not challenge that conclusion; it contested the separate inadequate-promotion finding. Compliance with the spending and overseer requirements did not exhaust the agreement. Under Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon, the exclusive arrangement also imposed reasonable efforts to promote nationally. Evidence of premature termination of one single’s promotion, local test marketing of another, and promotional staff reductions supported the jury’s finding, although the court called the issue close. (922–924.)

The spending evidence was uncontradicted but imprecise and self-serving, so the jury could find it insufficient. Moreover, an amendment allowing Famous to stop spending when promotion ceased to be effective and profitable was conditional, not a present waiver or absolute discretion; an exercise of that option required good faith, and no such determination was shown. The overseer requirement had been satisfied, but that did not defeat liability based on other obligations. The court reserved the unchallenged evidentiary question about oral proof of a lost business record. (923 & nn.8–10.)

## Delegation left Famous responsible, and notice was sufficient
Famous’s Crunch defense confused assignment of rights with delegation of duties. Assignment may end the assignor’s interest in transferred rights; delegation ordinarily does not discharge the delegant’s duty. Assuming, without deciding, that the promotional obligation was delegable despite Martell’s personal involvement, Famous remained responsible for performance. Contemporary needed to show that no one performed that obligation after the sale, which it did. It did not have to establish ABC’s independently actionable repudiation or negate an unpleaded and unproved affirmative defense of performance. The result therefore did not depend on admitting the ABC lawyer’s statement to prove repudiation. (924–925 & nn.11–13.)

The majority also rejected the notice defense. Although the letter emphasized paragraph 12 and the ultimate recovery rested elsewhere, its reference to removal of the records, read with the telegram and prior conversation, sufficiently identified the disruption and called for Famous to ensure continued performance. The notice-and-cure clause was not a pleading trap in which every misdescription defeated recovery. Famous, having disposed of the division, had stripped itself of the ability to cure. (925.)

Contemporary’s contention that the assignment was void did not estop its claim against Famous: liability rested on Famous’s own unperformed obligation, not vicarious liability for ABC. The majority expressly reserved possible recovery against ABC based on its assumption agreement and Famous’s possible contractual indemnification rights. Neither question was needed to decide Famous’s continuing responsibility. (925–926 & n.16.)

## Existing royalty loss versus uncertain additional ventures
Contemporary’s cross-appeal concerned excluded statistics and expert testimony about how well the Virgin single Fear No Evil would have sold absent the breach. New York distinguished certainty that damage existed from precision about its amount. Artistic success was particularly sensitive to taste and fancy, but this was an existing recording with a fixed price and a buying market, whose modest success was growing. Even after promotion ended and inventory was withdrawn, momentum produced another 10,000 sales and a rise from approximately number 80 to number 61 on Billboard’s Hot Soul Singles chart. Continued promotion and availability would have produced additional sales, making some lost royalties certain. (926–927.)

That did not establish the proposed chain of further success. Contemporary’s domino theory projected that a hit single would stimulate album sales and create concert or theatrical touring opportunities. Those additional benefits depended too heavily on taste and fancy to establish the existence of loss. Their exclusion was affirmed. The court thus separated the existing single’s foregone royalties from speculative later ventures, rather than approving every consequence of an imagined hit. (927.)

For royalty amount, Contemporary studied 324 songs that reached number 61 during 1974. Its figures showed 76 percent reached the top forty, 65 percent the top thirty, 51 percent the top twenty, 34 percent the top ten, 21 percent the top five, and 10 percent number one. A proposed expert would convert chart performance into sales and then royalties. The study omitted the speed of a recording’s rise, artists’ reputations, and promotional resources. Those omissions affected weight rather than relevance: the data had some tendency to show probable sales, and Famous could challenge the prediction with contrary evidence. Imperfect estimation did not become irrelevant merely because the breach made precision unavailable. (927–928.)

## Rule 403 and the partial dissent
Relevance did not finish the inquiry. Rule 403 could still warrant exclusion for substantially outweighing dangers of unfair prejudice, confusion, or misleading the jury. Bare statistics might acquire undue force or fail to supply a stable estimate without analysis of the omitted factors. The District Court had treated Freund’s substantive certainty requirement as dispositive and had not performed that balancing. The majority declined to undertake it initially on appeal, remanding so the trial judge could decide it and consequently whether a new damages trial was appropriate. Footnote 17 expressly distinguishes certainty that loss existed from evidentiary relevance concerning its amount. (928 & n.17.)

Van Graafeiland agreed with affirmance of the Virgin liability and award, although he might have evaluated the evidence differently as a juror. He would reverse the Crunch and unallocated awards because the notices alleged that the sale itself violated provisions it did not violate, rather than specifying an actual performance default and affording the promised cure period. His sole footnote also emphasized that no written notice went to ABC, which acquired the assignor’s notice rights. (928–930 & dissent n.1 at 929.)

He opposed any evidentiary remand. In his reading, the judge had carefully explored foundation, and the missing information about artistic reputation, recording quality, promotional resources, and speed of chart progress justified exclusion as misleading and distracting. He would defer to that discretionary ruling instead of requiring another inquiry. His objections to notice and the remand are distinct from his concurrence in the Virgin judgment. (929–930.)
