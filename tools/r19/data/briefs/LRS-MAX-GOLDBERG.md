# Goldberg v. Kelly
397 U.S. 254 (1970), decided March 23, 1970 | Legislation and the Regulatory State | Due process before welfare termination

## The assistance programs and the disputed procedures

Twenty named plaintiffs, including intervenors, brought two consolidated actions challenging the threatened or actual termination of New York City welfare payments. Fourteen received assistance through the federally supported, state-administered Aid to Families with Dependent Children program; six received Home Relief, financed and administered by New York governments. These were statutory assistance programs for eligible persons, not discretionary gifts awarded without eligibility rules. The complaint alleged that benefits had been or would be stopped without prior notice and a hearing. At filing, no prior-notice or hearing requirement existed. While litigation proceeded, New York adopted new procedures, and the recipients challenged those procedures' adequacy. 397 U.S. at 255–258 & nn. 1–3.

The underlying disputes illustrated why eligibility decisions could involve both factual mistakes and contested applications of policy. Altagracia Guzman disputed the applicability of a requirement that she cooperate in suing her estranged husband. Juan DeJesus denied the drug use on which officials based a requirement that he accept counseling and rehabilitation. Some plaintiffs later received hearings or resumed payments, but unresolved eligibility disputes and threats of termination remained. The Court did not adjudicate those individual disputes as a final determination that every plaintiff was eligible. Id. at 256–257 n. 2.

New York City's revised procedure began with a caseworker discussing doubts about eligibility with the recipient. A recommendation to terminate then went to a supervisor. If the supervisor agreed, the recipient received a letter explaining the reasons and allowing seven days to request review by a higher official. The recipient could submit a written statement, personally or with assistance. If that official upheld termination, benefits stopped and the recipient received an explanatory letter. The decisive defect was that the recipient could not appear before the reviewing official, present oral evidence, or confront adverse witnesses. Id. at 258–259.

After termination, an independent state officer could conduct a full administrative fair hearing, with personal appearance, oral evidence, confrontation, cross-examination, and a record. A successful recipient could recover benefits erroneously withheld; an unsuccessful recipient could seek state judicial review. Thus the question was whether reliable later proceedings could justify cutting off subsistence assistance before any evidentiary hearing. The validity of the post-termination fair hearing itself was not before the Court. Id. at 259–261 & nn. 5–7.

## The litigation and the constitutional issue

A three-judge federal District Court held that due process required an adequate hearing before termination. It regarded the recipient's immediate need for necessities as outweighing the administrative and fiscal reasons for stopping payments first. Only the city's Commissioner of Social Services appealed, although state officials also were defendants. The Supreme Court affirmed. Id. at 260–261.

The Commissioner did not deny that procedural due process applied. The disputed issue was timing and form: could informal review of written submissions, followed by a full hearing after termination, constitutionally protect a recipient whose continued eligibility was contested? The Court required an evidentiary opportunity before termination, adapted to the welfare setting. It did not require a judicial trial before every government deprivation or finally determine the recipients' eligibility. Id. at 261–264, 266–267.

## Why the entitlement and the immediate loss mattered

Justice Brennan's Court rejected the suggestion that describing welfare as a privilege removed it from constitutional protection. Qualified recipients had statutory entitlements, and termination was state action adjudicating important interests. The opinion situated those interests alongside unemployment compensation, tax exemptions, and public employment, while recognizing that modern security often rests on government-created entitlements. Id. at 262–263 & nn. 8–9.

The amount and timing of process depended on the government's function and the recipient's interest, including the severity of the potential loss and the competing interest in summary action. The distinctive factor here was immediate subsistence. Welfare paid for food, clothing, shelter, and medical care; an eligible recipient ordinarily lacked independent resources while awaiting review. Ending assistance could make the recipient desperate and impair the practical ability to challenge the agency. Retroactive restoration could correct an account balance but could not supply necessities during the interval. Id. at 263–264 & nn. 10–12.

The government also had an interest in accurate eligibility decisions and in avoiding the insecurity and exclusion resulting from wrongful termination. The public purpose of assistance supported uninterrupted payment to eligible persons. Against that, the Commissioner argued that prompt termination conserved public money and reduced the administrative cost of hearings, particularly because most terminations went unchallenged. The Court acknowledged both increased hearing expense and the likely inability to recover interim payments from ineligible, judgment-proof recipients. It nevertheless held that those costs did not outweigh the recipient's need and the public interest in avoiding erroneous deprivation. Prompt hearings and effective use of staff and facilities could reduce the burden. Id. at 264–266.

## The required hearing and its limits

Because a full administrative fair hearing would remain available, the preliminary hearing could have a limited purpose: checking the validity of the agency's grounds before subsistence payments ended. The Constitution did not require a complete record, comprehensive opinion, or judicial formality at that stage. Informal proceedings could satisfy the minimum safeguards. A state could also continue benefits until its full fair hearing was decided, avoiding the need for two hearings. Id. at 266–267 & n. 14.

Notice had to be timely and adequate, explaining the proposed termination. The Court found no inherent constitutional defect in the existing letter and caseworker conference and declined to hold that seven days was always insufficient. Particular cases could require more time. The fatal omissions concerned the recipient's ability to participate before the decisionmaker: oral presentation, disclosure and testing of adverse evidence, and confrontation and cross-examination of the witnesses the department relied upon. Id. at 267–270.

Written submissions were insufficient in this setting because many recipients could not write effectively or obtain assistance, and a written statement could not be adjusted to the decisionmaker's concerns. Credibility disputes required an opportunity to test testimony. Having the caseworker relay the recipient's position was inadequate when that same caseworker gathered the adverse facts. Procedure had to fit the capacities and circumstances of the people who would use it. These reasons concerned disputes of fact and the application of rules to particular facts; footnote 15 reserved what process might suffice for a purely legal issue unconnected to contested facts. Id. at 268–270 & nn. 15–16.

The recipient could retain a lawyer, but the Court did not require government-appointed counsel at the preliminary hearing. The decision had to rest on the legal rules and evidence presented at the hearing. The official should explain the reasons and identify the relied-upon evidence, although formal findings and a full opinion were unnecessary. Impartiality was essential: prior involvement in some aspects of the case did not automatically disqualify an official, but participation in making the determination under review did. Id. at 270–271.

## The three dissenting writings

Justice Black dissented. He regarded the benefits as conditional public assistance rather than property that constitutionally required payment while entitlement was disputed. More fundamentally, he believed the Court's balancing substituted judicial judgments about humane procedure for the Constitution's written limits and intruded into legislative policymaking. His extended discussion of constitutional structure defended a fixed allocation of governmental powers against standards turning on judges' assessment of fairness. Id. at 271–277 & dissent nn. 1–7.

Black also predicted practical harm. Continuing payments to ineligible recipients could drain funds that could not be recovered. He thought the majority's reasoning would encourage demands for appointed counsel and continued benefits through judicial review, even though the Court had not required either. Agencies might respond by conducting prolonged initial eligibility investigations, keeping needy applicants off the rolls. These were his predictions and objections, not extensions of the Court's holding. Id. at 277–279.

Chief Justice Burger, joined by Black, wrote a dissent applicable to both Goldberg and its companion Wheeler v. Montgomery. He favored allowing administrative experimentation rather than constitutionalizing a specific hearing arrangement. Federal regulations were already moving toward continued AFDC payments through a fair hearing, with protections exceeding the Court's minimum. He feared rigid judicial requirements and administrative costs would impede improvements or consume assistance funds. He also questioned how the Court's reasoning would apply to reductions, denied increases, initial applications, and special assistance, issues the decision did not resolve. 397 U.S. at 282–285 & nn. 1–3.

Justice Stewart separately regarded the constitutional question as close but concluded that New York's and California's existing termination procedures were adequate. His short dissent also applied to both cases. Id. at 285.

## Judgment and practical significance

The Supreme Court affirmed the District Court's judgment requiring constitutionally adequate process before termination. Five Justices supported the Court's decision: Brennan, Douglas, Harlan, White, and Marshall. Black, Burger, and Stewart dissented; eight Justices were serving. The judgment established the recipients' procedural protection, not a final determination of everyone's substantive eligibility or a damages award. Id. at 261, 271–279, 282–285.

The case shows how a hearing's timing can be as important as its eventual accuracy. When stopping payments can deprive an eligible person of immediate subsistence and impair access to review, a later hearing and retroactive repayment do not alone provide adequate protection. Its concrete safeguards also show that an evidentiary hearing can remain informal while giving the affected person a meaningful opportunity to challenge the government's grounds.
