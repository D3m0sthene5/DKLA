# Mineral Park Land Co. v. Howard
172 Cal. 289, 156 P. 458 (Cal. Mar. 13, 1916) | Contracts

## Transaction, performance, and claims
Mineral Park owned land in the Arroyo Seco at South Pasadena. The defendants were building a concrete bridge for public authorities. Their August 1911 agreement gave them the right to remove earth and gravel from Mineral Park’s land and required them to take there all material needed for the bridge’s fill and cement work, estimated at 114,000 cubic yards. The first 80,000 yards cost five cents each, the next 10,000 were free, and further quantities again cost five cents each. (172 Cal. 290.)

The builders removed 50,131 yards and used 101,000 yards overall, obtaining the other 50,869 elsewhere. Mineral Park’s first count sought payment for material actually taken: $2,506.55, less $900 paid, leaving $1,606.55. Its second count sought $2,043.45 for the omitted quantity under the agreement’s price schedule. It alleged the land contained enough material and that the unremoved material was valueless to it. The builders denied there was additional available material and alleged they had removed everything available for the contracted work. (Id. 290–291.)

## Findings and procedural posture
The trial court found more than 101,000 yards physically existed on the land, but only the 50,131 yards taken lay above water level. Removing more required a steam dredger, and the excavated material would need drying at substantial expense and delay. Further removal was physically possible but cost ten or twelve times the usual amount per yard, which the court found prohibitive. It also found neither party had calculated the available quantity and that there was no mutual misunderstanding about that quantity. It nonetheless entered a $3,650 judgment for Mineral Park. The builders appealed on the judgment roll alone, making the legal effect of the findings, rather than a retrial of the evidence, the issue. (Id. 289–291.)

## Governing principle and holding
The court acknowledged the general rule that an unqualified undertaking to do a possible act is not excused merely because performance becomes difficult or the promisor unable to perform. But where performance depends on the existence of a particular thing assumed as the agreement’s basis, performance is excused to the extent that thing disappears or proves nonexistent. Applying that principle practically, the court held that the required supply of usable earth and gravel was unavailable when it could be obtained only by extraordinary means at a prohibitive cost. The builders were excused from taking the additional quantity. (Id. 291–293.)

## Why physical existence was insufficient
The full opinion compares pasture leases that assume sufficient feed and mining leases requiring stated extraction or royalty payments. In those cases, the required quantity depends on the presence of material capable of supporting the contemplated operation. Mineral Park’s contract similarly assumed not simply that gravel existed underground, but that enough gravel was available for construction use. The defendants did not promise to extract material functionally unavailable for that project. (Id. 291–292.)

The court therefore treated the ten- or twelvefold expense, extraordinary dredging, and costly drying as equivalent, for this contract, to absence of the gravel. This was a practical interpretation of the bargain and its basic premise. It did not create an automatic excuse whenever a job loses money or costs more than expected: the opinion expressly rejects those broader propositions and relies on the extreme increase and the trial court’s impracticability findings. Nor does it identify a numerical cost multiplier applicable to every contract. (Id. 292–293.)

The finding that the parties were not mutually mistaken prevents casually restating the case as rescission for mutual mistake. The decision instead excuses the unperformed sourcing obligation under an assumed-subject-matter rule. Payment remained due for the material actually received. The excuse therefore affected only the second count, preserving the first count’s supported recovery.

## Judgment, concurrence, and scope
Justice Sloss wrote for Department One; Justices Shaw and Lawlor concurred. The reported disposition was unanimous among the three listed judges, with no separate opinions or judicial footnotes. The court modified the judgment by deducting $2,043.45 and affirmed it as modified, leaving $1,606.55 payable. (Id. 293.)
