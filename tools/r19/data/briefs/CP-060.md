# Gordon v. Harper
7 T.R. 9–14, 101 Eng. Rep. 828 (K.B. Nov. 11, 1796) | Civil Procedure | Historical trover and immediate possession

## A landlord's furniture seized under another person's judgment

Gordon owned furniture in a mansion-house at Shoreham and leased both the house and its furniture to Mr. Biscoe. On October 1, 1795, and until the subsequent seizure, Biscoe possessed them under a term that remained unexpired at the trial. On October 24, Harper, then sheriff of Kent, took the furniture in execution under a judgment against Borret. Borret had formerly owned the goods but had sold them to Gordon before the lease to Biscoe. Harper sold the seized goods. The actual judgment debtor was therefore the former owner, not Biscoe. 7 T.R. 9–10.

Gordon sued Harper in trover for the furniture. A verdict was found for Gordon subject to the court's opinion on the stated case. The court had to decide whether Gordon could maintain that action while the tenant held the right to possess the furniture. This was a reserved question governing a conditional verdict, not an appeal affirming an ordinary final plaintiff's judgment. The report gives no damages amount. Id.

## Gordon's argument and the question before the court

Burrough, for Gordon, accepted that trover required a property right and a right of possession, but argued that Gordon retained legal possession. The tenant had only the furniture's use. Once the sheriff's act prevented that use, counsel maintained, Gordon's full dominion revived. He compared timber cut from leased land, for which the owner of the inheritance could sue in trover, and relied on Lord Kenyon's earlier observation in Ward v. Macauley that trover rather than trespass was the proper landlord's remedy. Best, appearing against Gordon, was stopped by the court, so the report does not supply a developed opposing argument that can safely be invented. 7 T.R. 10–11.

The judges agreed that Gordon lacked the required present possessory interest. Ownership alone did not entitle a lessor to recover the value of the whole furniture in trover during a term in which possession belonged to the tenant. The sheriff's wrongful seizure did not itself end that term or transfer the tenant's possessory right back to the landlord. 7 T.R. 11–14.

## Kenyon: the earlier dictum did not determine this case

Chief Justice Kenyon distinguished Ward's actual holding on trespass from his own additional statement about trover. He called the latter extrajudicial and expressly declined to subscribe to it after reconsideration. Ward therefore did not establish the proposition for which Gordon invoked it. Kenyon framed the present question as whether a person retaining only a reversionary interest could nevertheless recover the whole property's value while another person's contractual possessory term continued. 7 T.R. 11.

The timber analogy failed because the land tenant's interest in the trees existed while they grew and ended when they were felled. The interest then belonged to the owner of the inheritance, who need not be the immediate landlord. By contrast, a wrongful interference with leased furniture did not terminate Biscoe's entitlement to it for the rest of the term. Kenyon declined to determine what alternative remedy Gordon might possess; rejecting trover was not a determination that every possible remedy was unavailable. 7 T.R. 11–12.

Kenyon's discussion of manufacturing machines concerned what a creditor could acquire under execution against a tenant: the beneficial use for the remaining term. That hypothetical illustrates the tenant's valuable, continuing interest. It does not validate Harper's actual execution against Borret or give a sheriff the landlord's complete ownership merely because goods are located in a tenant's house. Id.

## The other judges' reasons

Justice Ashhurst stated the concurrence requirement directly: a plaintiff needed both a property right and a right of possession. The admission that the tenant held possession for the continuing term left Gordon without one requisite. The limited practical usefulness of household furniture to someone other than the house's tenant did not change the analysis; leased manufacturing equipment made the tenant's beneficial interest easier to see. 7 T.R. 12.

Justice Grose used the form of the historical declaration, alleging possession followed by loss, to explain the need for actual or legally implied possession. An owner who delivers goods to a carrier can retain possession against a wrongdoer, because the carrier stands as the owner's servant for this purpose. A lessee with an independent right for a stated term differs: Gordon himself would trespass by taking the goods from that lessee. Grose also treated the availability of detinue as a useful general criterion for trover while refusing to state that the actions were universally interchangeable. Gordon could not maintain detinue for immediate return during this term. 7 T.R. 12–13.

Justice Lawrence similarly explained that ownership can draw legal possession where no other person has a competing possessory right, as in the executor example. It did not do so here. The tenant's interest survived the sheriff's trespass; the tenant could sue the wrongdoer and remained obliged to restore the furniture at the end of the lease. A stranger's removal did not excuse that restoration obligation. 7 T.R. 13–14.

## Result and source scope

The report ends with postea to Harper, the defendant. The conditional verdict for Gordon did not establish his right to recover through this action. Kenyon, Ashhurst, Grose, and Lawrence each supported the result in individually reported reasons, with no dissent: four recorded judicial speakers. No monetary award or adjudication of Gordon's reserved alternative remedy appears. 7 T.R. 14.
