# Power Engineering & Manufacturing, Ltd. v. Krug International
501 N.W.2d 490 (Iowa 1993) | Contracts

## Court, trial judgment, and affirmance
The Iowa Supreme Court decided the case June 16, 1993. Justice Harris wrote for the five-member court, with no separate writing. After a bench trial at law, the district court found Power Engineering had fully performed and Krug had breached, and entered judgment for the manufacturer for one-half the purchase price, plus interest and costs. The Supreme Court affirmed. Factual findings were binding if supported by substantial evidence; legal conclusions were reviewed for correction of errors. (501 N.W.2d at 490–493, 495.)

The opinion reports a $149,700 price and $75,000 initial payment, but characterizes the judgment as one-half the price without supplying a precise judgment-dollar amount. This brief retains that reported description rather than supplying an inferred amount that the court did not state. (Id. at 493.)

## An undisclosed Iraqi destination and conflicting forms
Tripod Laing promised Iraqi Airways an aeromedical laboratory in Iraq. It engaged Krug's U.K. subsidiary, which engaged Krug to make a human centrifuge for pilot training. Krug then ordered a custom gearbox from Iowa-based Power Engineering. The manufacturer did not know Krug's buyer or the ultimate consumer until the August 1990 stop-work order. (Id. at 491–492.)

The parties negotiated orally, then exchanged a March 14 quotation, March 16 purchase order, and April 26 acknowledgment. The manufacturer's quotation and acknowledgment said orders were noncancellable. Nevertheless, Power Engineering conceded an oral understanding permitting Krug to cancel while paying for completed work. The purchase order's front similarly allowed cancellation at any time with payment for work completed. (Id. at 492.)

Its reverse contained a materially different force majeure clause: causes beyond Krug's control that made acceptance unreasonable in Krug's judgment permitted termination or deferral without cost. Power Engineering disputed assent to this provision, though it had not specifically objected to it. It initially received only an oral order and a fax of the front; the complete form arrived April 16, after manufacturing began. Its quotation also barred waiver, alteration, or modification absent written agreement. Thus permission to cancel while paying for completed work was not agreement to cost-free cancellation. (Id. at 492–494.)

## Completed work and the embargo
Krug paid $75,000 during manufacturing. The gearbox was completed in late July, with a requested minor adjustment finished in early August. Krug authorized shipment August 7, but routine matters delayed immediate shipping. The opinion describes Gulf War hostilities and an embargo beginning around August 6. An August 7 upstream directive halted work and spending. Krug initially decided to accept equipment already in transit; learning August 10 that the gearbox had not shipped, it orally instructed Power Engineering not to ship, followed by written notice August 13. The actual report says the gearbox remained in storage because Krug refused shipment. (Id. at 493.)

## The force majeure clause never entered the contract
Article 2 applied to the specially manufactured movable gearbox. The decisive formation question was whether the reverse-side clause became a contractual term under Iowa Code § 554.2207, Iowa's UCC § 2-207. Contract interpretation gave effect to intent and would not place one party at another's mercy without clear language. (Id. at 493–494 & n. 1.)

The quotation's restriction on changes had two possible readings, both excluding the clause. If it allowed formation through Krug's acceptance while objecting to added terms, the force majeure provision was only a proposed addition excluded by the offer's limitation and prior objection under subsections (2)(a) and (c). Alternatively, if acceptance had to contain neither additional nor different terms, the forms did not themselves create a contract. The parties' conduct did so under subsection (3), incorporating agreed written terms and statutory supplements, but not the disputed force majeure provision. The court therefore affirmed its exclusion without needing to decide how that clause would operate if agreed. (Id. at 494.)

## Statutory impracticability did not shift the upstream risk
Krug alternatively invoked § 554.2615. Although its text referred to sellers, Iowa precedent applied it equally to buyers, as note 2 explains. The court required commercially impracticable performance caused by an unforeseen contingency defeating a basic assumption or good-faith compliance with a governmental regulation, and no assumption of that risk. Notes 3–5 elaborate the contingency, regulation, and assumed-greater-liability qualifications; a governmental-order theory did not eliminate the other necessary constraints. (Id. at 494–495 & nn. 2–5.)

The embargo prohibited shipment to Iraq, not a domestic buyer's purchase from a domestic manufacturer. Krug's performance under this contract therefore was not commercially impracticable merely because its intended downstream transaction failed. The upstream stop order was not a failed shared basic assumption: Power Engineering had not been told the proposed use or destination. In these circumstances Krug assumed the risk that its own purchaser would not or could not perform. The court affirmed the manufacturer's judgment. (Id. at 495.)

## Assigned hypothetical differs from the actual disposition facts
The complete assigned problem appears in C&M Textbook 1051–1313, physical PDF page 139, printed page 1145, problem 2. It substitutes Precision, Micro, and Macro and asks whether the seller's ignorance of the end use and a near-contract-price resale defeat frustration. That hypothetical describes cancellation before completion and subsequent completion and resale. The actual opinion instead describes completion before cancellation and a gearbox still in storage. It does not base affirmance on resale proceeds or adjudicate a separately framed Restatement frustration claim; its alternative-excuse analysis is Iowa UCC impracticability. The undisclosed downstream purpose directly supports the actual risk-allocation reasoning, while the hypothetical's resale remains an added pedagogical fact rather than a judicial finding.
