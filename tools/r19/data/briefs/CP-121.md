# Robb v. John C. Hickey, Inc.

**CP-121 · Civil Procedure · 19 N.J. Misc. 455, 20 A.2d 707 (Morris County Cir. Ct. 1941) · July 2, 1941 · Leyden**

A judge may correct the form of a verdict whose substance and intended result are clear. The judge may not choose one of two conflicting substantive results and call the other surplusage. Here the jury found negligence on both sides but awarded money to the plaintiff under a legal regime in which contributory negligence barred recovery. Its intent was uncertain, requiring a new trial.

## Parties, verdict, and competing motions

Clyde J. Robb, administrator ad prosequendum of Georgianna Robb’s estate, sued John C. Hickey, Inc. and Roger W. King. The pleadings put defendants’ negligence and the decedent’s contributory negligence in issue. The reported decision does not describe the underlying event or reconstruct the negligence evidence. It concerns what to do with the jury’s contradictory answer to those pleaded issues. The judge had instructed that, if the decedent’s contributory negligence was established, the comparative degrees of fault were immaterial. 19 N.J. Misc. at 455.

In the judge’s absence, the verdict was received and recorded at the clerk’s desk. The jury stated that both parties were negligent, said defendants were more negligent, and recommended $2,000 for Robb against both defendants. Each side objected, but sought a different remedy. Robb obtained a rule to set aside the verdict for ambiguity, inconsistency, inadequacy, and departure from the charge. Defendants sought to mould it into a defense verdict: they argued that the finding of negligence on both sides indicated their entitlement to prevail, while the recommendation of damages could be disregarded as surplusage. Id. at 455–456.

## Rule and reasoning

The court recognized that some jury recommendations can be removed without changing a verdict’s substance. Suggestions outside the submitted issues—such as donating the amount in suit to charity, dividing an unrelated fund, allocating costs after the plaintiff loses, or showing leniency in a criminal case—may be irrelevant surplusage. Such examples did not resolve this case. Id. at 456.

The recommendation of $2,000 to the plaintiff was directly pertinent to the issue submitted: whether defendants were liable to him in damages. The first part of the verdict, interpreted through the contributory-negligence instruction, indicated a defense result. The last part indicated a plaintiff result. The jury also improperly compared degrees of negligence. The court could not tell which result the jury intended merely by accepting the defendants’ preferred sentence and deleting the contrary one. Reading the verdict as a whole exposed a substantive contradiction, rather than an informal way of expressing one clear outcome. Id. at 456–457.

Leyden explained that a court may, and should, mould an informal verdict so that its form implements the substance actually agreed upon. That power requires the jury’s real purpose and intent to appear clearly, sufficiently, and convincingly. An uncertain or ambiguous verdict cannot be moulded: otherwise the court substitutes its own verdict for the jury’s. This limit, rather than a blanket prohibition on correcting verdicts, controlled the result. Id. at 457.

## Disposition and significance

The court denied defendants’ motion to mould the verdict, made the plaintiff’s rule absolute, and granted a new trial. It did not enter the $2,000 plaintiff judgment or convert the contributory-negligence language into a final defense judgment. This is a single trial judge’s ruling; the complete report contains no footnotes or separate judicial writing.

The decision is historical and applies contributory negligence as charged in this 1941 proceeding. Its procedural lesson remains distinct from choosing a substantive fault regime: a correction must preserve the jury’s ascertainable decision, while an unresolved conflict about who won requires further adjudication. The assigned Notes pose comparisons with charitable recommendations and zero-damages verdicts; those questions are not additional holdings of Robb.
