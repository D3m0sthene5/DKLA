# Cold Metal Process Co. v. United Engineering & Foundry Co.

**351 U.S. 445 (1956).** The Supreme Court decided the case on June 11, 1956. Justice Burton wrote the Court’s opinion. Justice Frankfurter dissented, joined by Justice Harlan, in a shared opinion printed with Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Mackey at 351 U.S. 439–444. The two Justices concurred in the companion Sears judgment but opposed this one. The assigned Cold Metal account is CivPro physical page 617, printed page 1221.

## Facts and the unusually long procedural history

Cold Metal and United entered a 1927 agreement pooling claims from their patent applications to secure a patent for a type of steel rolling mill in Cold Metal’s name and an exclusive license for United. They agreed to negotiate the license payment, resorting to arbitration if they could not agree. After the patent issued in 1930, Cold Metal argued that no license existed until the fee was determined and paid; United maintained that an enforceable license existed even though payment would be fixed later. (447 & nn.3–4.)

Cold Metal began the principal proceeding in 1934, seeking to enjoin United’s patent suits elsewhere and determine the license payment. A preliminary injunction initially denied by the district court was ordered on appeal in 1935. Cold Metal then sought cancellation of the agreement. After trial, the district court instead upheld the contract and directed an accounting. In 1939, the appellate court largely sustained United’s position, dissolved the injunction against its infringement suits, and held the exclusive license valid. A master was to determine payments based on the parties’ understanding. (447–448.)

United sought to counterclaim in 1941, alleging that Cold Metal’s recent conduct violated that appellate resolution. Initially rejected because of the existing mandate, the claims were refiled in 1949 as an ancillary cross-complaint with a separate case number. They sought protection of United’s licensees against Cold Metal’s infringement suits, an accounting of payments Cold Metal collected within the exclusive field, and a setoff against United’s own payment obligations. The district court dismissed the cross-complaint as nonancillary, but the Third Circuit reversed in 1951 and treated it as an ancillary counterclaim. (448–449.)

The master’s 1954 report addressed licensed mills, fees, exemptions, and failures by Cold Metal to respect the license or perform contractual obligations. The district court adopted it in 1955, entering $387,650 against United with six-percent interest from filing of the report. Both parties appealed. Cold Metal challenged United’s appeal because no Rule 54(b) certification had been made. With appellate permission, the district court added an express no-just-reason-for-delay determination and direction for final judgment. Both sides appealed again, and the Third Circuit rejected Cold Metal’s renewed jurisdictional objection. The Supreme Court reviewed that ruling before the circuit decided the appeals’ merits. (449–450 & n.5.)

## Issue and holding

Could the court of appeals review the finally adjudicated accounting claim despite an unresolved counterclaim arising partly from the same transactions? The Supreme Court held that it could under §1291 and amended Rule 54(b). The transactional relationship remained relevant to the certification decision, but did not categorically bar appeal. The Supreme Court did not decide the accounting’s substantive correctness or the pending counterclaim. (446, 450–453.)

The pending claim’s procedural status mattered to the exercise of discretion. Both parties had asked to address the master’s report before trying the counterclaim and recognized that appellate resolution might make its trial undesirable. The district court continued its pretrial conference indefinitely and removed it from the trial calendar without prejudice, subject to reinstatement. Thus the separate case number did not mean it ceased to be part of the multiple-claims problem, and the indefinite postponement did not amount to final adjudication of it. It supplied context for allowing review of the completed accounting now. (446, 450 n.5.)

## Why amended Rule 54(b) permitted the appeal

The Court applied the reasoning of its same-day Sears decision. Historically, the entire litigation was generally the judicial unit for appellate purposes. Original Rule 54(b) permitted limited departure for separate, independent claims, which could make appealability turn on whether a counterclaim was permissive or compulsory. Those distinctions generated uncertainty. The amended rule expressly grouped claims, counterclaims, cross-claims, and third-party claims within the multiple-claim certification framework. The Court therefore found it unnecessary to decide whether United’s claim was compulsory or permissive. (451–452 & nn.6–10.)

A claim’s relationship to an unresolved counterclaim became a factor for the district judge to consider rather than an automatic jurisdictional prohibition. Where a genuinely final claim was certified with no just reason for delay, and the appellate court found no abuse of discretion, the judgment could be reviewed even though the pending claim shared a transaction. Here the accounting claim was finally adjudicated, the judge supplied the required certification, and the Third Circuit found that certification proper in the circumstances. (452–453.)

The Court also rejected the argument that broader appealability necessarily made the amended rule invalid. Rule 54(b) still required final disposition of an individual claim; it did not authorize a judge to call an incomplete claim final. It adjusted the unit for appeal in modern multiple-claims litigation while leaving appellate review over the district court’s timing discretion. On that understanding, the rule did not impair §1291’s statutory finality requirement and remained within the Supreme Court’s rulemaking authority. (453; companion Sears 434–438.)

## Dissent and exact judgment

Frankfurter, joined by Harlan, objected that the majority weakened the established barrier against piecemeal appeals while saying statutory finality remained intact. For him, early appeal required a completed portion separate and independent of the remaining litigation. United’s counterclaim arose substantially from the same transactions as the adjudicated claim, even if it was not compulsory, and therefore prevented appeal. He distinguished Sears: one dismissed count was independent, and the other could be reviewed through the injunction-appeal provision. Those different grounds explained why he concurred in Sears but dissented here. (Shared opinion 439–444, particularly 443.)

The dissent also questioned leaving a statutory jurisdictional boundary to district judges under an abuse-of-discretion label. The trial judge could say when work on a portion was complete, but could not confer appellate jurisdiction beyond Congress’s limits. Frankfurter feared inconsistent appellate access and argued for independent appellate judgment on the statutory requirement. The disagreement thus involved both how to define finality and how much discretion could govern release of a completed part for appeal. (Shared opinion 439–440, 443–444.)

The Supreme Court **affirmed the Third Circuit’s jurisdictional ruling**, allowing it to decide the pending merits appeals. It did not affirm the master’s accounting as substantively correct or award relief on the counterclaim. (453.)
