# Fraass Surgical Manufacturing Co., Inc. v. United States
571 F.2d 34 (Ct. Cl. 1978) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and judgment
On February 22, 1978, the Court of Claims adopted Trial Judge David Schwartz's September 30, 1977 recommended decision and dismissed Fraass's petition for recovery. Judges Davis, Kunzig, and Bennett sat on the per curiam panel. Fraass had filed neither notice of intention to except nor exceptions within the allowed time, and the government moved to adopt the recommendation. The court acted without oral argument, agreed with the recommendation, and adopted it as the basis of judgment. The published opinion therefore includes both the court's adoption and Schwartz's substantive opinion; it is not simply another summary-judgment ruling. No concurrence or dissent is published. (571 F.2d at 34–36, 40.)

## Fire loss and requested reformation
Fraass manufactured medical and surgical equipment and contracted to produce airmen's survival kits for the Air Force. Government-furnished material worth approximately $126,220 was on its premises when a January 9, 1967 fire destroyed the plant. The fire occurred without Fraass's fault, but the contract assigned the contractor the risk of loss of government property, with exceptions for ordinary wear and consumption in performance. Fraass turned over $75,000 in insurance proceeds with rights reserved, and the government offset the remaining amount against contract payments. It sued to recover the loss by substituting a clause imposing liability only for fault or wrongdoing. On that proposed allocation, its absence of fault would permit recovery. (Id. at 36–37 & n. 1.)

A 1974 decision on the government's summary-judgment motion had dismissed portions of the petition and remanded for trial of an accrual dispute affecting the six-year limitations defense and, if timely, the mutual-mistake claim. Fraass later added unconscionability. The 1978 adopted decision resolves those remaining questions after trial; the earlier procedural stage is background rather than a second judgment briefed here. (Id. at 36.)

## Limitations: tentative withholding was not a final offset
Fraass filed suit on September 21, 1973. The government argued that notice of a withheld $12,382.02 invoice payment had arrived before September 21, 1967, placing suit outside the six-year period. But the advice of payment referred to authorization for tentative withholding. Such funds went into escrow pending final determination. The contractor could treat that action as temporary postponement rather than accomplished nonpayment requiring suit. (Id. at 36–37.)

The government issued a final loss statement dated September 8, which Fraass agreed to on September 21. Fraass delivered the $75,000 insurer's check on October 10, with rights reserved, leaving $51,220.38 according to the detailed account. The government set off the remainder on October 19. No actual payment by Fraass or offset against payments due was shown before October 10. Suit was therefore timely, although it ultimately failed on the merits. The opening account uses rounded amounts; the detailed chronology supplies the balance's cents. (Id. at 36–37.)

## Mistake and the regulatory allocation
Reformation ordinarily corrects mutual factual mistake that makes a written contract depart from the parties' actual intention. The claimant must show that the government would have agreed to the proposed corrected contract. A mistake known or chargeable to the government may also support an equitable claim. Failure to read does not invariably foreclose reformation, because the central question is whether the document reflects the agreement actually made. But nonreading alone cannot establish the government's mistake or make a unilateral misunderstanding mutual. (Id. at 37–38.)

Fraass advanced two different asserted mistakes. It said the government selected the wrong risk-of-loss provision under procurement regulations, while its own president assumed the 1966 contract contained the less demanding term used in the parties' 1960 and 1963 contracts. Under the applicable regulations, fixed-price contracts ordinarily used the strict risk clause. The less demanding clause applied to fixed-price contracts requiring certified cost or pricing data. Such data were not required where adequate price competition supported a negotiated contract. Footnote 2 explains that a disputed 1966 revision had the same relevant effect, so that version dispute did not change the result. (Id. at 37–38 & n. 2.)

The government had solicited fifteen firms and received six bids, from $11.97 per unit through $39.50; two bidders, including Fraass, had supplied the item previously. Contracting officials determined there was adequate price competition. That judgment was discretionary, reviewable for arbitrariness, caprice, or abuse. The number and range of bids and experienced suppliers supported it; variation in bid prices itself did not establish inadequate competition. Footnote 3 reproduces the regulatory criteria and exceptions to presumptive adequacy. (Id. at 38–39 & n. 3.)

The trial judge also rejected Fraass's account of a preaward survey and submission of cost data. Its president's trial testimony conflicted with his earlier inability to remember a survey, and government officials directly denied requesting one or requesting certified data. The asserted rough cost sheet was not the detailed certified data contemplated by the rule. The actual findings were that no survey occurred and certified data were neither solicited nor furnished. The strict clause was thus correct: there was no government mistake, no mutual mistake, and no basis for reformation. That conclusion made it unnecessary to decide Fraass's own alleged mistake or hypothetical consequences of government noncompliance. (Id. at 39.)

## Unconscionability and unread terms
The court used unconscionability to address oppression and unfair surprise rather than redistribution of risk merely because bargaining power differed. The clause appeared in the same size print as the rest of the document and stated its allocation clearly. The experienced president's assumption that earlier contracts controlled, without reading this contract, supplied no acceptable surprise defense. That passage is the focus of the assigned note at printed page 671. It should not displace the opinion's separate recognition that nonreading is not invariably fatal to a properly supported reformation claim. (Id. at 37–38, 40.)

Nor was the allocation oppressive here. Fraass could insure the property and had actually reduced its insurance shortly before the fire. Its underinsurance was not the government's fault. Its assertion that the government delayed shipping and then delivered a large quantity did not identify a legal wrong. The court also rejected the claimed unconstitutional discrimination against small contractors: insurance was available to large and small contractors, with premiums defrayed through contract payments. The petition was dismissed despite the timely filing and faultless fire. (Id. at 40.)

## Source and assignment limits
The assigned note offers a short knowledge-and-reformation explanation. The actual decision supplies a more developed reason for failure: the government used the correct clause under adequately competitive procurement and the alleged contrary factual predicates were not proved. It does not establish that every knowingly changed term must be reformed whenever the other party fails to read it.
