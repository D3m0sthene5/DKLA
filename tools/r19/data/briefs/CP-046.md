Citation
Guaranty Trust Co. v. York, 326 U.S. 99 (1945). Supreme Court of the United States. Argued January 3–4,
1945; decided June 18, 1945. Justice Frankfurter delivered the opinion. Justice Rutledge dissented, joined by
Justice Murphy. Justices Roberts and Douglas did not participate. Assigned excerpt: casebook pp. 454–459.
Full opinion: Full judicial opinion.
Parties
York was a holder of Van Sweringen Corporation notes and the plaintiff in a diversity suit brought on behalf of
noteholders who had not accepted a proposed exchange. Guaranty Trust Company was the indenture trustee
and defendant; it became the Supreme Court petitioner after losing in the Second Circuit. York alleged that
Guaranty breached duties owed to noteholders while pursuing interests arising from its own lending
relationships. The merits of that alleged breach were not established by the Supreme Court’s decision. 326 U.S.
at 100–101.
Material Facts
In 1930, Van Sweringen Corporation issued $30 million in notes under an indenture naming Guaranty as
trustee. Guaranty and other banks also made substantial advances to companies affiliated with the corporation.
When the corporation’s financial difficulties became apparent in 1931, Guaranty cooperated in an exchange
plan offering holders cash equal to half their notes’ face value and shares of the corporation’s stock. The
alleged conflict was that Guaranty’s interests as a lender could diverge from its obligations to protect the
noteholders. Id. at 100.
York received $6,000 in notes as a gift in 1934 from a donor who had not accepted the exchange. She alleged
that Guaranty failed to protect the noteholders’ interests and failed to disclose its own interest when sponsoring
the offer. Her claims sought enforcement of state-created obligations through equitable relief. Federal
jurisdiction rested solely on diversity, making the source of the substantive right central to the choice-of-law
problem. Id. at 100–101.
Procedural History
Other noteholders had previously sued Guaranty in the Hackner litigation. York’s effort to intervene was
denied. She then filed this action in January 1942 on behalf of nonaccepting noteholders. The district court
granted Guaranty summary judgment on the authority of Hackner. The Second Circuit held that the earlier
decision did not foreclose York’s suit and further held that a federal court sitting in equity need not apply the
state limitations rule governing an equivalent state-court action. Id. at 100–101. [The earlier proceedings and
filing date supplement the condensed casebook introduction.]
The Supreme Court granted review of the latter proposition. It expressly declined to determine whether New
York limitations law actually barred York’s particular suit. That question had not been decided below on the
basis under review. The issue before the Court was whether federal equity supplied an exemption from an
otherwise applicable state-law bar, not whether all elements of that bar were established on this record. Id. at
101; id. at 112–113 & n.1 (Rutledge, J., dissenting).

Issue
In a federal equity suit enforcing a state-created right, with jurisdiction based solely on diversity, may the
federal court allow recovery when the applicable state statute of limitations would bar recovery in an
equivalent action in the state’s courts? Does describing limitations as procedural or remedial, or invoking the
traditional independence of federal equity, answer that question? Id. at 107–110.
Rule and Holding
A federal court exercising diversity jurisdiction may not disregard a state limitations rule that would bar
recovery on the same state-created claim in state court. The relevant inquiry is the rule’s effect on the
enforcement of the state-created right, rather than its classification as procedural in some other legal setting.
Where ignoring the rule would permit recovery that the state denies, the difference is substantial enough to
require application of state law. Id. at 108–110.
This is a conditional holding about the governing source of law. The Court did not hold that York’s action was
in fact untimely. Nor did it require federal and state courts to use identical procedures or provide identical
equitable remedies in every circumstance. Id. at 101, 105–109.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Second Circuit and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion. The
federal court could not bypass an applicable New York limitations bar merely because York sought equitable
relief. Whether New York law actually supplied such a bar remained unresolved by this decision. Id. at 101,
112.
Reasoning
The Court located the question within Erie’s allocation of authority over state-created rights. Under Swift v.
Tyson, federal courts had sometimes treated judicial decisions as evidence of a general law that federal judges
could independently ascertain. Erie rejected that conception where state law created the rights being enforced.
A litigant’s access to a federal tribunal through diversity did not transfer authority to create a different
substantive entitlement. York’s claim against her trustee arose under state law, so the federal forum could not
treat it as the product of an independent federal body of general equity. Id. at 101–104.
Frankfurter nevertheless recognized that federal equity has its own institutional rules. Federal courts remain
subject to traditional limits on equitable relief, the availability of an adequate legal remedy, federal statutory
restrictions, and constitutional jury protections. A state’s decision to expand or restrict its own courts’ remedial
powers does not automatically alter those federal rules. This qualification matters: the opinion did not collapse
the federal court into a state tribunal for every procedural purpose. Id. at 104–107.
The crucial distinction concerned what the requested difference would accomplish. Different methods of
enforcing an existing state right may be permissible. Allowing a recovery that state law makes unavailable
changes the practical content of the right itself. A limitations rule that wholly defeats recovery therefore cannot
be disregarded as a minor incident of procedure merely because statutes of limitations have been called
remedial in other contexts. Id. at 107–110.
The terms substance and procedure were not universal classifications. The same rule may be categorized
differently when the question concerns retroactivity, conflicts of law, federal rights in state courts, or the
distribution of state and federal judicial authority. For Erie purposes, the relevant comparison is between the
result permitted by the governing legal rules in federal court and the result on the same claim between the same

parties in state court. The outcome need only be substantially the same so far as legal rules determine it; the
Court did not suggest that different judges or juries must produce identical factual findings. Id. at 108–110.
The limitations issue was a particularly direct example. If the state court could allow no recovery because the
claim was time-barred, a federal court’s willingness to hear it could change defeat into success. That is a
material difference, even where state law characterizes the defense as affecting the remedy, permits waiver, or
gives a barred obligation other residual legal consequences. Such features remain part of the state’s law; they
do not establish a general federal power to ignore its limitation on recovery. Id. at 110–111 & n.5.
The Court also considered the effect on forum choice. An equity exception would encourage litigants who
qualified for diversity to select federal court specifically to avoid an unfavorable state rule. People enforcing
the same state-created rights would then receive different legal treatment because one could invoke diversity
and another could not. Providing a federal tribunal against feared local bias did not warrant that difference in
substantive enforcement. The Court therefore extended Erie’s policy to this equitable action. Id. at 111–112.
None of that reasoning established how New York’s limitations rules applied to York’s dates, claims, or
possible exceptions. The Supreme Court expressly reserved that application. The correct reading separates the
instruction to respect an applicable state bar from a finding that the bar exists in this case. Id. at 101, 112.
Separate Opinions
Justice Rutledge, joined by Justice Murphy, first objected to deciding the larger question before determining
whether New York law actually barred the suit. If York remained timely under state law, the Court’s broader
decision might have no practical effect. He would have preferred a remand for resolution of that narrower
issue. Id. at 112–113. [Full-opinion supplement to the excerpted dissent.]
On the merits, Rutledge accepted Erie’s application to state substantive rights but emphasized the historical
independence of federal equitable remedies. He believed Congress’s grants of equity jurisdiction incorporated
a tradition under which limitations statutes were not invariably absolute barriers to equitable relief. Extending
Erie into that mixed procedural and substantive area risked displacing Congress’s authority over federal
remedies. Any alteration of the longstanding arrangement, he argued, should come from Congress. Id. at
113–117; CB 458–459.
Rutledge also noted that the forum’s limitations law might differ from the law creating the underlying right.
Requiring a federal court to follow the forum’s shorter period could defeat enforcement of a right still
enforceable where it originated, particularly when personal jurisdiction constrained where suit could be
brought. That possibility supported his concern about treating every remedial difference as a constitutionally
compelled matter of state law. Id. at 117–119; CB 459.
Notes
The narrow result rejects an independent federal-equity exemption from a state limitations bar in a diversity
action. The broader reasoning asks whether ignoring state law would substantially change enforcement of a
state-created right. The case must be read together with later decisions addressing valid Federal Rules and
countervailing federal interests; it should not be converted into an unqualified instruction to follow any state
rule capable of affecting an outcome. Id. at 105–110; see the separate Hanna and Byrd briefs.
The essential factual qualification is that the Court left York’s actual timeliness open. A classroom answer
should therefore state that the state rule governs if it bars recovery, and identify the reversal and remand
separately from that legal rule. Saying that the Supreme Court found the claim barred changes the decision. Id.
at 101, 112.
