# Songbird Jet Ltd., Inc. v. Amax Inc.

581 F. Supp. 912 (S.D.N.Y. 1984) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B2-001

## The proposed aircraft transaction

Songbird Jet and Jet Leasing bought, sold, brokered, and serviced business aircraft. Their principals were Alan Rosefielde, a former tax lawyer, and William Handy, a former airline pilot. Amax, a mineral and energy business suffering financial losses, decided in 1982 to reduce its corporate aircraft fleet. It owned two operating Falcon jets, leased another from plaintiffs, and owned an undelivered Falcon 50, serial number 108, under construction. Amax assigned financing manager David Ayres, administrative vice president Edward Miller, and flight manager Jack Esposito to implement the reduction. The parties' negotiations concerned several planes; this lawsuit principally concerned the 108. 581 F. Supp. at 914–915.

Plaintiffs' evidence described a June meeting at which they would locate a buyer for the 108 and earn the difference between their own purchase and resale prices. By September they had found Texas oil businessman L.R. French. French did not need the available tax benefits and wanted to trade in a Lear jet. Rosefielde proposed that plaintiffs buy the 108, accept the Lear in their resale to French, and let Amax sell the tax benefits separately. The arrangement required the aircraft to enter service before January 1983. According to Rosefielde, Ayres obtained senior-management approval and announced about October 28 that plaintiffs had a deal at $8,850,000, reduced from approximately $9 million to account for modifications. Conditions included a $250,000 deposit applicable to the price and a French resale agreement acceptable to Amax. Id. at 915–916.

Plaintiffs furnished the French agreement, first unsigned and subsequently signed by French, and tendered the deposit. Amax's lawyers prepared several drafts during November and early December, but neither side signed an Amax–plaintiffs agreement. Plaintiffs said the formal document would merely memorialize an existing bargain. Jet Leasing also sent personnel to monitor the plane's completion at AiResearch, seeking delivery and availability of the tax benefits by year end. On December 22 Amax denied that a contract existed and said the plane was unavailable for sale. Amax sold the tax benefits on December 29. Plaintiffs sued in January 1983. These are competing evidentiary accounts, not facts finally resolved at trial. Id. at 916–917.

Amax described a different June arrangement: an inseparable package involving purchase of the 108 for $9.4 million, purchase of another jet for $7.5 million, a lease back to Amax of plaintiffs' jet, and cancellation of an existing lease. Its July 1 board resolution authorized an agreement or agreements covering those transactions. Amax maintained that plaintiffs repudiated the package through later lower offers and disposition of the jet intended for lease back. Although negotiations continued, Amax said no separate enforceable sale or brokerage arrangement resulted. Id. at 916–917.

## Claims and the summary-judgment posture

The diversity action asserted breach of a sales contract, breach of a separate brokerage contract, fraud through a promise made without intent to perform, and unjust enrichment for negotiation, tax-structuring, and monitoring services. Amax asserted its own counterclaims against plaintiffs and their principals, then moved under Rule 56 for summary judgment dismissing plaintiffs' claims. New York substantive law was undisputed. The court had to draw reasonable inferences against the movant and determine whether material issues required trial; it could not resolve conflicting testimony on this motion. Id. at 914, 916, 918 & n. 9.

The deposit was particularly ambiguous. Plaintiffs' accompanying letter made it refundable on request and said it would be credited toward the price should an agreement be finalized. Amax treated it as good-faith consideration for removing the aircraft from the market pending negotiations. Plaintiffs treated it as purchase-price part payment. Ayres allegedly required changes of payee and threatened that the deal was off unless the money arrived immediately. Amax endorsed the final check, retained it more than two months, and returned it on January 3, almost two weeks after denying any agreement. It withheld $53,789.68 as a disputed debt; separate conversion and contract claims concerning that retention had been settled by consent order. Id. at 917 & n. 1, 920–922.

## Why the sales claim survived

Amax first argued that its executives lacked authority to bind it without fresh board approval. But the July resolution's reference to an agreement or agreements did not conclusively establish a nonseverable package. Although the alleged price differed from $9.4 million, evidence of executive duties, a directive permitting adjustments within ten percent of board-approved prices, prior aircraft dealings, and the parties' course of negotiations could support implied or apparent authority. Implied authority can arise from the powers ordinarily accompanying an assigned role; apparent authority depends on the principal's placement or holding out of the agent and the third party's reasonable reliance. A jury could also conclude that Ayres could negotiate offers only for eventual board approval. The conflicting evidence required trial rather than a judicial finding that authority existed. Id. at 918–920.

The same applied to contractual intent. The refund letter and unsigned drafts supplied strong support for Amax, but their significance depended on the disputed purpose of the deposit and surrounding conduct. If parties intend no obligation before formal execution, execution is necessary. If they intend an immediate bargain later to be memorialized, lack of signatures alone does not negate formation. Whether these parties followed the first or second model was a factual question. Id. at 920–921.

The UCC statute of frauds did not disappear simply because the pleadings mentioned a transaction. Amax's counterclaim alleged an all-or-nothing package, not an admission of the separate sale plaintiffs asserted. Nor did the board resolution, an executive memorandum requesting ratification, the French contract, and unsigned drafts collectively satisfy the writing requirement: no sufficient Amax-signed document established the alleged contractual relationship. Parol evidence could not supply the missing minimum signed writing. Id. at 921–922 & nn. 25–28.

Nevertheless, section 2-201(3)(c) allowed enforcement for goods for which payment had been made and accepted. For an indivisible item, accepted part payment could support enforcement of the whole oral sale despite covering only a fraction of the price. Plaintiffs' $250,000 theory therefore could overcome the writing defense if the money was accepted toward the price. The court also addressed New York's requirement that relied-on performance be unequivocally referable to the agreement. Such a large unexplained payment supported plaintiffs' account, but Amax's competing off-market-deposit explanation remained possible. The purpose of the payment and its relationship to a concluded contract had to be tried. The court denied summary judgment on the sales-contract claim; it did not adjudge that a sale existed or award expectation damages. Id. at 922–923.

## Why the other theories failed

The brokerage count attempted to characterize the same arrangement as services outside the UCC goods rules. The evidence instead showed plaintiffs buying for their own resale: they would determine French's price, bear the resale risk, and remain obligated to Amax whether the French transaction succeeded or failed. The July resolution did not separately engage them as brokers. Amax's knowledge of the intended resale did not transform its sale to an independent middleman into a services contract. Summary judgment dismissed that count. Id. at 923–924 & nn. 34–37.

New York recognized fraud through a contractual promise made with a contemporaneous undisclosed intent not to perform. Plaintiffs, however, supplied no evidence of that state of mind. Rosefielde's unsupported belief that Amax's chief executive wanted the newest plane for personal and family use did not establish a deliberate sham. The continued conferences, drafts, and other conduct contradicted that theory. An ambiguous diary entry, without evidence of its meaning or circumstances, did not fill the gap. The fraud count failed for lack of supporting proof, rather than under a rule that an insincere contractual promise could never be fraudulent. Id. at 924–925 & n. 43.

Unjust enrichment required both a benefit to Amax and injustice between these parties. Locating French, negotiating the resale, and exchanging transaction information were ordinary efforts to secure plaintiffs' own business advantage. The risk of losing those negotiation costs did not itself make an incidental benefit unjust. The tax-benefit idea was neither novel nor original: it was publicly known to Amax and the aircraft industry and specifically contemplated by Treasury regulations. Id. at 925–927 & nn. 46–53.

Monitoring completion presented a closer factual setting because plaintiffs might have believed they already held a purchase contract. But that belief did not justify compensation if no such contract existed. Their monitoring protected their own resale profit and the year-end price structure; the services were voluntary and directed toward their own interests. Amax disputed even the existence of a benefit, noting its independent entitlement to December delivery. The court held that an incidental benefit under these circumstances would not establish unjust enrichment. It thus rejected restitution without finally deciding the separate sales-contract question. Id. at 927 & nn. 54–56.

## Exact result and assigned scope

Judge Edward Weinfeld **denied Amax's summary-judgment motion on plaintiffs' sales-contract claim and granted it on all other claims**, directing submission of an implementing order within five days on at least three days' notice. The opinion neither dismissed the entire action nor resolved Amax's counterclaims. There was no separate writing. Id. at 927.
