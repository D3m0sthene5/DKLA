# Adams v. Lindsell
1 B. & Ald. 681, 106 Eng. Rep. 250 (K.B. 1818) | Contracts

## Facts and trial
Wool dealers in St. Ives, Huntingdonshire, wrote on Tuesday, September 2, 1817 to woollen manufacturers at Bromsgrove, Worcestershire. They offered 800 tods of wether fleeces of specified quality at 35 shillings and sixpence per tod, deliverable at Leicester, with payment by a two-month bill in two months and weighing by the buyers’ agent within fourteen days. The offer requested an answer in the course of post. The source’s unit is “tods,” not the “tons” in the assigned textbook account. (Report reproduced in Keener, A Selection of Cases on the Law of Contracts, vol.1, pp.93–94 (1898), at 93.)

The sellers misdirected the letter to Bromsgrove in Leicestershire. Consequently it did not reach the intended buyers in Worcestershire until 7 p.m. Friday, September 5. That evening the buyers wrote accepting on the offered terms. Mail between Bromsgrove and St. Ives traveled through London, so the sellers received the response Tuesday, September 9. They had expected an answer Sunday, September 7, the ordinary time if their own letter had been correctly addressed. Having received none, they sold the wool to another person Monday, September 8. (Keener reproduction at 93.)

The buyers sued for nondelivery according to the agreement. At the Worcester Lent assizes before Burrough, J., the judge directed that the sellers’ mistake required the jury to regard the answer as arriving in due course of post. The sellers were liable for the resulting loss, and the buyers recovered a verdict. The report gives no damages amount. The sellers obtained a rule nisi for a new trial in Easter Term, contending that there was no binding contract. The report identifies the King’s Bench proceeding as June 5, 1818. (Id. at 93.)

## Arguments about receipt and completion
Counsel for the buyers argued that acceptance bound the sellers on Friday evening, when the circumstances had not yet changed. The court stopped that argument and called on the sellers’ counsel to support the new-trial rule. Relying on Payne v. Cave and particularly Cooke v. Oxley, the sellers maintained that an offer did not bind them until they actually received acceptance. On their theory the Monday resale withdrew the offer before Tuesday’s receipt, notwithstanding the admitted treatment of the reply as arriving in the course of post. That was the sellers’ legal characterization of the resale, not an independent holding that a secret resale necessarily constitutes communicated revocation. (Id. at 94.)

## Reasoning and holding
The court rejected making postal contract completion turn on the receipt proposed by the sellers. Such a premise would allow a repeated demand for confirmation: if the sellers were not bound until receiving the buyers’ response, the buyers likewise could await notice that the sellers received and assented to it, followed by further confirmations indefinitely. The court treated the sellers as making the same offer throughout the letter’s journey and the buyers’ acceptance as completing the contract. Receipt of that response did not have to precede the sellers’ later resale for the agreement to bind. (Id. at 94.)

The court also resolved the stipulated timing condition against the sellers. Any delay in notification arose wholly from their addressing mistake, so as against them the buyers’ reply was taken as received in the course of post. The case thus combines the foundational postal-acceptance reasoning with a concrete allocation of the consequences of the offeror’s misdirected letter. It does not decide the operation of email, instantaneous communications, option contracts, or a subsequent overtaking rejection by the offeree. Those are later doctrinal questions raised around the assigned account. (Id. at 93–94.)

## Disposition and source limits
The exact disposition was “Rule discharged.” The sellers did not obtain a new trial; the buyers’ verdict remained undisturbed. The short report attributes the reasoning to the Court collectively, without individual authors, a numerical vote, or separate opinions. Those details are not invented. (Id. at 94.)
