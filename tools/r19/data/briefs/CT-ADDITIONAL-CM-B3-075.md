# David M. Somers and Associates, P.C. v. Busch
283 Conn. 396, 927 A.2d 832 (2007) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Justice Norcott wrote the Connecticut Supreme Court opinion officially released August 7, 2007. Justices Borden, Katz, Palmer, and Zarella concurred. The plaintiff law firm appealed after a three-day bench trial resulted in judgment denying further recovery from former client Lori Busch. The Supreme Court transferred the appeal from the Appellate Court and affirmed. The client’s unsuccessful statutory counterclaim was not cross-appealed. Thus the decision denies the attorney’s additional fee claim; it does not award the client a refund or reverse the judgment against her on that counterclaim. (396–398, 401 n. 5, 411.)

## Representation and disbarment
In fall 1998 Busch sought a swift, amicable divorce. She and her husband had agreed on custody and visitation for their three children and equal division of assets. Alimony and child support remained unresolved. She signed Somers’s preprinted retainer agreement, hiring the firm to process her dissolution proceedings. It called for a $2,500 retainer and $240 hourly attorney fees, with additional rates for legal-assistant and clerical work. Monthly invoices were due upon receipt, and the agreement also contemplated payment of outstanding invoices from settlement or other proceeds. A separate provision allowed Somers to demand payment within seven days and, upon nonpayment, decline further representation or seek court-approved withdrawal. (398–399 & n. 3.)

Somers commenced the dissolution action and represented Busch for about a year, until his September 15, 1999 disbarment. He did not disclose either the pending disciplinary proceedings or his disbarment; she learned of it from a friend and then contacted him. He confirmed it but asserted responsibility for all incurred fees. The reported disciplinary charges included false testimony, counseling false testimony, conflicts of interest, and meritless claims; this opinion reports those charges as disciplinary background rather than adjudicating them anew. (400 & n. 4.)

The firm sued for $13,124.62 for work from September 10, 1998 through July 2, 1999. Busch denied the debt and alleged excessive billing, frivolous motions, unnecessary court time, and other conduct maximizing charges. Her CUTPA counterclaim asserted deceptive nondisclosure and billing practices, but the trial court rejected it. After trial the court requested supplemental briefing on restitution, and Somers sought that alternative relief. The court concluded that the representation was one indivisible undertaking through final dissolution absent excusable withdrawal, that disbarment breached that undertaking, and that prior payments exceeded the benefit Busch received. (400–402 & nn. 5–7.)

## Hourly billing did not make the representation divisible
Somers argued that the agreement unambiguously created separable work units, permitting accrued contractual fees despite inability to finish the case. The Court found the phrase concerning processing dissolution proceedings ambiguous as to the duration of representation. Interpretation of intent therefore depended on facts, reviewed for clear error with deference to trial credibility findings. Ambiguity had to arise from the words used, not merely one party’s subjective view. (402–404.)

Five supported findings explained the trial court’s construction. Busch understood that representation would continue from beginning to end. The provision for paying invoices from a final award pointed toward completion of the dissolution. Somers had never made the seven-day payment demand needed to invoke the agreed withdrawal provision; absent such a demand, delayed payment led to interest rather than a contractual right to stop. Neither party had discussed a limited number of hours or additional retainers needed to reach final dissolution. Finally, representation ended because of disbarment, not completion or the client’s nonpayment. (404–405 & n. 9.)

Footnote 10 supplied another supporting rationale: construing ambiguous language against the attorney who drafted it. The Court saw no reason to restrict contra proferentem to insurance contracts, particularly where a drafter with superior knowledge also owed fiduciary duties to the other party. That rule supplemented the factual construction; it did not make every attorney agreement indivisible regardless of its terms. (405 n. 10.)

## Self-created inability did not excuse the breach
The attorney had neither fully performed nor tendered further performance, and his own disbarment did not supply a valid excuse. A party who creates the condition making continued performance impossible cannot claim legally excused nonperformance on that basis. Somers’s inability to practice followed his own actions. The Court accordingly upheld denial of contractual damages for the incomplete representation. It did not simply treat all professional loss of licensing, however caused, as identical. (406.)

## Two distinct restitutionary measures
Somers next sought the reasonable market value of services through quantum meruit instead of the trial court’s benefit-based unjust-enrichment measure. The Court reviewed the legal choice of doctrine plenarily, while recognizing that benefits and their value were fact questions subject to limited review. Because a valid express agreement governed the work, quantum meruit did not permit recovery of its reasonable market price in place of the contractual structure. The attorney’s inability to recover contract fees did not itself erase that express agreement. (406–408 & n. 11.)

Nevertheless, a breaching party could obtain restitution where the nonbreaching party accepted partial performance and otherwise would retain an unpaid benefit. Recovery rested in quasi-contract, not on the breached agreement, and measured the benefit advancing the contract’s objective rather than the market value of work performed. Applying that rule to attorneys as professionals did not call for a categorical exception denying every benefit-based claim after disbarment. (408–411.)

## Why nothing more was owed here
The trial court valued Busch’s benefit at no more than $3,625, while she had already paid about $4,730. Work not advancing her goals included challenging her husband’s suitability for custody without her authorization, seeking a sixty-forty asset split despite her agreed equal division, and refusing a four-way conference while falsely attributing the refusal to her. Conversely, beneficial work included initiating the case, answering the cross-complaint, financial discovery and affidavits, preparation for and attendance at the alimony-and-support hearing, a case-management agreement, and a discovery response. Footnotes 12 and 13 supply those concrete findings rather than treating every logged hour as beneficial or all work as valueless. (410–411 & nn. 12–13.)

Footnote 14 explains that reasonably undertaken work furthering the client’s goals and retainer objectives could count as beneficial; it did not require that every action produce a winning result. Whether benefit arose in a particular case remained for the factfinder. Since prior payment exceeded the supported benefit value, Busch was not unjustly enriched and no additional restitution was due. The judgment was affirmed, with all other justices concurring. (411–412 & n. 14.)
