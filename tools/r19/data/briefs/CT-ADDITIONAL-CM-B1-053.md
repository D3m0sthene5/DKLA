# CX Digital Media Inc. v. Smoking Everywhere Inc.
2011 WL 1102782, No. 09-62020-CIV-ALTONAGA/Brown (S.D. Fla. Mar. 23, 2011) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural posture

After a five-day bench trial, Judge Cecilia M. Altonaga found that the parties modified an advertising-services contract through instant messages and their subsequent conduct. The modifications replaced the specified landing pages and removed a daily limit on compensated registrations. The court calculated $1,260,805 in unpaid principal, authorized contractual interest, fees, and costs, and directed that a final judgment for CX Digital would be entered by separate order. This March 23 document supplies findings and conclusions; it is not the separate final judgment it directs CX Digital to propose by March 30. No separate judicial writing or numerical vote is reported. The parties agreed that Delaware law governed. (Official opinion pp. 1, 10–13, 33–35.)

## Facts and chronology

CX Digital operated an affiliate advertising network. Affiliates sent prospective customers to Smoking Everywhere’s electronic-cigarette offer, and tracking software credited a registration after successful credit-card submission and arrival at the confirmation page. A compensable “Sale” meant that contractual event, rather than proof that the customer ultimately kept or paid for a delivered product. CX Digital paid affiliates even if its client failed to pay CX Digital. On August 4, 2009, Smoking Everywhere’s advertising vice-president, Nick Touris, signed insertion order 6921, promising $45 for each qualifying registration, with a 200-per-day limit and specified destination URLs. During August, the affiliates produced 670 qualifying Sales and never exceeded the daily limit. The $30,150 charge, reduced by a $5,000 deposit, produced an unpaid $25,150 invoice due September 15. (Pp. 1–5 and nn. 1–6.)

On September 2, 2009, Touris and CX Digital account manager Pedram Soltani conducted a day-long instant-message exchange. They arranged two new landing pages, placement of tracking pixels, and tests of the new links. Later Soltani sought permission to send approximately 2,000 orders per day and also requested an agent-of-record arrangement that would give CX Digital exclusivity. Touris answered that there was no limit; Soltani responded enthusiastically and the affiliates immediately increased performance. Between September 2 and 23, the daily average was about 1,244 Sales, with a peak of 2,896 on September 22. The complete exchange matters because the court construed it as a counteroffer eliminating the volume limit without granting exclusivity, rather than a literal acceptance of Soltani’s two-part proposal. (Pp. 5–8, 17–20 and nn. 13–16.)

Smoking Everywhere raised concerns on September 10 about affiliates’ representations that the offer was free and about advertised terms; Soltani instructed affiliates to comply. The client did not contemporaneously demand restoration of the original URLs or the 200-Sale ceiling. CX Digital removed the offer around September 23–24 because of nonpayment. A promised October payment did not arrive; an October 7 complaint about identity fraud came after affiliates had already been paid. A September invoice initially used a $51 rate for some Sales, but CX Digital abandoned the proposed $6 increase at trial and sought the original $45 rate throughout. (Pp. 8–12 and n. 7.)

## Issues and competing positions

The original agreement’s validity was admitted. Smoking Everywhere argued that traffic sent to different URLs and Sales above the original limit were unauthorized, that the messages were ambiguous or incomplete, and that Touris could not bind the company without approval. It also relied on a clause requiring modifications to be in a signed writing, disputed consideration, asserted fraudulent Sales, and invoked frustration, good faith, and mistake. CX Digital relied on the recorded messages, Touris’s implementation of the new tracking arrangements, the increased performance accepted without an immediate objection, and his apparent authority. The questions therefore concerned modification and enforcement of an existing services agreement, rather than initial formation of the insertion order. (Pp. 10–13, 20–33.)

## Assent to the modifications

The URL modification rested on objective manifestations. Touris helped install and test the tracking apparatus on the new pages, and the later complaints about advertising terms reflected knowledge of those pages. His asserted private understanding could not negate the transaction the parties implemented. The court also explained that assent can exist even when one cannot identify a single precise moment of formation. It did not treat an unclear tracking objection as proof of actual customer fraud. (Pp. 13–16 and nn. 8–12.)

For volume, the court rejected CX Digital’s characterization of Touris’s response as acceptance of the exact preceding proposal: the proposal mentioned approximately 2,000 orders per day and exclusivity, while the response authorized unlimited volume without granting exclusivity. The response was a counteroffer that Soltani accepted through his reply and immediate performance. The actual delivery of more than 2,000 Sales on some days confirmed that the agreement was not a new 2,000-Sale cap. The interval between messages did not cause the offer to lapse; the day-long business conversation involved intermittent office responses, and Smoking Everywhere supplied no persuasive different subject to which the response referred. The court considered that the interval could also have allowed internal approval, without finding that approval actually occurred. (Pp. 17–20 and nn. 13–16.)

The court distinguished cases involving vague, unwritten modifications to complicated arrangements. These modifications were narrow and supported by the preserved messages and subsequent performance. The messages were a written record of the agreement, but the decision did not depend on declaring them electronically signed. Because the transaction concerned services, the court expressly declined to apply the goods rule in UCC § 2-209. (Pp. 20–23 and nn. 17–19.)

## Formality, consideration, reliance, and authority

Under the Delaware common-law rule the court applied, a contractual requirement of signed written modification can itself be waived by later agreement or conduct. The parties’ implemented changes supported waiver here. No statute of frauds independently required these changes to be signed; the court additionally observed that a statute-of-frauds affirmative defense had not been pleaded. The decision therefore should not be reduced to a general rule that all instant messages satisfy every signature requirement. (Pp. 23–24 and n. 20.)

The court alternatively relied on estoppel: CX Digital substantially changed its position by directing and paying affiliates for increased traffic while Smoking Everywhere participated and accepted performance. This was an enforcement rationale in the contract dispute, not adjudication of a separate promissory-estoppel cause of action. The volume change also involved consideration in the exchange of increased Sales for payment at the existing rate. Although changing URLs added no independent consideration, material reliance supported enforcement under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 89. An unmade proposed $20,000 deposit would concern breach of that promise, rather than prove that the agreement lacked consideration. (Pp. 25–26 and nn. 21–22.)

Touris had apparent authority. Smoking Everywhere had presented him as the vice-president responsible for advertising; he signed the original order and personally implemented the changes. CX Digital’s reliance on that position and conduct was reasonable. The judge rejected the late assertion that Soltani had been told all changes required further approval, explaining the conflicts with earlier evidence and counsel’s opening account. Additional trial testimony was permitted after closing arguments, but did not establish the asserted notice. The decision did not hold that every corporate vice-president possesses unlimited authority, nor did it find that the chief executive actually approved the changes. A later second insertion order excluded for discovery misconduct did not undo the modifications already implemented. (Pp. 27–30 and nn. 23–25.)

## Remaining defenses

The fraud provision required prompt, particular documentation of disputed Sales within five days. No August Sales were timely challenged, and the later allegations did not establish a contractual basis to withhold the invoices. Smoking Everywhere had received the qualifying registrations it purchased; the court rejected frustration of purpose and an implied-duty argument directed at performance that the modified agreement authorized. The mutual-mistake argument identified no shared material misconception at formation. Indeed, the defendant continued to describe the original limit and URLs as terms knowingly agreed upon, and later disagreement about modification did not establish an original mistake. (Pp. 11–12, 30–33.)

## Relief and source discrepancies

The court allowed $45 for 670 August Sales and 27,459 later Sales: $30,150 plus $1,235,655, less the $5,000 deposit, equals $1,260,805. It authorized contractual attorney’s fees and costs and monthly interest of 1.5%, stating an August base of $25,150 from September 15, 2009 and a further base of $1,240,655 from October 15. The latter printed figure exceeds the remaining principal after subtracting the August balance by $5,000. This brief preserves the court’s stated interest figure rather than silently replacing it; the separate final judgment has not been read. (Pp. 33–35 and n. 26.)

The official document also prints September 2, 2011 in the introductory account on page 5, although the transaction chronology and repeated later references establish September 2, 2009. Its damages introduction on page 33 says 600 Sales per day before modification, although the original agreement and factual findings specify 200. The awarded August quantity was 670 in total, with no daily limit exceeded, so the inconsistent sentence is not a finding that the original contractual ceiling was 600. These discrepancies were checked against the original PDF pages, not merely an extracted transcription.
