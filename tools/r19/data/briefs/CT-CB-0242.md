Dickinson v. Dodds
(1876) 2 Ch. D. 463 (Court of Appeal, Chancery Division)
Citation and assigned scope
Court of Appeal, Chancery Division, 1876. James, L.J., and Mellish, L.J., delivered separate
judgments reaching the same result; Baggallay, J.A., concurred in both. The assigned
Contracts excerpt occupies printed pages 242–245, source PDF pages 287–290. The complete
online law report also contains the arguments of counsel, Vice-Chancellor Bacon’s trial-level
decision, the appellate judgments, and the final discussion of costs. Reporter pages in
brackets in that reproduction permit the pinpoints used below. Counsel’s arguments and the
reporter’s headnotes are distinguished from judicial holdings.
Parties
George Dickinson was the prospective purchaser and plaintiff seeking specific performance.
John Dodds owned the property and made the written proposal at issue. Thomas Allan,
another defendant, entered into a subsequent agreement with Dodds to purchase the same
property. Allan’s interest explains why he was joined and why he separately appealed the
decree against him. Berry conveyed information to Dickinson and later acted as Dickinson’s
agent in attempting acceptance; he was not identified as Dodds’s authorized agent to revoke
the proposal.
Material facts
On Wednesday, June 10, 1874, Dodds signed and delivered a memorandum proposing to sell
Dickinson dwelling houses, garden ground, stabling, and associated outbuildings at Croft for
£800. It began in the language of agreement, but a signed postscript described the document
as an offer to remain open until Friday, June 12, at nine o’clock in the morning. Dickinson
wanted time to decide and provided no consideration for the undertaking to hold the offer
open. Both men apparently believed that the promise of time prevented Dodds from
withdrawing, but the appellate court did not give that belief the legal effect Dickinson
claimed. 2 Ch. D. at 464, 471–474.
Dickinson’s bill alleged that he decided to accept on Thursday morning. He did not then
communicate acceptance because he believed he could do so until Friday’s deadline. During
Thursday afternoon, Berry told him that Dodds had been offering or agreeing to sell the
property to Allan. James’s judgment treated Dickinson’s own allegations and conduct as
establishing that, before attempting acceptance, he knew Dodds had changed his position
and had actually agreed to sell to Allan. The report uses differing formulations of the
information in different places; the holding rests on the appellate judges’ conclusion that
Dickinson had knowledge of an inconsistent sale, not simply an unexplained rumor. Id. at
464, 472–475.
At approximately 7:30 Thursday evening, Dickinson went to the house of Dodds’s
mother-in-law, Mrs. Burgess, where Dodds was staying, and left a formal written acceptance.
Mrs. Burgess testified that she forgot to pass it to Dodds. About seven o’clock Friday
morning, Berry, now acting for Dickinson, handed Dodds a duplicate at Darlington railway

The report does not establish that Allan paid a higher price; the judgment’s hypothetical
reference to a more favorable offer is not a finding about the actual £800 transaction. Id. at
464–465, 473–474.
Procedural history
Dickinson sought a decree compelling Dodds to perform, an injunction against conveyance to
Allan, and, if conveyance occurred, an order treating Allan as trustee and requiring him to
convey to Dickinson. He also requested damages. This requested relief assumed that
Dickinson had acquired enforceable rights before Allan. Id. at 465.
Vice-Chancellor Bacon heard the case in January 1876 and granted specific performance. He
read the memorandum and postscript together as a sufficiently definite engagement, treated
acceptance within the stated time as effective, and reasoned that acceptance related back to
the date of the initial agreement. On that theory, Dodds could not defeat Dickinson’s
equitable interest by making the intervening agreement with Allan. Bacon declared that Allan
had no interest and permitted Dickinson to deduct the costs of suit from the purchase money.
These are the trial court’s conclusions, subsequently reversed. Id. at 468–471.
Both Dodds and Allan appealed. The appellate court rejected the premise that Dickinson had
obtained a binding sale contract and dismissed the bill. The parties’ disagreement over
whether one or both appeals should generate costs was resolved against Dickinson. Id. at
475–476.
Issues
Was the initial memorandum already a sale contract, or an offer awaiting Dickinson’s
acceptance? Did the promise to leave the offer open until Friday become binding without
consideration? If it remained revocable, did Dickinson’s actual knowledge of Dodds’s
inconsistent agreement with Allan prevent a later acceptance even without an express
revocation communicated by Dodds personally? A further asserted issue concerned priority
between competing purchasers, but the appellate court found it unnecessary to decide
priority between two binding contracts because Dickinson had none.

Rules and holdings
The memorandum was an offer. Its initial agreement-like wording did not overcome
Dickinson’s acknowledged intention to decide later, and the postscript itself called it an offer.
The promise of a deadline, unsupported by consideration, did not create an enforceable
obligation to keep the offer open. Dodds remained free to withdraw before acceptance. Id. at
471–474.
No particular formula of express withdrawal by the offeror was necessary on these facts.
Dickinson already knew that Dodds had agreed to sell to someone else and no longer
intended to proceed with him. After acquiring that knowledge, Dickinson could not form a
sale contract by attempting to accept the earlier proposal. The necessary continuing offer
was absent. Id. at 472–475.
Actual judgment
The Court of Appeal reversed Bacon’s specific-performance decree. Dickinson’s bill was
dismissed with costs, and he was required to pay the costs of both defendants’ appeals. No
damages were awarded to Dickinson. The decision eliminated the foundation of his requested
relief against both Dodds and Allan. Id. at 475–476.
Reasoning
An offer’s stated acceptance period is different from an enforceable option
James distinguished an unaccepted proposal from an agreement binding the parties.
Dickinson’s wish to retain a choice until Friday was evidence that he had not yet accepted the
sale bargain. The additional promise of time was legally unsupported because Dickinson
supplied nothing in exchange for it. The proposed purchase price would have been
consideration for the sale if accepted while the offer continued; it did not, by itself, purchase
a separate obligation to preserve the opportunity to accept. Id. at 471–473.
The written signature likewise did not resolve this defect. Mellish explained that a signed
land offer could, in principle, become binding against the signer through verbal acceptance
sufficient under the Statute of Frauds. His point was that statutory writing and contractual
agreement are distinct questions. A sufficient written memorandum does not establish that
an acceptance occurred before the offer ceased to be available. Id. at 473–474.
Actual knowledge can communicate withdrawal without formal words
James rejected the proposition that Dodds had to say expressly that he withdrew. The
question was whether Dickinson possessed information equivalent to that message before he
tried to accept. On the court’s view of his allegations, he did: his hurried attempts to deliver
acceptances followed his knowledge of the sale to Allan and were intended to hold Dodds to
the mistaken belief that Friday’s deadline made withdrawal impossible. The agency status of
the information’s messenger was not decisive once the offeree actually knew of the
inconsistent transaction. Id. at 472–473.
Mellish framed the same sequence as a question about knowledge of a completed agreement
with a third person. A prospective purchaser who knows the property has been sold cannot
afterward purport to create the earlier bargain merely by accepting its old terms. His

The deadline did not preserve a power already terminated
Dickinson tried to make the nine-o’clock Friday term conclusive: his attempts preceded that
time. The court instead asked which legal power the term created. Without a binding option,
the deadline set an outside period for acceptance only while the offer remained open. It did
not immunize the offer from earlier withdrawal. Thus the chronology that mattered was
knowledge before acceptance, rather than acceptance before the stated final hour.
The court did not need to decide competing equitable priorities
Bacon had treated acceptance as relating back and therefore regarded Dickinson’s interest
as prior to Allan’s. The appellate court’s first answer was more basic: there was no sale
contract between Dickinson and Dodds. Mellish expressly declined to resolve which
purchaser would prevail if two binding contracts existed. Although he also observed that
Allan’s agreement was first in time, the reserved priority problem must not be reported as a
fully decided alternative holding. Id. at 470–471, 475–476.
Separate judicial positions
James and Mellish gave complementary appellate judgments. Both treated the writing as a
revocable offer and Dickinson’s knowledge as fatal to subsequent acceptance. Mellish added
explicit treatment of the Statute of Frauds, Allan’s assumed knowledge, and the hypothetical
priority issue. Baggallay expressly concurred in their judgments without a separate analysis.
Id. at 471–476.
Bacon’s substantially different reasoning appears in the same report because it was the
decision under review. It is not an appellate dissent. His conclusion that the engagement and
timely acceptance established an equitable interest, together with his relation-back
reasoning, explains why the lower court ordered specific performance and why reversal
mattered. Id. at 468–471.
Limits, source traps, and study use
The reporter’s headnote includes a tentative “semble” proposition that sale alone might
withdraw an offer even without the offeree’s knowledge. That broader formulation is not the
operative holding supported by the appellate application. A study statement should preserve
actual knowledge of conduct inconsistent with the offer, rather than replacing it with
automatic revocation by a secret sale.
Nor does the case turn on a general rule that an acceptance left with a relative is always
ineffective. Mrs. Burgess’s failure to deliver was part of the record; James’s decisive point
was that Dickinson already knew of withdrawal before any attempted acceptance. Likewise,
no finding that Allan offered more money, no general entitlement to revoke a supported
option, and no binding resolution of competing purchasers’ priority should be inferred.

The practical analytical sequence is to identify the offer, determine whether its duration is
protected by an enforceable commitment, identify any legally effective termination, and then
locate acceptance in time. Modern option and reliance doctrines require their own analysis;
this historical case establishes the effect of an unsupported promise to leave an offer open in
the circumstances actually before the court.
