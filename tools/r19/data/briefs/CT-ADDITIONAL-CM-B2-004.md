# Gruen Industries, Inc. v. Biller

608 F.2d 274 (7th Cir. 1979) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B2-004

## Negotiation, legal expenses, and the competing purchaser

Gruen Industries manufactured watches; Gruen Industries International was its subsidiary. Earl Biller and Robert Hersch each owned 170,000 of the Windsor Group's 400,000 shares. Twenty-three minority shareholders owned the remaining 60,000. Gruen's vice president Charles Evans approached the owners early in 1975 about an acquisition. Gruen alleged that the parties orally agreed in early May to an asset sale. Evans and board chairman Peyser supplied affidavits supporting that account. They also said they warned that they would not incur drafting expenses without a firm commitment, and that Hersch assured them they had one. 608 F.2d at 277.

The parties then employed lawyers. On May 20 they restructured the proposed transaction for sellers' tax purposes as a share purchase involving the principal owners and some minority shareholders. Signing was planned for mid-July; the unsigned draft contemplated completing the sale on or before November 1. The resulting document was 58 pages long. According to Gruen, drafting was virtually finished in early July. But Premium Corporation of America (PCA) offered a higher price on July 17, and the owners sold to PCA. The Gruen document was never signed. Id. at 277, 279 n. 4.

Gruen sued in October 1975 for breach of the alleged stock-sale contract, alternatively for reliance expenses under promissory estoppel, and against PCA for tortious interference. The district court granted summary judgment on every count. It treated the alleged sale as unenforceable under Wisconsin's securities statute of frauds, denied reliance recovery, and reasoned that an unenforceable contract could not support the interference claim. Gruen challenged both the legal conclusions and the propriety of deciding the case without trial. Id. at 276–277.

## The claimed admissions did not overcome the statute of frauds

The historical Wisconsin UCC provision governing securities sales, section 408.319(4), permitted enforcement despite lack of a signed writing when the party charged admitted in pleadings, testimony, or otherwise in court a contract for a stated quantity of described securities at a defined or stated price. An admission did not have to expressly concede the legal conclusion that a contract existed or repeat every term. It could describe circumstances permitting an inference of agreement; a genuine factual dispute over such an admission would preclude summary judgment. Id. at 277–278.

But the seven statements on which Gruen relied did not create that dispute. Nearly all came from Hersch's 193-page deposition. Biller supplied only an affidavit denying agreement. The court assumed, without deciding, that Hersch's statements could bind Biller through agency; it similarly considered the lawyer's statement without deciding whether it qualified as a statutory party admission. Those were assumptions favorable to Gruen, not resolved agency or evidence holdings. Id. at 278, 279 n. 5.

Three statements were too vague to support an inference: agreement to employ lawyers, a possible approving response to a comment that everything looked set, and silent attendance during discussion of the transaction's philosophy. The more specific statements described interest if the professionals could assemble a satisfactory deal, rather than an existing binding purchase. Neither the parties' basic philosophy nor general interest supplied the statutory admission. Id. at 278–279 & n. 3.

Hersch also advanced $2,000 toward a financing commitment, and his attorney delivered draft schedules. Read in context, however, the advance was conditioned on closing and the schedules came with express caveats that they had not been reviewed or approved. These acts remained preparation for an unsigned agreement. Where parties understand that they will be bound only upon executing the contemplated writing, negotiating and settling individual details does not itself form the contract. No genuine issue existed concerning a sufficient admission under section 408.319(4), so summary judgment on the contract count was proper. The district court had not independently decided whether the parties actually reached the oral agreement asserted by Gruen. Id. at 277, 279–280 & n. 6.

## Why the reliance expenses remained with Gruen

For promissory estoppel, the court assumed the truth of the alleged firm-commitment assurances, despite finding no admission of them for statute-of-frauds purposes. Wisconsin's Hoffman v. Red Owl test asked whether the promisor should reasonably expect definite and substantial action or forbearance, whether the promise induced it, and whether enforcement alone could avoid injustice. The first two inquiries concerned facts; the third was a judicial policy determination involving discretion. Id. at 280 & n. 7.

Gruen principally sought attorneys' fees and other preparation expenses. But its proposed sale contained contingencies that could leave those same expenses unrecoverable even if the owners honored the alleged assurances. A decline in Windsor's net worth could permit either buyer or owners to terminate. At least 75 percent of the minority-held shares had to be sold; encumbrances on the principal owners' shares had to be discharged; and the buyer subsidiary's shares had to meet ownership and encumbrance conditions. Some matters depended on third parties. The claimed ability to waive favorable conditions did not prove that Gruen probably would have waived them. Id. at 280–281 & n. 8.

The court therefore concluded that reimbursement was unnecessary to avoid injustice. It would place Gruen in a better position for a broken promise than it might occupy if the conditional transaction proceeded normally and failed at closing. Other circumstances reinforced that conclusion: the assurances were informal, formal documentation remained contemplated, experienced businesspeople represented Gruen, and there was no alleged unjust enrichment of the sellers, who had retained their own lawyers. This was a case-specific evaluation of reliance and injustice, not a categorical rule against recovery by businesses. Id. at 281 & nn. 9–10.

Restatement section 91 did not change the analysis. That provision addressed when performance of an otherwise binding conditional estoppel promise became due; it did not establish that the alleged promise first satisfied section 90. Ordinary negotiation costs were a business risk, and the contingent, complex transaction did not justify turning negotiations into an arrangement in which Gruen could never lose its expenditures. Id. at 281–282.

## The competing buyer prevailed on a different ground

The appellate court questioned the district court's premise that PCA could invoke the owners' statute-of-frauds defense. It left that question unresolved and affirmed instead because the evidence did not create a factual issue concerning intentional interference. Wisconsin required a purpose to interfere, supported by actual knowledge or sufficient notice of a contract. Merely causing the other transaction to fail was insufficient. Id. at 282.

PCA knew that negotiations with another buyer were advanced and that a closing was planned. It also was expressly told when submitting its proposal that no agreement had been made and its offer would be considered. That evidence showed no actual knowledge of a contract, and Gruen did not contend otherwise. Knowledge of a prior contract supposedly terminated could require inquiry, as in Sweeney, but these communications described pending negotiations. The court declined to impose an affirmative inquiry duty on every potential buyer learning slight circumstantial indications of a competitor's contract. Statutory agricultural recording cases did not require that expansion. PCA also enjoyed the competitor's privilege against the alternative noncontractual business-interference theory. Id. at 282–283 & n. 11.
