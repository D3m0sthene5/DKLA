# Lamonds v. General Motors Corp.

**180 F.R.D. 302 (W.D. Va. 1998) — United States District Court, Western District of Virginia, Charlottesville Division, July 2, 1998.** Senior District Judge Michael. Civil Procedure: attorney work product and information considered by testifying experts. Assigned: CivPro PDF 495, printed 967–968, Note 3, including its subsequent editorial discussion of the 2010 amendments.

## Facts and procedural posture

Angela Lamonds alleged that a design defect in a General Motors automobile caused severe injuries in an accident. Her legal team created two memoranda and supplied them to retained experts George Greene and Byron Bloch. General Motors sought the documents; Lamonds resisted on work-product grounds. This opinion resolves that discovery dispute, not the merits of the product-liability claim. The district court had previously denied General Motors’ summary-judgment motion, but the report supplies no detailed account of that earlier merits ruling. **303–304.**

Magistrate Judge B. Waugh Crigler examined the documents and denied the motion to compel. He concluded that General Motors already possessed the requested information in other forms, so it could not establish the inability to obtain the substantial equivalent elsewhere. General Motors filed timely objections. Under 28 U.S.C. § 636(b)(1)(A) and Rule 72(a), the district judge reviewed a nondispositive pretrial order for clear error or inconsistency with law. He held the magistrate judge’s approach contrary to law and independently reviewed the documents in camera. The accompanying order granted General Motors’ June 10 objections and May 8 motion to compel. **303–304.**

## Issue and arguments

The issue was whether work-product protection permitted Lamonds to withhold materials furnished to experts who considered them in forming opinions for trial, despite the expert-disclosure requirements of the then-governing Rule 26. General Motors relied on Rules 26(a)(2) and 26(b)(4). Lamonds invoked Rule 26(b)(3) and argued specifically that neither expert had actually relied on the memoranda. The distinction between considering information and ultimately relying on it became important to both the legal rule and its application. **304, 306.**

## Ordinary work-product protection and the expert-disclosure qualification

The court began with the privacy interests protected by *Hickman v. Taylor* and codified in Rule 26(b)(3). Lawyers need room to prepare a case without routinely surrendering their investigation and analysis to the opposition. Fact work product ordinarily required substantial need and inability to obtain its equivalent without undue hardship. Opinion work product received particularly strong protection in the Fourth Circuit, approaching immunity except in rare and extraordinary circumstances. The court did not discard those general principles. **304.**

It instead emphasized that Rule 26(b)(3) was subject to the expert-discovery provisions of Rule 26(b)(4). Read with the 1993 version of Rule 26(a)(2)(B), those provisions required disclosure of information an expert considered in forming an opinion. That requirement created a specific tension when otherwise protected legal material was supplied to a testifying expert. The Fourth Circuit had not squarely resolved that interaction. **304–305.**

The court treated the 1993 amendments and their Advisory Committee Note as controlling guides. The note explained that vague summaries under the earlier regime had often frustrated effective preparation and that the new expert report should disclose the data and other information considered. In particular, material furnished for use in forming an opinion could not be withheld simply because the expert ultimately did not rely on it. The note did not create a distinction between factual and opinion work product once those materials were furnished and considered in that setting. **305 & n.1.**

The resulting rule was straightforward: work-product material supplied to a retained expert and considered in formulating testimony had to be disclosed. The district judge understood this as a qualification of ordinary protection in the expert context, while recognizing that the circuit’s generally protective approach remained applicable in other work-product disputes. **305.**

## Why the court favored disclosure

The policy rationale followed from the expert’s role. Experts may offer opinions on matters outside the factfinder’s ordinary knowledge. Their apparent expertise makes effective cross-examination important, particularly where an advocate may have selected the information presented to them. An opponent needs to discover whether the expert assessed relevant facts independently or worked from a lawyer’s selected account. Without the material supplied by counsel, that possible source of bias or weakness may remain hidden. **305–306.**

The court also considered the lawyer’s ability to control disclosure. Counsel could keep private legal analysis separate from information furnished to an expert, or supply relevant facts without embedding counsel’s conclusions. On the court’s reasoning, the disclosure consequence did not prevent confidential preparation with the client; it attached to a deliberate decision to use work product in developing expert testimony. **306.**

This explanation supported a predictable rule. A test confined to actual reliance would require inquiry into an expert’s internal thought processes and make counsel uncertain whether shared documents remained protected. The operative inquiry was consideration, not whether a document proved decisive to the final opinion. **306.**

## Application and exact order

Both Greene and Bloch admitted receiving and reading the memoranda before forming their opinions. There was no evidence that they had received the materials without reading, reflecting on, or thinking about them. The court therefore found that both experts had considered the documents. Lamonds’ denial of actual reliance did not defeat that finding. **306.**

The judge’s own inspection also showed that the two memoranda contained only an insignificant amount of opinion work product. Disclosure would not give General Motors a windfall of sensitive legal analysis. That observation reinforced application of the stated rule; the opinion did not make the ordinary substantial-need and undue-hardship test the governing basis for these expert materials. **306.**

The court ordered Memorandum I and Memorandum II produced **in unredacted form** under Rules 26(a)(2) and 26(b)(4), granting the objections and motion to compel. It did not award General Motors a merits judgment in the product-liability action. There was one district judge’s opinion and order, no numerical vote, separate writing, or appendix. **303, 306.**

## Assigned discussion and historical limits

The assigned Note 3 reproduces the decision’s disclosure rule and cross-examination rationale, rather than providing the party facts or the actual order. Those details above come from the full report. The assignment then asks whether that rule survives Rule 26(b)(4)(C), adopted in 2010, and quotes a commentator’s proposed interaction between draft-report protection and exceptions concerning facts, data, and assumptions. That proposal is editorial commentary, not a holding of this 1998 court. The brief accordingly preserves *Lamonds* as an interpretation of the rules then in force and does not present its broad disclosure rule as a verification of current law.

The original report’s background refers to a June 26, 1992 order even though this proceeding’s caption bears a 1996 case number and the discovery motions are dated 1998. The scan confirms that wording; it has not been silently converted into a supposedly verified 1998 date. This discrepancy does not affect the reported sequence of magistrate denial, timely objections, and district-court order.
