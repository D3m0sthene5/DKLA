# Amaral v. Cintas Corporation No. 2
163 Cal. App. 4th 1157, 78 Cal. Rptr. 3d 572 (2008) | Contracts

## Decision and scope
On June 11, 2008, California's First District, Division Three, affirmed the judgment and postjudgment orders awarding a class of laundry and stockroom employees unpaid living wages, vacation benefits, Labor Code penalties, attorney fees, and costs. Cintas had agreed with Hayward to comply with the City's living wage ordinance but failed to compensate covered workers at its rates. The employees could enforce that promise as intended third-party beneficiaries even though their workplaces were outside Hayward. The Court of Appeal also rejected the employees' cross-appeals seeking enhanced penalties and additional litigation expenses. Presiding Justice McGuiness wrote; Pollak and Jenkins concurred without separate opinions. (1157, 1170, 1193–1194, 1219.)

## Service contracts and the employer's undertakings
Between July 1999 and June 2003, Cintas leased uniforms and janitorial items to the City, collected them weekly, and processed them at Union City and San Leandro plants. Production workers sorted, laundered, pressed, inspected, and reloaded items. Stockroom employees replaced and repaired garments and handled labels and logos. City items were commingled with other customers' items; workers were not assigned distinct City-only tasks. The City contract supplied less than one percent of the facilities' revenue. (1171, 1173, 1188.)

Hayward adopted the ordinance in April 1999 for service contracts entered from July 1. Covered contracts involved expenditures above $25,000 for listed services, including laundry. Initially, covered workers were entitled to at least $8 hourly with health benefits or $9.25 without them, subject to annual adjustments. The ordinance defined employees as individuals employed by a service contractor on or under the authority of a City service contract. It also provided compensated time off and required contractors to accept civil enforcement by aggrieved employees. (1171–1172 & nn. 2–3; 1178.)

Before renewal, the City sent Cintas the complete ordinance and expressly advised that its purchase order was subject to it. In June 1999, a Cintas manager signed a compliance certification without recalling that he had read or discussed the ordinance. Another certification followed in 2000 after notice of adjusted rates. Purchase orders also incorporated the compliance undertaking. Note 5 identifies a missing order for one fiscal year but records that Cintas did not dispute the contract or its compliance term. Cintas sought no clarification during the contract period. In 2003, another manager noticed the requirement, consulted his superior, and terminated the contract by July 3 letter. (1172–1173 & nn. 4–5.)

## Class litigation, awards, and review standards
The employees sued June 23, 2003, alleging ordinance, Labor Code, unfair-competition, and contract violations. After PAGA became effective, they amended to seek additional statutory penalties. The certified class comprised production and stockroom workers employed at the two facilities during July 1, 1999–June 30, 2003; Hayward intervened. The court rejected an early extraterritoriality challenge and, on September 23, 2005, ruled on cross-motions for summary judgment or adjudication. It found the ordinance constitutional and enforceable, applicable to all hours of covered workers, and violated by Cintas. (1173–1174.)

The resulting awards were $790,489 in unpaid hourly wages, $14,254 in unpaid vacation benefits, and $258,900 in Labor Code penalties. The trial court found no willfulness warranting enhanced rates or waiting-time penalties. Following an appeal-facilitating stipulation, it entered judgment for the employees and City; note 6 records a later correction to the stipulated form. It awarded $1,199,550 in attorney fees, based on a $727,000 lodestar multiplied by 1.65, plus $60,611 for work on the fee motion. Both sides appealed the merits and postjudgment rulings, and the appeals were consolidated. Most legal issues arose on undisputed facts and received de novo review; discretion governed fee enhancement and penalty reduction, while factual findings on good faith received substantial-evidence review. (1174 & n. 6; 1203, 1213–1214, 1217.)

## Contracting power and constitutional objections
Cintas argued that Hayward could not regulate work outside its boundaries. The court distinguished municipal regulatory power from proprietary contracting power. The City chose the employers with which it would do business and set service-contract conditions; it did not purport to regulate all outside employers. A company could avoid the ordinance by declining a City contract, as Cintas eventually did. Extraterritorial effects did not invalidate this exercise of contracting authority. The Council's finding that better compensation would improve City services supplied a proprietary connection. Agreed employee enforcement and fines did not transform the contract condition into impermissible outside regulation. (1174–1178.)

The ordinance was not unconstitutionally vague. Context, remedial purpose, and reasonable certainty sufficed; due process did not require exact answers to every unusual commingling arrangement. Economic regulation permitted more flexibility than penal regulation. The court rejected testimony by City officials about statutory meaning as inadmissible opinion on legal questions, even where the City had designated an official as its knowledgeable deposition representative. Cintas's failure to ask questions was not itself fatal to the challenge, but undermined its assertion of hopeless ambiguity. (1178–1183.)

## Which workers and hours were covered
Workers laundering and maintaining uniforms for City use performed under the service contract even though Cintas owned the garments and employed them. The argument that they served only their employer was both waived and inconsistent with the ordinance. Cintas's truncated challenge to the laundry-service classification also failed, including because it had admitted providing laundry services. (1183–1185; 1181 n. 11; 1184 n. 12.)

Once an employee worked on or under the City contract, the ordinance required its minimum rate for all that employee's hours, not only the minutes spent handling City items. Neither the pay provision nor compensated-leave provision imposed the proposed City-hours limitation. The absence of time-record requirements and the remedial purpose supported this construction. A contractor could limit the employees affected by segregating City work and assigning it to a subset; the court did not require the ordinance rate for every employee regardless of any City work. There was no textual de minimis exception. (1185–1187, 1191.)

Plaintiffs established an inference that class members worked on City items. Cintas had commingled work and admitted lacking records identifying individual workers' City assignments. It controlled workflow and the pertinent information, so fairness required it to negate the inference by showing which employees did no covered work. The court upheld this burden shift notwithstanding the absence of an express ordinance recordkeeping duty. It relied on wage-and-hour precedents and the wider principle of unequal access to proof, rather than making an express statutory recordkeeping obligation indispensable. (1187–1191 & n. 13.)

## Enforceable promise and intended beneficiaries
The same reasonable certainty that defeated the vagueness objection defeated Cintas's claim that its compliance promise was too indefinite to enforce. Contract law favored carrying out ascertainable intentions rather than destroying agreements for uncertainty. Cintas repeatedly agreed to comply, and the ordinance specified an ascertainable wage obligation. (1191–1193.)

Civil Code section 1559 allowed a nonparty to enforce a contract made expressly for that person's benefit when the intent appeared in the terms. Analogous prevailing-wage decisions treated employees as beneficiaries of a contractor's express wage promise to a public agency. Here, requiring higher wages plainly benefited employees doing City work; they were intended beneficiaries of the incorporated compliance provision and could sue for its breach. Note 15 emphasized that statutory wage recovery and contract recovery were distinct inquiries. The contractual right did not depend upon an asserted absence of a statutory private action. (1193–1194 & nn. 14–15.)

## PAGA, relation back, and willfulness
The court distinguished PAGA's new standing to recover existing penalties from the creation of new liabilities. Allowing these employees to pursue penalties that the Labor Commissioner previously could collect did not change the legal consequences of past violations and was permissible in the pending action. It did not authorize retroactive new penalties: the newly available vacation-pay penalty was limited to employees terminated after January 1, 2004. Plaintiffs did not seek newly increased penalty rates for earlier violations. Their amended penalty claims related back to the original complaint because both concerned the same underpayments and injuries; a subsequently available legal theory did not defeat that factual continuity. (1195–1201 & nn. 18–19; 1206 n. 22.)

The employees nevertheless could not obtain penalties requiring willfulness. Under the applicable regulation, a good-faith legal or factual defense that would defeat recovery if successful could negate willfulness, even if ultimately rejected. Cintas's first-impression challenges to this ordinance were substantial rather than frivolous. Its neglect could be described as cavalier, grossly negligent, or reckless, but the record did not undermine the finding that it entertained a good-faith belief that these workers were not covered. The court rejected a requirement that the particular litigation defense must have been formulated when payment became due. No other evidence of bad faith justified enhanced penalties. The opinion did not hold that every unsuccessful wage defense automatically establishes good faith. (1201–1204 & nn. 20–21.)

## Base penalties and limits on discretion
Cintas violated section 223 by certifying payment at the required scale to the City while actually paying less. The secret underpayment could be concealed from the contracting or enforcement party; it need not be concealed from employees who knew their actual pay. Section 227.3 also required payment of vested vacation on termination: the employment contracts provided paid vacation, and the ordinance added accrued entitlement that Cintas failed to pay. The $500 vacation penalty concerned only post-PAGA terminations. (1204–1207 & n. 22.)

Under the former versions of sections 210 and 225.5, the $50 initial-violation penalty applied per employee per pay period. Repeated underpayments did not automatically become subsequent violations at the double rate merely after the first payday. Notice that the conduct violated the law supplied the relevant transition. The trial court correctly used the initial rate here. Note 23 explains that plaintiffs sought the lower former rates, not amended $100/$200 rates. (1207–1209 & n. 23.)

Base penalties were mandatory, although PAGA permitted reduction where the specified circumstances made the maximum unjust, arbitrary and oppressive, or confiscatory. A good-faith dispute defeating willfulness did not erase liability for nonwillful violations. The court respected the agency's persuasive statutory interpretation on notice and pay periods but rejected its informal enforcement policy excusing all penalties for good-faith disputes, which contradicted the statutes. Neither that policy nor its rulemaking defect constrained the court to forgive penalties. Given the notices, failure to comply, proportionality, and financial evidence, refusing to reduce $258,900 was not an abuse of discretion. The company also waived belated constitutional objections unsupported by argument. (1209–1214 & nn. 24–26.)

## Interest, fees, costs, and final result
The court did not decide whether prejudgment interest was available on unpaid-wage restitution under the unfair-competition law. The same interest—$372,546 plus $216.57 daily from April 29, 2006 to judgment—was included under the contract claim, and Cintas had not challenged interest on that claim. The judgment expressly prevented double recovery; deciding the separate restitution-interest question could provide no effective relief. (1215.)

The 1.65 fee multiplier was justified by novel and difficult issues, skill, efficiency, and contingent risk of receiving no compensation. That risk was not limited to proof of specific paid work forgone. The lodestar itself was undisputed. Plaintiffs received $8,174.44 in statutory costs and an additional $498 for reasonable transcript and translation expenses, but could not recover $65,641.28 in other expenses, including expert fees, without statutory authorization. Their sole supporting precedent had been disapproved in Olson. (1174, 1216–1218 & n. 27.)

The entire judgment and all appealed postjudgment orders were affirmed, with appellate costs to plaintiffs. Rehearing was denied July 2, 2008; California Supreme Court review was denied September 10, 2008. These later entries report review denials, not newly reasoned merits opinions. (1219.)

This 2008 California judgment did not decide the later federal pharmaceutical-program question in Astra.
