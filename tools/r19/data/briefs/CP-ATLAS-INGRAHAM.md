# Ingraham v. United States; Bonds v. United States

**Civil Procedure — 808 F.2d 1075 (5th Cir. 1987), Nos. 83–1154 and 83–1160. United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, January 16, 1987. Judge Politz wrote the panel opinion; Chief Judge Clark and Judge Rubin were also on the panel. Both judgments were affirmed, with no separate opinion.**

## Two malpractice awards and a late damages-cap defense

These consolidated appeals involved injuries caused by negligent Air Force physicians and suits against the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act. Texas’s 1977 Medical Liability and Insurance Improvement Act imposed a $500,000 limit on medical-malpractice damages, but excluded necessary medical, hospital, and custodial expenses incurred before judgment or required for future treatment. The government sought the benefit of that limit only after the district courts had entered judgment. 808 F.2d at 1077–1078 & nn.1–2.

Ingraham suffered severe, permanent spinal-cord injury when a drill was negligently used during back surgery on February 12, 1979. The court awarded $1,264,000: $364,000 for lost wages and $900,000 for pain, suffering, and disability. The government had not mentioned the Texas statute in its pleadings or during trial. It filed a notice of appeal from the judgment, then raised the cap in a Rule 60(b) motion. The district court denied that motion, and the government did not separately appeal the denial. Id. at 1077.

The Bonds case involved mismanagement of Jocelyn Bonds’s pregnancy and a failure to perform a timely caesarean delivery in March 1979. Stephanie Bonds suffered oxygen deprivation and extensive brain damage, with permanent disabilities including spastic quadriparesis, cortical blindness, and seizures. The court awarded Stephanie $1,814,959.70 for medical expenses and $1,675,595.90 for other losses. Jocelyn received $750,000 for her losses, including loss of her daughter’s society. Again, the government had not invoked the statutory cap in its pleadings or at trial. Id. at 1077–1078.

The government’s initial Rule 59 motion to amend the Bonds judgment also omitted the statute. Three months after judgment, it raised the cap in a motion styled as reconsideration. The district court denied that motion. The government appealed the judgment and the ruling on its initial motion to amend, but did not designate the denial of reconsideration for appeal. The panel treated the later motion as a Rule 60 request because the Rule 59 period had expired; the title “Motion for Reconsideration” did not itself identify a recognized procedural vehicle. Id. at 1078, 1080 & n.5.

The government did not challenge findings of negligence or liability in either appeal. It argued that the awards should be reduced under the statute, and also challenged two aspects of the Bonds damages as excessive. Thus, the case involved both preservation of a statutory avoidance and independent review of the amounts awarded. Id. at 1078, 1081–1082.

## Issue and holding under Rule 8(c)

Was the Texas damages limitation an affirmative defense that the government had to raise timely, rather than a limit automatically applied after trial? The Fifth Circuit held that it was. The government waived the defense by failing to raise it before judgment in these cases. The appellate court also found that the denials of the motions first asserting the cap were not properly brought before it. It separately rejected the preserved excessiveness objections in Bonds and **affirmed both judgments**. Id. at 1078–1082.

Rule 8(c) listed specified affirmative defenses and also covered other matters constituting an avoidance or affirmative defense. The absence of a statutory cap from the enumerated list was therefore not decisive. The court looked to the defense’s logical relationship to the plaintiff’s claim, relative access to evidence, relevant policy, and the danger of unfair surprise. Note 4 explains that classifying a defense under Rule 8(c) was a matter of federal law, although state decisions could offer guidance. Id. at 1078–1080 & nn.3–4.

## Why a cap was an avoidance rather than a denial

The cap did not disprove that the physicians acted negligently, that the plaintiffs were injured, or that ordinary tort law supported the claimed damages. Instead, it introduced an additional statutory reason why those established facts should not produce their usual legal effect. The government’s position was effectively that, even if the plaintiff proved a compensable malpractice claim, the new limitation prevented full recovery of otherwise recoverable damages. That structure made the limitation an avoidance within Rule 8(c)’s residual clause. Id. at 1079.

The fairness consequences reinforced the classification. The plaintiffs maintained that notice of the cap would have changed how they presented their cases: they would have made greater efforts to establish uncapped medical expenses and developed evidence supporting constitutional attacks on the statute. Waiting until after judgment denied them an opportunity to respond during the proceedings that produced the awards. The court accepted this as a concrete illustration of the rule’s protection against unfair surprise; it did not adjudicate the proposed constitutional challenges. Id. at 1079.

The result was not an inflexible rule that omission from the initial answer always defeats a defense forever. The panel distinguished *Lucas v. United States*, where an unpleaded cap had been raised during trial and the district court permitted an effective amendment without unfair surprise. Existing precedent allowed timely presentation by another means when the technical pleading omission caused no such surprise. These defendants, by contrast, had allowed the cases to reach judgment without mentioning the limitation. Id. at 1079–1080.

## Postjudgment relief and appellate notice were separate problems

A late motion did not place every issue automatically before the appellate court. In Bonds, the government knew that its reconsideration motion had been denied but specifically appealed only the judgment and the earlier amendment ruling. Neither designated ruling contained the cap issue. Although notices of appeal were construed liberally, identifying particular determinations did not include a distinct ruling the appellant knowingly omitted. Id. at 1080 & n.6.

In Ingraham, the sequence differed: the cap motion was filed after the notice of appeal from the judgment. The panel explained that a Rule 60(b) motion could be separately considered despite a pending appeal and that its denial was separately appealable. To preserve the new issue for review, the government needed an appeal from that denial. It had not taken one. Thus, neither companion appeal provided a proper basis for reviewing the later motion’s ruling. Id. at 1080–1081 & n.7.

These points concern preservation and the designated decisions under the rules applied in 1987. They do not establish that every Rule 60 ground today has the same deadline, or that a notice of appeal from a judgment invariably includes later rulings.

## The preserved Bonds damages objections also failed

For the independently preserved challenges to the Bonds awards, the components and measure of FTCA damages came from state law, but the amount found by the trial judge was a factual finding reviewed for clear error under Rule 52(a). The panel did not substitute its own preferred award simply because the amount was large. Id. at 1081.

The government first objected that the judge had indicated during trial that future-care damages would use the less expensive institutional-care model, then adopted residential care in the written findings. The panel held that the final findings and judgment governed. Preliminary oral comments did not irretrievably commit a judge to an earlier view, and the residential-care award was amply supported by the record. Id.

The government also attacked Jocelyn’s $750,000 award. The panel consulted Texas awards as guidance, without treating them as mechanically dispositive ceilings. Considering Stephanie’s devastating injuries and their continuing effects on her mother, it was not persuaded that the amount was so excessive as to warrant appellate intervention. Id. at 1081–1082.

## Assigned excerpt and practical significance

The assigned passage at CivPro printed 682–684, PDF page 358, concentrates on Rule 8(c)’s classification and notice functions. It omits much of the actual injury chronology, both postjudgment sequences, and the separate review of Bonds damages. The continuation at the top of PDF page 359 cautions that an omitted affirmative defense can sometimes be added by amendment. The full opinion’s Lucas discussion supplies the corresponding qualification: fairness and timely notice matter, rather than an automatic penalty for every pleading defect.
