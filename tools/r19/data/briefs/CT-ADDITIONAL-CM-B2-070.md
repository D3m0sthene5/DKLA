# Junk v. Aon Corp.
2007 WL 4292034 (S.D.N.Y. 2007) | Contracts

## Exact ruling and posture
District Judge Lawrence McKenna granted Aon's motion to dismiss Daniel Junk's breach-of-contract and fraudulent-inducement claims, but denied dismissal of his separately pleaded promissory-fraud claim. The latter allegations sufficiently described knowingly insincere software-development promises. This was a Rule 12(b)(6) memorandum and order, not a final fraud verdict, damages award, or appellate judgment. The preserved court-hosted report reproduction dates it December 3, 2007. (*1, *9.)

The assigned account accurately identifies survival of promissory fraud, but its statement that the motion was denied compresses a mixed ruling. Its mention of an appeal does not convert the cited district decision into an appellate stage; no appellate disposition is supplied or determined here. The surviving claim remains an allegation-based ruling rather than proof that Aon lacked an intention to perform. (*9; assigned printed 519–520.)

## Recruitment, software, and termination allegations
Junk was a South Carolina resident, a New York Law School graduate, and an experienced executive and manager in electronic-discovery software and services. According to his complaint, Aon executive vice president Jerry Barbanel persuaded him to meet management in New York through descriptions of a proprietary end-to-end electronic-discovery system in its final development stages. The alleged assurances referred to completion within three months and, in another assurance, three weeks. Barbanel also allegedly guaranteed employment until the system's rollout and attainment of a dominant market position, conditioned on selling Junk's South Carolina home and moving to New York within a year. (*1.)

Junk alleged that these statements induced him to leave Renew Data Corporation, accept Aon's position, and sell his home. The opinion reports his relocation in October 2006 and signing the offer letter on November 9; those dates are retained. He alleged that development had not started, funding had not been approved, and Aon instead repackaged another company's File Control software. He also alleged an absence of the required license and instructions to conceal the third-party origin from customers. These were allegations accepted for the pleading inquiry, not established findings of unauthorized use or customer fraud. (*1–*3 & nn. 1–3.)

Junk alleged that in March and April 2007 he reported these concerns to management and objected to making deceptive statements. Colleague Anne Kemp emailed executive management and human resources about Barbanel's activities. Junk was told the next day that there would be no investigation and received termination notice two days after the email. He asserted contractual protection from premature termination and retaliation, as well as two fraud theories, and sought compensation including alleged lost commissions and professional harm. (*2, *5.)

## Pleading review and integral documents
The court accepted well-pleaded allegations and reasonable inferences in Junk's favor. Its discussion cited both older no-set-of-facts language and the then-recent Twombly requirement of factual grounds raising relief above speculation. The brief describes that historical analysis rather than presenting it as an independent statement of current pleading doctrine. (*2.)

Junk objected to consideration of the offer letter and merger clause as extraneous materials. The court rejected that objection because the employment agreement was integral to the contract claim. A plaintiff could not avoid review of a document on which the claim depended merely by omitting it from the complaint. The court therefore considered the letter without converting the motion to summary judgment. (*3–*4 & n. 4.)

## Contractual job security failed
The signed offer expressly made employment indefinite and at will, allowing either side to end it at any time. Under the New York authorities applied, an indefinite hiring was presumed at will, and prior oral assurances were insufficient to alter that status. The written merger clause superseded prior written or oral representations and agreements. The court also treated the facially complete offer as integrated and applied the parol-evidence rule against a prior oral guarantee contradicting it. It dismissed the contractual claim based on employment through the promised software rollout. (*3–*4.)

This analysis concerned the alleged guarantee limiting discharge. It should not be generalized to mean that an at-will employee can never enforce any contractual obligation concerning compensation or another subject; those different claims were not adjudicated. (*3–*4.)

Junk separately invoked Aon's Code of Business Conduct, which prohibited retaliation for good-faith reports of ethical or legal concerns. The court recognized that an express written policy limiting discharge could, with detrimental reliance, support a contract claim. But the same code expressly disclaimed being an employment contract or creating contractual rights. The employee could not reasonably rely on the selected anti-retaliation language while disregarding that disclaimer. This contract theory was dismissed as well. The order did not adjudicate a separate statutory whistleblower cause of action. (*4–*5.)

## Fraudulent inducement passed two tests but failed reliance
The court rejected Aon's contention that the fraudulent-inducement theory merely duplicated breach of the employment contract. Representations about software under development described collateral opportunities inducing employment, rather than only the employer's intent to perform an employment term. Under the cited Stewart line of cases, that distinction permitted a fraud theory independent of contract breach. (*5–*6.)

It also found the alleged existing-development statements sufficiently factual rather than merely puffery or predictions. The general merger clause was not itself a categorical bar to fraud: an outright contractual exclusion required specification of the particular representations, which this clause lacked. Junk further met Rule 9(b)'s particularity requirements by identifying Barbanel, the statements, the New York office and October 2006 timing, and the allegedly contrary facts. (*6–*7 & n. 5.)

Despite satisfying those preliminary requirements, the fraudulent-inducement claim failed on reasonable reliance. The court considered Junk sophisticated because he was an attorney, the transaction formalized by the offer, and the merger clause evidence of an intention that the writing control. It concluded that in these circumstances reliance on the prior oral representations could not be reasonable as a matter of law. Thus the merger clause mattered contextually even though it was not an automatic bar. (*7.)

## Why promissory fraud remained
The separately pleaded promissory-fraud count required specific allegations that the promisor lacked an intention to perform when making the promises. Junk identified the particular development and completion assurances, alleged Barbanel knew they were false, and supplied facts supporting insincerity beyond subsequent nonperformance: no programming had begun, no resources had been approved, third-party software was being sold as proprietary, employees allegedly were directed to conceal that fact, and the necessary license allegedly remained unsecured. (*7–*8.)

The court held those allegations sufficient under its stated pleading standard and denied dismissal. It did not find deceptive intent established, determine damages, or explain a separate reasonable-reliance analysis for this surviving count equivalent to its analysis of fraudulent inducement. The brief preserves the court's distinct treatment of the pleaded counts rather than silently harmonizing them into a broader holding that every related oral representation was reasonably relied upon. (*7–*9.)

## Disposition and complete-source limits
The order expressly granted dismissal of breach of contract and fraudulent inducement and denied dismissal of promissory fraud. It contained no reinstatement order, compensatory or punitive award, or determination of an appeal. (*9.)
