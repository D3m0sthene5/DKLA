# Strong v. Sheffield

144 N.Y. 392, 39 N.E. 330 (1895)

## Court, author, and reading scope
Chief Judge Andrews delivered the New York Court of Appeals' opinion concerning consideration for a wife's endorsement of her husband's demand note. The assigned excerpt is at printed pages 98–100. The complete reported opinion at 144 N.Y. 394–96 was read, including its account of requested forbearance, its distinction between a performance bargain and exchanged promises, and the direction for judgment absolute. The casebook supplements the opinion with facts from the record; those additions are identified as such rather than attributed to the court's own narrative.

## Parties and material facts
Benjamin Strong held an overdue debt owed by Gerardus Sheffield. The casebook explains that the debt arose from Strong's sale of a business to Gerardus and that Strong was related to Gerardus's wife, Louisa Sheffield. Gerardus later gave Strong a promissory note payable on demand, endorsed by Louisa at her husband's request as security for the antecedent debt. Strong then sued Louisa on that endorsement. Because the controversy was between the original parties to the undertaking, the court did not apply rules protecting a bona fide holder for value who acquired negotiable paper before maturity. 144 N.Y. at 394; casebook 98–99.

The only asserted new consideration for Louisa's endorsement was Strong's undertaking concerning collection. Strong testified that he said he would not transfer the note or place it in a bank for collection, but would hold it until he wanted the money. Asked again whether he would transfer it, he repeated that he would keep it until he wanted the money. There was no agreed fixed period of forbearance and, under the court's construction, no promise to wait even a reasonable time. Strong actually refrained from collecting for approximately two years. Id. at 394–96.

That last fact creates the doctrinal difficulty. There was actual delay, and the parties may have expected delay. But the court asks whether delay was the requested performance supporting the wife's undertaking or whether the parties intended an immediate exchange of promises, one of which left the creditor free to demand payment whenever he chose. The note's being payable on demand reinforced the absence of a legally fixed extension. Id. at 395–96.

## Procedural history
Strong obtained judgment against Louisa in the trial court. The General Term reversed. Strong appealed to the Court of Appeals under a stipulation that permitted judgment absolute against him if the challenged order was affirmed. The Court of Appeals affirmed the General Term's reversal and directed judgment absolute for Louisa, with costs in all courts. The disposition thus ended the claim on the stipulated posture rather than remanding for another trial on the same evidence. Id. at 392–96.

## Issues
Did Strong's promise to hold the demand note until he wanted the money bind him to forbear collection sufficiently to furnish consideration for Louisa's endorsement? If his promise imposed no such constraint, did his actual two-year delay nevertheless establish consideration for the transaction as the parties had structured it? Id. at 394–96.

## Rules and holdings
An agreement to forbear collection of a presently due debt can supply consideration for another person's promise to answer for that debt. Consideration may also arise from actual forbearance for a reasonable time when a debtor requests it and the third party's undertaking is given in exchange for that performance, even if the creditor makes no binding promise to forbear at the outset. Immediate mutual promises are therefore not invariably necessary. Id. at 394–95.

This transaction, however, was intended as an exchange of promises. Strong expressly reserved the right to demand payment whenever he wanted it. He could have sued immediately without violating that promise, because doing so would simply signify that he wanted the money. His undertaking supplied no consideration for Louisa's endorsement. Actual subsequent delay did not cure the deficiency in the bargain the evidence established. Id. at 395–96.

## Actual judgment
The Court of Appeals affirmed the order reversing Strong's judgment and directed judgment absolute for the defendant, with costs in all courts, pursuant to the stipulation. Louisa prevailed on the endorsement claim. The decision did not declare Gerardus's original overdue debt nonexistent, and it did not eliminate the general possibility of enforceable surety undertakings supported by real forbearance. Id. at 396.

## Reasoning
### The endorsement remained subject to ordinary consideration principles
Andrews first rejects any suggestion that the note's form alone resolves the case. Between the parties before the court, a promise on commercial paper remains subject to the requirement of consideration. Special protection for subsequent bona fide holders is irrelevant because this suit is brought by the payee on the original transaction. The court therefore examines what was given or promised for the additional obligation undertaken by the wife. Id. at 394.

The husband's antecedent debt explains why the note and endorsement were sought. It does not, under the rule applied in this historical decision, establish the asserted new exchange supporting her obligation. The relevant inquiry is the creditor's forbearance arrangement. A modern negotiable-instruments analysis may differ under later statutes, and the casebook flags that point; the historical holding should not silently be presented as a complete account of current commercial-paper law. Id.; casebook 100, note 2.

### Forbearance can be either promised or performed
The opinion expressly recognizes two routes. A creditor can bind himself to delay collection, supplying a return promise. Alternatively, a request for delay can invite actual forbearance as the performance supporting another person's undertaking. A creditor who then refrains for a reasonable time in reliance on that undertaking may supply consideration even without having promised the delay in advance. Id. at 394–95.

This passage prevents an overbroad reading of the case. Strong does not announce that conduct after an endorsement can never matter, that every forbearance must have a fixed calendar endpoint, or that bilateral exchanges are the only enforceable bargains. Its next step is to characterize the specific transaction proved. Where the parties chose exchanged promises, the court tests the content of those promises rather than substituting a different performance bargain. Id. at 395–96.

### The creditor's express reservation removed any meaningful constraint
The evidence did not leave the creditor's undertaking open to a general implication that he would wait a reasonable time. Strong described an express arrangement allowing collection whenever he wanted the money. An immediate lawsuit would have complied with it. Accordingly, the promise did not expose him to liability for exercising a right he already possessed or give the debtor and endorser an enforceable period of repose. Id. at 395–96.

The demand note itself did not extend the debt's maturity. Its provision for interest might be consistent with an expectation of delay, but it did not prevent an immediate suit on the note or original debt. Expected indulgence therefore differed from an enforceable commitment to provide it. The court uses that distinction to explain why the wife's new undertaking lacked the promised reciprocal obligation. Id. at 395–96.

### Actual delay did not rewrite the intended exchange
Strong waited approximately two years, but the court regarded that conduct as insufficient to change the transaction's character. Louisa endorsed in reliance on the express promise described, and the parties intended mutual promises at that time. They did not arrange that a specified or reasonable period of actual forbearance would be the performance that completed the exchange. The consideration question had to be tested by their agreement rather than by what the creditor later chose to do under it. Id. at 396.

This is narrower than saying later conduct is always irrelevant. The opinion's preceding discussion acknowledges the opposite for a genuine request-and-performance bargain. The result turns on how this agreement reserved choice, not merely on the chronological fact that forbearance occurred after the note was delivered. Id. at 395–96.

## Separate opinions and participation
No dissenting opinion is reported. The report states that the participating judges concurred; Gray and Bartlett did not vote and Haight did not sit. That is different from treating every member of the court as having joined the judgment. Id. at 396.

## Study analysis and useful comparisons
A promise to delay collection until a specified date would change the case by relinquishing an immediate right to sue. A request that the creditor actually refrain for two years in return for the wife's undertaking would implicate the performance route the opinion expressly recognizes. By contrast, a promise to wait only until the creditor decides not to wait preserves unrestricted choice. The difference is an identifiable legal constraint or requested performance, rather than the presence of sympathetic motives or a long delay in hindsight.

Comparison with Lake Land exposes a genuine analytical tension. Lake Land's majority treats continuation of an at-will relationship as requested forbearance supporting a new obligation; its dissent emphasizes retention of the termination right. Strong distinguishes an actual-forbearance bargain from an illusory return promise. Identifying which act or promise supplies consideration is therefore more useful than merely calling both relationships terminable at will or both outcomes inconsistent.

The casebook notes a later Uniform Commercial Code provision affecting the precise antecedent-debt setting. That note is subsequent doctrinal context, not authority applied by Andrews in 1895. The transferable study problem remains the difference between a promise that constrains conduct and one that leaves the supposed promisor free to do exactly as he pleases.
