# Rednour v. JC & P Partnership
996 P.2d 487 (Okla. Civ. App. 1999), 2000 OK CIV APP 10 | Contracts

## Holding and vote
The Oklahoma Court of Civil Appeals affirmed summary judgment rejecting an injured apartment resident's bad-faith claim against the premises owner's liability insurer. Although the insurer admitted that the medical-expense provision covered his expenses, that entitlement did not itself create the relationship necessary for tort liability for bad faith. The provision primarily protected the business owner's assets and reduced litigation risk; the injured claimant was an incidental beneficiary for purposes of the asserted bad-faith action. Adams wrote, Jones concurred, and Hansen dissented. The report records the dissenting vote without a separate explanation. The judgment was issued November 5, 1999; the printed 2000 public-domain designation and January 11, 2000 certiorari denial do not change that decision date. (487–490; ¶¶ 1–16.)

## Fall, coverage, and asserted delay
Delbert Glenn Rednour fell in a common area of Hampton Woods Apartments, owned and operated by JC & P Partnership. He requested payment from Acceptance Insurance Company under the medical-expense portion of its Businessowner Liability policy. When payment did not arrive as quickly as he considered appropriate, he sued the premises owner for negligence and the insurer for breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing, seeking actual and punitive damages. No precise expenses, delay duration, or damage amount is specified in the opinion. (488; ¶ 1.)

The insurer conceded his entitlement to medical-expense payments but denied owing him the duty necessary for a bad-faith tort claim because he was not a party to its policy. After responses and additional briefing, the trial court granted summary judgment on that claim. The material facts were undisputed; the appeal presented the legal availability of the bad-faith theory, not whether coverage existed or whether the insurer's payment speed had actually been unreasonable. (488; ¶¶ 2–3.)

Note 1 explains appellate finality. The trial court made the findings required for finality under the applicable statute, but the appellate court still asked whether a separate contractual recovery claim remained. Rednour responded that his only claim was bad faith, including punitive damages, and that the order had resolved it. Thus this appeal did not decide an independent claim for payment under the contract or the negligence claim against the premises owner. (488 n. 1.)

## Relationship required for insurance bad faith
The court examined Oklahoma decisions distinguishing persons entitled to proceeds from persons entitled to enforce the insurer's good-faith duty. Roach allowed a named life-insurance beneficiary's claim because the policy was expressly for that beneficiary's benefit and protection of the beneficiary supplied a substantial reason for buying it. The court emphasized Roach's qualification that the duty did not extend to everyone entitled to insurance money: a contractual or statutory relationship was required. (488–489; ¶¶ 4–5.)

Gianfillippo rejected a passenger's bad-faith claim under another person's liability policy despite coverage for her injury. That contract protected the insured, with only incidental benefits for the third-party claimant. Section 29's express-benefit test directed attention to the contracting parties' intention as reflected in the agreement, rather than payment entitlement alone. Keel showed that a beneficiary need not be named individually, provided the agreement expressly and unmistakably conferred the relevant benefit. (489; ¶¶ 6–8.)

Rednour relied on Townsend, which recognized an insured passenger's statutory and contractual relationship under uninsured-motorist coverage. The court distinguished that protection, bought for the insured and classes including family, permissive users, and passengers, from ordinary liability coverage. Uninsured-motorist insurance promises compensation to the insurer's own insured for loss caused by an uninsured or underinsured driver. Like life insurance in the relevant respect, it could be bought to protect the eventual recipient of its proceeds. The decision did not deny all passengers or all unnamed beneficiaries bad-faith rights. (489; ¶¶ 9–10.)

## Primary purpose of premises medical-expense coverage
The business owner's liability policy served a different primary purpose: protection against damage claims and preservation of its assets. Its medical-expense provision promoted that purpose by paying injured persons promptly without requiring proof of the owner's negligence, thereby reducing the incentive to bring more extensive litigation. The injured person's receipt of money was therefore not sufficient to classify him as an intended beneficiary entitled to bring this bad-faith claim. The court characterized that benefit as incidental for this purpose even though coverage and payment entitlement were admitted. (489–490; ¶¶ 11–12.)

This reasoning concerned the availability of an independent tort remedy. It did not convert the insurer's concession of medical-payment coverage into a denial of that coverage, hold that the apartment owner was free of negligence, or determine the conduct required for a bad-faith judgment where the necessary relationship exists.

## Rental payments did not establish insured status
Rednour also argued that part of his rent contributed to the owner's insurance premium, making him an insured. Note 2 records that he raised the theory only on reconsideration after judgment. The court rejected it on the merits rather than treating its late timing as the sole answer. (490; ¶ 13 & n. 2.)

Sutton, on which he relied, concerned a tenant whose alleged negligence caused fire damage to leased property. Equitable principles and the tenant's insurable interest barred the property insurer from pursuing subrogation against him, treating him as a coinsured for that purpose. That protection from subrogation did not establish a separate affirmative right to sue a premises liability insurer for bad faith in paying personal-injury expenses. Sutton therefore did not apply. (490; ¶ 13.)

## Result and assigned comparison
Because the undisputed facts established no good-faith duty running from this insurer to Rednour under the asserted theory, the court affirmed summary judgment. The actual and punitive remedies sought through that theory were unavailable; the opinion did not merely reduce a punitive award or set an amount payable for the fall. (490; ¶¶ 14–16.)

The assigned discussion at printed 1189 describes the premises-policy purpose and resulting unavailability of punitive damages. Its comments about medical coverage sometimes being bought benevolently and its ensuing hypothetical are editorial discussion, not findings about this policy or a separate holding. The full opinion makes clear that the entire insurer bad-faith claim failed, not only its punitive component.
