# Wells Fargo Bank Minnesota, National Association v. B.C.B.U.
143 Cal. App. 4th 493, 49 Cal. Rptr. 3d 324 (2006), modified Oct. 27, 2006 | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
California’s Fourth District Court of Appeal, Division Three, affirmed a judgment enforcing an assigned equipment lease and its personal guaranty. Acting Presiding Justice Bedsworth wrote; Justices O’Leary and Ikola concurred. The opinion was filed September 27, 2006. The October 27 modification added a footnote addressing a late statutory-effective-date argument, denied rehearing, and left the judgment unchanged. The trial court’s award against the corporate lessee was $107,656.24 plus interest; it also declared the president’s guaranty enforceable. The respondent received appellate costs. (493, 498, 507–508 & n. 9.)

## Facts and procedure
B.C.B.U. operated a Utah nursing home and in-home care service. In October 2000 it began negotiating with Crocker Capital to finance computer equipment. Crocker told B.C.B.U.’s president, Dee R. Bangerter, that signing documents before completion was customary and that Crocker would hold them in escrow pending agreement. On November 22 Bangerter signed an undated lease, an equipment-acceptance certificate, Schedule A, and a personal guaranty. The lease permitted assignment and waived defenses, counterclaims, and setoffs against an assignee that the lessee might have against the lessor. The acceptance certificate stated that the equipment had been received and accepted unconditionally, although a warning beside the signature instructed the signer to wait until actual receipt and satisfaction. (497.)

On December 4 Crocker dated the documents without B.C.B.U.’s permission and assigned the lease and guaranty to a third party for approximately $99,000. The court understood the stipulated unauthorized dating to include Crocker’s signature and December 4 date. The lease entered a securitized pool. Wells Fargo, acting as trustee, reviewed the pool on December 8, found nothing amiss, and received assignment of the lease and guaranty on December 11. B.C.B.U. and Crocker never reached their contemplated deal. On January 30, 2001 they rescinded the lease, and Crocker returned the first and last months’ payments. No equipment was ever delivered or accepted. The apparent acceptance in the signed documents must therefore be distinguished from actual acceptance. (497–498.)

Wells Fargo sued in January 2003 for lease breach and declarations concerning the lease and guaranty. B.C.B.U. and Bangerter asserted cancellation, failure of consideration, and other defenses. B.C.B.U. later dismissed its cross-complaint against Crocker. The case was tried on stipulated facts and declarations, rather than a pleading-stage assumption that allegations were true. The trial judge held the waiver enforceable under California Uniform Commercial Code section 9403 and entered the monetary and declaratory judgment. (498 & n. 5.)

## Issue and statutory framework
Could this qualified assignee enforce the contractual waiver despite nondelivery and the original parties’ subsequent rescission? Section 10407 makes promises in a nonconsumer finance lease irrevocable and independent upon acceptance of the goods. B.C.B.U. argued that its lack of actual acceptance prevented enforcement. Section 9403, however, permits an assignee to enforce an account debtor’s agreement not to assert defenses against an assignor when the assignment is taken for value, in good faith, without notice of a property or possessory claim to the assigned property, and without notice of specified defenses or recoupment claims under section 3305(a). Section 9403(c) preserves defenses assertable against a holder in due course under section 3305(b). This places a qualifying assignee in an analogous protected position; it does not establish that the equipment lease itself was a negotiable instrument. An account may be a payment right not yet earned by performance. (499–501.)

## Holding and reasoning
The court held that section 9403 protected Wells Fargo. The official comments contemplate enforcement by a qualified assignee even when other law would prevent enforcement of the underlying undertaking by the assignor. The agreement must still fall within the provision and the assignee must satisfy its conditions. A lease containing this waiver did so, and Wells Fargo met all four conditions. Section 10407 did not erase the additional protections created by assignment to that qualified purchaser. The court declined to resolve the dispute merely by labeling one statute more specific: the relevant transaction was an assignment of an account and the statutory comments directly explained its treatment. (499–502 & n. 6.)

The court also allocated the loss according to who could prevent the misleading documents from entering commerce. B.C.B.U. could have refused to sign an acceptance certificate contrary to the facts and its express warning. Wells Fargo had no way to discover that the apparently completed transaction remained unfinished. This reasoning protects reliance on facially regular documents under the statutory requirements; it does not grant every assignee immunity from defenses. (501–503.)

Schedule A accompanied the documents in the file but was not physically attached to any of the several documents that referenced it. The trial court found this arrangement neither unusual nor notice of a defense, and the appellate court found no unsupported evidentiary finding. Failure of consideration was not a defense good against a protected holder in due course. Footnote 7 additionally explains why omitting the statute’s requirement of absence of notice changes the operation of section 9403, and why ordinary contract defenses under section 3305(a)(2) are not necessarily defenses under section 3305(b). No recoupment defense was suggested on this record. (503 & n. 7.)

B.C.B.U.’s asserted lack of mutual assent and material alteration likewise did not supply an available defense here. The court expressly declined to decide a hypothetical forgery case: Bangerter had knowingly signed these documents. Nor did calling an altered contract void transform it into an illegal transaction. The court distinguished a void or voidable agreement from one having an unlawful object or consideration; neither kind of illegality was asserted for this lease. Its discussion must be confined to these facts and the statutory defenses it actually applied. (504–505 & n. 8.)

## Rescission, modification, and payment
The assigned account emphasizes the court’s distinction between modification and rescission. Section 9405 permits a good-faith modification or substitution in specified circumstances and gives the assignee corresponding rights under the modified or substituted contract. Cancellation leaves no replacement contractual rights. It therefore was not the kind of modification or substitution the statute protected against an assignee. The court recognized that reducing payments to one dollar could have nearly the same economic effect as rescission, but explained that good faith would remain a constraint on such a modification. The existence of that hypothetical did not authorize the parties to extinguish the assigned contract through rescission. (505–506.)

Section 9406 did not change the result. Before authenticated notice of assignment, an account debtor may discharge its obligation by paying the assignor. The provision does not authorize discharge through all dealings with the assignor. B.C.B.U.’s theory that its January 2001 cancellation was effective because it did not learn of the assignment until January 2003 substituted cancellation for the statutory requirement of payment. (506–507.)

## Evidence, modification, and exact disposition
The acceptance certificate was properly admitted. The parties stipulated that Bangerter had signed it in his authorized corporate capacity. Whether the absence of a physically attached schedule affected the certificate’s legal consequences was different from whether the offered document was authentic. The trial judge acted within his discretion under that distinction. (507–508.)

The later modification addressed B.C.B.U.’s new contention that section 9403 became operative July 1, 2001, after the December 2000 assignment. The petition for rehearing was seven days late, and the argument had not previously been raised. The court additionally explained that former section 9206 supplied the same relevant rule concerning enforceable waivers and defenses good against a holder in due course. This footnote is part of the published, modified report and qualifies the opinion’s discussion of statutory chronology. The judgment was affirmed, with costs to Wells Fargo; no equipment delivery, forgery ruling, or recovery from Crocker was ordered. (499–500 n. 6; 507 n. 9; 508.)
