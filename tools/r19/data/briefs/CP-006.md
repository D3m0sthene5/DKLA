# Alderman v. Baltimore & Ohio Railroad Co.
113 F. Supp. 881 (S.D.W. Va. 1953) | Civil Procedure

## Derailment and the free-pass condition

Alderman, a West Virginia citizen, sued the Maryland railroad for injuries suffered when a train derailed near Adrian, West Virginia, on February 14, 1952. She was traveling within West Virginia on a free round-trip pass between Flatwoods and Morgantown, rather than as a fare-paying passenger. The pass required its holder to assume the risk of injury and property loss and release the railroad from liability, and to declare that receiving and using the free transportation was lawful. That provision determined which level of misconduct could support recovery. 113 F. Supp. at 882.

Alderman's original complaint alleged negligent track maintenance and train operation. After a pretrial conference concerning the release's effect, she amended to allege willful or wanton conduct. The railroad sought summary judgment under Rule 56, supported by affidavits addressing the cause of the derailment. The court was therefore evaluating both the legally required degree of misconduct and whether the record supplied a genuine issue on that misconduct, rather than merely choosing between competing accident accounts.

## The undisputed technical record

The train derailed because a rail broke as the train crossed it. The railroad's undenied affidavits identified an internal transverse fissure within the cap of the rail, which broke vertically under the train's weight. Such defects could occur in new as well as old rails, were not visible during ordinary inspection, and had not been detected in the visual inspection performed the day before the accident. Alderman did not offer a competing account that the railroad actually knew of this particular fissure. Id. at 882.

Her theory instead was that the railroad knowingly retained old and obsolete rails because paying for derailments cost less than replacing the rails. She treated that alleged economic choice as willingness to expose passengers to probable accidents. The court accepted that the contention might bear on negligence but concluded that it did not establish the particular knowledge and conscious wrongdoing required for willful conduct on this record.

## Why ordinary negligence did not suffice

Because the trip was intrastate and the injury occurred in West Virginia, the court applied West Virginia law to the release and the railroad's duty. It acknowledged the normally high care owed by common carriers, including to gratuitous passengers where no assumption-of-risk condition exists. But no identified West Virginia decision directly resolved the duty to a free-pass holder who accepted a release of this kind. Id. at 882–883.

The court considered state indications that such conditions had some validity and compared federal and state statutes authorizing free passes. Supreme Court decisions concerning the federal statute allowed a carrier to contract against ordinary negligence liability to someone accepting a free pass, while public policy prohibited avoiding liability for willful or wanton injury. From those authorities, the District Court concluded that this railroad's operative duty to this plaintiff was to refrain from willfully or wantonly injuring her. Id. at 883.

The applicable West Virginia formulation required consciousness of the conduct and of existing conditions making injury likely or probable, coupled with reckless indifference and an intentionally wrongful act or omission producing injury. Kelly and Stone supplied that state-law formulation. The court did not declare that every free passenger lacks negligence protection regardless of pass conditions; it relied on the actual accepted release.

## Applying the required misconduct standard

The court reasoned that Alderman would need to establish knowledge of the particular defect, knowledge that running the train over it would probably break the rail and derail the train, and an intentional decision to proceed with indifference to those consequences. The undenied affidavits showed an invisible fissure capable of occurring in either new or old rail and an inspection that failed to reveal it. Alderman did not contend that she could establish actual knowledge of that defect. General allegations about old rails and cost-saving policy therefore did not supply the facts necessary for the willfulness theory. Id. at 883.

Although the court described the complaint as lacking sufficient willfulness facts, its order was on a supported summary-judgment motion. The affidavits and absence of contrary proof mattered. The court did not find negligence after a trial, and observing that plaintiff alleged negligence did not establish an actionable negligence claim in light of the release.

## Requested continuance and disposition

At the motion hearing, plaintiff's counsel requested a continuance to substantiate a newspaper report that the railroad retained old rails because derailments were cheaper than replacement. The court denied the request because even proof of that report would, in its analysis, concern negligence rather than the particular defect knowledge required for willful misconduct. The refusal thus rested on the proposed evidence's failure to bridge the legally material gap, rather than a general rule forbidding discovery or delay before summary judgment. Id. at 883.

Chief Judge Moore sustained the railroad's summary-judgment motion and directed entry of an appropriate order. This is a District Court disposition, not an appellate affirmance. The complete opinion contains no separate opinion or judicial footnotes. Its historical release analysis and demanding particular-defect formulation should be understood as the court's application to the 1953 record, rather than a statement that modern passenger claims or all wanton-misconduct theories have identical requirements.

## Assigned scope and procedural significance

The assigned excerpt appears on PDF pages 53–54, printed pages 46–48. The full opinion, including the state carrier-duty discussion and free-pass precedents shortened by the excerpt, was read before drafting. The subsequent editorial questions concern discovery opportunities, amending pleadings, and the risks of deciding complex matters on summary judgment; they are not additional judicial rulings.

The case illustrates the interaction between substantive law and a genuine factual dispute. Evidence suggesting poor maintenance policy does not necessarily create a trial issue where the surviving cause of action requires conscious knowledge of a specific dangerous condition. It also illustrates why a continuance request should identify evidence capable of satisfying the controlling elements. Whether the court chose too narrow a misconduct standard is a distinct question from whether the affidavits were contradicted.
