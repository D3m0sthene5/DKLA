# Cable & Computer Technology, Inc. v. Lockheed Saunders, Inc.
175 F.R.D. 646 (C.D. Cal. 1997) | Civil Procedure | Contention interrogatories and cooperation in discovery

## Two projects and an early discovery dispute

Cable & Computer Technology alleged that Lockheed entities deprived it of military equipment contracts. The complete opinion describes two projects. In 1993 the companies began working together on a mission computer for B-52 and B-1B aircraft. Their arrangement divided responsibility for parts of the equipment. Although the teaming agreement expired in 1995, they continued working together, and the Air Force awarded B-52 work in February 1996. The complaint alleged that, after Lockheed acquired an Owego operation, defendants secretly withdrew from the plaintiff's team to eliminate it from competition for a B-1B upgrade. The plaintiff said it could not find a replacement before the November 1996 proposal deadline. It alleged an eventual award to Owego and claimed development expenditures and lost profits totaling more than $20 million. Those were allegations and claimed damages, rather than findings that the defendants had breached an agreement or caused those losses. 175 F.R.D. at 647-649.

The other project concerned radar simulators under an Advanced Technology Support Program arrangement. Cable & Computer alleged that defendants diverted work covered by a 1994 subcontract and claimed approximately $300,000 arising from that project. The assigned excerpt's introductory description concentrates on radar simulators; the full judicial opinion supplies the broader mission-computer dispute. Id. at 648-649.

The plaintiff filed its seven-count complaint in state court on June 19, 1997. Defendants removed the action on July 18 on diversity grounds. The parties held their Rule 26(f) conference on August 14, a date identified in the full opinion's second footnote. By September, discovery disagreements had produced competing motions. The decision here is Magistrate Judge Rosalyn M. Chapman's October 22, 1997 order resolving discovery matters, not a judgment on the contractual or other claims. Id. at 648-650 & n.2.

## The plaintiff's withdrawn motion still produced a sanction

The plaintiff sought expedited consideration of a deposition dispute, citing the personal and professional availability of participants. Its September 2 ex parte request to shorten time was denied on September 5. It then brought its discovery motion without following the applicable conference and joint-stipulation procedures. The court criticized the resulting unnecessary burden on the court and opposing parties. Local discovery procedures required a real effort to resolve disagreements and presentation through the prescribed joint stipulation. Id. at 649-650.

The plaintiff withdrew its motion on October 15, as footnote 1 records. Withdrawal did not cure its earlier failure to comply with the local procedures. The court imposed a **$500 sanction on plaintiff's counsel**, payable to the clerk within ten days under Local Rule 7.15. This sanction should be distinguished from defendants' motion to compel: it responded to counsel's procedural misconduct, and the withdrawn motion was not decided adversely on its substantive merits. Id. at 649-650 & n.1.

## Defendants sought facts underlying the plaintiff's contentions

Defendants used the joint-stipulation process to move to compel answers to interrogatories 1, 5 through 11, and 14 through 16. Cable & Computer objected to all of them as premature contention interrogatories. It argued that answers should await further discovery, relying on *In re Convergent Technologies Securities Litigation*. The dispute therefore concerned when a plaintiff must explain the factual basis of its claims, rather than whether the plaintiff must supply a final trial presentation before it has had discovery. Id. at 649, 651-652.

The court described several uses of the term “contention interrogatory”: questions about what a party contends, the facts supporting an allegation, its position about how law applies to facts, or the legal basis or theory of a specified contention. That category includes quite different questions. A label alone does not establish that a particular interrogatory demands an unreasonable premature disclosure. Id. at 651.

The operative historical rules are those discussed by this 1997 court. Rule 33(c) allowed an interrogatory seeking an opinion or contention concerning fact or the application of law to fact. It also allowed a court to defer an answer until designated discovery had occurred, a pretrial conference, or another later time. Thus, permission to ask did not remove judicial discretion over timing. Rule 26(b)(1), as then worded, used the “reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence” standard, and Rule 33 limited the number of interrogatories to twenty-five. These are the rules addressed in this decision; the brief does not present their historical wording as the wording of today's rules. Id. at 650-651.

## The burden of showing why an answer should be deferred

The court placed the burden on the party opposing discovery to justify its refusal. It rejected the plaintiff's reading of *Convergent Technologies* as automatically shifting the burden to the proponent whenever an interrogatory could be classified as a contention interrogatory. Judge Brazil's earlier discussion warned against premature contention questions and emphasized their justification early in a case. His later discussion in *McCormick*, however, recognized that contention interrogatories could be a more reliable and cost-effective method of discovery than asking the same contention questions at depositions. Judge Chapman evaluated the particular requests under the ordinary burden borne by the party resisting discovery. Id. at 651-652.

The opinion also addressed the concern that an early answer might freeze a party's case. It explained that an interrogatory answer did not invariably confine the party's trial proof. Answers could be amended with leave of court, and Rule 26(e) required supplementation when appropriate. A party could therefore provide the information it presently had without pretending that discovery was complete. That qualification matters: the court required current factual disclosure, not an irrevocable final commitment to a fully developed case. Id. at 651-652.

## Application to these interrogatories

Interrogatory 1 sought information about damages. The court accepted that it was too early to require the plaintiff to provide expert opinions on that subject. Nevertheless, the plaintiff could answer based on the information available to it at the time, later amending or supplementing the answer. The absence of a completed expert analysis did not justify withholding all presently available damages information. Id. at 652.

The other disputed requests were straightforward factual contention interrogatories about allegations the plaintiff had made. Footnote 3 reproduces requests seeking the facts supporting particular allegations and related documents, statements, or actions. In particular, requests 8 and 9 addressed the documentary and other support for the plaintiff's asserted account. The plaintiff should have been able to identify the factual basis then available for its own allegations. Requiring those responses was consistent with the obligations underlying Rule 11. Id. at 651 n.3, 652.

The court linked that conclusion to reasonable discovery cooperation and Rule 1's objective of a just, speedy, and inexpensive determination. The holding is that the plaintiff's blanket prematurity objection failed as to these requests. It does not establish a categorical rule that every contention question must be answered immediately regardless of burden, complexity, the information sought, or a court's authority to defer answers. Id. at 652.

## Exact disposition and assigned coverage

The court granted defendants' motion to compel responses to interrogatories 1, 5-11, and 14-16 and required compliance **within thirty days**. It separately required counsel's $500 payment within ten days. These orders did not alter the deadlines already established by District Judge Ideman. The concluding order also directed service through the clerk, including the court's financial officer for the sanction. No liability or damages issue on the underlying seven-count complaint was resolved. Id. at 652.
