# Coney Island Auto Parts Unlimited, Inc. v. Burton

**607 U.S. 155 (2026) · Supreme Court of the United States · January 20, 2026**

## A default judgment challenged after enforcement

Vista-Pro Automotive entered bankruptcy in 2014 and brought an adversarial proceeding against Coney Island Auto Parts to collect $50,000 in allegedly unpaid invoices. Vista-Pro attempted service by mail, but Coney Island later maintained that the mailing did not comply with Bankruptcy Rule 7004(b)(3). Coney Island did not answer, and the bankruptcy court entered a default judgment in 2015. The Supreme Court described the asserted service defect as alleged; it did not determine whether service was defective or whether any defect made the judgment void. 607 U.S. at 157, 160 n.4.

The trustee spent several years attempting to enforce the judgment. In April 2016, the trustee sent a demand letter to Coney Island’s chief executive; the lower courts concluded that the letter gave the company notice of the judgment and enforcement efforts. A marshal seized funds from Coney Island’s bank account in 2021. The company then moved under Civil Rule 60 to vacate the judgment, arguing that improper service rendered it void. Id. at 157.

The bankruptcy court denied relief because the motion had not been made within a reasonable time. The district court and Sixth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted review to resolve disagreement over whether Rule 60(c)(1)’s reasonable-time requirement applies to a motion asserting voidness under Rule 60(b)(4). The dispute concerned the governing timing requirement, rather than a Supreme Court calculation of a reasonable period on these particular facts. Id. at 158, 162.

## Question and holding

Must a motion seeking relief from an allegedly void judgment under Rule 60(b)(4) satisfy Rule 60(c)(1)’s requirement that Rule 60(b) motions be made within a reasonable time?

Yes. Subdivision (b)(4) falls within Rule 60(b), and subdivision (c)(1) applies its reasonable-time requirement to motions under Rule 60(b). Coney Island did not contend that its motion satisfied that requirement, so the Supreme Court affirmed the Sixth Circuit’s judgment without further defining what would have been a reasonable time in this case. Id. at 158, 162.

## Text and structure of Rule 60

Justice Alito’s opinion began with the rule’s direct language. Rule 60(b)(4) authorizes relief from a void judgment; Rule 60(c)(1) requires a motion under Rule 60(b) to be made within a reasonable time. Because a voidness motion is such a motion, the timing requirement applies. The opinion cited *Kemp v. United States*’s statement that all Rule 60(b) motions must satisfy that requirement. Id. at 158.

The rule’s structure confirmed that reading. It expressly imposes an additional one-year limit on motions alleging mistake, new evidence, or fraud under subdivisions (b)(1)–(3). It does not create an analogous exception allowing voidness motions at any time. The Court therefore declined to infer an unlimited period contrary to the rule’s stated default. The decision applies a reasonable-time requirement to subdivision (b)(4); it does not impose the separate one-year cap on that subdivision. Id.

## Why a legal nullity does not imply unlimited time to move

Coney Island and supporting authorities argued that a void judgment is a legal nullity and cannot become valid through the passage of time. The Court answered that the inability of time to cure an error does not establish an unlimited opportunity to seek judicial relief from it. Procedural rules routinely restrict the period for challenging legal errors. To establish an unlimited period notwithstanding Rule 60’s language, a litigant would need a separate legal basis for that right. Coney Island expressly disclaimed a constitutional challenge. Id. at 159–160.

The majority suggested that a reasonable opportunity to seek relief might satisfy due process. It also identified the far-reaching consequences of treating alleged voidness as eliminating deadlines altogether, including possible disregard of appeal and certiorari filing deadlines when subject-matter jurisdiction was contested. Those observations formed part of the majority’s reasoning; they did not resolve a properly presented due-process challenge to the rule in this case. Id. at 159–160 & n.3.

The company argued that defective service could leave a defendant unaware of proceedings long after entry of judgment. The Court considered the reasonable-time standard capable of accommodating that circumstance. Unlike a fixed numerical deadline, it could permit consideration of when the defendant learned of attempted enforcement. The Court did not hold that the clock invariably begins at judgment, nor did it announce an automatic entitlement to delay until collection occurs. Id. at 160.

## Historical practice and the other arguments

The historical record did not establish a consensus allowing voidness challenges indefinitely. Some courts had granted relief many years after judgment, particularly when service had failed; others applied laches or other timing limitations. In any event, the Court gave Rule 60(b)’s text and structure priority over the asserted practice. It expressly reserved the limits governing alternative means of obtaining relief preserved by Rule 60(d). Its holding is accordingly about Rule 60(b) motions, rather than every possible collateral challenge or independent action. Id. at 160–161 & n.5.

*Insurance Corp. of Ireland* did not supply a contrary rule. Although that decision permitted a defendant to ignore proceedings, risk default, and later challenge personal jurisdiction collaterally, it also recognized that the exercise of legal rights can be subject to procedural requirements and that failure to object in time can forfeit a right. That qualification undermined the claim of unlimited time for a Rule 60(b)(4) motion. The Court also rejected reliance on policy, drafting history, and constitutional avoidance to override language it found unambiguous. Id. at 161.

## Sotomayor’s concurrence and the exact judgment

Eight Justices joined Alito’s opinion. Justice Sotomayor concurred in the judgment, agreeing that Rule 60’s text and structure required a reasonable-time limit. She declined to join because the majority discussed the potential validity of a due-process challenge that Coney Island had neither raised below nor advanced in the Supreme Court. Her separate opinion favored adhering to the Court’s usual practice of leaving such unpresented questions alone. Id. at 162.

All nine Justices agreed with the result; the Court’s opinion had eight votes. The Sixth Circuit’s judgment was affirmed. Because Coney Island did not argue that its motion was timely under the applicable standard, no remand was ordered to reconsider the reasonableness of its delay. The Court separately reserved whether the asserted service defect would make the judgment void. Id. at 160 n.4, 162.
