# Donovan v. RRL Corporation (Supreme Court)
26 Cal. 4th 261, 27 P.3d 702 (2001), July 30, 2001, as modified September 12, 2001 | Contracts

## Holding and divided judgment
The California Supreme Court held that a dealer’s advertisement for a specific used car at a stated price, interpreted in the setting of California’s advertised-price regulation, was an offer accepted by the customer’s tender. The advertisement also satisfied the goods statute of frauds. Nevertheless, the dealer could rescind for a good-faith unilateral mistake of fact: the substantial pricing error concerned a basic assumption, materially disadvantaged the dealer, was not a risk allocated to it, and would make enforcement unconscionable. The Court therefore reversed the Court of Appeal, concluding that the municipal court correctly entered judgment for the dealer, although the trial court’s contract-formation rationale was mistaken. Chief Justice George wrote for himself and Justices Kennard, Chin, and Brown. Justice Werdegar dissented, joined by Justice Baxter: four votes to two among the participating justices. The published report includes the September 12 modification and rehearing denial; Werdegar and Baxter favored rehearing. (266–267, 294–296.)

## Transaction and trial
The April 26, 1997 Daily Pilot advertisement announced a two-day used-car sale and identified a sapphire-blue 1995 Jaguar XJ6 Vanden Plas by its vehicle identification number, at $25,995. It displayed the dealer’s name, address, and location map, listed fifteen other cars, disclosed additional charges and an approved-credit condition, and expired April 27. Donovan also visited a Jaguar dealership on April 26, where other 1995 Jaguars were priced $8,000–$10,000 higher. At RRL’s dealership the next day, he verified the vehicle identification number and test-drove the car with his spouse. A salesperson suggested that a Lexus dealership could offer a better Jaguar price, but neither initially mentioned the specific advertised amount. The buyers noticed high mileage, apparent rust, worn tires, and less cleanliness, yet considered the price attractive. When Donovan offered to buy at about $26,000 and showed the advertisement, the salesperson immediately identified the mistake. (267–268.)

The sales manager apologized and offered reimbursement for Donovan’s fuel, time, and effort. Donovan declined, asserted that there had been no mistake, and offered a check for the advertised price. The manager refused and offered a price of $37,016, based on RRL’s $35,000 investment. Donovan again insisted on the advertised amount and left. RRL later advertised the car at an intended price of $37,995 and sold it for $38,399. The intended advertisement had been for a different, 1994 Jaguar at $25,995: the newspaper replaced the old advertisement’s “Save” with that price but failed to replace the 1995 vehicle description. Its staff made both typesetting and proofreading errors and acknowledged them in a retraction. RRL did not check the revised proof. Its other newspaper advertisements correctly described the 1994 vehicle. (268–269, 289–290.)

Donovan sued for contract breach, fraud, and negligence. The municipal court disallowed the negligence cause of action, found a good-faith mistake without deceptive intent and Donovan’s lack of awareness of the mistake, and entered judgment for RRL. It thought the mistaken price negated contractual intent despite otherwise sufficient advertising and signature. Donovan confined his appeal to contract breach. The superior court’s appellate department reversed and directed calculation of damages; the Court of Appeal likewise reversed after transfer, treating the advertisement as an offer and finding RRL negligent under its appellate factfinding authority. The Supreme Court granted review and requested briefing on the applicable Uniform Commercial Code provisions. (269–270.)

## Formation and the writing
Objective manifestations govern formation. A price error unknown to the buyer does not itself erase an otherwise operative offer; mistake instead concerns whether the resulting agreement can be avoided. Because the trial court made no relevant offer-interpretation findings, the Supreme Court reviewed that question de novo. It discussed ordinary advertising invitations and specific-performance exceptions, but expressly declined to resolve the validity of the general advertising rule. The decisive setting was a licensed automobile dealer’s advertisement of one particular vehicle under Vehicle Code section 11713.1(e) and its implementing regulation. Those rules required advertised vehicles to be sold at the advertised price while unsold and within the advertisement’s specified period, creating reasonable consumer expectations of an operative offer. (270–275; notes 1–2 reproduce the relevant statutory rule and identify licensing and misdemeanor consequences.)

The Court distinguished a statute’s relevance to reasonable expectations from displacement of all common-law contract defenses. Nor did the required later written sales agreement and its additional terms prevent present assent on vehicle and price. Tender must be commercially acceptable, and formal statutory disclosures, title delivery, and registration requirements still must be completed; the holding did not eliminate them. The transaction was governed by the goods provisions of the California Uniform Commercial Code. (275–276 & notes 3–4.)

Under then-applicable section 2201, the advertisement was a sufficient written memorial for the goods transaction. A signature could be a printed symbol adopted to authenticate a writing. The dealer’s printed name, in an advertisement objectively communicating its own offer, served that authenticating function even without a separate handwritten sales contract. The Court did not impose a universal rule that every merchant name on every advertisement binds the merchant. (276–278.)

## Mistake and allocation of risk
The Court distinguished an unknown factual pricing error from the subjective interpretation of an actual contractual duty in Hedging Concepts. California’s Kemper and Elsinore decisions already authorized equitable relief beyond the first Restatement’s strict requirement that the other party know of or cause a unilateral mistake. The Court expressly adopted Restatement Second section 153(a), permitting avoidance for an unconscionable effect if the mistaken party does not bear the risk. Where the counterparty neither caused nor had reason to know of the mistake, the mistaken party must establish a mistake about a basic assumption, a material adverse effect on the exchange, absence of allocated risk, and unconscionability of enforcement. (278–282 & note 6.)

The $12,000 disparity between the intended $37,995 price and the erroneous $25,995 price was approximately 32 percent. It was material, making the transaction substantially less favorable to RRL and correspondingly more favorable to Donovan. The parties neither assigned the risk by agreement nor dealt on the basis of consciously limited knowledge, leaving reasonable judicial allocation under section 154 as the relevant issue. (282–283.)

Ordinary negligence did not necessarily constitute neglect of a legal duty under Civil Code section 1577. Equitable considerations and the standards of good faith and fair dealing distinguished an excusable business error from fault severe enough to preclude relief. Even a statutory duty of reasonable care, or a negligence-per-se argument, did not automatically convert a careless omission into disqualifying neglect. The advertised-price regulation did not occupy the contract field or eliminate mistake defenses. (283–289 & notes 7–10.)

The actual evidence supported good faith: RRL regularly reviewed advertisements, correctly advertised the replacement vehicle elsewhere, and had procedures for informing sales staff and customers of discovered errors. Nothing showed prior knowledge, an intent to mislead, or a practice of deliberate indifference. Relying on the newspaper’s revised proofreading, instead of checking again itself, might be negligent but was not the extreme failure of fair dealing warranting allocation of all pricing risk to RRL. The Court left an actual statutory violation unresolved and declined an amicus’s unpreserved request for a private statutory damages action. Note 11 explains credible testimony about a later $2,000 price reduction and differing newspaper press dates; note 12 records that the statutory recovery theory had not been raised. (289–291 & notes 11–12.)

## Unconscionability and rescission procedure
Unconscionability supporting relief for mistake differs from the usual inquiry into an oppressive formation process under Civil Code section 1670.5. The mistake itself supplied surprise, so an excessively harsh substantive exchange could justify relief even though the dealer was not the weaker bargaining party. Enforcement would cost RRL more than $9,000 of its purchase investment and give Donovan the $12,000 benefit of an unintended price. Immediate explanation, apology, and an offer to reimburse the actual expense of responding permitted restoration of the status quo. Loss of the mistaken bargain’s advantage was not the detrimental change of position that barred relief in Drennan. These circumstances, rather than a mechanical rule invalidating every low price, supported rescission. (291–294.)

The majority acknowledged that RRL had not requested rescission as an affirmative remedy but invoked the rule sustaining a trial judgment correct on another ground. It treated rescission as a defense to the contract action, found the mistake issue extensively briefed and supported by trial evidence, and considered the immediate refusal, explanation, and reimbursement offer adequate informal notice. Those propositions appear in note 5 and are contested, rather than unanimously accepted procedural facts. (278–279 & note 5.)

## Dissent and final result
Werdegar agreed that an enforceable contract formed but objected to granting unsolicited rescission on an undeveloped factual and procedural record. RRL’s position had been that no contract formed; the rescission theory came from amici. The buyer had not had a fair opportunity to brief its prerequisites. In her view, refusing a proposed contract was different from notice rescinding an existing one, and delay could have caused litigation expense or other prejudice. Her sole note distinguishes Wilson’s prompt repudiation of an executed written contract. (294–295 & dissent note 1.)

She also questioned the assumption that all relevant evidence had been presented: the trial court did not address unconscionability, additional evidence could concern RRL’s economic loss or Donovan’s lost opportunities, and the Court did not adjust equitable compensation under section 1692. She would affirm the intermediate decision or, at most, transfer for briefing and decision on rescission, with further trial proceedings if necessary. These are dissenting procedural objections, not the majority’s disposition. (295–296.)

The formal judgment reversed the Court of Appeal; the majority concluded that judgment for RRL was correct because rescission was available.
