# Masterson v. Sine
68 Cal.2d 222, 436 P.2d 561 (Cal. 1968) | Contracts | Partial integration and a collateral restriction on assignment

## A family conveyance with a reserved repurchase option

Dallas and Rebecca Masterson owned a ranch as tenants in common. On February 25, 1958, they conveyed it by grant deed to Dallas's sister Medora Sine and her husband Lu. The deed reserved an option for the grantors to repurchase on or before February 25, 1968. It described the price by reference to the consideration previously paid, augmented by the depreciation value of improvements the grantees might add beginning two and one-half years after the conveyance. Dallas subsequently became bankrupt. His trustee and Rebecca brought a declaratory-relief action to establish their right to enforce the option. Whether the option was transferable thus determined whether the trustee could realize its value for creditors. (68 Cal.2d at 224.)

At the bench trial, the court admitted extrinsic evidence explaining the price terms: both sides meant a base price of $50,000, plus capital expenditures for improvements less depreciation allowable under federal income-tax rules at exercise. But it excluded the Sines' evidence that the parties agreed the option was personal to the grantors and nonassignable so that the ranch would remain in the Masterson family. Judgment declared the plaintiffs entitled to exercise, specified how, and reserved jurisdiction over exercise and improvement compensation. The Sines appealed, challenging both enforceability of the option's uncertain wording and the evidentiary rulings. (Id. at 224–225.)

## Explanation of the written price was proper

Chief Justice Traynor agreed with the trial court that the option should not fail through an excessively exacting demand for completeness and clarity. The parties plainly intended a repurchase option; evidence explaining their price terminology supplied sufficient certainty for specific enforcement. The majority therefore did not treat all outside evidence as inadmissible whenever the transaction concerned a deed. Nor did reversal establish that the option was void for indefiniteness. The error concerned exclusion of the asserted restriction on assignment. Justice Burke's dissent agreed that admitting evidence to explain the unclear price phrases was proper. (Id. at 224–225, 232.)

## Integration depends on the parties' intended scope

If the parties adopt a writing as the complete and final embodiment of their agreement, outside evidence cannot add to or vary it. If they integrate only part of their agreement, the rule protects that part while allowing proof of elements not reduced to writing. The crucial question is whether they intended the writing to be exclusive. A merger clause may bear on that inquiry, but the court also must examine the alleged collateral agreement and the circumstances to determine whether the writing was meant to include, exclude, or otherwise affect its subject. The document's apparent completeness cannot alone establish the scope of integration. (Id. at 225–227.)

The majority found earlier California formulations restricting the inquiry to the instrument's face inconsistently applied. A writing can look complete while memorializing only part of a broader transaction: a promissory note, for example, does not reveal by itself whether it embodies all present obligations or only one component. The court balanced the comparative reliability of writing against risks of fabricated recollection and the countervailing risk of defeating actual intent. Directly contradictory agreements remain excluded. For collateral terms, the majority used the first Restatement's test whether similarly situated parties might naturally make the agreement separately. It also discussed the more permissive UCC formulation excluding terms that certainly would have appeared in the writing, without treating this real-estate option as an Article 2 sale. (Id. at 226–228.)

The first majority footnote additionally discussed Corbin's suggestion that even an unusual separate agreement could be proved where the court was persuaded it actually occurred. That discussion should not erase the opinion's operative application of the natural-separate-agreement standard to this family transaction. The second footnote supplied a deed example involving an oral promise of nearby boat spaces despite a written condition against building a pier or boathouse. These notes support an inquiry into actual integration rather than a universal permission to contradict deeds. (Id. at 228; majority nn.1–2.)

## The alleged personal restriction could be proved

The option clause contained no statement that it embodied the whole bargain and said nothing about assignability. The majority thought the formal structure of a deed made inclusion of every collateral agreement less likely. Recording the reservation could protect the grantors against subsequent purchasers without reciting its asserted personal character. It also found no indication that these family participants had warning of the disadvantage of omitting the entire agreement. A family-retention restriction therefore might naturally have been made separately and was certainly not a term that necessarily would have been included. (Id. at 228–229.)

Assignable options ordinarily are presumed where the agreement prohibits no transfer and involves no personal performance. But the majority distinguished a presumed legal term from an express contractual term: a writing need not bar evidence rebutting a term the law would otherwise supply. Its third footnote explained that several contrary authorities depended on finding complete integration, rather than independently making implied terms conclusive. A statute can foreclose rebuttal, but the majority found no controlling statute doing so here. Contract rights may be made nontransferable, and personal obligations sometimes arise by inference where substituted performance would differ materially. The alleged family restriction consequently was admissible. (Id. at 229–231; majority n.3.)

The holding was an evidentiary and integration ruling. The court did not find after hearing the excluded testimony that the parties actually agreed to nonassignability, declare the trustee defeated on the merits, or finally determine what sum was due to exercise. Those were consequences the Sines sought, not facts adjudicated by the Supreme Court. Its express judgment was reversal because the trial court wrongly excluded the offer of proof. (Id. at 231.)

## Burke's dissent: contradiction, property rights, and creditor reliance

Burke, joined by McComb, would affirm. He regarded the unrestricted option as carrying a substantive right of transfer, reinforced by Civil Code provisions favoring transferability of property and rights arising from obligations. Adding a personal limitation by oral testimony contradicted that grant; silence was not an invitation to subtract an incident of ownership. The majority's examples concerning presumed times, places, or durations concerned missing contractual details, in his view, rather than a fundamental property right. Authorities on personal services likewise did not justify restricting an ordinary option exercisable by payment. (Id. at 231–235, 240–242; dissent nn.4–6, 12–13.)

The dissent challenged the majority's description of a relatively informal family transaction. The sale used written escrow instructions and a title company. Dallas testified that he consulted his attorney, described his discussions with the Sines, obtained the attorney's wording, and took it to the title company. Neither the escrow instructions nor the deed contained the supposed restriction. Adding a short nonassignment clause, Burke argued, posed no serious structural difficulty. He also disputed the majority's treatment of prior cases, distinguishing genuinely incomplete writings and outside evidence that did not contradict them. (Id. at 233–236, 239–240.)

Burke saw the statute-based parol-evidence rule as substantive legislative policy already chosen to protect credible written agreements, rather than a fresh policy balance for judges. A test of what families might naturally agree separately would create variable, unpredictable decisions. More concretely, the excluded evidence was the bankrupt optionee's own testimony, offered by his sister and brother-in-law. Allowing relatives to claim an unwritten personal restriction could remove valuable assets from creditors and unsettle title reliance. He raised further questions about Rebecca's interest, later transfers after repurchase, and future purchasers' ability to claim the same restriction. These were dissenting concerns and hypotheticals, not findings that the family had committed fraud. (Id. at 231–232, 236–242; dissent nn.7–11, 14.)

## Judgment and complete reading

The California Supreme Court reversed on February 6, 1968, by five votes to two. Traynor's opinion was joined by Peters, Tobriner, Mosk, and Sullivan; Burke dissented with McComb. Rehearing was denied March 6, and the opinion was modified to the form reproduced in the reporter. Burke and McComb favored rehearing. The published disposition is reversal, not an affirmance or an appellate determination that the oral restriction was proved. (Id. at 231, 242.)
