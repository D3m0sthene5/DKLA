## Parties and requested relief

The respondents were prisoners sentenced to death in Oklahoma and Texas, where executions were to occur by lethal injection. They petitioned the Food and Drug Administration to stop what they alleged were violations of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, or FDCA, in the distribution and use of execution drugs. Margaret Heckler, Secretary of Health and Human Services, was the petitioner defending the FDA's refusal to take the requested enforcement action. The underlying regulatory dispute involved federal control over drugs used by state prison authorities; the judicial proceeding sought review of the federal agency's response. 470 U.S. at 823-825. The prisoners requested several different interventions: labeling changes warning against execution use; notices to manufacturers, distributors, and prison officials; seizure of drugs intended for that use; and recommendations that those involved in the distribution chain be prosecuted. The breadth of these requests mattered because the Court evaluated an agency choice among enforcement measures, rather than a demand to perform a single expressly mandated ministerial act. Id. at 823-824, 831-832.

## Material facts and statutory setting

The drugs had been approved for therapeutic uses, but had not been approved for use in executions. The prisoners alleged that administering them to cause death amounted to an unapproved use of new drugs and to misbranding under the FDCA. They also asserted that the drugs had not been shown to produce the intended result safely and effectively in that setting and that untrained personnel could cause prolonged suffering. These were the prisoners' allegations; the Supreme Court did not adjudicate the medical adequacy of a particular execution protocol. Id. at 823-824.

The FDA Commissioner declined the petition on two grounds. First, he questioned the agency's jurisdiction over the drugs' use in state executions, distinguishing its authority over products in commerce from the use asserted here. Second, even assuming jurisdiction, he regarded a decision to take enforcement action as discretionary. The response referred to the agency's practice concerning unapproved uses, the absence of the kind of serious public-health danger or blatant fraud that normally prompted action, and the state criminal-justice context. That independent discretionary ground allowed the Court to decide reviewability without deciding the disputed jurisdictional question. Id. at 824-825, 828.

The FDCA prohibited specified conduct involving adulterated, misbranded, and unapproved drugs and supplied several enforcement mechanisms. The prisoners relied particularly on provisions authorizing investigations and enforcement and on a provision excusing referral for prosecution of minor violations when the public interest would be served by a warning. Their theory was that the statutory scheme and an FDA statement about dangerous unapproved uses constrained the Commissioner's discretion. The distinction between a substantive prohibition and a direction requiring the agency to prosecute a particular violation became central to the case. Id. at 835-837.

## Procedural history

The prisoners sued federal officials in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia. The district court granted summary judgment for the government, treating the enforcement decision as committed to agency discretion. A divided D.C. Circuit reversed. It concluded that the FDA possessed jurisdiction, that the refusal was reviewable, and that the refusal was arbitrary and capricious. Its reasoning relied in part on an FDA policy statement that it understood to require action against dangerous unapproved uses. Id. at 825-828.

The Supreme Court granted review and reversed the court of appeals. It addressed the threshold availability of judicial review under the Administrative Procedure Act, or APA. Because the particular refusal fell within § 701(a)(2), the majority did not reach whether the agency had correctly interpreted its jurisdiction or reasonably evaluated the merits of the prisoners' regulatory concerns. Marshall would have reached the reasonableness of the refusal and sustained it on that different basis. Id. at 828, 837-842.

## Questions presented

Does APA § 701(a)(2), which excludes action committed to agency discretion by law, ordinarily preclude judicial review of an agency's refusal to institute enforcement proceedings? Can that presumption be displaced by statutory restrictions or other binding standards, and did the FDCA or the materials relied upon here supply such standards? A further question was whether the general presumption was consistent with the APA's express authorization to review agency action for abuse of discretion. Id. at 828-837.

## Rule and holdings

An agency's decision not to enforce is presumptively unreviewable under § 701(a)(2). The presumption rests on the nature of enforcement choices and the usual absence of a judicially manageable standard. Congress may displace it by providing substantive guidelines that restrict enforcement discretion. The FDCA provisions invoked in this case did not supply the necessary restrictions, and the purported FDA policy statement did not establish a binding enforcement duty. Id. at 831-837.

The rule is limited. The Court did not resolve the treatment of a refusal based solely on the agency's belief that it lacks jurisdiction, an extreme general policy amounting to abdication of statutory responsibilities, or a colorable constitutional claim. It also did not decide the broader proposition that a genuine agency rule could never provide law to apply. These matters were reserved or left open; the opinion did not announce that every asserted exception would necessarily secure review. Id. at 833 n.4, 836, 838.

## Actual judgment

The Supreme Court reversed the D.C. Circuit's judgment. The FDA's particular refusal was not subject to the requested APA review. The Court did not order relabeling, seizure, investigation, prosecution, or any change in the execution practices. It left FDA jurisdiction undecided and did not hold the agency's substantive assessment correct after arbitrary-and-capricious review. The majority ended the case at reviewability. Id. at 828, 837-838.

## Court's reasoning

### The two exclusions in § 701(a) do different work

Section 701(a)(1) concerns statutes that preclude judicial review. That inquiry examines whether Congress intended to foreclose review in the relevant statutory scheme. Section 701(a)(2) addresses a different circumstance: even absent affirmative evidence of preclusion, the law may provide no meaningful standard against which a court can judge the challenged discretion. The latter exclusion is narrow, but it is not empty. Id. at 828-830.

This reading reconciles § 701(a)(2) with § 706's authorization to set aside an abuse of discretion. Courts can review the exercise of discretion when law supplies criteria for identifying abuse. When the relevant decision is committed to agency judgment without such criteria, § 706 does not manufacture a standard by its own reference to abuse. The prisoners therefore could not establish reviewability merely by pointing to that general review provision. Id. at 830.

### Enforcement choices ordinarily depend on considerations entrusted to the agency

An agency deciding whether to act must assess more than whether a violation may have occurred. It considers the strength of the available proof, the likelihood of success, the use of limited resources, competing priorities, and whether the requested intervention advances its overall statutory program. The agency generally has greater competence than a court to make that combined assessment. A court cannot infer a duty to pursue each possible violation simply from the law's prohibition of the conduct. Id. at 831-832.

Nonenforcement also ordinarily leaves the agency without exercising coercive power over an individual's liberty or property. Affirmative enforcement presents a concrete exercise of authority whose statutory basis and legal consequences can be evaluated. A refusal often lacks that same focus. The Court used criminal prosecutorial discretion as an analogy supporting the usual allocation of responsibility, while stopping short of deciding that the Constitution immunizes all civil regulatory inaction. Id. at 832-833 & n.4.

### Overton Park did not resolve the distinct nonenforcement problem

Citizens to Preserve Overton Park concerned affirmative approval of a highway through a public park. The governing law restricted that approval unless specified conditions were met. Those substantive conditions furnished law to apply. The Court thus distinguished both the nature of the challenged conduct and the statutory framework from the FDA's refusal here. Overton Park's description of § 701(a)(2) as a narrow exception remained valid without creating an ordinary presumption of review for every enforcement refusal. Id. at 830-831.

### Congress can replace the ordinary presumption with enforceable instructions

The Court reconciled its holding with Dunlop v. Bachowski, which permitted review of the Secretary of Labor's refusal to sue over a disputed union election. The labor statute required investigation and directed suit when the Secretary found probable cause to believe a qualifying violation had occurred. Its structure and mandatory directions meaningfully limited enforcement discretion. The result in Dunlop demonstrated the capacity of a specific statutory scheme to rebut the usual presumption; it did not establish that the general APA alone makes every refusal reviewable. Id. at 833-835.

### The FDCA authorized enforcement without requiring the requested action

The FDCA's investigation, injunction, and seizure provisions supplied enforcement powers, but did not tell the FDA when it must use them. The criminal provision's direction concerning punishment did not itself require the Commissioner to recommend prosecution whenever a violation was alleged. The agency's role in recommending proceedings also differed from the Attorney General's role in bringing them. Id. at 835-836.

The prisoners drew a negative implication from the provision allowing minor violations to be handled by warning. They argued that Congress's express permission to decline referral in minor cases implied a duty to pursue serious cases. The Court rejected that inference. The provision preserved a limited discretion concerning known minor violations; it did not transform the rest of the Act into a comprehensive command to investigate and enforce whenever a private petitioner alleged a major violation. The statutory scheme supplied no workable mandatory standard for the particular refusal. Id. at 835-837.

### The supposed binding policy did not supply the missing standard

The D.C. Circuit relied on language accompanying a proposed 1972 rule concerning unapproved uses by physicians. That proposal was never adopted. The statement arose in a different regulatory setting, did not establish the asserted mandatory duty, and could not be turned into a binding regulation through statements made by government counsel in litigation. The agency's actual regulation treated the decision to take enforcement action as discretionary. Id. at 836-837.

The Court accordingly rejected the status and content of the particular materials presented. It expressly avoided deciding whether an agency rule could ever provide law to apply when the underlying statute was otherwise discretionary. That reservation matters: a litigant relying on an operative binding regulation presents a question the Court did not categorically foreclose. Id. at 836.

### The decision leaves several kinds of inaction for another case

The Commissioner had relied independently on enforcement discretion, so the case did not present a refusal based solely on a legal conclusion that the agency lacked jurisdiction. Nor did the Court find an extreme general policy amounting to abdication of its statutory responsibilities. Footnote 4 reserved those situations. The final paragraph similarly reserved a refusal alleged to violate constitutional rights because no colorable constitutional claim had been presented. These are limits on what was decided, not findings that the FDA had committed any such violation. Id. at 833 n.4, 838.

## Separate opinions

### Brennan: agreement depended on the majority's limited reach

Brennan joined the Court while emphasizing the obligations that remained. He understood the decision as addressing an individual enforcement refusal in the statutory circumstances before the Court. It did not authorize an agency to disregard clear statutory commands, binding regulations, constitutional requirements, or the possibility that a refusal rested solely on a mistaken view of jurisdiction. He stressed the majority's reservations concerning general policies and constitutionally defective reasons. Id. at 838-840.

His discussion of a refusal procured through bribery illustrated the danger of reading the decision as a universal immunity for inaction. Bribery was a hypothetical, not an allegation the Court found substantiated here. Brennan also separated the statutory-review issue from approval of lethal injection or capital punishment. His concurrence explains how a Justice could join the presumption without accepting the proposition that agencies are legally unconstrained whenever they decline to act. Id. at 839-840 & n.2.

### Marshall: ordinary review would sustain the refusal without a special presumption

Marshall agreed with reversal because he considered the FDA's refusal reasonable on this record. The FDCA did not require pursuit of every possible violation; the agency could give weight to its resources, other public-health responsibilities, the relatively limited group affected, and the state criminal-justice setting. Those considerations supported deference to this decision. They did not, in his view, justify insulating such decisions from ordinary APA review at the threshold. Id. at 840-842, 854-855.

He argued that the APA's broad review provisions and inclusion of a failure to act were inconsistent with a new general presumption against reviewing nonenforcement. The historical authorities cited by the majority arose in materially different statutory or prosecutorial settings and did not establish a categorical tradition covering civil regulatory refusals. Courts had also reviewed failures to enforce when statutory purposes, regulations, settled practice, or other legal limits supplied a basis for assessing agency conduct. Id. at 842-848.

Marshall disputed the majority's distinction based on coercion. People protected by a regulatory statute can suffer concrete harm when an agency fails to act against others. The agency's inaction may leave that injury unremedied, even though it does not directly impose a sanction on the person seeking review. Likewise, resource allocation and expertise justify restrained judicial scrutiny, but many reviewable agency decisions also involve those considerations. Their presence does not by itself show that no meaningful review is possible. Id. at 848-852.

His principal institutional concern was that the presumption could permit an agency to defeat the statutory protection it was created to administer. Alternatively, if courts had to examine every potentially unlawful ground or binding constraint before invoking the presumption, its operation might differ little from deferential review of the decision itself. Marshall preferred to acknowledge review and sustain reasonable decisions on their merits. His agreement with the judgment therefore supplies no ninth vote for the majority's reviewability rule. Id. at 852-855.

## Scope and casebook relationship

The assigned excerpt presents the presumption, the institutional arguments supporting it, and the disagreement about protecting statutory beneficiaries. The full report supplies Brennan's separate treatment and the complete FDCA and policy-statement analysis. The casebook's discussion of whether a refusal affecting executions resembles a general policy is an editorial question; it is not a Supreme Court finding that the FDA had adopted an unlawful policy of abdication. Casebook pp. 1048–1060; 470 U.S. at 833 n.4, 835-855. For application, identify the precise refusal, the substantive statute and any binding regulation, the legal constraints they impose, and the ground the agency actually gave. Then distinguish the majority's ordinary presumption from the situations it reserved. A determination that a refusal is reviewable would only open the next inquiry into its legality; a determination that it is unreviewable does not establish that the regulated conduct was lawful. This brief states the 1985 decision and its expressed limits rather than certifying subsequent law. Id. at 828-838.

## Further source distinctions

The prisoners also requested an evidentiary hearing, but did not challenge the district court’s characterization of it as preliminary to the same enforcement steps. The Court therefore expressly excluded refusal to initiate rulemaking from this case. Id. at 825 n.2.

The majority’s animal-euthanasia footnote explains the Commissioner’s distinction: those products were new drugs intended by their manufacturers for that purpose. That was the agency’s jurisdictional rationale, not a Supreme Court holding finally resolving FDA jurisdiction over execution drugs. Id. at 827 n.3.

Marshall suggested that §555(e) could require the agency’s explanation of its denial and criticized reading the majority’s reservations without first examining reasons. His final footnote allows that a plaintiff challenging an asserted resource-priority rationale might need a basis for questioning it or explaining why such evidence could not yet be supplied at summary judgment. Neither point creates an additional majority holding. Id. at 841 & n.1, 852–854 & nn.9–12.

Brennan’s death-penalty objection, continuing from 839 to 840 n.2, likewise remains his separate position.
