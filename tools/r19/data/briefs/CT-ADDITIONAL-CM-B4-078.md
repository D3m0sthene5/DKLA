# Stewart-Smith Haidinger, Inc. v. Avi-Truck, Inc.
682 P.2d 1108 (Alaska 1984) | Contracts

## Decision and judgment
On March 30, 1984, the Alaska Supreme Court affirmed Avi-Truck's $60,000 aircraft-hull insurance recovery. Although Avi-Truck was neither named in the insurance nor in existence when negotiations began, the court implied beneficiary rights at law because it belonged to the contemplated class of aircraft owners and its ownership did not alter the insured risk. The court also upheld coverage against airworthiness-certificate and pilot-qualification exclusions and rejected a hearsay objection to testimony bearing on reasonable expectations. Justice Compton wrote for the court of Chief Justice Burke and Justices Rabinowitz, Matthews, Compton, and Moore; no separate writings appear. (1108–1114, 1117–1119.)

## Aircraft purchase, insurance, and loss
Trans-Northern Aleutian, or TNA, operated smaller aircraft and became interested in a surplus military Chase YC-122 for moving large equipment. Two TNA employees ferried the unusual, large twin-engine aircraft to Anchorage in December 1976. TNA considered several acquisition arrangements but ultimately preferred leasing. Two of its mechanics, Robert Murnan and Bob Coward, and their wives formed Avi-Truck to buy the plane. Avi-Truck was incorporated December 20, purchased it December 29, and leased it to TNA January 14, 1977. The lease required $60,000 hull insurance, $1 million liability insurance, declaration copies to the lessor, and advance cancellation notice. (1110, 1114 & nn. 5–6.)

TNA's president, Lloyd Sudder, sought coverage through broker Jack Good, who contacted Stewart-Smith's representative John Chapman. Good knew the aircraft's make, model, unusual size and age, and cargo-only use. The parties disputed whether Sudder also disclosed its public-use status and TNA's proposed leasing rather than ownership. No one discussed an airworthiness certificate; Good assumed one existed. In practice the surplus plane could not obtain a certificate for carrying private cargo for hire but could carry government cargo as a public-use aircraft. (1110–1111, 1115–1117 & nn. 7–12.)

Good issued a binder December 13 for the ferry flight and ensuing ground coverage; flight coverage was added January 24. It contemplated freight carriage for hire and specified pilot qualifications. Despite the reference to a forthcoming new policy, the aircraft was eventually added by endorsement to TNA's existing fleet policy. That endorsement was issued February 8, after the January 31 crash. The plane crashed on its second flight after takeoff from Anchorage while carrying Bureau of Indian Affairs school supplies. The insurers disclaimed coverage under exclusions in the later policy. (1109–1111.)

## Procedure and questions preserved
Alaska Statebank first sued Avi-Truck to recover the purchase loan. Avi-Truck brought a third-party claim against the broker and insurers for the insurance proceeds. On June 5, 1980, Judge Singleton's partial summary judgment recognized Avi-Truck's right to sue and rejected the airworthiness exclusion. After a five-day trial beginning July 20, 1981, Judge Souter found coverage despite the pilots' alleged licensing deficiencies and entered a final judgment as to fewer than all claims and parties for the $60,000 hull limit. The insurers appealed those coverage and beneficiary rulings and admission of testimony about FAA statements. (1111.)

Note 1 distinguishes alternative assignment theories. The trial court rejected an asserted TNA assignment and did not reach the bank's assignment; neither was raised on appeal. The Supreme Court therefore did not decide assignment rights. This decision affirmed the appealed insurance recovery without resolving every other claim in the underlying multiparty litigation. (1111–1112 n. 1.)

## Beneficiary rights implied at law
The insurers argued that conventional beneficiary status required both contracting parties' intent at formation. Avi-Truck was unnamed, unknown to them until after the crash, and not yet formed when negotiations began. The court agreed that the parties could not specifically have intended this then-nonexistent corporation as the beneficiary; it did not manufacture an express or factually intended beneficiary contract. Instead, the special insurance circumstances supported implication at law. (1112.)

Prior Alaska cases concerning implied co-insureds made the assumed risk central. Decisions elsewhere had allowed unnamed property owners to recover directly where coverage was obtained for them and the insurer intended to cover ownership interests. Alaska adopted a limited principle: an insurance beneficiary contract could be implied at law when the third party belonged to the contemplated beneficiary class and recognizing it did not change the insurer's risk. Note 3 preferred this label to reformation, which ordinarily corrects a mutual mistake to reflect clear intentions. Note 4 declined contrary precedent grounded in added-risk concerns because those concerns were absent here. (1112–1113 & nn. 2–4.)

Ownership made no practical risk difference. The same mechanics would maintain the YC-122, and TNA would operate it, so Avi-Truck's lack of its own carrier certificate was irrelevant. TNA's early ownership plans had not been fixed; it sought coverage for itself or whoever ultimately purchased the plane. By January 24, it was contractually obliged to insure for Avi-Truck. The insurers intended to protect the owner's interest under the same contemplated operations. Undisputed evidence thus placed Avi-Truck in the intended class without increasing risk, supporting its direct insurance action. Lack of additional risk alone was not the entire test. (1113–1114 & nn. 5–6.)

## Airworthiness exclusion and the pre-loss binder
The later policy excluded flight without an effective airworthiness certificate. The trial court first treated that language as ambiguous, potentially requiring only preservation of whatever certification status existed when insured. The Supreme Court rejected that reasoning: the clause unambiguously required an existing, effective certificate. Favoring coverage did not permit an unreasonable interpretation. (1114–1115.)

Coverage nevertheless followed from the earlier bargain. The insurers had agreed to cover this plane for cargo carriage, and competent inquiry would have disclosed its practical certification limits. There was no evidence of intentional misrepresentation, either at partial summary judgment or at trial; merely failing to volunteer information the insurers never requested was insufficient. Its unusual characteristics should have prompted investigation. Note 12 describes Chapman's inquiry and decision to send the risk to London, without relaying his information because he expected further inquiry there. (1115–1117 & nn. 8–12.)

Crucially, the restrictive endorsement did not exist at the loss. The binder was then the written coverage document. Although a binder ordinarily includes usual policy terms, TNA's ordinary fleet policy was not a usual policy for a plane practically unable to obtain the certificate for the contemplated cargo use. The binder reasonably promised coverage for lawful government cargo carriage without such a certificate; a later exclusion destroying that coverage impermissibly changed the bargain. The court expressly did not decide whether failure to investigate alone would justify striking the exclusion had the policy been issued before the crash. It affirmed on the binder's timing and reasonable meaning. (1117 & n. 13.)

## Pilot qualifications and statements from the FAA
The insurers also invoked a pilot-certificate exclusion because the pilot lacked a large-aircraft type rating and the copilot lacked required takeoffs and landings. Avi-Truck disputed whether those federal requirements applied to public aircraft. The trial court concluded they did but found TNA reasonably expected coverage nonetheless. The Supreme Court affirmed on reasonable expectations and expressly left the federal applicability dispute undecided in note 15. (1117–1118 & nn. 14–15.)

Sudder's experience operating and insuring aircraft did not make him an insurance expert; he relied on agents and had a lay understanding of policy coverage. TNA employees had received FAA assurances, including from legal counsel, that type ratings were unnecessary or not issued for public aircraft. The binder specified flight hours and approval requirements without mentioning type ratings, and the insurers discussed particular pilots' credentials and approved them. Those circumstances together supported the objective reasonableness of expecting coverage for the contemplated pilots. The court did not hold that FAA assurances were legally correct. (1118–1119.)

Testimony about those assurances was relevant to the employees' belief and conduct, not offered to prove the underlying federal rule. It therefore fell outside the hearsay definition: the pertinent fact was that statements had been made and reasonably relied upon. The court affirmed admission for that limited purpose and affirmed the insurance judgment. (1119.)

## Assigned treatment and reading scope
The complete assigned note at printed 1185 accurately identifies implication at law for an unnamed, initially nonexistent beneficiary. The actual opinion supplies an additional necessary element—membership in the contemplated class—and the separate binder and reasonable-expectation holdings that made recovery possible despite exclusions. Its occasional reference to a truck is a textbook slip; the insured property was the YC-122 aircraft.
