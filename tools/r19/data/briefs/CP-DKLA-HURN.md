# Hurn v. Oursler
289 U.S. 238 (1933) | Civil Procedure

*Written for the atlas on 7 October 2026 from the full opinion (Legal Information Institute text of 289 U.S. 238). It is not in the casebook's assigned pages and has not had an independent review.*

## Why it is here

Hurn is the pre-Gibbs test for what the Court then called pendent jurisdiction: when a federal court that has a federal claim may also decide a state-law claim between non-diverse parties. *United Mine Workers v. Gibbs* (1966) called Hurn's approach "unnecessarily grudging" and replaced it with the common-nucleus-of-operative-fact test, and 28 U.S.C. § 1367 now governs. Reading Hurn shows what Gibbs changed.

## Facts

Hurn and a co-author wrote a play, *The Evil Hour*, and copyrighted it. They later revised it; the revision was not copyrighted. Both versions were submitted to some of the defendants, who discussed producing it. The play's feature was a spiritualistic seance staged with the audience taking part. The defendants owned their own copyrighted play, *The Spider*, which as first produced had no seance. Instead of producing the plaintiffs' play, the defendants allegedly altered *The Spider* to add a seance on stage, together with certain "business and effects" and portions of *The Evil Hour*. Opinion ¶ 1.

The plaintiffs sued in federal court to enjoin performance of *The Spider*, with damages and an accounting. The bill alleged (1) infringement of the federal copyright in *The Evil Hour* and (2) that the same conduct was "unfair business practices and unfair competition", a non-federal claim. By amendment during the suit the unfair-competition allegations were extended to (3) the uncopyrighted revised version. All parties were citizens of the same state, so jurisdiction rested on the copyright claim alone. ¶¶ 1, 22.

## Proceedings below

The trial court decided the infringement claim on the merits and found that no version of *The Spider* infringed *The Evil Hour* in plot, material, arrangement, sequence of events, incidents or otherwise. Having found no infringement, it held that it had no jurisdiction over the allegations resting on anything other than the copyright law, and dismissed the bill. The Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed. 61 F.2d 1031. The Supreme Court read the trial court as having rejected both unfair-competition claims for lack of jurisdiction, not on the merits. ¶¶ 2–3, 21.

## Question

Once a federal court has properly taken jurisdiction of a substantial federal claim and decided it against the plaintiff on the merits, may it also decide a non-federal claim joined with it, or must that claim be dismissed for want of jurisdiction?

## Holding

Modified and, as modified, affirmed (Sutherland, J.).

1. The unfair-competition claim about the **copyrighted** play should have been dismissed on the merits, not for want of jurisdiction. The federal question was "not plainly unsubstantial", so jurisdiction attached, and it extended to the non-federal ground. The decree was modified to say so; no further proceedings were needed because the finding of no infringement also negated unfair competition on the same facts. ¶¶ 5, 21.
2. The unfair-competition claim about the **uncopyrighted** version was rightly dismissed for want of jurisdiction. It "did not rest upon any federal ground and was wholly independent of the claim of copyright infringement." ¶ 22.

Justices Brandeis and Stone thought the decree should be affirmed without modification. ¶ 24.

## Reasoning

**The general rule.** Starting from *Osborn v. Bank of the United States* and *Siler v. Louisville & Nashville Railroad*, the Court restated that a federal court which acquires jurisdiction through a federal question may "decide all the questions in the case", even if it decides the federal question against the party raising it or does not reach it. *Moore v. New York Cotton Exchange* applied the same idea to a non-federal counterclaim arising from the same transaction after the federal claim failed on the merits. ¶¶ 8–13.

**No exception for patent, trademark and copyright cases.** Two trademark decisions, *Leschen & Sons Rope* and *Elgin National Watch*, could be read to make jurisdiction over unfair competition depend on the validity of the registered mark. The Court declined to harmonise them and held that any such exception is "altogether unsound": the Siler rule is "as controlling in patent, trade-mark, and copyright cases" as anywhere else. ¶¶ 14–17.

**The limit: one cause of action versus two.** The rule "does not go so far as to permit a federal court to assume jurisdiction of a separate and distinct nonfederal cause of action because it is joined in the same complaint with a federal cause of action." The line is between

- "two distinct grounds in support of a single cause of action", only one of which is federal, where the court may dispose of the case on the non-federal ground even though the federal ground fails, provided the federal question is not plainly wanting in substance; and
- "two separate and distinct causes of action", only one of which is federal, where it may not decide the non-federal one. ¶ 18.

**Applying it.** For the copyrighted play the bill alleged the violation of a single right, the right to protection of the copyrighted play. Infringement and unfair competition rested on identical facts and were "little more than the equivalent of different epithets to characterize the same group of circumstances." Quoting *Baltimore Steamship Co. v. Phillips*, a cause of action "does not consist of facts, but of the unlawful violation of a right which the facts show." So these were two grounds for one cause of action. ¶ 19. The Court added that it was not laying down a hard and fast definition of "cause of action" for all purposes; the term may mean different things for different purposes, but for marking the boundary between state and federal jurisdiction it should be kept within these limits. ¶ 20.

The uncopyrighted version was different: the amended bill asserted "two distinct rights", protection of the copyrighted play and protection of the uncopyrighted play, giving "two separate and distinct causes of action", one arising under federal law and the other under general law. A federal court has no power to entertain a cause of action outside its jurisdiction "merely because that cause of action has mistakenly been joined in the complaint with another which is within its jurisdiction." ¶ 22.

## What to take from it

- Test: pendent jurisdiction exists when the state and federal theories are two grounds for a single cause of action (a single right violated), and the federal claim is substantial. It does not exist for a separate state cause of action.
- The federal claim losing on the merits does not strip jurisdiction over the pendent ground; only a plainly insubstantial federal claim does.
- The test's weakness is its dependence on the slippery phrase "cause of action", which the opinion itself concedes (¶ 20). That is the point *Gibbs* seized on.

## Later history (not from the opinion)

*United Mine Workers v. Gibbs*, 383 U.S. 715 (1966), replaced the single-cause-of-action test with the common nucleus of operative fact and made the exercise of pendent jurisdiction discretionary. Congress codified supplemental jurisdiction in 28 U.S.C. § 1367 (1990). For unfair-competition claims joined with a substantial and related copyright, patent or trademark claim, see 28 U.S.C. § 1338(b). These three statements are from general knowledge of the later law, not from the Hurn opinion.
