# Association of Data Processing Service Organizations, Inc. v. Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System

**Course:** Legislation, Regulation & the State  
**Citation:** 745 F.2d 677 (D.C. Cir. 1984)  
**Court and date:** United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, October 2, 1984  
**Opinion:** Judge Scalia for the unanimous panel of Judges Ginsburg and Scalia and Senior District Judge Van Pelt, sitting by designation; no separate writing appears.  
**Assigned source:** LRS November pages 1–165 scan, PDF pages 142–146, printed pages 922–926, ending before Notes.  
**Brief ID:** LRS-MAX-ADAPSO

## Case orientation

The Board reasonably approved Citicorp's data-processing activities and broadened a related regulation governing bank holding companies. The familiar assigned passage explains that, when reviewing factual support, the ordinary APA substantial-evidence and arbitrary-and-capricious standards require the same quantum of support. Formal proceedings still have distinctive closed-record requirements. The court denied both petitions after examining the actual banking relationship, technological change, hardware restrictions, and procedural explanations; it did not decide the case by announcing a review standard alone.

## Facts and statutory setting

Bank holding companies generally needed prior approval before undertaking nonbanking activities. Under 12 U.S.C. § 1843(c)(8), the Board could permit activities sufficiently closely related to banking. A separate inquiry considered whether public benefits—such as convenience, competition, and efficiency—outweighed adverse consequences, including concentration, unfair competition, conflicts, and unsound practices. Those inquiries were distinct. (Opinion introduction and note 8.)

Citicorp sought authority in 1979 to provide data processing and transmission through its proposed subsidiary Citishare. The application included timesharing, electronic funds transfer, home banking, other techniques, excess computing capacity, and hardware. After protests from ADAPSO, the Board set a formal hearing. Citicorp then requested related amendments of Regulation Y. The ALJ considered both matters, with written rulemaking submissions from more than sixty companies and individuals. He recommended approval and regulatory amendments in March 1982.

The Board approved Citicorp's application on July 9, 1982 with restrictions, then adopted regulatory amendments on August 23 through notice-and-comment rulemaking. ADAPSO and member companies Comshare and Tymshare petitioned for direct review under § 1848. The court consolidated the challenges to the formal adjudicatory order and informal rulemaking order. Petitioners challenged the closely-related determination and procedural adequacy, but not the public-benefits determination in the Citicorp order. (Introduction; discussion of the data test.)

## Questions and holdings

Section 1848 made factual findings conclusive if supported by substantial evidence. The court held that this provision applied to both orders, and that its ordinary meaning demanded the same quantum of factual support as APA review for arbitrariness. It further upheld the Board's data-based method of determining which processing activities were closely related to banking, its inclusion of economic data and timesharing, and its appropriately limited authorization of hardware. The court rejected the alleged failure to address material aspects of the application. Both review petitions were denied.

## Factual support and the two review standards

Citicorp urged substantial-evidence review of the adjudication but arbitrary-and-capricious review of the rule. The court rejected treating the last sentence of § 1848 as limited to adjudication when the review provision generally covered rulemaking too. The statute and its history provided no basis for that selective limitation. Earlier judicial references to deference were reconcilable with substantial-evidence review because the normal APA tests did not demand different quantities of factual support. (Opinion, standard-of-review discussion.)

The § 706 grounds were cumulative. Supported findings could still be arbitrary for another reason, such as an abrupt unexplained departure from precedent. Section 706(2)(A) supplied a catchall when the more specific substantial-evidence provision did not apply. In its function of testing factual support, it could not allow an agency to act on an insubstantial factual basis that would fail substantial-evidence review.

This did not erase § 706(2)(E). Its special role was requiring supporting evidence within the closed record of formal proceedings. Informal action likewise had to be reviewed on the materials before the agency when it acted, rather than a new record created in court. That administrative record could differ from the closed hearing record and could contain material not known to private participants. In informal rulemaking, the duty to expose critical supporting facts for comment derived from § 553(c)'s participation requirement; it did not turn every informal agency action into a formal record proceeding. Nor did it require publication of every item of data. (Assigned printed pages 923–925.)

The opinion acknowledged the possibility that another substantive statute could use substantial-evidence language to demand something different. It declined to impute such a change casually and did not resolve every possible statutory variant. For § 1848, it found no alteration of normal APA factual-support requirements. The broader exposition of APA equivalence should be distinguished from the specific holding applying this statute to both orders. (Assigned printed pages 925–926.)

## The data test and rulemaking's permissible generality

The Board relied on the *National Courier* criteria: banks actually provided the proposed services; provided services sufficiently similar to equip them particularly well for the proposal; or provided services integrally related enough to require the proposed specialized form. These were disjunctive and nonexclusive guides, rather than statutory language to parse as an exhaustive code. (Opinion, merits introduction.)

The government denied employing a data test, but the court found that both orders did so: permissible processing depended on whether the data were financial, banking, or economic, rather than the particular technological method. Examples of budgeting, bookkeeping, and financial analysis demonstrated the approach; they were not an exhaustive list of individually permitted uses.

The court also rejected an attempted justification that subsequent case-by-case public-benefits review could correct an invalid general closely-related determination. Once Regulation Y conferred the latter qualification, the Board could not simply deny it during a different inquiry without amending the rule. The data test therefore had to stand on its own statutory and rational basis. (Opinion, data-test discussion; notes 7–9.)

It did. Banks already developed and processed large amounts of the relevant data. The Board reasonably concluded that they were operationally equipped to provide these services. Rulemaking traded a small risk of imperfect coverage for efficiency and predictability, particularly in a rapidly changing field where detailed lists would soon become obsolete and discourage innovation. The record supplied concrete examples of the principle. Generality was supported here; the court did not approve rules untethered to the statutory banking relationship.

Note 10 carefully addresses record support. The underlying historical survey cited in briefing was not found in the record. The court did not rely on that missing survey itself. A Legal Division memorandum included among applicant exhibits summarized relevant banking practices—payroll, account reconciliation, sales analysis, and inventory analysis. In the absence of contrary evidence, that record material sufficiently supported the bookkeeping and accounting conclusion. The note illustrates the record distinction emphasized earlier.

## Economic data and timesharing

The Board rejected the proposed category of economic-related data as too broad, but authorized economic data. Evidence showed banks had long developed economic information and projections for customers' investment decisions. More sophisticated delivery did not make related activity impermissible merely because older banks used simpler methods. In context, the category adequately included subjects such as agriculture, retail sales, housing, and corporate profits relevant to banking and financial decisions. (Opinion, economic-data discussion.)

Timesharing let multiple customers interact with computing facilities. Petitioners feared its use for nonfinancial tasks such as race handicapping or employee evaluation. The Act restricted the services banks offered; the mere possibility of an unknown customer misuse did not invalidate a legitimate service. Petitioners supplied no actual instance. The orders also required written agreements limiting services to qualifying data, and required facilities designed, marketed, and operated for those purposes, with necessary technical precautions. These protections supported the judgment rather than authorizing unrestricted general computing businesses. (Timesharing discussion.)

## Hardware and incidental activities

Specialized hardware, exemplified by ATMs, could be part of the authorized processing service. The record supported treating software and hardware as a technologically and commercially integrated offering. The court did not confine closely-related activities to traditional check collection, lending, or deposit gathering or exclude equipment merely because earlier tests used the word services.

General-purpose hardware was not itself closely related to banking, but could be incidental to a permitted processing package where economic necessity linked them. The court distinguished mere convenience from a justified need to provide the package. It also recognized a limit: a large hardware enterprise could not qualify simply by attaching a smaller banking-related activity. The orders' approximately 30% cost limitation, grounded in industry hardware costs of roughly 25%, addressed that danger. (Hardware discussion.)

Note 12 identifies cost as cost to the bank holding company and records qualifications in the Citicorp order: individual cases could exceed the percentage so long as hardware sales did not consistently do so. The amended regulation's text specifically referred to general-purpose hardware, while the opinion's explanation and orders discussed hardware more broadly. This brief does not treat the percentage as a universally identical strict per-transaction cap across every text.

## Material issues and judgment

Petitioners invoked § 557(c), arguing that the Citicorp order omitted separate explanations for applications software, facilities management, turnkey systems, and software-related services. The court found the first three adequately resolved within the approved processing activities. The agency need not accept a challenger's preferred segmentation when its reasonable analysis disposed of those components and stated the reasons. An independent consulting business was neither separately requested nor authorized; incidental advice associated with processing was covered by existing incidental-activity authority. (Procedural objection discussion.)

The unanimous court denied both petitions. Its deference rested on a supported, reasoned, and procedurally regular response to technological change under the statute's broad closely-related standard. No separate opinion appears.

## Exam use and source scope

Distinguish the quantity of factual support from the record in which that support must appear. Substantial support does not cure an unexplained departure or another procedural defect. On regulatory generality, examine whether categorical coverage has a supported relation to the statute and whether a separate later inquiry genuinely can revisit the disputed qualification. Do not confuse the 1984 Board-review case with the 1970 *ADAPSO v. Camp* standing case.
