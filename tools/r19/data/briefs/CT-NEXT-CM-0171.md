# Canadian Industrial Alcohol Co. v. Dunbar Molasses Co.
258 N.Y. 194, 179 N.E. 383 (N.Y. Jan. 5, 1932) | Contracts

## Bargain and nonperformance
On December 27, 1927, Canadian Industrial Alcohol ordered approximately 1.5 million wine gallons of refined blackstrap molasses, around 60% sugars, of the usual run from the National Sugar Refinery at Yonkers. Dunbar accepted. Deliveries were to begin after April 1, 1928 and continue through the warm weather. Dunbar delivered 344,083 gallons, its entire allotment from a refinery that produced 485,848 gallons during the contract period, well below capacity. It delivered no more. The buyer sued for damages and obtained judgment; Dunbar appealed. (258 N.Y. 197–198.)

Dunbar argued that the named source implicitly limited its duty to the output the refinery chose to supply, so diminished production discharged the remaining obligation. It had not shown that it contracted with the refinery to secure sufficient output, attempted to do so between acceptance and shipment, or told the buyer that its own source arrangements were unsecured. The buyer maintained that the middleman’s unrestricted promise could not be defeated by a third party’s reduced production.

## Issue and governing approach
The question was whether naming a source conditioned the seller’s delivery obligation on that source’s willingness to produce enough molasses. Chief Judge Cardozo’s approach asks whether the contract, interpreted in its setting, makes continuation of a particular group of circumstances an implied presupposition conditioning the obligation. The court held that this contract did not silently assign to the buyer the risk of the refinery voluntarily reducing production. Dunbar remained liable. (Id. 198–200.)

The court assumed, favorably to Dunbar and without deciding those hypothetical cases, that destruction of the refinery, crop failure, war, or perhaps unavoidable strikes could discharge performance. It also assumed that a contract expressly tying supply to a particular seller-refiner contract might present a different case if that underlying contract was broken without the seller’s fault. Those reservations concern destruction or frustration of a genuine assumed basis, not an automatic rule that every failure at an identified supplier excuses a middleman. (Id. 198–199.)

## Why the seller retained this risk
A buyer dealing with a middleman would not reasonably understand a fixed quantity commitment to lapse whenever the producer decided to diminish output. Such a presumption would undermine commercial security. Dunbar could apparently have secured a sufficient supply by contracting with the refinery, but did not show it had done so or even tried. Nor did it disclose this omission or condition its obligation on later securing an agreement. A buyer informed of that uncertainty might have dealt directly with the producer instead. (Id. 199–200.)

Dunbar thus relied on the chance that output would repeat earlier years, then sought to impose an equivalent gamble on its customer. The court refused to insert that “aleatory” element. Its reference to “contributory fault” concerns the seller’s failure to protect its supply position, not a tort-negligence finding about refinery operations. The contrast at the close of this analysis is between a mill destroyed before delivery and a mill producing less because of adverse business conditions or labor expense. The particular source matters, but the reason for failure and the seller’s undertaken responsibility matter too.

## Additional damages and evidence rulings
The full opinion contains issues omitted from the short assignment. Dunbar challenged exclusion of correspondence exchanged after deliveries began; the court held the letters would not have changed the result. It also argued that the buyer should have mitigated by accepting alternative offers below the contemporaneous market. (Id. 200–201.)

On June 27, while Dunbar still held out hope of delivering under the original agreement, it offered 400,000 gallons from another refinery at 6.5 cents per gallon, about a cent below June market but above the original 4.75-cent contract price. The offer expired unless accepted by July 2 and was made as an accommodation rather than performance of an obligation. The buyer kept its original contract open until October 25, then notified Dunbar it would buy in the market and charge the difference. On October 26, Dunbar offered a new contract for 400,000 to 850,000 gallons at its option, at 7.25 cents, about half a cent below market, with immediate acceptance required. (Id. 200–201.)

The buyer was not required to accept the earlier higher-priced substitute while the original contract remained open. Nor was it required to enter another executory agreement with a seller whose readiness or ability it no longer trusted. The second proposal tendered another promise rather than presently delivered molasses. The court rejected the claimed duty to repeat that experiment and found no error in the other challenged rulings. These fact-specific rulings should not be generalized into permission to disregard all reasonable mitigation alternatives.

## Judgment, vote, and source scope
Cardozo wrote; Pound, Crane, Lehman, Kellogg, O’Brien, and Hubbs concurred. The seven listed judges unanimously affirmed the buyer’s judgment with costs.
