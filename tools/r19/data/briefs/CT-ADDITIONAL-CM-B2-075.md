# Delta Dynamics, Inc. v. Arioto
69 Cal. 2d 525, 446 P.2d 785, 72 Cal. Rptr. 785 (1968) | Contracts

## Court, result, and division

On November 13, 1968, the California Supreme Court reversed a judgment for a manufacturer after a nonjury trial because the trial court had excluded evidence offered to interpret the distributor's termination clause. Chief Justice Traynor wrote for himself and Justices Peters, Tobriner, and Sullivan. Justice Mosk dissented, joined by Justices McComb and Burke: a 4–3 decision. The court ordered reversal rather than entering judgment that termination was necessarily the manufacturer's exclusive remedy. The original report also records denial of rehearing on December 11, with the same three dissenters favoring rehearing. (69 Cal. 2d at 525, 529–530, 532.)

## Contract, performance, and litigation

Delta developed safety trigger locks for firearms. On March 23, 1961, it contracted with the partners operating Pixey Distributing to distribute the locks exclusively throughout the United States. The term was five years from first delivery, with a five-year renewal option. Delta would supply the locks, and Pixey would pay specified prices, promote them diligently, and sell at least 50,000 units in the first year and 100,000 in each succeeding year. If Pixey missed a yearly minimum, the agreement was subject to termination by Delta on thirty days' notice. A separate clause awarded reasonable attorney fees to the prevailing party in an action for damages or enforcement after a breach by either side. (Id. at 526–527.)

Pixey paid for 10,000 locks delivered in August 1961. Its October order for another 10,000 called for delivery as needed, but it never requested their delivery or ordered the remaining 30,000 necessary to reach the first-year quota. Delta terminated on October 1, 1962, and sued for failure to purchase the quota. After the nonjury trial, Delta obtained judgment. The trial court treated the quota as an obligation to purchase, rejected termination as the exclusive remedy, and excluded negotiation evidence offered by Pixey. (Id. at 527.)

## Issues and governing interpretation rule

The court first rejected Pixey's argument that it had promised only to sell locks to outsiders, without promising to buy them. Delta was its sole source of supply; a commitment to sell the stated number therefore necessarily implied a commitment to buy that number from Delta. Pixey's stronger argument concerned the remedy for breach of that promise. Could it introduce evidence that the termination clause was intended to make termination the exclusive remedy for failure to meet the quota? (Id.)

Under Pacific Gas, the admissibility inquiry asks whether evidence is relevant to an interpretation that the written language can reasonably bear. A judge does not decide admissibility simply by finding the instrument facially clear. The court first considers credible evidence of the parties' intended meaning; evidence supporting either interpretation is admissible if the language in its circumstances is fairly susceptible to both. (Id. at 528.)

## Majority reasoning and the evidentiary record

The termination clause could identify when Delta was excused from further performance, specify the sole remedy for a missed quota, or perform both functions. Pixey's exclusive-remedy interpretation consequently was reasonable enough to warrant consideration of extrinsic evidence. The attorney-fee clause did not defeat it: fees and damages could still apply to different breaches, such as failure diligently to promote the locks or failure to pay for delivered goods. Excluding the offered interpretive evidence was prejudicial error. This did not decide that the offered account of the parties' agreement was true. (Id. at 528–529 & n. 2.)

Footnote 1 explains preservation. The pretrial order expressly identified exclusivity of termination as an issue. At trial, defense counsel asked a client about negotiations over the consequence of failing to meet the quota. Delta objected, and counsel responded that the contract was ambiguous and that the questioning sought the parties' intentions. Although that offer standing alone was too general, the pretrial order and trial context made the expected answer, purpose, and relevance clear: the parties intended termination to be exclusive. The majority held that no more complete offer was necessary in that setting. It did not eliminate the ordinary requirement to disclose the substance and relevance of excluded evidence. (Id. at 527–528 n. 1.)

The majority also rejected Pixey's requested default construction on retrial. In Newby, the quota was a condition for retaining a distributorship, without an undertaking to purchase or sell the quota. Here Pixey had made a promise, whose breach ordinarily supported damages. Accordingly, absent extrinsic evidence showing the different intended meaning, this clause should be read as stating a termination condition rather than displacing damages. The court thus allowed proof of exclusivity while refusing to declare exclusivity the inevitable meaning of the writing alone. (Id. at 529–530.)

## Mosk's dissent

Mosk would have affirmed. He viewed the single question as an improper attempt to introduce negotiations superseded by the writing under Civil Code § 1625. Counsel did not make a proper offer or explain an ambiguity after the objection was sustained. A pretrial description of an issue was tentative, not a substitute for trial disclosure permitting the judge to reconsider and an appellate court to assess prejudice. He also relied on the intermediate appellate court's view that the question did not lay a foundation for latent ambiguity or special usage, but appeared to change or add terms. (Id. at 530–531.)

His broader concern was predictability. Although he had joined Masterson and Pacific Gas with misgivings, he believed this extension threatened the reliability of written bargains drafted by lawyers for experienced parties. Permitting self-interested recollections of negotiations to undermine written terms made stable commercial drafting difficult. These criticisms were dissenting reasons, not limitations adopted by the majority. (Id. at 531–532.)

## Assigned significance and reading scope

The casebook's Trend Lines account describes the reversal and then emphasizes Mosk's change of position. The complete decision adds two important qualifications: the quota remained a promise rather than merely a condition, and Pixey still needed persuasive extrinsic evidence to establish an exclusive remedy. Reversal for excluding that evidence was not a final victory on the interpretation or damages merits. (C&M printed 552–553, PDF 245–246.)
