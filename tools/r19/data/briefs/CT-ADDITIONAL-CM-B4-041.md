# Lenawee County Board of Health v. Messerly
417 Mich. 17, 331 N.W.2d 203 (1982) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and disposition
The Michigan Supreme Court decided the case on December 23, 1982, after argument on November 9, 1981. Justice Ryan wrote for six participating justices: Chief Justice Fitzgerald and Justices Kavanagh, Williams, Levin, and Coleman joined him. Justice Riley took no part. The court reversed the Court of Appeals and denied the purchasers equitable rescission, even though it found a mutual mistake affecting a basic assumption and materially altering the exchange. No separate opinion was filed at this stage. (417 Mich. at 17, 33.)

## Property history, transaction, and latent defect
The Messerlys acquired approximately one acre plus 600 square feet in 1971. A three-unit apartment building stood on the small portion. Before that purchase, a predecessor, Bloom, had installed a septic tank without a permit and contrary to the health code. The Messerlys used the building as a rental investment, then sold the property on land contract to Barnes in 1973. With the Messerlys’ permission, the Barneses sold the approximately one-acre portion in 1976. The remaining 600-square-foot parcel and building were offered for sale after they defaulted. (Id. at 20 & n. 1.)

The Pickleses wanted a new contract directly with the Messerlys instead of taking over the Barnes contract. The Barneses therefore quitclaimed their interest back. After inspection, the Pickleses signed a $25,500 land contract on March 21, 1977. An added clause stated that the purchaser had examined the property, accepted its present condition, and had no additional written or oral understandings. Five or six days later, while visiting the tenants, the buyers discovered raw sewage escaping from the ground. (Id. at 20–21.)

The problem was more than a repairable malfunction discovered after closing. An adequate system required at least 2,500 square feet for a three-family dwelling, even with ideal soil conditions, or 750 square feet for a single-family home. The 600-square-foot parcel could not accommodate either. The earlier division had made it impossible to repair the already unlawful septic system within the remaining property. Pumping and hauling sewage would cost twice the rental income and was economically infeasible. Although the trial judge mentioned the possible use of adjoining land, no testimony established a solution on that basis. The property was found valueless or of negative value for the contemplated residential use. (Id. at 23 n. 7, 25–26 & n. 10.)

## Litigation and issues preserved
The Board of Health obtained an injunction prohibiting human habitation until the sanitation problem was corrected, then withdrew by stipulation. When no payments were made on the land contract, the Messerlys sought foreclosure, sale, and a deficiency. The Pickleses sought rescission for failure of consideration and alleged concealment and misrepresentation by the Barneses, seeking to charge the Messerlys in equity with that conduct as well. Other real-estate defendants were dismissed by stipulation. (Id. at 21–22 & n. 2.)

After a bench trial, the circuit court found no fraud or misrepresentation: none of these parties knew about Bloom’s unlawful installation or caused the sanitation problem. The buyers had inspected and purchased in the existing condition. It ordered foreclosure and entered a $25,943.09 judgment, an amount the parties had stipulated was due if the contract was enforceable. The Court of Appeals unanimously upheld the disposition concerning the Barneses but divided two to one in granting relief against the Messerlys. It considered the mistake basic because the parties had intended income-producing rental property, not a valueless asset. (Id. at 22–23 & nn. 3–7.)

Mutual mistake had not been expressly pleaded. The trial judge had treated the failure-of-consideration contention as grounded in mistake, and the Court of Appeals had addressed it; the Supreme Court considered it necessary because it was dispositive below. The buyers did not appeal the no-fraud ruling, and the disposition concerning the Barneses was not before the Supreme Court. Thus, the case did not decide that an “as is” term defeats a proved fraud claim. (Id. at 23 n. 5–6, 24 n. 8, 32–33 n. 16.)

## A mistake about an existing fact
A contractual mistake concerns a belief inconsistent with facts existing when the contract is made, rather than a prediction about a future event. The sellers argued that the sanitation defect arose after execution; the buyers distinguished discovery from inception. Although the trial court had not directly resolved that distinction, the Supreme Court drew an inference from its findings and the undisputed evidence: the unlawful installation and inadequate available land preceded the sale. The parties’ common belief in the building’s capacity to produce residential rental income was therefore mistaken at formation. Discovering it later did not turn it into a future-event problem. (Id. at 24–26 & nn. 9–10.)

The court emphasized two inquiries: establishing a mistake did not automatically establish a right to relief. The sellers relied on A & M Land Development’s treatment of a septic-permit problem as affecting merely value; the buyers relied on Sherwood’s distinction between the substance and quality of a breeding cow. Such labels did not satisfactorily explain an error affecting both value and the very purpose of a transaction. The court limited those decisions’ material-versus-collateral classifications to their facts and adopted a case-specific inquiry into basic assumptions and material effects on agreed performance. (Id. at 26–30.)

## Material mistake followed by allocation of risk
Using Restatement (Second) of Contracts sections 152 and 154, the court found both basic assumption and material effect. Each side assumed that the transferred property could house people and generate rent; its inability to do so changed the character of the exchange. That made enforcement disadvantageous to the buyers and advantageous to the sellers, and only rescission could remedy the mistake. But rescission remained discretionary equitable relief and was unavailable to a party bearing the relevant risk. (Id. at 29–31 & nn. 11–13.)

Section 154 recognized allocation by agreement, conscious treatment of limited knowledge as sufficient, and judicial allocation reasonable in the circumstances. Here the court looked first to the parties’ agreement. Although it did not expressly mention an uninhabitable building or defective sewage system, the added acceptance-of-present-condition clause indicated that the purchasers were to bear the risk of unknown defects. Limiting it to known conditions would deprive it of meaningful effect. The parties themselves had therefore allocated this loss to the buyers. (Id. at 30 n. 12, 32.)

The notes reinforce the limits and significance of that reasoning. Note 13 explains that a risk inquiry was absent from the earlier Sherwood and A & M decisions and might have changed or clarified their outcomes. Note 15 links “as is” treatment to latent-defect risk. Note 16 expressly reserves fraud and misrepresentation as possible grounds for rescission notwithstanding such a clause, while explaining that the unappealed adverse finding bound these buyers. The actual result was denial of rescission and reversal of the appellate judgment, not a finding that no mutual mistake had occurred. (Id. at 31 n. 13, 32–33 & nn. 15–16.)
