# Cummings v. Premier Rehab Keller, P.L.L.C.
596 U.S. 212, 142 S. Ct. 1562 (2022) | Contracts

## Court, question, and disposition
On April 28, 2022, the Supreme Court affirmed the Fifth Circuit’s judgment denying emotional-distress damages under the Spending Clause antidiscrimination statutes at issue. Chief Justice Roberts wrote for six Justices: Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett joined. Kavanaugh, joined by Gorsuch, concurred; Breyer, joined by Sotomayor and Kagan, dissented. The question concerned the remedy available under an implied private cause of action, rather than an adjudication that the provider had lawfully treated the plaintiff. (215, 217–220, 230.)

## Facts and procedural history
Jane Cummings is deaf and legally blind and communicates primarily in American Sign Language. In October 2016 she sought physical therapy from Premier, a small business in the Dallas–Fort Worth area, and requested an interpreter at her appointments. Premier declined, explaining that written notes, lip reading, or gestures could be used instead. Cummings sought and obtained treatment elsewhere. Because Premier received Medicare and Medicaid reimbursements, it was subject to the Rehabilitation Act and Affordable Care Act antidiscrimination provisions. (216–217.)

Cummings sued under those statutes for declaratory and injunctive relief and damages. The District Court dismissed the complaint, reasoning that her only alleged compensable injuries were humiliation, frustration, and other emotional distress, and that those damages were unavailable. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, 948 F.3d 673. The Supreme Court granted review of the remedial question. The alleged discriminatory refusal remains an allegation for this decision; the Court did not conduct a trial of the provider’s liability. (217.)

## Funding conditions and notice of remedies
The Court situated the two claims within four Spending Clause statutes: Title VI prohibits discrimination based on race, color, or national origin; Title IX addresses sex discrimination; the Rehabilitation Act addresses disability; and the Affordable Care Act incorporates specified antidiscrimination prohibitions in covered health programs. The statutes did not expressly supply the private causes of action at issue. The Court had recognized implied actions, subsequently ratified by Congress, and monetary relief; the Rehabilitation Act and Affordable Care Act incorporate Title VI’s remedial framework. The question was which monetary remedies that framework permits. (218–220.)

The controlling analogy was a funding recipient’s voluntary agreement to comply with conditions in return for federal money. Such a recipient must have clear notice both of the condition and of the liability attached to violating it when accepting the funds. When statutory text does not identify available remedies, the recipient can expect the remedies traditionally available for breach of contract. This analogy limits implied statutory remedies; it does not transform every funding condition into a literal contract or incorporate contract law wholesale. (219–224.)

Barnes v. Gorman had therefore excluded punitive damages: they are generally unavailable in contract, even though some breaches accompanied by an independent tort may justify them. Compensation and injunctions are ordinarily available; exceptional remedies are not necessarily apparent from an undertaking to comply with funding conditions. The same notice reasoning, the majority concluded, excluded emotional-distress damages because they ordinarily are unavailable for breach of contract. (220–222.)

## Why the contract exceptions did not establish sufficient notice
Cummings relied on exceptions for contracts in which serious emotional disturbance is a particularly likely result of breach, reflected in Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 353. She argued that a promise not to discriminate belongs to that category. The majority gave two independent answers. First, Barnes had looked to general contract rules rather than asking whether a particular transaction could fit an exception. Its rejection of punitive damages despite tort-related exceptions would be undermined by a fine-grained inquiry into exceptional emotional remedies. Both kinds of damages are generally unavailable subject to exceptions; the recipient cannot be charged with notice of every exceptional rule merely because ordinary contract remedies are foreseeable. (222–226.)

That limitation also followed from the judicial character of the implied action. Without congressional specification, the Court would not expand a remedy through a comprehensive survey of obscure exceptions across state contract law. The funding analogy supplies a notice constraint, rather than a license for courts to construct new statutory damages categories from every contractual variation. (223–226.)

Second, even accepting Cummings’s proposed exception-based method, the majority found no sufficiently settled general rule. Some jurisdictions rejected emotional-distress recovery for breach altogether; others confined exceptions more narrowly, including willful conduct or circumstances closely associated with tort duties. Familiar examples involving carriers, innkeepers, telegrams, or burial arrangements did not establish the necessary consensus about a generally available contract remedy for a promise not to discriminate. The majority also characterized many historical examples as involving conduct actionable in tort or a nominally contractual claim closely resembling one. Its sole judicial footnote addressed the dissent’s use of McCormick in that connection. (226–230 & sole note at 229.)

The Court accordingly affirmed. Its ruling bars emotional-distress damages under the Spending Clause statutes considered, not every remedy under every civil-rights statute. It does not abolish available compensatory relief of other kinds or adjudicate an independent tort claim. (230.)

## Concurrence and dissent
Kavanaugh, with Gorsuch, joined the Court while emphasizing a different underlying rationale. He regarded the competing contract arguments as persuasive and the analogy as imperfect. The constitutional background, in his view, assigned the creation of causes of action to Congress. Once the Court had recognized these implied actions, expansion of their remedies should likewise be left to Congress. His concurrence supports the result without purporting to add express statutory language excluding emotional injury. (230–231.)

Breyer’s dissent agreed that funding recipients require notice and that contract law supplies the analogy, but disagreed about the relevant contract comparison. Ordinary commercial bargains principally protect pecuniary interests; promises serving personal or dignitary purposes may foreseeably protect against emotional harm. A promise not to discriminate resembles the latter category. The dissent discussed carriers, inns, marriage promises, funeral services, and telegrams to show a longstanding compensatory exception, rather than an extraordinary punitive remedy. In its view, expectation damages that make good a promise protecting personal interests include foreseeable emotional loss. (231–239.)

The dissent also rejected the majority’s attempt to collapse those examples into independent tort remedies. It read the historical authorities as supporting the contract principle even without relying on tort. Punitive damages punish; emotional-distress damages compensate the kind of harm a nondiscrimination promise is designed to prevent. The dissent contrasted the decision with express civil-rights remedies allowing emotional damages and warned that students or patients suffering discrimination without pecuniary injury could be left without a remedy. That warning and the proposed analogous-contract approach are dissenting positions, not qualifications adopted by the Court. (236–241.)
