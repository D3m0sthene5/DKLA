# Northern Indiana Public Service Co. v. Carbon County Coal Co.
799 F.2d 265 (7th Cir. August 13, 1986) | Contracts

## Facts and commercial setting
In 1978 NIPSCO, an Indiana electric utility, agreed to purchase approximately 1.5 million tons of low-sulphur coal annually from Carbon County's Wyoming mine for twenty years. The initial $24-per-ton price could escalate and had reached $44 by 1985. The adjusted price was a floor: renegotiation could operate in Carbon County's favor, but NIPSCO had no matching right to reduce it. Quantity was fixed rather than tied to NIPSCO's requirements. NIPSCO accepted those commitments to secure a dependable supply. (267–268.)

When NIPSCO sought a fuel-cost rate increase in 1983, customers argued that buying electricity from neighboring utilities would cost less than generating it with the contracted coal. The Indiana Public Service Commission granted the increase but issued economy-purchase orders in December 1983 and February 1984 directing good-faith efforts to buy cheaper electricity when available. It warned that management must bear the burden of disadvantageous long-term coal commitments. NIPSCO did find cheaper outside electricity. It did not seek judicial review of the orders and could not expect to pass the unnecessary coal costs to ratepayers. It stopped accepting coal and sought relief from the contract. (267–268.)

## Litigation and appellate posture
NIPSCO filed a diversity declaratory action on April 24, 1985. It asserted illegality under § 2(c) of the Mineral Lands Leasing Act because Carbon County was affiliated with Union Pacific, and excuse or suspension under the force-majeure clause, frustration, and impracticability. Carbon County counterclaimed and obtained a preliminary injunction requiring continued deliveries. The district court rejected a continuance request and commenced a six-week trial on August 26. A jury awarded Carbon County $181 million. The judge entered damages, denied specific performance, dissolved the preliminary injunction, and allowed a stay of execution without a bond. The mine, whose only customer was NIPSCO, then closed. (268.)

NIPSCO appealed the injunction and damages judgment, contesting trial preparation time and the rejection of its defenses. Carbon County appealed the denial of specific performance and the bond-free stay. The opinion resolves all those issues; the assigned abridgment chiefly presents force majeure and supervening-event defenses. (268–269.)

## Force majeure and allocated market risk
The clause allowed NIPSCO to stop deliveries for causes beyond its reasonable control, including governmental acts that wholly or partly prevented use of the coal. The economy orders did not prevent use: they prevented NIPSCO from charging ratepayers for uneconomical generation when cheaper electricity was available. The orders therefore did not trigger the clause, and the force-majeure issue should not have gone to the jury. NIPSCO's complaint about the jury instructions could not change that result. (274–276.)

The fixed quantity and price floor assigned the downside of changing market conditions to NIPSCO. A broadly worded force-majeure clause did not erase that central allocation when the gamble became unfavorable. The Commission's regulation substituted for competitive pressure that would likewise have prevented an unregulated utility from shifting inflated input costs to customers. Governmental participation in making a bargain less advantageous did not automatically turn ordinary price risk into force majeure. Posner contrasted an order closing a generating plant for safety or pollution reasons, which would actually impede coal use. Whether prudent management should receive some regulatory allowance for losses remained a matter between NIPSCO and the Commission; NIPSCO had not challenged the orders. (275–276.)

## Frustration and impracticability
The district court had ruled that Indiana did not recognize frustration and that a buyer could not claim impracticability. The Seventh Circuit did not adopt those categorical propositions. It questioned the authority supporting the frustration ruling but found the present facts insufficient even if Indiana recognized the defense. It likewise left open whether Indiana allowed buyers to invoke UCC § 2-615, whether a force-majeure clause displaced common-law defenses, and whether the clause covered different ground. (276–278.)

Impossibility, impracticability, and frustration can address contingencies for which parties did not provide by assigning risk in accordance with their presumed intentions. They cannot transfer back a risk the contract expressly assigns. Here a market decline, including one operating through governmental economic intervention, was the very risk NIPSCO's price floor and fixed-volume commitment placed on it. The bargain becoming substantially less attractive supplied no excuse. The UCC's reference to sellers and Comment 9's possible application to buyers did not require resolution: § 1-103 preserved consistent common-law principles, and neither statutory nor common-law reasoning helped NIPSCO on these facts. (277–279.)

## Illegality and preparation-time defenses
Carbon County was a partnership owned equally by an independent company and a subsidiary of Union Pacific's holding company. Only approximately fifteen percent of anticipated contract output came from federal land. The court did not conclusively determine whether the leasing statute reached affiliates beyond railroad alter egos or whether this affiliation violated it. Carbon County was not an alter ego, and the coal-sale contract itself was not intrinsically illegal. The statute regulated holding mining rights, rather than sales of mined coal. Potential affiliation problems could be corrected without cancelling the sale contract, and NIPSCO had not requested a limited damages abatement for federal-land coal. (270–273.)

Even assuming a violation affecting the contract, unenforceability did not follow automatically. Federal law supplied the effect of violation of the federal statute. The court weighed statutory deterrence against contractual stability: the broader agency interpretation postdated this contract and applied prospectively; the attenuated connection produced no demonstrated injury, complaint from competitors, or enforcement action. Ordinary statutory remedies remained available. Denying enforcement would confer a windfall on NIPSCO while unsettling other contracts. The statute therefore furnished no defense, without any assertion that courts could repeal a statute they considered obsolete. (273–274.)

The accelerated schedule was also upheld. NIPSCO controlled when it sued, had retained substantial trial counsel months earlier, and could begin preparation before filing. The court allowed additional damages discovery during trial. NIPSCO identified no reasonable probability of outcome-changing prejudice, did not challenge the verdict's size, and could obtain nothing material from further affiliation discovery under the court's disposition of illegality. Trial-management discretion had not been abused. (269–270.)

## Remedies, bond, and exact judgment
Damages adequately protected Carbon County's contractual interest. The award represented an undisputed reasonable estimate of the present value of expected sales revenue less mining costs. Specific performance would compel uneconomical production and waste resources, or create bargaining leverage to obtain payment exceeding the contract loss. Posner described this as an efficient breach while preserving the duty to compensate the injured promisee. Lost employment and business in Hanna, Wyoming, did not justify additional recovery: those persons were neither parties nor third-party beneficiaries, and Carbon County did not represent their legally protected interests. The opinion distinguished the duty to consider genuine third-party effects of equitable relief from creating new contractual rights for every person economically affected. (279–281.)

Carbon County's pursuit of specific performance in place of damages weakened its demand for security against delayed execution of the damages judgment. Independently, the then-operative Rule 62(d) made a bond a route to an automatic stay, rather than a condition of every discretionary stay. NIPSCO's assets, revenues, net worth, regulated status, and required financial reports supported the conclusion that a costly $181 million bond was unnecessary. Supplementary relief remained possible if its ability to pay deteriorated. (281.)

The court dismissed the appeal from the dissolved preliminary injunction as moot because NIPSCO sought only dissolution, and affirmed the remaining appealed orders. It awarded neither side appellate costs. Posner wrote for a panel with Ripple and Eschbach; no separate opinion or dissent is reported. The full opinion contains no numbered judicial footnotes. (268, 281.)
