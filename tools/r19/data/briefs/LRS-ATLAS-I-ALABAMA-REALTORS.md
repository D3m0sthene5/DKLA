# Alabama Association of Realtors v. Department of Health and Human Services

**594 U.S. 758 (2021) — Supreme Court of the United States — August 26, 2021.** Per curiam; **6–3**. Justice Breyer dissented, joined by Sotomayor and Kagan. Application No. 21A23. Assigned references: November second casebook, physical PDF pages 28, 34, and 37, printed pages 972, 978, and 981. Pinpoints use the separately numbered original slip writings.

The Court vacated the stay of a district-court judgment setting aside the CDC's eviction moratorium, making that judgment enforceable. It considered the landlords virtually certain to establish that the Public Health Service Act did not authorize such an expansive intervention in rental property. The instrument concerns lifting a stay during ongoing appeal, not a final Supreme Court merits affirmance. (Per curiam 1, 5–8.)

## Legislative and administrative chronology

Congress's March 2020 CARES Act imposed a 120-day eviction moratorium on properties participating in federal assistance programs or subject to federally backed loans. After it expired in July, Congress did not renew that provision. The CDC adopted a broader moratorium under §361(a) of the Public Health Service Act, covering residential property nationwide and imposing criminal penalties for violations. It initially expired December 31, but Congress extended it for one month through separate relief legislation. The CDC then successively extended it through March, June, and July 2021. (Per curiam 2–3.)

Section 361(a), codified at 42 U.S.C. §264(a), authorized regulations necessary to prevent introduction, transmission, or spread of communicable disease from abroad or between States. Its second sentence listed inspection, fumigation, disinfection, sanitation, pest extermination, and destruction of infected or contaminated animals or articles, together with other necessary measures. CDC invoked that provision through delegated authority. Previous regulations had generally involved infected individuals or disease-carrying animals rather than rental-property restrictions. (Per curiam 2–3.)

Realtor associations and property managers in Alabama and Georgia sued. The District of Columbia district court granted them summary judgment May 5, holding that CDC lacked statutory authority, but stayed its own judgment pending appeal. It considered a serious legal question plus the other stay factors sufficient even without a substantial likelihood of Government success. The D.C. Circuit declined to lift the stay. The Supreme Court also declined relief on the plaintiffs' first application. Kavanaugh separately agreed that CDC lacked authority but thought the short remaining period and orderly distribution of rental assistance justified leaving the stay temporarily intact. Four other Justices would have vacated it then. Those prior votes and concurrence were procedural history, not the August order's separate writings. (Per curiam 3–4.)

The moratorium expired July 31. Three days later CDC reissued it, limiting coverage to counties with substantial or high COVID transmission and tenants making financial-need declarations. The district court thought the Government's merits prospects and the equities no longer supported a stay: vaccine and rental-assistance distribution had improved while landlord losses accumulated. Nevertheless, it believed the D.C. Circuit's earlier decision constrained it under law of the case. After the circuit again refused relief, the plaintiffs returned to the Supreme Court. (Per curiam 1, 4–5.)

## Statutory reasoning

The Government read the first sentence of §361(a) as a broad authorization for whatever CDC deemed necessary to prevent interstate spread. The Court read it with the second sentence's examples. Those examples directly targeted disease by identifying, isolating, or destroying infection. The eviction rule depended on a more indirect chain: some evicted tenants might move across state lines, and some of those might carry infection. That downstream connection differed from the enumerated measures, making the claimed authority a strained reading of the combined text. (Per curiam 5–6.)

Even assuming ambiguity, the magnitude of the claim reinforced the need for clear congressional authorization. Coverage encompassed at least 80 percent of the country and millions of tenants at risk of eviction. Nearly $50 billion in appropriated rental aid offered a rough indication of economic scale, although the parties disputed the exact burden on landlords. The rule also intruded into landlord-tenant relationships traditionally governed by state law, implicating the balance of federal and state power and control over private property. (Per curiam 6.)

The Government identified no limiting principle beyond CDC's own necessity judgment. The Court posed examples such as compulsory free groceries, computers, or Internet service to illustrate the potential breadth. The unprecedented assertion was amplified by substantial criminal penalties, including possible imprisonment. If a federal eviction moratorium were to continue, Congress needed specifically to authorize it. (Per curiam 6–8.)

## Equities and order

The Court considered the landlords at risk of irreparable financial loss without assured recovery, including owners of modest means. Preventing eviction for lease breaches also burdened the right to exclude. Meanwhile, the Government had additional months to distribute rental assistance, reducing the transition justification that had previously favored the stay. Congress knew further extension would likely require legislation but had not enacted it before expiration. (Per curiam 7–8.)

The Court acknowledged the strong public interest in containing Delta transmission. Desirable public-health ends nevertheless did not authorize unlawful agency means. It **granted the application to vacate the stay**, allowing the district court's favorable judgment to operate while the appeal remained pending. The Court made a forceful likelihood-of-success assessment; its procedural disposition was not itself a merits affirmance after ordinary appellate review. (Per curiam 1, 5, 8.)

## Breyer's complete dissent

Breyer, joined by Sotomayor and Kagan, would leave the stay in place. Vacating a lower-court stay required clear and demonstrable error under accepted standards. Statutory authority remained contested, with circuits divided, and the modified rule was more closely tailored to high-transmission counties. Protection ceased when a county no longer met its transmission threshold. Tenants had to attest to efforts to obtain assistance and make partial payments, qualifying income and financial losses, and absence of other housing. Landlords could challenge the declarations' truthfulness in court. (Breyer 1–3.)

He read the first statutory sentence broadly and the second as expanding or emphasizing means of enforcement, rather than limiting the initial delegation. Historical public-health regulation had included restrictions on eviction of infected people; the express authorization of other necessary measures and drafting history supported that reading. Quarantine powers could intrude more heavily than an eviction rule, and Congress had expressly extended the earlier CDC moratorium. The Court's previous refusal to disturb the stay had not resolved the merits merely because one Justice's concurrence expressed a view. (Breyer 3–5.)

The equities also favored preserving protections. Congress appropriated more than $46.5 billion for rent and arrears, and tenants remained obliged to make payments to the extent possible. Delay in reimbursement injured landlords, but mass displacement could produce harder-to-reverse public-health harms. The dissent reproduced a CDC daily-cases chart showing the renewed surge and discussed rising hospitalizations, overcrowded housing, shelters, and studies linking eviction to transmission. Those data and projections were the dissent's cited record, not fresh findings made by this brief. The Court should respect expert judgment and resolve disputed statutory questions through full briefing and argument rather than this summary procedure. (Breyer 5–8, including chart on 6.)
