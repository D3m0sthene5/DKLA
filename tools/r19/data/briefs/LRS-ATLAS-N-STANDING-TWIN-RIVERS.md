# Twin Rivers Paper Company LLC v. Securities and Exchange Commission

**934 F.3d 607 (D.C. Cir. 2019) — United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit — August 16, 2019 — No. 18-1213.** Judge Katsas wrote for the panel of Judges Henderson, Rogers, and Katsas. There were no separate opinions. The assigned account appears in the November continuation at physical page 169, printed page 1259.

The court denied a petition challenging an SEC rule that permitted online shareholder reports while preserving a right to request paper copies. The consumer organization failed to establish timely evidentiary support for its members' Article III standing. The paper-industry petitioners faced a different barrier: their interest in selling paper was outside the zone of interests of the securities statutes. The court did not decide whether the rule was substantively valid.

## Regulation, parties, and claimed harm

Investment companies, including mutual funds, had generally been required to mail shareholder reports unless an investor selected electronic delivery. Rule 30e-3 changed that default. A company could post reports online and mail notice of their availability; it still had to provide paper copies to investors who requested them. The SEC expected approximately $140 million in annual savings, ordinarily benefiting investors because printing expenses came out of fund assets. It also found that many shareholders preferred internet access. **Official slip 2–3.**

The rule included protections for paper users. Companies had to continue paper delivery through an extended transition, no earlier than January 1, 2021, with advance warnings of the change. After switching, each online report required a mailed notice with a toll-free number for requesting that report or all future paper reports. A requested copy had to be mailed within three business days without charge. The rule therefore changed the default rather than eliminating paper reports. **Slip 3.**

Consumer Action, a nonprofit membership organization, joined Twin Rivers Paper Company and three industry organizations in seeking direct appellate review. They alleged violations of the Securities Act of 1933, Securities Exchange Act of 1934, Investment Company Act of 1940, and APA. They argued, among other things, that the SEC had not adequately protected shareholders who preferred paper. The industry petitioners alleged reduced demand for paper; Consumer Action claimed to represent members harmed by the new arrangement. **Slip 4, 12–13.**

## Consumer Action's constitutional standing

An organization relying on its members' standing must establish that at least one member could sue individually. In direct review of an agency action, the court required evidence in the administrative record or supplementary material establishing the organization's standing. When standing was not apparent from the record, the opening brief needed both the argument and supporting evidence. This rule enabled an adversarial response; it did not impose Article III requirements on participation in the underlying agency rulemaking. **Slip 4–7.**

Consumer Action supplied no individual member affidavits with its opening brief. Its organizational affidavit referred broadly to members, followers, supporters, retirees, and community organizations without identifying a particular injured member or even clearly describing which people were its members. General statements about preferences and unspecified burdens were inadequate. Statements in the brief were not evidence. Its agency comments likewise discussed diminished readership, transparency, and access without connecting those concerns to identified members. **Slip 6–7.**

The organization submitted member affidavits with its reply, but the court declined to accept that late showing. A failure to preserve standing evidence was not itself a jurisdictional rule incapable of exception: good cause could excuse it when a party reasonably believed its initial evidence sufficient or reasonably thought standing apparent from the record. Neither explanation fit this organization's vague initial submissions. **Slip 7–9.**

The court distinguished a previous airport-expansion case in which opening affidavits identified nearby residents and reply affidavits made the already foreseeable noise injury clearer. Consumer Action had not supplied comparable initial evidence, and its reply introduced a different injury theory. Instead of impaired access to or understanding of financial information, some members objected ideologically to the change and others complained of time spent making a free telephone call. No reply affiant alleged the originally suggested access or comprehension problem. **Slip 8–10.**

An ideological objection to administration of the laws was not concrete injury. The lost-time theory presented a closer question, but the court did not categorically hold that every small expenditure of time was constitutionally insufficient. Consumer Action had identified neither a relevant statutory protection against making the call nor a traditional analogue supporting its theory. By raising debatable questions only in reply, it frustrated the ordinary adversarial process. The court consequently could not find standing from those submissions. **Slip 10–11.**

## Paper suppliers and the statutory zone

The industry petitioners' alleged commercial loss did not resolve the distinct question whether the securities laws authorized their challenge. The opinion explained that *Lexmark* had recast the zone inquiry from prudential standing into identification of persons permitted to invoke a cause of action. The requirement also applied to direct appellate agency review. A challenger could be an intended statutory beneficiary or another suitable challenger whose interests aligned systematically, rather than by accident, with those beneficiaries. **Slip 11–12.**

The opinion's sole footnote distinguished the review provisions: all three statutes allowed review of certain SEC orders, but “order” included rules under the Securities Act and Investment Company Act, not under the Exchange Act. The APA neither independently conferred jurisdiction nor expanded the specific statutory review routes. The petitioners could not bypass those distinctions simply by citing the APA. **Slip 12 n.1.**

Investors, not paper suppliers, were the securities laws' intended beneficiaries. Paper companies' unconditional preference for greater use of paper did not reliably promote investors' interests. The SEC's findings that many investors preferred online reports and would benefit from lower printing costs indicated a potential conflict, likely to increase as internet use grew. Occasional agreement between an investor wanting paper and a supplier selling it did not show the systematic alignment the court required. **Slip 13–14.**

The industry petitioners invoked cases permitting competitors to enforce restricted statutory entry boundaries. The court distinguished those cases because the statutory limits confined the challengers to guarding a specific congressional boundary. Here, general requirements to consider public interest, investor protection, efficiency, competition, and capital formation did not similarly channel the paper industry's incentives. Paper sellers could advocate more paper regardless of investor benefit. The court applied its waste-treatment and pollution-equipment precedents concerning that mismatch. **Slip 13–15.**

## Judgment, assignment, and significance

The panel **denied the petition for review**. Consumer Action lacked a properly established Article III basis for proceeding; the industry petitioners asserted interests beyond those arguably protected or regulated by the securities laws. There was no rule vacatur, merits determination that electronic delivery satisfied every statutory requirement, or remand to the SEC. **Slip 15–16.**

The assigned paragraph accurately highlights the paper companies' statutory barrier. The complete opinion adds the independently important problem of proving an association's standing on time. Its formulation does not mean that only direct statutory beneficiaries can sue: suitable challengers may qualify, and the competitors' boundary-enforcement cases explain one route. Nor does the decision turn every evidentiary deadline into a jurisdictional prohibition; the panel considered good-cause exceptions and rejected them on these facts.

Historically, the case illustrates why constitutional injury and statutory authorization must be assessed separately and why direct agency review requires evidence rather than merely plausible allegations in a complaint. This brief reports the 2019 judgment without independently certifying the present regulation or every later development in standing law.
