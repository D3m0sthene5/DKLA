# Investment Company Institute v. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation
728 F.2d 518 (D.C. Cir. 1984) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and the district court's intervention

In spring 1982, Boston Five Cents Savings Bank planned to sell mutual-fund shares through wholly owned subsidiaries. The Investment Company Institute petitioned the FDIC on April 13 to declare the plan unlawful and prevent implementation. After a May 17 public board meeting, the FDIC declined to decide the petition's merits, explaining that the bank had not yet submitted an application for approval of mutual-fund activities. ICI sued, alleging arbitrary agency action under the APA and a Sunshine Act violation: it asserted that the public meeting merely ratified a decision made beforehand in private. Those were separate claims, not findings that the bank's activity was unlawful or that a secret meeting had been proved. (520–521.)

Expedited discovery followed. When the FDIC sought dismissal or relief from a discovery order, it offered to reconsider the petition at a new meeting. ICI objected that reconsideration could still end in a discretionary refusal to reach the merits. On June 25, 1982, the district court issued an order drafted by ICI without accompanying findings or explanation. It required an open meeting, decision on every asserted legal violation, an opportunity for interested persons to be heard, a written merits opinion subject to district review, and an FDIC direction preventing the bank and related entities from implementing the plan meanwhile. It stayed the earlier discovery order and required preservation and sealed deposit of records. The FDIC immediately appealed. (521–522.)

## An appealable injunction, despite its discovery label

The district court characterized its order as discovery-related and later said the FDIC could escape its obligations by complying with the earlier discovery order. The appellate majority rejected the label. The order's central mandate governed agency action outside the management of pending litigation and supplied substantive relief resembling a final injunction. Its text was categorical, not an optional substitute, and the district court threatened attorney sanctions for noncompliance. It therefore was appealable under § 1292(a)(1). What the court imposed also exceeded the FDIC's offer of reconsideration: mandatory merits adjudication eliminated the discretion the agency's proposal retained. (522–523.)

The later statement could not retroactively negate an already perfected appeal. Nor did it end the parties' live dispute about the court's authority to compel agency action. Even considering only the contested injunction, recurrence of the same issue was predictable because it embodied the relief demanded in the complaint. The majority therefore heard the appeal and the fully briefed merits. The February 28, 1984 per curiam opinion represented Judges Bork and Scalia; Chief Judge J. Skelly Wright dissented. (518, 520, 522–524 & nn. 1–2.)

## Banking-law restrictions on compulsory cease-and-desist relief

Section 1818 governed the enforcement relief requested. Its structure protected banks through notice, an adjudicatory hearing, and specified judicial-review channels. Temporary cease-and-desist relief required the prescribed danger of insolvency or serious financial weakening and initiation of formal proceedings toward a permanent order. Section 1818(i)(1) barred courts from affecting issuance or enforcement of FDIC notices or orders except through the statute's specified routes. Those routes presupposed an outstanding agency order; they did not authorize a district court to compel its issuance. (523–525 & nn. 3–4.)

The district court's direction to stop the bank's activities had exactly the effect of requiring a temporary cease-and-desist order, regardless of its failure to use that name. It thus violated the jurisdictional restriction. It also bypassed substantive and procedural safeguards: no relevant insolvency evidence existed, no formal proceedings had given the bank notice or a hearing, and immediate action disregarded the statutory role of state banking authorities. The majority therefore held that the district court lacked authority to command the FDIC to halt the proposed activity. It did not decide whether the bank's plan complied with substantive banking law. (525 & n. 5.)

## The refusal to consider enforcement was committed to discretion

The separate direction to decide ICI's petition on its merits also failed. The Sunshine Act alone could not justify setting aside the agency's decision. Under the APA, the refusal was committed to agency discretion; the majority did not need to resolve the separate exception for statutory preclusion of review. It applied the circuit's pragmatic framework: need to safeguard the plaintiff, effect on agency effectiveness, and suitability of the issue for judicial resolution. A note acknowledged the majority judges' disagreement with that framework in other litigation while recognizing it as circuit law. (525–526 & n. 6.)

ICI faced no direct compliance burden or penalty from an order addressed to it. Nothing cast doubt on its ability to sue the bank directly, although that route might be inconvenient. The agency was not the exclusive avenue of protection as in Dunlop. The FDIC also described extensive supervision and examination obligations and hundreds of endangered banks requiring attention. ICI did not dispute those representations; the court found them plausible, while acknowledging a less than fully satisfactory basis for an independent assessment and treating that consideration as nondecisive. (526–527.)

Most importantly, this was a refusal to invoke a permissive, nonexclusive enforcement power. The agency had not initiated and then terminated a proceeding, adopted a consistent policy of abandoning statutory duties, or decided a legal question that the court could review against substantive standards. There might be law governing the bank's conduct, but that was different from law requiring the FDIC to decide or prosecute this petition. Section 1818's authorizations were permissive and supplied no criteria mandating action; priorities and budget choices were poorly suited to judicial reassessment. The majority distinguished A.G. Becker, where an agency had taken up a matter and rendered a legal determination, while also recognizing that even there enforcement timing remained discretionary. (527–528 & nn. 7–8.)

The litigation concerned declaratory and enforcement requests regarding this bank, not a general rulemaking challenge. The opinion separately noted ICI's later rulemaking request and the FDIC's interpretive ruling and advance notice of proposed rulemaking. Those developments did not turn the challenged May decision into a rulemaking denial or a substantive decision approving the bank's plan. (523–524 n. 3.)

## Disposition and dissent

The majority **invalidated the injunction and disposed of ICI's case insofar as it challenged the FDIC's refusal to take up the petition**. Only the Sunshine Act claim remained on remand. The court expressed no view on its legal merits; if the offer of a new meeting remained available, that meeting would nullify the earlier meeting's effect and moot the remaining claim. Thus the opinion did not direct unconditional dismissal of every claim without regard to the remand circumstances. (523–524, 528.)

Wright would have stayed appellate action until the district court conducted contempt proceedings concerning alleged discovery noncompliance. In his substantive asterisk note, he argued that the majority's general nonreviewability premise departed from the circuit's recent Chaney decision, which narrowly construed the agency-discretion exception even for nonenforcement. The majority's note responded that a particular instance of review did not establish general reviewability and that Chaney's broad language was either dictum or itself inconsistent with earlier Kixmiller language. This was a dispute within the circuit at the time, not a basis to substitute later Supreme Court doctrine into the 1984 judgment. (527 n. 7; 528, Wright dissent & * note.)
