# Pittston Stevedoring Corp. v. Dellaventura

544 F.2d 35 (2d Cir. 1976) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, posture, and separate outcomes

On July 1, 1976, a panel of Lumbard, Friendly, and Oakes resolved four employers’ petitions challenging Benefits Review Board decisions sustaining workers’ compensation awards under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act. Friendly wrote the principal opinion, joined by Oakes. Lumbard agreed with dismissal of two petitions on threshold grounds but dissented from rejection of the other two on the merits. The captioned Dellaventura petition was dismissed as untimely; Scaffidi’s was dismissed for want of a justiciable controversy; Blundo’s and Caputo’s were denied on the merits. Treating all four as merits affirmances would erase the court’s jurisdictional distinctions. (39–43, 57–58.)

## Statutory change and the disputed coverage boundary

The 1972 amendments changed both remedies and coverage. They eliminated longshoremen’s unseaworthiness actions against vessels and prohibited the employer-indemnity arrangements that had produced the Sieracki–Ryan litigation triangle. Workers received increased compensation benefits and an extension of federal coverage in return. Negligence actions against vessels remained. (39–41.)

Previously the injury generally had to occur on navigable waters, including a dry dock. The amended situs provision extended to adjoining piers, wharves, terminals, and specified other areas used for vessel-related activities. A new employee-status definition covered people engaged in maritime employment, including longshoremen, people engaged in longshoring operations, and specified harbor workers. Thus being injured at an eligible location was necessary but did not alone settle occupational coverage. Congress had not defined the precise extent of longshoring operations or maritime employment. (41–42 & nn. 1–3.)

Employers advocated a first/last-point-of-rest rule: unloading coverage would stop where cargo was first deposited after leaving the vessel, and loading coverage would begin where cargo last rested before movement onto it. Workers and the government advocated a considerably broader unloading/loading process extending to delivery to or receipt from the consignee’s vehicle. Friendly rejected the employers’ restriction without adopting every possible implication of the opposing theory. (46–47.)

## The four workers and administrative proceedings

Blundo was a checker for International Terminal Operating Company. A container unloaded at one Brooklyn pier was transported over city streets to another, opened by Customs, and stripped there. He slipped on ice while checking its contents at the waterfront stringpiece. The accident occurred during container handling at a terminal adjoining the water, even though that particular finger pier was used for containers rather than vessels. (41; 51 n. 19.)

Scaffidi operated a hustler transporting containers for Pittston. He took a container over public streets between piers and was injured when a case fell as it was opened at the receiving platform before loading aboard a ship. Dellaventura, a sorter sometimes working in ships’ holds, slipped on loose coffee beans while helping load a consignee’s truck. That cargo had been discharged 133 days earlier, with no explanation for the pickup delay. The majority’s account states that the pier had no warehouse facilities. (41–42.)

Caputo usually performed terminal labor and accepted available longshoreman shape-up work. On the accident day he worked for Northeast Marine Terminal and was injured while rolling a dolly of cheese into a consignee’s truck; the cheese had been discharged at least five days earlier. The parties stipulated that this task involved the same risk as comparable truck loading elsewhere. Administrative law judges made awards, and the Board sustained them. (42.)

## Dellaventura: filing period measured from issuance

The Board issued its order on October 9, 1975, but the employer and insurer filed their review petition on February 5, 1976. Section 921(c) required filing within sixty days following issuance. Although the agency apparently failed to send notice directly to the parties as its regulation required, their attorney received the decision within the filing period and offered no explanation for the delay. Issuance, not eventual personal notice, controlled. Even if an excusable-neglect exception could be implied, these circumstances would not justify it. The court granted Dellaventura’s dismissal motion and did not need to decide the Solicitor of Labor’s entitlement to make a parallel motion. (43–44 & nn. 5–6.)

## Scaffidi: payment left no effective appellate remedy

The insurer paid the award and declined further contest. Pittston nevertheless appealed, asserting that the award could worsen its experience rating and future premiums. The court found only conclusory assertions about that effect. More decisively, reversal would not recover the voluntarily paid award from Scaffidi, and Pittston identified no effective relief available through its petition. The request would yield an advisory opinion about a paid award. This differed from partial insurance payment leaving the employer’s own unpaid liability at stake. The petition was dismissed. (44–46 & nn. 7–10.)

## Why the court construed the statute independently

The assigned account quotes Friendly’s diagnosis of conflicting Supreme Court approaches to deference. Some decisions strongly deferred to administrative application of statutes; others permitted judicial substitution when the issue concerned statutory meaning. That tension required selecting the appropriate approach for the question actually presented. The court accepted substantial-evidence review of factual findings while rejecting the claim that statutory coverage must be sustained merely because the Board’s answer was rational. (48–50.)

Several features supported independent judgment here. The Board adjudicated disputes rather than making national policy; it had not developed a comprehensive factual record about waterfront working arrangements; its initial interpretations arose before substantial experience under the new amendments; and the issue depended significantly on legislative history and prior judicial decisions. Repetition of early decisions did not manufacture expertise or statutory authority. Friendly distinguished this inquiry from agencies’ substantive rulemaking and interpretation of their own rules. (49–50 & n. 15.)

The opinion also declined to make the Act’s rebuttable presumption of coverage answer a general question of statutory construction. A presumption rebuttable by factual evidence did not itself locate the statutory line. Definitions adopted for waterfront policing, safety regulations, or carriage-of-goods liability were likewise not automatically transferable. Liberal construction of remedial legislation carried more weight, but the court still examined text, structure, and legislative history. (48–51 & nn. 14–18.)

Friendly criticized the thin administrative records and explained what a comprehensive inquiry into port practices might have supplied. Nevertheless, in light of related cases pending in several circuits, the panel chose to resolve the cases before it rather than remand for that inquiry. Footnote 13 allowed reconsideration if another circuit ordered such a remand. This criticism did not become the judgment actually entered. (47–48 & nn. 12–13.)

## Coverage of Blundo and Caputo

Text and committee reports supported two related conclusions. Container stripping and stuffing performed on shore were functional components of unloading and loading, reflecting modern cargo-handling techniques. A checker directly involved in those operations did not lose coverage because the container had crossed a street or moved away from its initial resting point. Blundo therefore met status as well as situs. (51–53.)

Congress also sought uniform coverage for workers who would be covered during part of their activities. Caputo’s responsibilities included typical vessel-related longshoring, rather than only collecting stored cargo for onward transportation. Moving the cheese into a consignee’s truck remained part of the unloading process on these facts. Drawing the line at the truck’s interior would be artificial, and a five-day pickup delay did not establish excluded storage. The court refused to create a task-specific hazard test from the stipulation about ordinary truck-loading risks. (53–55.)

The majority held at least that coverage reached eligible-situs workers stripping or stuffing containers, and cargo handlers through departure from the pier who spent a significant portion of their time in typical vessel-loading or unloading work. It reserved whether the latter occupational proviso was essential and how public-warehouse situations should be treated. Thus the decision neither adopted the first-point-of-rest rule nor covered every employee injured anywhere within the enlarged geographical area. (55–56 & nn. 24–27.)

The court upheld Congress’s constitutional authority to extend compensation landward. The admiralty grant supported maritime-related injuries on adjoining structures; maritime employment contracts furnished an additional basis if needed, without the tort-locality limitation. (56–57 & n. 28.)

## Lumbard’s partial dissent and exact judgment

Lumbard agreed with the untimeliness and justiciability dismissals. For Blundo and Caputo he preferred the first/last-point-of-rest rule, regarding it as more consistent with Congress’s intended correction of water/land anomalies and easier to administer. Both injuries occurred after the cargo’s first resting point; he would set aside those awards. His reference to Dellaventura’s cargo being stored in a warehouse differs from the majority’s stated no-warehouse finding, but Dellaventura’s substantive coverage was not decided by the court. (57–58; majority 42–43.)

The resulting merits denials in Blundo and Caputo left their awards intact; the two other petitions were dismissed without merits coverage decisions.
