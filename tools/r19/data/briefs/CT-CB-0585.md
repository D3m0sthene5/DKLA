# Nanakuli Paving & Rock Co. v. Shell Oil Co.
664 F.2d 772 (9th Cir. 1981) | Contracts | Price protection, commercial usage, performance, and good faith

## A mutually dependent supply relationship

Nanakuli, a division of Grace Brothers and Hawaii's second-largest asphaltic paving contractor, bought its asphalt requirements from Shell from 1963 through 1974 under successive long-term arrangements. It had initially handled smaller jobs on Oahu and sought to compete for major government paving work against Hawaiian Bitumuls, which obtained asphalt from Chevron. Shell's expansion depended on greater volume from Nanakuli, while Nanakuli needed dependable supply and competitive pricing. Shell built a terminal and agreed to supply Nanakuli; a distributorship commission largely operated as a discount because most sales were for Nanakuli's own use. Their close commercial relationship was described by witnesses as a partnership, and Nanakuli displayed Shell's colors and logo. Those descriptions conveyed commercial closeness, rather than a judicial holding that the firms formed a legal partnership. (664 F.2d at 777, 780–781.)

When Nanakuli's quarry landlord sought upgraded facilities, the contractor estimated an investment of $250,000–$300,000. Shell considered direct financing but instead offered another volume discount to support the expansion. Shell officials joined meetings with Nanakuli's bank. On April 1, 1969, the firms executed supply, distributorship, and discount arrangements paralleling loan amortization. They extended through December 31, 1975, with cancellation possible thereafter on six months' notice, making their minimum duration longer than seven years. The unusually long relationship helped explain why Nanakuli expected treatment different from ordinary short-term purchasers. The written supply price was Shell's posted price at delivery, free on board Honolulu. (Id. at 778, 781–782.)

Price protection held an old price temporarily or for identified quantities already committed in fixed-price work and commonly involved advance notice of increases. The background mattered: major local, state, and federal government jobs generally prohibited material-price escalation, and most general contractors likewise would not pass increases through. A contractor could bid before an increase, receive an award later, and face substantial unrecoverable costs. Nanakuli contended that Shell's written pricing term incorporated the commercial practice protecting that committed work. (Id. at 778–779, 784; n.4.)

## An abrupt 1974 increase and three jury claims

Shell raised the price from $44 to $76 per ton effective January 1, 1974, by a letter dated December 31 that Nanakuli received January 4. Nanakuli requested protection for 7,200 tons committed at the former price. Its officers wrote repeatedly and met Shell's new mainland management, but protection was denied. The oil embargo tightened supplies and increased petroleum costs; asphalt itself was not under the federal petroleum price controls. Shell also reorganized asphalt sales, replacing managers involved in the 1969 negotiations with officials unfamiliar with the Hawaiian relationship. At the meeting, they had not previously seen the three contracts, knew nothing of the earlier protections, and searched incomplete files rather than consulting the local representative effectively. Their denial relied on the written pricing term and current company policy. (Id. at 786–789, 805.)

Nanakuli sued in Hawaii state court in February 1976; Shell removed on March 2. A jury awarded $220,800 on the price-protection claim. It denied the two other claims: commissions and discounts on substitute asphalt purchased elsewhere, and profits lost when shortages required abandoning already signed work. The appeal did not turn those rejected claims into additional awards. The district court set aside the favorable verdict through judgment notwithstanding the verdict. Nanakuli appealed, and Shell cross-appealed evidentiary rulings. (Id. at 777; nn.1–3.)

The Ninth Circuit applied the verdict-supporting standard: consider the whole evidence and reasonable inferences favorably to Nanakuli, without weighing witness credibility, and ask whether reasonable jurors could find the breach. It did not decide the case as an independent appellate factfinder selecting among conflicting testimony. The stated award is $220,800; the opinion describes 7,200 requested tons and a $32 increase but does not simply identify that award as their unadjusted arithmetic product. (Id. at 777–778, 793–794.)

## Two supporting theories and Shell's objections

Nanakuli first argued that established paving-industry usage formed part of the 1969 agreement, reinforced by Shell's actual performance. It alternatively argued that commercially reasonable fair dealing required protection when implementing the 1974 increase, even if protection was not separately incorporated as a term. Shell disputed the breadth of the relevant trade, characterized its earlier protection as isolated waivers, and maintained that any usage conflicted with the express delivery-price provision. It also challenged the specificity of the practice for calculating damages. (Id. at 778–780, 792–794.)

The court upheld admission of asphalt and aggregate suppliers' practices within Hawaii's asphaltic-paving trade. The Code defined usage through regular observance in a place, vocation, or trade sufficient to justify expected observance, and recognized practices parties knew or should know. A supplier was not bound only by conduct of sellers of its identical product. Here, the small market, two principal asphalt suppliers, Shell's investment tied to Nanakuli's expansion, and its representative's intimate knowledge of bidding and material costs justified the broader geographic-commercial frame. The court still upheld exclusion of cement suppliers, whose material played too small and infrequent a part in asphalt paving. (Id. at 779, 790–792; nn.6, 26.)

Shell's local representative Bohner visited frequently, followed successful bids, sometimes attended bid openings, and knew the contractor's supply needs well before shipments from California. His construction-industry involvement and familiarity with aggregate's importance allowed jurors to infer knowledge broader than his cautious trial answers admitted. The court's scope ruling therefore was tied to an unusually close, small-market record, not a universal rule charging every seller with every buyer-side custom. (Id. at 783–784, 792.)

## Usage and damages were supported by concrete evidence

Two experienced aggregate-supplier witnesses testified that protection long predated 1969. Suppliers accepted a paver's list of committed jobs or tonnage and trusted its word without demanding individual written orders. Documentary evidence supported this practice. Chevron likewise routinely protected Hawaiian Bitumuls, including 12,000 tons in March 1969, even though their written agreement did not mention protection. Its earlier ownership connection with that paver affected weight, not automatic admissibility. Shell produced no competing instance of a Hawaiian paving supplier refusing such protection before its own 1974 refusal. (Id. at 784, 792–793; n.5.)

The Code abandoned requirements that a usage be immemorial or universal; justified expectation from sufficiently regular observance sufficed. Its existence and scope ordinarily were factual questions, although interpretation of a written trade code was for the court. The evidence here went substantially beyond the minimum by showing a dominant, effectively universal local pattern. Nor did varying implementation by time or tonnage prevent recovery. Code remedies were administered to restore the performance position with the precision the facts allowed, rather than demanding mathematical certainty. Shell's smaller protected tonnages in 1970 and 1971 and Chevron's 12,000-ton protection supplied concrete points of reference; Nanakuli's growth explained a larger commitment in 1974. The panel found adequate precision to support the jury's $220,800 figure. (Id. at 792–794; nn.27–30.)

## Performance was not necessarily a gratuitous waiver

Shell held the former price through two earlier increases under the 1969 contract, for four months in 1970 and three in 1971. They were the only occasions before 1974 calling for protection. In an internal memorandum, manager Blee, who knew the negotiated arrangement, described bargaining over how long to protect existing work rather than simply declaring that none was owed. Although a single act did not establish a course of performance under the cited comment, the Code specified no mechanical minimum beyond it. Jurors could treat these two relevant episodes as performance of an understood obligation. (Id. at 785–786, 794.)

The comment's preference for waiver applied when the conduct was ambiguous and that construction was needed to preserve flexibility and avoid surprise or hardship. The jury could find these acts sufficiently clear to indicate agreement rather than waiver. Later protection episodes supported the general picture, but the complete eighth footnote expressly says they occurred under different contracts and were not themselves performance of the 1969 agreement. The opinion generally identifies them as 1977 and 1978, while footnote 31 notes possible uncertainty in the witness's year references. They should not be counted as four prior episodes under the disputed contract. (Id. at 779–780, 785–786, 794; nn.8, 31.)

## The posted-price term and a limited exception could coexist

The Code's factual agreement included commercial context as well as the paper. Performance was especially probative of the parties' meaning; dealing concerned conduct before the agreement, while performance concerned carrying it out. Express terms retained priority where reasonable reconciliation failed, followed by performance, dealing, and usage in the hierarchy the court discussed. But admitting commercial-context evidence did not require facial ambiguity or a finding that the writing was incomplete. (Id. at 794–796; nn.16, 33–35.)

The court surveyed decisions permitting commercial evidence alongside apparently conflicting price, quantity, delivery, and other provisions and distinguished stricter exclusions. It also identified safeguards: practices had to be established and sufficiently specific, fit comparable transactions, and provide an objective basis preventing a party from rewriting terms after the dispute arose. Detailed transactions unlike those supporting a supposed custom could make that custom irrelevant. The strength and relevance of this record, including Chevron's comparable long-term arrangement, supported the jury's conclusion. (Id. at 796–805; nn.36–44.)

Protection did not transfer general price-setting authority to Nanakuli or entirely displace Shell's delivery price. It operated at increases, temporarily or for specific work already committed at the former price without escalation. Shell's posted price still governed the remaining transactions. That substantial but bounded exception could reasonably be reconciled with the express term on these facts. The panel did not hold that any usage, however incompatible, automatically controls a negotiated term. (Id. at 780, 803–805.)

## Shell's notices did not conclusively withdraw an existing right

The complete opinion also analyzes a November 1970 general pricing-policy letter requiring a new firm commitment within fifteen days of a project award. The district judge apparently regarded Nanakuli's noncompliance as important, although he did not state fresh reasons when granting judgment notwithstanding the verdict. The panel explained that an award-stage commitment would leave a bid-to-award price increase unprotected; Shell could not unilaterally terminate a protection already included in the 1969 bargain. The letter promised to honor prior commitments, and Nanakuli reasonably could regard its long-term supply arrangement as one. It also described purchase-date pricing rather than that contract's delivery-date wording. (Id. at 786–788; nn.18, 20–22.)

Bohner's knowledge of the jobs supplied a further explanation for not sending repetitive formal notices. A similar November 1973 letter came after the relevant projects had already been bid, according to Nanakuli's testimony. Requests phrased courteously did not admit that protection lacked a legal foundation; the contemporary letters asked Shell to honor its previous treatment. These points preserved the jury's ability to credit reliance without conclusively resolving every disputed understanding in Nanakuli's favor. (Id. at 788–789; nn.21, 23–25.)

The trial judge also excluded substantial prior dealings and additional oral terms. The fourteenth footnote criticizes use of boilerplate waiver and merger clauses as a blanket bar to commercial context, while the fifteenth reserves whether refusing additional deposition excerpts was an abuse of discretion because that issue was unnecessary. The result rested on sufficient admitted evidence; it did not order a new trial to consider every excluded passage. (Id. at 782–783; nn.14–16.)

## Good faith concerned how the increase was implemented

The alternative theory applied the statutory duty to fix a seller-set price in good faith and merchants' reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing. A posted price normally satisfies that duty, but the normal-case qualification did not resolve every implementation. The challenge was not to the amount Shell selected. It concerned giving no advance notice and refusing protection for already committed work. Chevron raised its price to the same $76 but gave at least six weeks' notice. That directly comparable asphalt evidence could support the jury even though aggregate sellers did not face the same 1974 petroleum constraints. (Id. at 805–806.)

The panel did not generally decide whether evidence of a usage continuing after execution proves the parties' earlier justified expectation. Footnotes 7 and 17 instead upheld the evidentiary result: later asphalt evidence was relevant to 1974 fair dealing, and the limited later aggregate evidence caused no prejudice. The original distinction limits reliance on those rulings as a blanket rule for all postcontract usage evidence. (Id. at 779, 785; nn.7, 17.)

Kennedy concurred specially. Specific price protection could not arise from generalized unfairness alone; it required established custom, usage, or other objective standards of which the parties had clear notice. The largely uncontradicted local evidence permitted findings that the firms knew or should know the practice when contracting. He regarded that foundation as necessary to either theory, constraining the opinion's good-faith discussion. (Id. at 806.)

## Exact judgment and complete source

On December 21, 1981, the panel reversed the district court judgment, reinstated the $220,800 price-protection verdict with interest according to law, and remanded with directions to enter final judgment. It did not merely reopen factual proceedings. Hoffman, a senior district judge sitting by designation, wrote the principal opinion on a panel with Chief Judge Browning and Kennedy. Kennedy's special concurrence accompanied the unanimous result. Rehearing was denied February 8, 1982. (Id. at 777, 806.)
