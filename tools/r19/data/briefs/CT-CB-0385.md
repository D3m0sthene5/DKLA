# C. R. Klewin, Inc. v. Flagship Properties, Inc.
220 Conn. 569, 600 A.2d 772 (1991) | Contracts

## A construction-management agreement without a stated duration
Flagship Properties and DKM Properties, doing business together as Conn-Tech, planned a development near the University of Connecticut's Mansfield campus. The master plan included twenty industrial buildings, a 280-room hotel and convention center, and housing for 592 graduate students and professors, at an estimated total cost of $120 million. C. R. Klewin provided general construction and construction-management services. At a March 1986 dinner, their representatives discussed Klewin becoming construction manager for the project. Klewin proposed a fee of four percent of construction cost plus four percent for overhead and profit, subject to adjustment according to the timing of different phases. Flagship's representative ended the meeting with a handshake and the assurance that Klewin had the job and they had a deal. 220 Conn. at 570–571.

The parties publicized their agreement, held a videotaped press conference, and ceremonially signed an American Institute of Architects form without filling its blanks. Those facts suggested a commitment, but did not supply a detailed written project-wide agreement. No other specific terms were conclusively established in the facts presented to the Connecticut Supreme Court. The particular omission relevant here was an express time for completion. Id. at 571–572.

They separately entered a written agreement for the first phase, Celeron Square. Construction began May 4, 1987 and was completed by mid-October. By then Flagship was dissatisfied with Klewin's work and was negotiating with other contractors. In March 1988 it retained another contractor for sitework on the next phase, Celeron Square II. Klewin claimed that the original oral agreement had given it construction-management work on the entire development, rather than merely the first phase. Id. at 571–572.

## Federal litigation and the certified questions
Klewin sued in the District of Connecticut for breach of the project-wide oral agreement, quantum meruit for services anticipating later phases, and detrimental reliance on a promise to pay for preconstruction services. Flagship sought summary judgment, including on the ground that the one-year statute of frauds barred the alleged oral agreement. The federal district court agreed. It reasoned that the undertaking was not indefinite because it would end upon completion of the project, and that a project of this scope could not possibly be completed within one year. Klewin had acknowledged an intended construction period of three to ten years. Id. at 572.

The Second Circuit certified two questions of Connecticut law because it found no clear controlling precedent: whether an oral agreement that does not explicitly state a performance time is of indefinite duration and outside the one-year provision, and whether an oral agreement is unenforceable when its method of performance contemplates more than a year but its terms do not expressly exclude completion within a year. The Connecticut Supreme Court accepted the questions under its statutory certification jurisdiction. It answered the first yes and the second no. Id. at 572–573, 584.

This procedural distinction limits the result. Connecticut's court supplied state-law answers to the federal appellate court. It did not enter a damages judgment, resolve every claim, or decide whether the parties had made a sufficiently definite contract in the first place. A full-opinion footnote expressly reserves that last question. Id. at 570; n.3.

## The one-year clause and why the court reads it narrowly
General Statutes § 52-550(a)(5) required a signed writing for an agreement not to be performed within one year from its making. The issue was what “not to be performed” means: contractual terms that require performance beyond a year, or the practical improbability of faster completion. Chief Justice Peters, writing for a unanimous five-member court, chose the former. Judges Shea, Glass, Covello, and Berdon concurred without separate writings.

The opinion began with the statute's origins in the 1677 English law aimed at fraud and perjury, and discussed changes in the forms of action, evidentiary rules, and jury practice. But the court found the one-year clause especially difficult to justify on modern policy grounds. It does not measure the time between agreement and litigation, so a claim filed immediately after breach can be barred even while memories are fresh. Conversely, an agreement capable of completion within a year can be litigated much later without triggering this clause. Nor does the clause reliably distinguish substantial undertakings from minor ones: a single day's work scheduled thirteen months after formation may fall within it, whereas a year's work beginning immediately may not. Id. at 573–577.

These criticisms did not repeal the statute or permit the court to disregard an express multi-year term. They explained why it declined to extend the statute beyond a narrow reading of its language. Treating realistic duration as an independent factual inquiry would make the provision more destructive without advancing adjudication of the underlying contractual merits.

## Connecticut precedent: the agreement's terms control
The court traced a consistent restrictive approach. Russell v. Slade required the inability to perform within a year to appear from the agreement itself. Clark v. Pendleton distinguished the parties' expectation that a voyage would last eighteen months from a contractual term requiring that duration; expectation was not part of the undertaking. Appleby and Burkle likewise focused on terms that precluded full performance within the year, while Finley rejected the actual course of performance and the parties' views about probability as controlling measures. Id. at 577–579.

The legislature's repeated reenactment of materially similar wording against that decisional background supported retaining the restrictive interpretation. The court did not regard the district court's practical assessment of this project's scale as the statutory test. A finite objective, such as completing a development, is different from an express contractual duration beyond one year. An agreement can have an identifiable endpoint while leaving its timing unspecified.

The Supreme Court framed the disputed meaning of “possibly” directly. One interpretation asks whether completion within a year conflicts with the contract's terms; the other also excludes agreements whose completion is realistically impossible within that time. Connecticut adopted the first. Thus a contract without express provisions regulating its performance time is not drawn into the statute merely because the undertaking is enormous and the parties expect it to take several years. Id. at 579–580.

## Responses to Flagship and the contrary illustration
Flagship argued that the possibility of one-year performance must be reasonable rather than merely theoretical. It invoked Burkle's refusal to treat the possible death of all members of a partnership within a year as sufficient to remove the contract from the statute. Peters distinguished that decision: refusing to extend a death-contingency exception did not change the underlying rule that the one-year provision turns on the undertaking's terms. A further footnote distinguished a perpetual obligation from an indefinite one. Although Burkle had used the word “indefinite,” the contract there was understood to impose permanent or perpetual obligations. It did not establish that all contracts lacking a fixed completion date fell within the provision. Id. at 580; n.4.

The court also relied on Warner v. Texas & Pacific Railway, where parties expected a rail switch to be needed for many years but had not stipulated a term requiring that result. The agreement was outside the provision despite its expected and actual long duration. The question remained what the parties' undertaking required, not what subsequent experience or practical probability suggested. Id. at 580–582.

The full opinion acknowledged an adverse Restatement illustration, rather than pretending all authorities agreed. Illustration 4 to § 130 treats an oral promise to sell five crops of Minnesota potatoes as within the statute because five crops cannot mature in one year. That approach considers the nature of performance and realistic time even without an express term. Connecticut preferred the Restatement's Warner-based illustration and its own narrower interpretation. The contrary illustration and the Texas authority supporting it did not supply Connecticut's rule. Id. at n.9 (full-opinion footnote).

Finally, the court rejected collateral litigation about whether an unspecified completion period was realistically feasible. Such litigation would consume resources on an issue unrelated to breach, formation, or the merits and expand a statutory defense the court considered ill suited to its purported purposes. Id. at 582–583.

## Holding and exact result
For Connecticut's one-year provision, an oral contract that does not expressly prescribe a specific duration beyond one year is the functional equivalent of an indefinite-duration agreement. It lies outside that provision regardless of how long performance will actually take. The court answered the first certified question yes and the second no and taxed no costs to either party. Id. at 583–584.

Klewin therefore defeated the particular reading of the statute that depended on the project's expected three-to-ten-year completion period. The opinion did not hold that the handshake created an enforceable contract on every necessary term, that the blank signed form satisfied all writing requirements, or that Flagship owed the entire contemplated management fee. Those questions exceeded the certification. Its Contracts significance is the separation between an agreement's stated terms and expectations about performance: an oral undertaking may avoid the one-year writing requirement while still facing ordinary problems of proof, definiteness, formation, breach, and remedy.
