# Strawbridge v. Curtiss

**Course:** Civil Procedure

## Citation

Strawbridge v. Curtiss, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 267–268 (1806). Supreme Court of the United States, February Term 1806. Chief Justice Marshall delivered the Court's opinion. The report identifies no separate writing or individual vote tally. Assigned account: Civil Procedure casebook printed pp. 305–306, repository PDF pp. 176–177.

## Parties and material facts

Strawbridge and the other complainants brought a bill in chancery against Curtiss and other defendants. Some complainants were alleged to be Massachusetts citizens. The defendants were likewise described as Massachusetts citizens except Curtiss, who was alleged to be a Vermont citizen and was served with a subpoena there. Thus, the presence of one defendant from another state did not eliminate the overlap in citizenship between the opposing sides. The report does not describe the underlying transaction or equitable relief in sufficient detail to reconstruct its merits. 7 U.S. at 267.

## Procedural history

The federal Circuit Court for the District of Massachusetts dismissed the bill for want of jurisdiction. The complainants appealed. Counsel submitted the jurisdictional question without argument, and Marshall announced the Court's decision on a subsequent day. This was review of a federal court's jurisdictional dismissal, rather than removal from a state court or an adjudication of the underlying claim. Id.

## Issue

Did the statutory diversity grant support jurisdiction where parties sharing Massachusetts citizenship appeared on opposing sides, although another defendant was a Vermont citizen? Id. at 267–268.

## Rule and holding

The Court construed the statute to require that every person representing a joint interest be competent to sue, or liable to be sued, in the federal courts. It found jurisdiction unsupported in this case. The opinion expressly reserved the situation in which different parties represented several distinct interests and only some were qualified to litigate in federal court. That reservation belongs in an account of the actual opinion even though the case is conventionally taught as the origin of complete diversity. Id.

## Judgment

The decree dismissing the bill for want of jurisdiction was affirmed. The Court did not decide the merits of the complainants' claim. Id. at 268.

## Reasoning

Marshall began with the statutory language describing a suit between a citizen of the state in which suit was brought and a citizen of another state. He read that language by reference to the persons representing each distinct interest. When an interest was joint, each person concerned in it had to qualify. The out-of-state citizenship of Curtiss therefore did not, by itself, support the suit as constituted. Id. at 267.

The reasoning is statutory. The Court quoted the congressional grant and interpreted it; it did not announce that Article III itself prohibited every suit lacking complete diversity. Its express reservation also shows that the short opinion did not attempt to resolve every arrangement of multiple parties and distinct interests. Id. at 267–268.

## Separate writings and notes

The complete report contains no separate judicial writing or judicial footnote for Strawbridge. The reporter's marginal summary is distinct from Marshall's text. The next case, Gordon v. Caldcleugh, begins on page 268 and supplies no facts or holdings for this case.

The textbook explains the later complete-diversity formulation under § 1332(a): no plaintiff may share state citizenship with a defendant. It separately asks whether that requirement comes from the Constitution and whether Congress could alter it. Read that editorial account alongside the actual opinion's joint-interest language and distinct-interest reservation; do not erase the reservation or substitute the modern statute for the statutory text Marshall construed.
