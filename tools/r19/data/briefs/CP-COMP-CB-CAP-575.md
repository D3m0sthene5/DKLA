# Finley v. Kesling

**Citation:** 105 Ill. App. 3d 1, 433 N.E.2d 1112, 60 Ill. Dec. 874 (1982). **Court and date:** Illinois Appellate Court, First District, Fourth Division, March 18, 1982; No. 81-945. **Opinion:** Romiti; Johnson and Jiganti concurred without separate writings. **Course:** Civil Procedure — interstate issue preclusion and judicial estoppel. **Assigned:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 684, printed pages 1359–1360, Note 1 after Hart.

## Facts and posture

In 1954 Charles Finley incorporated an insurance brokerage business, contributing its $5,000 capitalization. For estate-planning and tax reasons related to his poor medical history, stock was put in the names of himself, his wife, and four children: 31%, 29%, and 10% for each child. Later-born children received no shares. The corporation acquired a major-league baseball franchise in 1961, sold it in 1980, and adopted liquidation and distribution plans when shareholder equity exceeded $20 million. Those amounts describe corporate history, not damages awarded in this appeal. (2–3.)

During Indiana divorce proceedings begun around 1974, Finley repeatedly testified under oath that the children owned their combined 40%. His son Paul, called by him, likewise testified to his 10% ownership; counsel's filings repeated the allocation. The Indiana decree treated only the parents' combined 60% as marital property and left their issued shares separately with them. An Indiana appellate account also described the children's shares as outside the marital estate. The children were not parties to the divorce litigation. (3–5.)

Finley then sought an Illinois declaration that he was the beneficial owner of the children's stock and that they had no right to liquidation distributions. Defendants moved to dismiss under section 48(d) of the Illinois Civil Practice Act on prior-judgment grounds. The trial court dismissed with prejudice, relying on the prior representations, the decree, and Illinois public policy. Finley appealed. He did not deny his prior statements or assert mistake or lack of knowledge; he instead invoked Indiana's mutuality requirement and Full Faith and Credit. (3–6.)

## First holding: Illinois collateral estoppel could be applied

**Did Full Faith and Credit require Illinois to import Indiana's mutuality limitation and permit this suit? The court held that it did not.** Illinois could apply its own collateral-estoppel rules, under which the action was concededly barred. (6–8, 10.)

The court described Indiana as still requiring mutuality, unlike Illinois. It doubted that Indiana would insist on that limitation under these circumstances, where the determination arose from Finley's own representations, but expressly declined to decide what Indiana would actually do. Its holding therefore did not rest on an adjudication that Indiana had abandoned mutuality. (6.)

The court accepted that a sister-state judgment must receive the validity and effect required in its originating state. It reasoned, however, that the constitutional minimum did not forbid the forum from giving an issue determination greater collateral effect through its own rules. It distinguished enforcing a judgment itself from using an earlier issue determination to prevent inconsistent relitigation. Hart supplied comparative reasoning. It also acknowledged the ordinary conflicts principle pointing to rendering-state law, but treated that principle as subject to forum public policy. These are this court's historical holdings and rationale, rather than a certification of the governing rule in every jurisdiction today. (6–8.)

## Independent alternative: judicial estoppel

**Even if Illinois could not apply its own collateral-estoppel rules, the court held that judicial estoppel independently barred Finley's claim.** This doctrine addressed his sworn testimony and successful position in litigation, rather than the interstate force of the judgment. On that characterization, neither the constitutional provision nor section 1738 prevented Illinois from applying its own rule. (8–10.)

The opinion described judicial estoppel as protecting courts against inconsistent litigation positions and protecting the integrity of sworn testimony. A party who successfully maintained one position could not later maintain its contrary. Here, Finley had persuaded the Indiana courts that the children owned 40% and that those shares were not marital property. He could not now claim beneficial ownership of the same shares. He had also made no effort to correct the Indiana courts' supposedly mistaken understanding. The result depended on the specific sworn, accepted representations; it should not be recast as an automatic prohibition on every inconsistent pleading. (8–10.)

The dismissal motion invoked section 48(d), concerning prior judgments, rather than section 48(i), concerning other affirmative defenses. The court nevertheless sustained the alternative rationale because the effect of Finley's pleadings and testimony had been fully raised below and supported the trial court's decision. It did not introduce undisclosed evidence on appeal. (10.)

## Disposition and textbook comparison

**Affirmed the dismissal with prejudice.** The court expressly sustained both Illinois collateral estoppel and the independent judicial-estoppel ground. No monetary recovery or recalculation of the divorce award was ordered. (4, 10.)

The textbook accurately identifies the stock allocation, accepted Indiana testimony, Illinois beneficial-ownership action, doubt about Indiana's mutuality position, and the two grounds for affirmance. It places Finley alongside a competing rendering-state approach and questions whether preclusive effect can be expanded. That editorial comparison should not be collapsed into a single universal rule. The neighboring Hart judgment and Rourke discussion are separate cases. (Assigned 684/1359–1360.)
