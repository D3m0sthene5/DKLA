# Pioneer Investment Services Co. v. Brunswick Associates Limited Partnership

**507 U.S. 380 (1993) · Supreme Court of the United States · March 24, 1993**  
**Course:** Civil Procedure  
**Assigned accounts:** CivPro, physical PDF page 627, printed page 1242, Note 5; additional quotation and discussion within *Briones*, physical pages 596–597, printed pages 1177–1178.

Justice White wrote for Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, and Kennedy. Justice O’Connor dissented, joined by Justices Scalia, Souter, and Thomas. The Court **affirmed, 5–4**, holding that negligent or inadvertent delay can fall within “excusable neglect” under Bankruptcy Rule 9006(b)(1). Whether the neglect is excusable requires an equitable assessment of all relevant circumstances. The particular late claims were allowed, although the creditors were accountable for their lawyer’s omissions. (381, 395–399.)

## Facts and deadlines

Pioneer filed a voluntary Chapter 11 petition on April 12, 1989, in the Eastern District of Tennessee. Its schedules treated the respondent creditors’ claims as contingent, unliquidated, or disputed, except that one partnership’s claim was not scheduled at all. Those circumstances required the creditors to file proofs of claim by the court’s bar date to participate on those claims. (382–383 & n.1.)

The next day, the court mailed a notice principally announcing a May 5 creditors’ meeting. The notice also stated that certain creditors must file proofs and that the bar date was August 3, 1989. Mark Berlin, president of the corporate general partners of the respondent partnerships, received and read the notice and attended the meeting. In June, the creditors retained experienced bankruptcy lawyer Marc Richards. Berlin stated that he supplied the entire case file, including the notice, and asked about deadlines. According to his affidavit, Richards assured him that no bar date had been set and that filing was not urgent. Both attended another creditors’ meeting in June. (383–384.)

No proof was filed by August 3. The creditors filed on August 23, twenty days late, and sought permission under the rule’s excusable-neglect provision. Richards explained that withdrawal from his law firm on July 31 caused substantial professional disruption and left him without his copy of the case file until mid-August. (384.)

## The differing rulings below

The bankruptcy court initially treated excusable neglect as limited to circumstances outside the filer’s reasonable control and denied relief. The district court required a broader analysis and remanded. On reconsideration, the bankruptcy court found no debtor prejudice, no adverse administrative effect from the twenty-day delay, and good faith by the creditors and counsel. It nevertheless found the delay within their control, described counsel as negligent and somewhat indifferent, and placed weight on Berlin’s sophistication and actual knowledge of the deadline. It again denied relief, and the district court affirmed. (384–386.)

The Sixth Circuit reversed. It reasoned that the clients had consulted counsel about the deadlines and should not be penalized for his error. It also found the placement of a critical bar date in a creditors’-meeting notice unusually inconspicuous and ambiguous. The Supreme Court agreed with the Sixth Circuit’s ultimate result but rejected its attempt to separate the clients from their lawyer’s responsibility. (386–387, 396–399.)

## What counts as neglect

Pioneer argued that certainty and finality required a rule excluding delays caused by a party’s own fault. The majority responded that “neglect” ordinarily encompasses carelessness, as well as some omissions without fault. A categorical rule limited to outside forces would disregard that ordinary meaning. The rule could therefore reach inadvertence, mistake, or negligence; that did **not** mean that every such omission was excusable. (387–388, 392, 395.)

The bankruptcy setting reinforced that reading. Chapter 11 aims at rehabilitation and avoiding unnecessary forfeiture, and its claims process permits broad equitable management. The rule’s predecessor and accompanying historical notes likewise supported enlargement in accordance with the circumstances. The majority carefully distinguished Chapter 7 claim deadlines, which were expressly excepted from this general excusable-neglect enlargement provision. The case did not announce an unrestricted power to extend every bankruptcy deadline. (389–391 & n.4.)

The Court also compared provisions in the Civil Rules. Rule 6(b)’s similar language allowed some inadvertent delays, although ignorance of rules and mistakes interpreting them usually did not establish an excuse. Its discussion of then-existing counterclaim and judgment-relief provisions supplied additional analogies. In the Rule 60(b) comparison, it emphasized that subsections (1) and (6) are mutually exclusive: a neglect-based request could not evade subsection (1)’s one-year limit by being relabeled under subsection (6). Those comparisons supported interpretation of the bankruptcy provision; the case’s actual disposition concerned late Chapter 11 proofs of claim. (391–395 & nn.5–12.)

## The equitable test and responsibility for counsel

The majority identified four principal considerations: danger of prejudice to the debtor; length of delay and its potential impact on the proceedings; the reason for delay, including whether it was within the movant’s reasonable control; and good faith. These were relevant circumstances rather than a mechanical formula under which any single favorable item automatically won relief. The Court rejected a threshold rule that would first exclude sufficiently blameworthy neglect before considering other equities. (395–396 & nn.13–14.)

Critically, responsibility included counsel’s conduct. A represented party could not avoid the acts and omissions of its freely selected agent merely by showing that it had asked questions or tried to supervise the lawyer. The inquiry was whether the neglect of **both the creditors and their counsel** was excusable. The Court relied on the representative-litigation principle explained in *Link* and *Boyle*. It therefore disapproved the Sixth Circuit’s reasoning that the creditors should not bear their attorney’s error. (396–397.)

## Application and judgment

The findings of good faith, absence of prejudice, and absence of administrative disruption were not challenged. The debtor’s later reorganization plan also took the creditors’ claims into account. Those findings strongly favored permission to file. The Court gave little weight to counsel’s professional upheaval as an excuse. It instead regarded the unusual notice as significant: a bar date would ordinarily be prominent and explained, whereas this one appeared within a meeting notice without an adequate account of its significance. (397–398 & n.15.)

The majority acknowledged that counsel had been remiss. But, in the combined circumstances, the unusual notice warranted an excusable-neglect finding. Had there been evidence of prejudice, disruption, or bad faith, the Court said it could not have found the bankruptcy court’s refusal an abuse of discretion. It affirmed the Sixth Circuit’s judgment permitting the tardy claims. This was a fact-specific application, not immunity for lawyer negligence or an endorsement of law-office disruption as independently sufficient. (398–399.)

## O’Connor’s dissent

O’Connor read the rule as containing two sequential decisions. First, the missed deadline must result from neglect whose cause and degree of culpability make it excusable. Only then could the court exercise discretion in light of the consequences and equities. Under the majority’s approach, she thought good consequences could make an otherwise inexcusable omission acceptable and render the word “excusable” largely circular. She also criticized the uncertainty and litigation costs of open-ended balancing. (399–405, 408–409.)

The dissent did not insist on resolving whether **all** negligence was inexcusable. It considered the actual finding of some indifference sufficient to defeat relief here. Both the client and lawyer had notice, and the bankruptcy court had not found that the unorthodox notice caused the failure. If that causal question remained uncertain, O’Connor thought the answer should be further factual findings rather than a Supreme Court inference. She agreed that clients bore responsibility for their attorney but would have upheld denial on this record. (405–409.)
