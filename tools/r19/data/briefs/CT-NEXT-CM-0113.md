# Hassler v. Circle C Resources
2022 WY 28, 505 P.3d 169 (Wyo. Feb. 25, 2022) | Contracts

## Care relationship and restriction

Circle C provided day and residential habilitation for disabled clients in Natrona and Converse Counties and held authorization to operate in five additional Wyoming counties. It hired nursing assistant Charlene Hassler in March 2015 to care for a long-term, nonverbal adult client in Hassler’s own home. After about a month’s training, the client moved in. Hassler provided extensive daily personal care and transportation to Circle C’s Casper day facility. The opinion reports $121,142.10 in annual Medicaid-waiver residential payments to Circle C and about $26,400 in annual pay to Hassler, describing the difference as $94,742.10 annual net profit. ¶¶3–5.

At hiring, Hassler signed a confidentiality and noncompetition agreement. Its Section 2 prohibited broadly defined competition, similar services, or services to Circle C customers during employment and for twenty-four months afterward. It covered areas where Circle C marketed services, expressly including at least seven counties. A separate paragraph barred client solicitation for the same period. Paragraph C declared the restrictions negotiated and reasonable and directed substitution of the maximum restrictions a court later deemed reasonable if any term failed. Those declarations could not themselves establish legal reasonableness or obligate a court to draft a replacement bargain. ¶4, ¶25.

## Departure, demands, and trial-court judgment

The client’s mother and guardian became dissatisfied with Circle C’s day program and selected another provider. In January 2017, the case manager notified Circle C that both the client and Hassler were leaving. The client remained in Hassler’s home, with residential services initially billed through another provider while Hassler sought her own Medicaid number. In May, Circle C demanded that she stop soliciting and serving the client for at least twelve months or face suit. She obtained a Medicaid number that July but stopped paid services from August 2017 until August 2018. Her husband and daughter supplied most paid care during that period, while she sometimes helped. ¶¶6–7 & n.1.

Circle C sued for damages in July 2019. On cross-motions for summary judgment, the district court reduced the restriction from twenty-four to twelve months and from seven counties to Natrona and Converse, found a breach of that modified covenant, and awarded $94,742.10 in projected profit. Hassler appealed enforceability and causation. The Supreme Court requested supplemental briefing on whether courts should continue judicial modification. Although unreasonableness of duration and geography had not been the original basis of her summary-judgment motion, the supplemental proceedings supplied notice and an opportunity to respond, permitting relief on the newly dispositive ground under Rule 56. ¶¶8–11, 30.

## Restrictive covenants and the change in precedent

The court balanced freedom of contract against free competition and an employee’s freedom to work. An employer could protect legitimate interests from unfair competition, but not eliminate ordinary competition. A noncompete was prima facie invalid; the employer bore the burden of showing a written employment covenant supported by reasonable consideration, reasonable time and geography, and consistency with public policy. ¶¶12–15.

The opinion distinguished invalidating an unreasonable covenant, deleting severable language while preserving grammatical coherence, and substantively reforming restrictions into reasonable ones. Hopper had adopted the third, liberal blue-pencil approach. Its rationale was protection of investments in training, customer contacts, and confidential information while avoiding arbitrary invalidation. But the court had not since affirmed an application of that approach, and Skaf had already rejected extensive arbitral rewriting. A special concurrence in Skaf had suggested reconsidering the doctrine; there was no separate concurrence in this Hassler decision. ¶¶16–21.

The court concluded that liberal modification conflicted with ordinary contract principles and actually undermined its asserted goals. Employers usually drafted from a superior position. Assured judicial narrowing encouraged overbroad terms: the employer could deter mobility through the written threat and still obtain a narrower enforceable covenant if sued. Many employees and prospective employers would avoid opportunities rather than fund a challenge. The prospect of a court-created future restriction also made the actual agreement unpredictable. Parties could not enlist courts as their agents to manufacture a reasonable restraint through Paragraph C. These defects justified departing from stare decisis and overruling Hopper’s liberal blue-pencil rule. ¶¶22–29.

## Application and disposition

Circle C did not try to justify geography beyond the two counties where it actively operated, and it did not challenge the finding that twenty-four months was unreasonable. With those restrictions undisputedly unreasonable and judicial redrafting unavailable, the noncompete was void on public-policy grounds. The court did not need to decide whether Circle C proved a legitimate protectable interest or whether Hassler caused the projected loss. The remand required summary judgment for Hassler, rather than another effort to narrow the covenant. ¶¶1, 30–32 & n.2.
