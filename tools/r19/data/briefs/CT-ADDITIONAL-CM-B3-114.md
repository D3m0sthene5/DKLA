# John T. Brady & Co. v. City of Stamford
220 Conn. 432, 599 A.2d 370 (1991) | Contracts

## Court, proceedings, and outcome
The Connecticut Supreme Court released its decision on November 19, 1991. Chief Justice Peters wrote for a unanimous five-justice court, with Justices Callahan, Covello, Borden, and Berdon concurring. The case combined the contractor and surety’s action for unpaid construction compensation with the owner and redeveloper’s action for delay and defective performance. The court reversed the judgment and remanded for a new trial because extensive evidence contradicted an unambiguous settlement release and was not effectively cured by the jury instructions. It nevertheless approved the exclusion of the surety’s alternative quantum meruit claim, the restrictions on certain extras, and the trial-management ruling challenged by the contractor. (432–435, 445–453.)

## Three contracts and a prolonged construction dispute
Brady undertook construction of the Stamford Town Center’s nine-story parking garage and the shell of an adjacent shopping mall. The Urban Redevelopment Commission was the owner, and Rich-Taubman Associates would complete and operate the mall. Federal Insurance and reinsurers provided a performance bond. The amended contract price was $36,014,993, of which $33,028,201 had been paid. The city itself, although named originally, was no longer a party. (435–436 & n. 1.)

Three agreements successively governed performance. The December 19, 1978 construction contract required timely completion, set January 20, 1981 as the deadline, and provided $3,000 in liquidated damages for each day of delay. Substantial completion included a final certificate of occupancy; final payment depended on acceptance, certification, approval, and releases of claims. (436–437 & nn. 2–4.)

A September 28, 1981 settlement, negotiated when the project was 94 percent complete, addressed delay and extras. The owner promised $2,250,000; Brady immediately released preexisting claims; the owner waived liquidated damages until after January 20, 1982 and conditionally waived $800,000 in stipulated actual delay damages if substantial completion occurred by that date. Specified circumstances allowed an extension. Brady missed the date but attributed the delay to late instructions and changes. The owner disputed that explanation, paid only $1,375,000 of the settlement amount, and refused the actual-damages waiver. Financial difficulties led Brady to assign its contractual rights to the surety in March 1982. (436–438.)

The June 1, 1982 completion agreement, made when the project was 99 percent complete, required surety financing for completion by July 9, allowed specified extensions, and arranged lien releases, progress payments, and $1 million upon timely substantial completion. The owner waived delay damages for the intervening period. That completion date also passed. The owner withheld further payments, asserting unfinished or unaccepted work and unreleased liens. Brady sued in August 1982. Work continued, a temporary occupancy certificate issued in October, and Brady left in January 1983 claiming completion. The owner asserted defects and hired another contractor after the surety declined corrective work. (438–439.)

The owner and redeveloper sought $3,225,907.14 beyond the remaining contract balance for delay and deficient performance. Following a lengthy consolidated trial, the jury instead found for the contractor and surety in both actions, awarding $2,612,098.84 plus $1,456,520.49 interest. The verdict did not separate the issues through interrogatories. The owner and redeveloper appealed, and the surety and contractor cross-appealed. (435, 439–440.)

## Contract conditions and the prejudicial evidence
The owner argued that unfulfilled acceptance, certification, occupancy, and subcontractor-payment conditions required judgment as a matter of law. The court rejected that argument on this record. Viewed favorably to the verdict, the evidence permitted a finding that the owner’s conduct, including withheld payments, significantly contributed to the completion difficulties. The dispute could therefore go to a jury rather than being resolved by a directed verdict or judgment notwithstanding the verdict. The court distinguished a precedent in which contractor default was undisputed and no owner bad faith or impracticability was alleged or proved. (440–441 & n. 5.)

A different error required retrial. The settlement unambiguously released pre-September 1981 extra-work and delay claims and acknowledged that the owner had met its obligations to that date. The surety withdrew its contention that the contractor had agreed under duress. Nevertheless, for the first six weeks of trial, it introduced extensive evidence blaming the owner for the earlier delays, over repeated objections. The court eventually recognized the settlement but declined to strike the evidence or declare a mistrial. Its brief final caution months later failed to identify effectively what the jury must disregard. At least as to the surety’s case-in-chief enforcing the contractual rights, the motion to exclude should have been granted. The resulting prejudice required a new trial. (442–443 & n. 6.)

For retrial the court also cautioned against using generic fairness, common sense, or equitable estoppel to displace the parties’ three agreements. Silence cannot create estoppel without a duty to speak. Contractual intent and the actual provisions must guide the factual inquiry. The jury could decide prejudgment interest as a factual damages question, but needed meaningful guidance rather than unfettered discretion or a skeletal reference to justice. (444–445.)

## Why quantum meruit was unavailable
The surety proposed quantum meruit as an alternative measure for the value of work furnished, arguing that the owner’s payment failures were material breaches. The court upheld its exclusion. When the action was filed, the work was 99 percent complete. In these circumstances, Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 373(2) governed: an injured party that has performed all contractual duties cannot obtain restitution when only payment of a definite sum remains due from the other party. The complaint itself asserted unpaid contractual sums and extras, without a rescission or restitution count. For this remedial discussion, reserved complaints about timeliness or workmanship did not determine whether performance was complete. (445–447 & nn. 7–8.)

The court explained that restitution ordinarily prevents the breacher’s unjust enrichment and measures its gain, rather than simply compensating the injured party’s loss. But after full performance, the parties’ agreed valuation supplies the appropriate measure, avoiding judicial revaluation and difficult benefit measurement. The opinion retained a qualification for a special showing such as lack of good faith, unconscionability, or abuse of contract. It also expressly reserved whether the full-performance rules applied equally to substantial performance. Thus this decision does not announce that every project passing a universal 99-percent threshold necessarily bars restitution, or decide every substantial-performance case. Its conclusion rests on the circumstances and contractual course before it. (447–449 & nn. 9–10.)

## Remaining cross-appeals and exact disposition
The surety could not recover extras the engineer had rejected as incomplete or nonconforming where the agreed expert decision was not arbitrary or in bad faith. A separate dispute-resolution clause did not displace the inspection provision. Nor were contemporaneous cost estimates an adequate substitute for actual necessary costs where the change-order provision required costs incurred and paid and the evidence had additional infirmities. (449–451.)

The contractor also objected to limits on its separate cross-examination, objections, and closing argument. Identity of interest with the surety was established here, not presumed for all coparties. The contractor had assigned its claims, and its liability as principal in the bond action depended on the surety’s liability. The trial court allowed a particularized showing that their interests diverged, but Brady made no such showing as individual witnesses were examined. The court found no abuse or prejudice, while preserving the opportunity to demonstrate separate interests on retrial. (451–453 & nn. 11–13.)

The final disposition was reversal and remand for a new trial, not reinstatement of a fixed recovery for either side. (453.)
