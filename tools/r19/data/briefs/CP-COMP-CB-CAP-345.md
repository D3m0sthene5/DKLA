# Gratz v. Bollinger

**Civil Procedure — Class representatives, standing, and a common challenged policy.** Supreme Court of the United States, June 23, 2003. **539 U.S. 244.** Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote the Court’s opinion, joined by Justices O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. Justice Breyer concurred in the judgment, producing a 6–3 reversal. The assigned account appears in the Civil Procedure casebook, physical PDF page 427, printed page 824.

## Facts and the admissions system

Jennifer Gratz and Patrick Hamacher were white Michigan residents who sought admission to the University of Michigan’s College of Literature, Science, and the Arts as freshmen. Gratz sought entry in 1995; the University deferred and then rejected her application, and she attended its Dearborn campus instead. Hamacher sought entry in 1997, was likewise deferred and rejected, and attended Michigan State. Both were qualified for consideration under the University’s standards. Their complaint alleged that the University treated applicants less favorably because of race. Hamacher also alleged that he intended to apply to transfer if the challenged discrimination were eliminated. He did not establish that he had actually filed a transfer application. Both had graduated elsewhere by the Supreme Court’s decision. 539 U.S. at 250–251, 260–263 & n.15; id. at 282–285 (Stevens, J., dissenting).

The University’s policies evolved during the litigation. Earlier guidelines used admissions tables and supplementary categories; from 1995 through 1998, the University also protected seats for several categories, including underrepresented minorities. In 1998 it introduced a selection index with a possible 150 points. Academics accounted for up to 110; residence, alumni connections, essays, personal achievements, and other characteristics contributed additional points. African-American, Hispanic, and Native-American applicants automatically received 20 points for membership in an underrepresented minority group. A score of 100 generally guaranteed admission, making that racial bonus substantial. Applicants did not obtain an individualized assessment of what their particular experiences contributed to diversity before receiving it. Id. at 253–257, 270–274; id. at 277–278 (O’Connor, J., concurring).

In 1999, an Admissions Review Committee became an additional route to individualized consideration. Counselors could flag academically prepared applicants meeting specified index thresholds and possessing characteristics such as unusual experiences, hardship, talent, or an underrepresented background; exceptionally, they could flag applicants below the thresholds. But the committee considered only a portion of applications. Most decisions still followed index scores after the automatic allocation of points. This distinction between a general mechanical system and an exceptional discretionary review became central to the merits. Id. at 256–257, 273–274; id. at 277–280 (O’Connor, J., concurring).

## Litigation and the questions before the Court

The plaintiffs filed suit in October 1997 against the Regents and University officials, asserting equal protection, Title VI, and 42 U.S.C. § 1981 claims. They requested damages for past rejection, declaratory and injunctive relief, and an opportunity to transfer. The district court certified a Rule 23(b)(2) class represented by Hamacher, comprising relevant applicants rejected under the challenged racial preferences. It separated liability from damages. On cross-motions for summary judgment, it held the 1995–1998 policies unlawful but upheld the 1999–2000 policies as a permissible effort to obtain educational diversity. The University did not seek review of the adverse ruling on its older policies. Id. at 251–252, 257–260; id. at 283–284 (Stevens, J., dissenting).

The parties appealed, but the Sixth Circuit had not entered judgment when the Supreme Court granted certiorari before judgment. The Court considered the undergraduate case alongside the distinct law-school dispute in *Grutter*. The questions here were whether the representatives could maintain the challenge to the current undergraduate policy and, if so, whether that policy’s automatic racial preference satisfied strict scrutiny. The Court did not adjudicate the amount of damages or every official’s liability: the individual officials’ qualified-immunity and the University’s Eleventh Amendment rulings were outside the requested review. Id. at 259–260 & n.10.

## Standing and class representation

The majority treated the injury as unequal competition, rather than the ultimate loss of a place the applicant would otherwise certainly have obtained. A person challenging a discriminatory barrier need not prove that removing it would guarantee success; the relevant injury is inability to compete on equal terms. Hamacher’s past rejection and stated readiness to seek transfer supplied a personal stake when the class was certified. His later graduation did not erase the certified class’s controversy. Id. at 260–263.

Justice Stevens argued that a prospective transfer applicant could not represent those seeking freshman admission. The majority noted that the University had challenged standing at certification, but had not asserted that transfer and freshman admissions presented different concerns. The majority distinguished a new governmental practice presenting materially different concerns from a shared challenged policy applied through different entry routes. The transfer guidelines cross-referenced the diversity criteria used in freshman admissions. Both treated membership in an underrepresented racial group as a contribution to diversity; virtually all minimally qualified minority freshmen obtained admission through the points preference, while minimally qualified minority transfer applicants generally were admitted outright. Those mechanics could matter to narrow tailoring, but they did not defeat standing to challenge the common diversity justification. Id. at 264–267 & n.16.

The Court compared *General Telephone Co. of Southwest v. Falcon*. There, an employee’s individual promotion claim rested on intentional discrimination, while his proposed class’s hiring claims rested on different statistical proof. A bare assertion that the employer discriminated did not bridge that gap. Here, the same stated diversity rationale governed undergraduate admissions across the relevant categories, and the plaintiffs challenged its legal sufficiency. The district court could therefore treat the challenge as a common classwide issue. Class treatment also addressed the danger that individual applicants’ claims would become moot. Id. at 267–268 & n.17.

This reasoning does not establish that any injured person may attack every related policy or represent every affected group. The majority expressly acknowledged a possible tension between Article III standing and Rule 23(a) adequacy, but did not resolve whether the certification inquiry belonged more properly under one doctrine: it found the requirements satisfied here. Id. at 263 n.15. Nor did it decide a separate narrow-tailoring challenge to the transfer system. Id. at 266–267 n.16.

## The merits: a diversity interest did not validate these means

The plaintiffs argued that diversity could never justify racial preferences. The Court rejected that categorical argument for the reasons given in the companion *Grutter* decision. But recognizing a compelling interest did not eliminate the requirement of narrow tailoring. All racial classifications remained subject to strict scrutiny under the governing precedents. Id. at 268–270.

The undergraduate policy failed because its automatic 20-point preference did not assess each applicant’s individual contribution. The majority contrasted a system capable of preferring one applicant’s unusual experiences over another applicant of the same race, or an applicant’s exceptional artistic talent over both. Michigan instead awarded the same racial bonus to each minority applicant, while even extraordinary talent could earn only five points. For virtually every minimally qualified underrepresented applicant, race became decisive. Id. at 270–273 & nn.19–20.

The review committee did not cure that problem. Its individualized review was an exception, operated after the points were assigned, and was not shown to encompass a meaningful share of applicants. The University’s contention that individualized review was impractical given the application volume did not make an otherwise unconstitutional mechanism valid. Administrative difficulty did not relax strict scrutiny. Id. at 273–275 & n.21. The resulting equal-protection violation also violated Title VI and § 1981 under the principles explained in note 23. Id. at 275–276 & n.23.

## Separate opinions

Justice O’Connor emphasized the contrast with the law school’s individualized evaluation. The undergraduate committee’s operation was insufficiently documented to offset the system’s mechanical presumptions. Justice Breyer joined her concurrence except its final sentence joining the Court’s opinion. Justice Thomas joined the Court while explaining that he would categorically prohibit state racial discrimination in admissions; he also distinguished equal preferences between minority groups from the missing nonracial differentiation among individual applicants. Id. at 276–281.

Breyer concurred only in the judgment. He agreed with O’Connor’s assessment of this mechanism but also joined Part I of Ginsburg’s dissent, which distinguished government policies of inclusion from policies of exclusion. His agreement with that constitutional perspective did not lead him to dissent from the reversal. Id. at 281–282.

Justice Stevens, joined by Souter, would dismiss for lack of jurisdiction. Neither plaintiff intended to reapply as a freshman. Past rejection supported damages but not an injunction against a different future policy. A conditional, unexercised intention to transfer was speculative, and the transfer policy had different mechanics. The interests of unnamed class members could not manufacture the named representative’s own standing. Stevens accepted that later mootness might leave a properly initiated class action alive, but denied that the representative had the necessary prospective stake at the outset. Id. at 282–291 & nn.1–7.

Souter separately argued that even the majority’s standing theory permitted only the attack on diversity as a justification. Once that attack failed under *Grutter*, Hamacher had no stake in the distinct narrow-tailoring challenge to freshman admission. On the merits, Souter considered the system closer to a permissible plus-factor program than a quota: everyone competed for all places, and other characteristics could outweigh race. He regarded numerical weighting as an express version of the implicit values used in holistic review. At minimum, the thin committee record warranted further factfinding rather than invalidation. Ginsburg joined Part II of his opinion. Souter would vacate for lack of jurisdiction; if a plaintiff with proper standing presented the merits, he would affirm. Id. at 291–298 & nn.1–4.

Ginsburg, joined by Souter, would affirm on the merits. Her dissent documented continuing racial inequalities and argued that inclusion should not be equated with oppressive exclusion, while still requiring careful scrutiny of ostensibly benign preferences. No places were reserved, admitted applicants were qualified, and the record did not show undue foreclosure of others’ opportunities. She preferred transparent consideration of race to disguised substitutes; her note 11 expressly denied advocating alteration of the Constitution to accommodate university conduct. Breyer joined only Part I. Id. at 298–305 & nn.1–11.

## Judgment and assigned significance

The Court **reversed the portion of the district court’s decision granting the University summary judgment on liability and remanded**. It did not order these plaintiffs admitted, award damages, or decide a separate transfer-policy narrow-tailoring claim. Id. at 276.

The assigned paragraph illustrates congruence between a representative’s claim and those of absent members. Its description of Hamacher as “a transfer student” compresses the actual posture: he had sought freshman admission and alleged an intention to transfer. Its statement that entry and transfer criteria were the same should be read as referring to the shared diversity criteria and justification, not identical admissions mechanics. The surrounding *Falcon* comparison and *Spano* quotation are editorial context. This brief explains the 2003 judgment on its historical terms; it does not certify the present validity of that admissions framework.
