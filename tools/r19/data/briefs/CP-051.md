Parties
William Gasperini, a journalist and photographer, was the plaintiff in the Southern District of New York and
the petitioner in the Supreme Court. The Center for Humanities, Inc., which borrowed and lost his original
photographic transparencies, was the defendant and respondent. The Center conceded liability; the dispute
reaching the Supreme Court concerned the permissible damages award and the respective functions of the
federal trial and appellate courts in reviewing it. Diversity jurisdiction rested on Gasperini's California
citizenship and the Center's incorporation and principal place of business in New York. 518 U.S. at 419–420 &
n.1.
Material Facts
Between 1984 and 1991, Gasperini reported from Central America and made more than 5,000 slide
transparencies depicting armed conflict, political figures, and daily life. In 1990 he supplied 300 original
transparencies for the Center's educational video, which used 110 of them. The Center promised to return the
originals but could not locate them after finishing the project. Their loss prevented further use of the originals,
making the value of the photographic collection the central damages question. Id. at 419.
Gasperini's expert described $1,500 per lost transparency as an industry standard. The jury awarded $450,000,
explicitly multiplying that figure by all 300 slides. The appellate record also raised questions about whether
every photograph was unique and about Gasperini's actual and projected photographic earnings. He had earned
just over $10,000 from photographs during the relevant years and asserted plans for a book. These matters
affected whether the asserted industry figure accurately measured the value of this collection; liability for
losing it was already conceded. Id. at 419–421.
New York had changed its excessiveness standard in 1986. CPLR § 5501(c) required review of whether an
award materially departed from reasonable compensation, a more demanding check than the former test asking
whether it shocked the court's conscience. Although the statute expressly addressed the Appellate Division,
New York decisions applied the standard to trial judges too. Courts used awards in comparable cases to
evaluate the permissible range. The standard therefore constrained recoveries without specifying a single dollar
cap. Id. at 422–425.
Procedural History
Gasperini sued on state-law theories of breach of contract, conversion, and negligence in federal court. After
the three-day damages trial, the Center moved for a new trial under Rule 59, challenging, among other things,
the award's size. The district judge denied the motion without explaining his assessment of excessiveness.
Judgment remained $450,000. Id. at 419–420.

The Second Circuit applied the New York material-deviation standard directly. Comparing state decisions
concerning lost transparencies, it concluded that industry-standard testimony alone could not justify $1,500 for
every image. Giving Gasperini the benefit of the doubt, it allowed that amount for 50 photographs and $100
each for the remaining 250, producing $100,000. It vacated the judgment and required a new trial unless
Gasperini accepted that reduced award. This conditional reduction is a remittitur: the prevailing plaintiff may
accept the specified amount or undergo a new trial. Id. at 420–421.
The Supreme Court granted certiorari to decide the federal review standard. It agreed that the New York
damages constraint applied, but held that the district court had to apply it first, subject to deferential appellate
review. The Supreme Court did not itself calculate the proper value of the photographs. Id. at 422, 437–439.
Issue
When state law supplies a damages claim in a federal diversity action, must the federal court apply New York's
material-deviation standard for excessive verdicts? If so, may the federal court of appeals perform the same
direct reassessment as New York's Appellate Division, or do the Seventh Amendment and the federal
allocation of trial and appellate functions require another method? Id. at 426, 431–432.
Rule and Holding
New York's standard controls the permissible size of the award because it has substantive significance under
Erie. A federal district judge must apply that standard when considering a new-trial or remittitur request. The
federal court of appeals reviews the district judge's application for abuse of discretion rather than
independently undertaking the state appellate court's reassessment. That arrangement preserves the state's
damages limitation while respecting the federal division of responsibility. Id. at 426–431, 437–439.
The Seventh Amendment permits appellate abuse-of-discretion review of a district court's denial of a motion
attacking a verdict as excessive. The Court expressly approved that practice while assigning primary
responsibility for evaluating the evidence and permissible award to the trial judge. The ruling does not exempt
damages from judicial review, require federal courts to copy every state review procedure, or turn the appellate
court into a second jury. Id. at 433–438.
Judgment
The Supreme Court vacated the Second Circuit's judgment and directed that court to remand to the district
court. The trial judge was to revisit the new-trial motion under CPLR § 5501(c), using the relevant New York
decisions. The disposition neither approved the Second Circuit's $100,000 figure nor unconditionally reinstated
the jury's $450,000 award. The permissible amount remained for the prescribed proceedings. Id. at 439.
Reasoning
The Court separated the state standard's two functions. Its material-deviation test affects how much a plaintiff
can recover; its assignment of authority to the Appellate Division concerns who performs the review. Calling
the entire statute either substantive or procedural would conceal that distinction. The task was to give effect to
the recovery limitation without importing a state allocation of judicial functions inconsistent with the federal
system. Id. at 426–427.
The damages limitation implicated Erie's concerns about forum shopping and unequal administration of
state-created rights. New York deliberately tightened review in order to narrow the range of acceptable awards.
A federal court retaining a materially more permissive test could allow a substantially larger recovery on the
same state claim. Although the limitation emerged through comparison with prior decisions rather than a fixed

statutory amount, its effect resembled a damages ceiling. Diversity supplies another forum for a state right, not
a materially enlarged right to compensation. Id. at 427–431.
Outcome differences did not settle every procedural question. Drawing on Byrd, the Court recognized
countervailing federal interests in the allocation of functions within the federal judiciary. Here the relevant
concern involved the Seventh Amendment's Reexamination Clause and the relationship between trial and
appellate courts. A state appellate court's authority to reassess the award did not automatically transfer to a
federal court of appeals. Id. at 431–432.
Trial judges historically could grant new trials when verdicts were against the weight of the evidence, including
for excessive damages. They could also condition a new trial on the plaintiff's refusal to accept a remittitur. The
Seventh Amendment therefore did not prevent the district judge from applying the state damages standard
through an ordinary Rule 59 motion. In addition, New York itself applied the material-deviation standard at the
trial level, making it possible to respect state policy without reproducing its appellate procedure. Id. at 425,
433, 437–438.
The Court recognized that the historical legitimacy of appellate excessiveness review had been more doubtful.
Its earlier decisions had repeatedly reserved the question, while the courts of appeals had adopted
abuse-of-discretion review. It now approved that approach as a limited check on the trial judge's exercise of
authority. Deference matters because the trial judge has seen the witnesses and evidence in the courtroom; an
appellate court sees a written record. The standard is consequently not an invitation to substitute the appellate
judges' preferred damages figure whenever they disagree. Id. at 434–438.
Rule 59 did not, in the majority's view, prescribe a competing substantive measure of excessiveness. It
authorized the new-trial mechanism, but some body of law still had to determine the acceptable damages for
the claim. Here that body of law was New York's. The majority therefore rejected Scalia's proposed direct
collision between Rule 59 and the state rule. This scope determination was essential: a genuinely controlling,
valid Federal Rule would invoke Hanna's different analysis. Id. at 427 n.7, 437–438 n.22.
The district judge's unexplained denial gave no indication that he had tested this verdict against New York
decisions demanding evidence beyond a generalized industry figure. The uniqueness of the images and the
photographer's earnings were relevant to the inquiry. The Second Circuit had identified pertinent
considerations, but it had performed the primary assessment itself. Remand allowed the trial judge to apply the
proper standard first, after which the appellate court could review the exercise of discretion. Id. at 439.
Separate Opinions
Justice Stevens agreed that New York's standard was substantive and that federal appellate review was
constitutionally permissible. He would have affirmed the Second Circuit, because the district judge had already
had an opportunity to assess the award and the appellate court had corrected the error while favoring Gasperini
in its factual assumptions. Requiring another pass in the district court served no useful purpose in his view. Id.
at 439–441 (Stevens, J., dissenting).
Stevens also disputed the majority's suggestion that the Seventh Amendment required the particular degree of
appellate deference. Enforcing a state-law limit on damages involved correcting legal error, even when the
legal inquiry depended on facts. He found the common-law history compatible with appellate review and
considered practical deference to the trial judge more defensible than a constitutional restriction. His
disagreement about the standard's basis should not obscure his agreement that state damages law governed. Id.
at 442–448.

Justice Scalia, joined by Rehnquist and Thomas, would have reversed the Second Circuit. He read the
Reexamination Clause and the Court's early cases to prohibit appellate reconsideration of a jury's damages
assessment as against the weight of the evidence. On his historical account, the Westminster court's
consideration of a new-trial motion was part of the original proceeding, not the equivalent of a modern appeal.
Calling review an abuse-of-discretion inquiry did not remove its factual character. Id. at 448–462 (Scalia, J.,
dissenting).
Scalia independently rejected the Erie analysis. He distinguished state rules fixing compensable injuries and
amounts from a review standard specifying how closely judges supervise a jury. The latter concerned federal
judicial procedure. He argued that Rule 59 supplied a controlling federal standard and that importing the state
test disrupted the trial judge–jury relationship. He also doubted the majority's predicted outcome uniformity
because state appellate reassessment and deferential federal review would remain different. Id. at 462–469.
These objections did not command a majority.
Notes
The operative sequence is to identify the state damages limit, apply it in the district court through the federal
new-trial procedure, and reserve appellate intervention for abuse of discretion. A statement that Gasperini
simply requires state law for damages omits the central institutional accommodation. Conversely, a statement
that federal appellate procedure controls cannot justify ignoring the state's allowable-recovery standard.
Majority at 426–438.
For comparison with Hanna and Walker, the threshold issue is the scope of Rule 59. The majority regarded it
as compatible with state damages law; Scalia regarded it as controlling the disputed review standard. For
comparison with Byrd, federal interests did not require an all-or-nothing choice here because the district judge
could perform the state-law check. Those are explanations of the opinions' reasoning, not claims that every
future Erie dispute can be resolved by the same accommodation. Id. at 427 n.7, 436–438 & n.22, 468.
