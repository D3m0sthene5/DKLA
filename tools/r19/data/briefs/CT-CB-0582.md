# Hurst v. W.J. Lake & Co., Inc.
141 Or. 306, 16 P.2d 627 (Or. 1932) | Contracts | Trade meanings and judgment on the pleadings

## Protein percentages controlled the price

On March 20, 1930, Hurst agreed in writing to sell W.J. Lake 350 tons of horse-meat scraps, each ton 2,000 pounds, for $50 per ton free on board cars at Portland. Payment was due on delivery with an analysis certificate, and shipment was to occur before April 20. The specifications required a minimum 50% protein content, grinding, and packing in 100-pound sacks. A supplementary letter provided a $5-per-ton discount for scraps analyzing below 50% protein, a further month of storage at an additional 50 cents per ton if the buyer delayed collection, testing certificates at the buyer's expense, and final resolution of disputed analyses by a mutually selected referee chemist. (141 Or. at 307–308.)

Hurst delivered 349.25 tons. Lake paid $50 per ton for 180 tons exceeding 50% protein and $45 for 29.25 tons containing 48.66%. The dispute concerned 140 tons testing between 49.53% and 49.96%, for which Lake also paid $45. Hurst sought the remaining $5 per ton, a $700 balance. He alleged that both parties were established participants in the horse-meat-scrap trade, knew a prevailing usage equating contents of at least 49.5% with 50%, and used the written percentage terms with that trade meaning. Under the alleged usage, the disputed lot earned the full $50 rate. (Id. at 308–310.)

The circuit court granted Lake judgment on the pleadings. On Hurst's appeal, the allegations material to that motion had to be treated as true. The Supreme Court consequently did not receive trade testimony or make a trial finding that the usage actually existed. Its decision determined whether the pleaded usage could interpret the written terms despite their apparently precise mathematical sense. (Id. at 307–310, 317–318.)

## A trade can give ordinary words a special meaning

Justice Rossman explained that dictionaries do not exhaust the meanings used in commerce. Trades, localities, and technical communities can attach specialized senses even to ordinary words and numbers. Prior examples included bricklayers paid by a standardized unit of wall rather than an exact thousand bricks, shingles sold by conventional packs, and a locality counting a thousand rabbits as one hundred dozen. Those were illustrations from other authorities, not facts about Hurst's trade or rules requiring every number in a contract to be rounded. Understanding traders' language requires their vernacular as well as their situation. (Id. at 310–312.)

The court surveyed treatise distinctions among ordinary community usage, local or trade usage, meanings shared by the particular parties, and a meaning held by one individual alone. Its holding rested on pleaded trade terms known to both participants, rather than enforcement of an undisclosed individual meaning. Authorities recognized that usage may explain a special meaning even where the document gives an ordinary reader no reason to doubt its facial meaning. (Id. at 312–314.)

Lake invoked decisions refusing usage evidence where a contract appeared unambiguous. The court acknowledged the attractions of simplicity, protection against fabricated custom, and preservation of writings, but found them insufficient to require only the dictionary meaning. It distinguished Lake's Oregon precedents: apart from one case, the offered usage in those decisions sought to add another term or establish an incompletely recorded bargain, rather than interpret words already chosen. The remaining case involved an asserted special mutual meaning rather than trade custom. The distinction matters because Hurst offered an explanation of the stated protein terms, not an additional discount provision absent from the document. (Id. at 314–316.)

## Oregon's statutes supported the interpretation inquiry

The complete opinion, beyond the assigned excerpt, relied on Oregon Code 1930 section 9-217. Although written terms presumptively bear their ordinary meaning, the statute admitted proof of a technical, local, or otherwise special meaning when the words were used and understood that way in the particular transaction. Oregon cases had interpreted trade expressions under that principle. California authorities applying a similar statute permitted proof that a gross ton meant 2,240 pounds in a petroleum transaction despite a general statutory definition of a ton as twenty hundredweight. (Id. at 316–317.)

Lake also pointed to section 9-226(12), allowing usage to explain an otherwise unclear contractual character while confining it to interpretation. The court read the statutory provisions together and concluded that traders employing trade terms could prove both their use and their meaning. The existence of a familiar ordinary meaning did not eliminate the statutory opportunity to show an established commercial one. This was a historical Oregon statutory and common-law decision; the later UCC discussion in the casebook is editorial, not the court's basis for judgment. (Id. at 317.)

## Using the terms did not itself disclaim their trade sense

Lake's final argument was that writing the minimum and less-than-50% phrases showed rejection of any rounding usage. On the motion's record, however, those very phrases had to be regarded as trade terms carrying the alleged special significance. Traders ordinarily use such terms in their trade sense unless they indicate a contrary intention. If the parties wished to strip the terms of that significance, they could say so or use different language. Treating the term's ordinary meaning as automatically defeating its specialized meaning would make every trade term contradict itself. Nothing else in this contract excluded the alleged usage. (Id. at 317–318.)

The court thus permitted Hurst to pursue proof of the pleaded meaning. It did not finally award him $700, decide that every protein test of 49.5% legally equals 50%, or excuse a shortfall in the 48.66% lot. The latter lot remained outside the asserted full-price category, and the motion posture limited the decision to the sufficiency and interpretive relevance of his allegations. (Id. at 308–310, 318.)

## Disposition and complete source

On December 6, 1932, the Oregon Supreme Court reversed the circuit court's judgment on the pleadings. Rossman was joined by Brown, Belt, Campbell, and Kelly. Rand concurred in the result; Chief Justice Bean dissented. No separate opinion accompanies either notation in the full report. Rehearing was denied January 10, 1933. The merits result therefore had six votes for reversal, with five joining the opinion, and one dissent. (Id. at 306, 318.)
