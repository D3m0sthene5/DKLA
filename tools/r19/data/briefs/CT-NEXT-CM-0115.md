# Balla v. Gambro, Inc.
145 Ill. 2d 492, 584 N.E.2d 104 (1991) | Contracts

## Facts and proceedings
Gambro distributed kidney dialysis equipment manufactured by its German affiliate. Roger Balla, an Illinois lawyer, joined it in March 1980 as director of administration, responsible for legal matters, personnel, and regulatory compliance. He supervised the regulatory affairs manager and assumed that position’s duties in August 1983. The earlier manager was not a lawyer, and the position’s qualifications did not require legal training. Balla nevertheless continued serving as general counsel, with overlapping responsibility for assuring compliance with law. 145 Ill. 2d at 494–496.

In July 1985 the German affiliate advised that it was shipping dialyzers whose clearances differed from the package insert. Its letter described risks of ineffective treatment and worsening patients’ conditions. Balla advised Gambro’s president to reject them for noncompliance with FDA regulations. The president initially did so, but a week later agreed to accept them for sale to a purchaser concerned principally with price. Balla maintained that he learned this through other employees and told the president in August that he would do whatever was necessary to stop the sale. Gambro discharged him September 4; he reported the shipment to the FDA the next day. The FDA seized it and determined that the product was adulterated under the federal act. Id. at 496–497.

Balla sued for retaliatory discharge, seeking $22 million. Emotional-distress counts and the company president were dismissed earlier in the case. The circuit court granted the remaining corporate defendants summary judgment, reasoning that Balla had been applying law to facts within the attorney-client relationship and that the client could discharge its attorney. The appellate court reversed for factual inquiry into whether he obtained the information as a lay employee, whether it was privileged or the privilege waived, and whether competing policies favored disclosure. The Illinois Supreme Court reversed the appellate court and affirmed the circuit court. Id. at 497–498, 510. The $22 million was a demand, not a recovery.

## Rule and majority reasoning
Justice Clark’s majority recognized that protecting citizens’ lives and property was a clear public policy and that the alleged discharge appeared retaliatory. It nevertheless held that in-house counsel generally could not bring the common-law retaliatory-discharge tort against their employer-client. The decision expressly confined its reasoning to Balla’s status as an Illinois-licensed attorney subject to the court’s professional rules. The ordinary at-will rule has a narrow retaliatory-discharge exception, but the exception’s foundation is protection of public policy, rather than compensation for every unfair employment loss. Id. at 498–502.

The majority considered that policy adequately protected by counsel’s mandatory professional duties. It invoked the rule requiring disclosure necessary to prevent a client’s act resulting in death or serious bodily injury. Because counsel had to obey that obligation, it rejected Balla’s claim that the law forced him to choose between his job and ethical compliance. Withdrawal obligations also applied when continued representation would violate the rules or the client discharged counsel. In the majority’s view, lawyers sometimes must forgo economic gains to preserve professional integrity, and their compliance should not become the foundation of a damages claim shifting those costs to the client. Id. at 501–505.

The majority also emphasized the client’s freedom to discharge counsel and the trust necessary for candid legal consultation. A retaliatory-discharge suit could discourage employers from confiding questionable practices to counsel, undermining the informed legal advice that promotes lawful conduct. It distinguished New Jersey’s statutory whistleblower protection and Michigan’s enforcement of a just-cause contract from the Illinois common-law claim before it. Id. at 503–507. This was an assessment of competing public policies; the court did not deny that the underlying medical-safety policy existed.

Balla’s additional regulatory title did not create a material factual issue. The two job descriptions imposed equivalent compliance responsibilities, and his deposition acknowledged the legal character of determining whether the products could be sold, the absence of consciously switching roles, and his provision of advice as corporate counsel about criminal penalties. Applying law to investigated facts was legal work. The roles were inseparable on this record, notwithstanding the nonlawyer predecessor and nonlegal qualifications. Id. at 508–510.

## Dissent and disposition
Justice Freeman dissented. He rejected the assumption that mandatory ethical duties ensure compliance when the lawyer risks losing the means to support a family. A tort remedy could supply a practical incentive to obey those duties. He also disputed the prediction about chilled consultation: counsel’s central corporate role includes advising how to obey the law, and liability for retaliating against that advice should principally trouble clients determined to act unlawfully. The employer’s insistence on illegal conduct and resulting discharge, rather than counsel’s ethics alone, imposed the economic burden. Freeman distinguished a discharge from voluntary withdrawal and emphasized the professional stigma of termination. He further challenged the majority’s treatment of Parker’s statutory protection because that court also regarded the statute as recognizing a preexisting common-law policy. Id. at 510–517.

The court reversed the appellate court and affirmed summary judgment for the corporate defendants. It did not conduct a trial on retaliation or award damages. The preserved report identifies Clark’s majority opinion and Freeman’s dissent and contains no separately numbered judicial footnotes. Id. at 510, 517.
