# SEC v. China Intelligent Lighting & Electronics, Inc.
2014 WL 338817 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 30, 2014) | Civil Procedure

## Decision and assigned account

District Judge Jesse M. Furman denied the SEC's proposed email and newspaper service on two individual defendants without prejudice to a better-supported application. The ruling addressed alternative service in 13 Civ. 5079 (JMF), not the merits of the securities claims. The assigned account is Civil Procedure PDF page 158, printed page 268, Note 4. The complete four-page memorandum and order contains no judicial footnotes, separate opinions, or appendix. Pinpoints below use its original numbered pages. [Complete judicial order](https://cases.justia.com/federal/district-courts/new-york/nysdce/1%3A2013cv05079/415066/25/0.pdf).

## The enforcement action and the proposed methods

The SEC alleged fraud and other securities-law violations against China Intelligent Lighting and Electronics, Inc. (CIL), NIVS IntelliMedia Technology Group, Inc. (NIV), and two executives. Xuemei Li was CIL's CEO, president, chairman, and a 28% shareholder. Tianfu Li was NIV's CEO, chairman, and a 30% shareholder. The court recited those allegations and corporate roles to identify the defendants; it made no liability finding. Page 1.

The January 6, 2014 motion sought Rule 4(f)(3) service only on Tianfu Li and Xuemei Li, Chinese nationals residing in China. The SEC proposed publication in the *International New York Times* and email to two addresses it associated with them. The company defendants were part of the action, but this application did not request alternative service on every party. Pages 1–2.

The court accepted that resort to alternative service was appropriate in principle. The SEC had exercised due diligence in pursuing Hague Convention service, there was reason to believe that route would not effect service, and the court saw no other apparent method under American, Chinese, or international law apart from Rule 4(f)(3). The rejection therefore did not rest on a failure to attempt ordinary service. Pages 1–2.

## Treaty compatibility did not resolve constitutional adequacy

An acceptable court-directed alternative had to be both unprohibited by international agreement and consistent with due process. The court concluded that publication and email satisfied the first requirement. Following *Pecon Software*, it treated China's Article 10 objections as not barring email, while expressly acknowledging the contrary approach in *Agha v. Jacobs*. That is the historical treaty interpretation adopted in this order, rather than a claim about current international-law positions. Page 2.

The separate constitutional question was whether the proposed means were reasonably calculated, in the particular circumstances, to notify these defendants and allow them to object. The SEC had not supplied competent evidence establishing that either proposed method would likely reach them. A method's legal availability did not substitute for evidence about its expected effectiveness. Pages 2–3.

## Why the newspaper proposal lacked support

The SEC did not provide basic information about the newspaper's circulation, its circulation within China, the locations where it was distributed there, or the languages in which it appeared. Nor did it provide other circumstances connecting this publication to Tianfu Li or Xuemei Li. Without that information, the court could not evaluate whether publication was reasonably likely to notify either defendant. Pages 2–3.

This was an evidentiary deficiency, not a categorical holding that newspaper publication could never constitute adequate notice. The order invited a supported renewed application. It did not require a particular circulation threshold or announce that international reputation alone always made a newspaper sufficient. Pages 3–4.

## Why the two email addresses also lacked support

The SEC's memorandum asserted that one address had been used by CIL's auditor to communicate with Xuemei Li and that NIV had produced the other in response to a subpoena as Tianfu Li's contact information. But those assertions were unsworn, and the exhibits did not establish that emails sent to the addresses at the time of the motion would likely reach the individuals. Page 3.

For the first address, the only supporting item was an email copied to that address on March 23, 2011. Nothing in that email linked the address to Xuemei Li. Even if the address had belonged to that defendant in 2011, there was no evidence of continued use. The problem thus concerned both attribution and currency. Page 3.

For the second address, the SEC supplied a spreadsheet that appeared to list NIV executives' contact information, including a “Li Tianfu.” Even assuming it was authentic, the spreadsheet had no date or other information permitting an inference that Tianfu Li continued to use the address. The court did not find the spreadsheet fraudulent or the address definitely inactive. It found the record insufficient to establish present reliability. Page 3.

## Exact order and doctrinal significance

The court denied the Rule 4(f)(3) motion without prejudice to a new application supported by affidavits or other appropriate evidence showing that the proposed methods, or other alternatives, were reasonably calculated to give these defendants notice and an opportunity to object. It directed termination of Docket No. 22. It did not dismiss the SEC action, resolve securities liability, enter default, or permanently foreclose email or publication. Page 4.

The assigned parenthetical accurately quotes the deficiency in the undated spreadsheet. The complete ruling supplies the separate weakness in the other address, the unsupported newspaper proposal, and the court's initial acceptance of alternative service as appropriate in principle. The decision illustrates why Rule 4(f)(3) authorization requires evidence about the particular notice channel even when conventional service appears impracticable. Assigned printed 268; order pages 1–4.
