# Chaplin v. Hicks
[1911] 2 K.B. 786 (C.A.) | Contracts

## Court and result
The English Court of Appeal dismissed the defendant’s appeal, leaving intact a £100 jury award for loss of an opportunity to compete for a theatrical engagement. Vaughan Williams, Fletcher Moulton, and Farwell L.JJ. each gave reasons agreeing in that result. The report records proceedings on May 15 and 16, 1911. Its complete account extends through page 801, beyond the page 799 ending used in some reproductions. This was an affirmance of a particular jury valuation, not a direction that every disappointed competitor receive the full prize or a fixed percentage of it. (786, 788, 790–801.)

## Competition, notice, and trial
Hicks, an actor and theatrical manager, advertised a competition in November 1908. The initial plan was to select photographs for newspaper readers to vote on, with twelve winners receiving three-year engagements: four at £5 weekly, four at £4, and four at £3. Chaplin submitted her signed application and photograph on November 10. The response amounted to about six thousand photographs, prompting a revised scheme to which she assented. Approximately three hundred photographs were selected; readers in ten districts would select five candidates in each, and Hicks would choose twelve winners from those fifty. (786–787.)

Chaplin placed first in her section and became one of the fifty eligible candidates. The poll closed on January 2, 1909. On January 4, Hicks’s secretary wrote asking her to attend the Aldwych Theatre at four o’clock on January 6. The letter went to her London address, the only one supplied in the application, and arrived there on January 5. She was then performing in Dundee; forwarding brought it to her there on January 6, too late to attend in London. The other forty-nine kept their appointments, and Hicks selected twelve. Her efforts to obtain another appointment were unsuccessful. (787–788.)

At trial before Pickford J. and a common jury, the jury specifically found that Hicks had not taken reasonable means to give Chaplin an opportunity to present herself for selection. It assessed £100 damages, and the judge directed judgment for that amount after argument. Hicks sought judgment or a new trial. His appellate argument proceeded on the assumption that a contract and breach existed, but contended that only nominal damages were permissible: the loss was either too remote or impossible to assess because winning depended on contingencies and another person’s choice. Counsel for Chaplin was not called upon to answer. (788–790.)

## Vaughan Williams: direct loss may require imperfect valuation
Vaughan Williams treated remoteness and valuation as distinct questions. The very contract gave Chaplin a chance to present herself for a prize. Denying that promised opportunity, and failing to give her a later one, directly caused the kind of loss contemplated by the transaction. The damages were therefore not remote. (790–791.)

The many contingencies made precision impossible, but did not necessarily make the lost opportunity valueless or judicially unassessable. Twelve prizes among fifty selected competitors suggested an average chance of roughly one in four. That was a consideration for valuation, rather than a finding that each candidate’s individual prospects were mathematically identical. There need not be an active market for a personal, nontransferable right to make it worth something. A jury could recognize that this narrowed competitive opportunity would command value if it could be transferred and estimate the loss accordingly. (791–793.)

He acknowledged limits: some losses may depend so entirely on another person’s unrestricted choice that no assessable loss can be established, or lack the figures needed for an averages-based calculation. He did not announce that a judge must send every uncertain contract loss to a jury. His narrower conclusion was that inability to reach certainty did not eliminate the jury’s function in this case. (792–793.)

## Fletcher Moulton: the promised opportunity itself had value
Fletcher Moulton likewise rejected a rule confining every contingent contractual benefit to nominal damages. Future events ordinarily involve contingencies; that proposed rule would wrongly turn mathematical precision into a universal prerequisite. Contract damages seek to place the injured party as nearly as possible in the position performance would have produced, while limiting compensation to loss arising from the undertaking rather than extraneous consequences. Here the opportunity to be selected was the object of the contract itself. (793–795.)

Being excluded from a limited group eligible for twelve appointments was an actual injury. A contractual right to belong to such a group has pecuniary value, illustrated by his hypothetical employee promised consideration for advancement among a small group. The jury had to consider that Chaplin might not win and that her approximate chance was only one in four, but those considerations reduced the value rather than making it necessarily zero. He supplied no compulsory formula for translating them into the award. (795–797.)

He distinguished Sapwell’s nominal award because the evidence there did not establish that a promised stallion service was worth more than the fee that would have been paid. A nominal result based on deficient proof of net value did not establish a categorical rule that an opportunity involving a contingency could never support substantial damages. (797.)

## Farwell: uncertainty and third-party choice were not categorical bars
Farwell emphasized that remoteness was for the judge and assessment for the jury. Besides losing the competitive opportunity, being passed over could affect Chaplin’s professional prospects; he discussed that possible consequence without identifying an additional separately quantified reputation award. The necessary elements were present: breach, a reasonably probable consequence, and accrued loss. For this purpose he rejected a rigid distinction between a chance and a probability. (797–798.)

A very slender chance might justify only nominal damages, but Chaplin had already passed successive selections from six thousand entrants to fifty, placing first within her district. Her position was therefore more valuable and assessable than that of an original applicant. A contingency dependent on another person’s will did not itself defeat assessment. Farwell discussed Richardson’s prospective second voyage, the carrier authorities, and Sapwell, ultimately concluding that this was a proper jury question. Even a nominal assessment might have been beyond appellate interference; that did not authorize withdrawal of the present damages question from the jury or displacement of its actual £100 finding. (798–801.)
