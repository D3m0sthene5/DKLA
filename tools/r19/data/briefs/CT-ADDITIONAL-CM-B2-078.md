# Hartig Drug Co. v. Hartig
602 N.W.2d 794 (Iowa 1999) | Contracts

## Court and disposition

On November 17, 1999, the Iowa Supreme Court reversed and remanded a percentage-rent judgment against a drug-store tenant. Justice Cady wrote for the reported panel of Chief Justice McGiverin and Justices Larson, Carter, Cady, and Senior Judge Andreasen. No dissent or separate opinion was reported. The court required a revised judgment based on retained lottery commissions and postal-service compensation, rather than the full face value of lottery and stamp transactions. Rehearing was denied December 9. (602 N.W.2d at 794–795, 799.)

## Family transaction, lease, and new services

Richard Hartig acquired the family drug company's stock in 1984. Under an agreement negotiated with his brother Kenneth, Kenneth acquired the land and buildings occupied by the stores and was to lease them to the company. The Central Avenue store's lease set a $3,208.33 minimum monthly rent, subject to an increase to 2.75 percent of the business's gross sales if that percentage exceeded the base amount. The base reflected 2.75 percent of the prior year's sales. The percentage provision gave Kenneth a stake in the business's success, with annual record-inspection rights. Its broad definition included retail sales of every kind and services for customers by the tenant or other occupants. (Id. at 795–796.)

After the lease, two new services arose. In 1985 the store became a United States Postal Service substation. Stamp and service money belonged to USPS, had to be separately processed and deposited daily, and could not be commingled with the store's funds. USPS supplied equipment and paid the store $5,000 annually through 1993 and $9,000 annually thereafter to help with operating costs. Total postal transactions exceeded $2.3 million, but the store excluded both those receipts and its annual compensation from percentage rent. (Id. at 796.)

The store also began selling tickets after Iowa authorized a lottery in 1985. It received a five-percent commission on tickets and a ten-percent bonus for selling a $100,000 winning ticket. Between 1985 and 1994, its total lottery commissions including that bonus were $42,435.29, against $634,714 in total ticket sales. It stopped selling tickets in 1994. Neither the face-value sales nor the retained compensation had been included in percentage rent. (Id.)

## Litigation and issue

In 1996 the company sued to compel building repairs and maintenance. Kenneth counterclaimed for unpaid percentage rent attributable to stamp and lottery transactions. Kenneth died during the case, and his estate became a party. After the bench trial but before judgment, the estate agreed to the requested repairs; only its rent claim remained. The trial judge treated the broad reference to all retail sales as unambiguous and concluded that excluding low-profit transactions would impermissibly create an exception. The judge awarded the estate $128,075.95 plus statutory interest. The company appealed. (Id. at 797.)

The question was what this lease meant by gross sales: the entire amount handled for government-operated services, only compensation retained by the store, or neither. The Supreme Court chose retained compensation. It did not erase all additional rent attributable to those activities. (Id. at 797–799.)

## Rules and reasoning

The court generally reviewed contract interpretation and construction as questions of law. Findings supporting an interpretation based on extrinsic evidence could bind it if supported by substantial evidence; absent relevant extrinsic evidence, resolution of ambiguity remained a legal question. Mere disagreement did not establish ambiguity. The interpretive rules used to identify reasonably possible meanings also governed whether there was genuine uncertainty between two reasonable meanings. (Id. at 797.)

Intent at execution controlled. The court read ordinary language in the context of the whole agreement rather than isolating the word all. The lease predated both the postal operation and the state lottery. Even so, ordinary retail sales in a percentage-of-sales business lease did not reasonably include government receipts merely passing through the tenant. The store was a regulated state licensee or postal agent and had to turn over the proceeds. Unlike its own merchandise sales, the volume of tickets and stamps did not directly correspond to its business revenues. A reasonable tenant would not agree to percentage rent based on government-owned receipts. Applying the interpretive rules, the court found only one reasonable interpretation on this point. (Id. at 797–798.)

Another lease provision excluded collected taxes from gross sales. That showed an intent to exclude amounts the business did not retain and instead remitted to government. Lottery receipts financed public welfare, and stamp receipts financed postal services; the business resembled a collector rather than the owner of the face-value proceeds. The court used this provision and the nature of the transactions together, not a free-standing assumption that every unprofitable sale must be exempt. It also found persuasive decisions involving similar percentage leases that distinguished ticket receipts from commissions or bonuses. (Id. at 798–799.)

The contract expressly included services for customers. Hartig acknowledged that lottery and postal operations provided such services. Its actual revenues for performing them therefore belonged in gross sales: lottery compensation retained by the business and the annual USPS payment. The tenant's proposed calculation excluding all compensation was consequently incorrect too. (Id. at 799.)

## Exact result and assigned significance

The court reversed the face-value calculation and remanded for entry of judgment for additional rent based on actual lottery commissions and actual postal compensation. It did not itself state the revised dollar award. The resolved maintenance claim was not revived or decided on appeal. (Id. at 797, 799.)

The casebook's brief drafting exercise asks whether customers' ticket payments count as gross sales. The actual answer requires separating the money owed to government from money the store earns for its services, and also covers the postal operation omitted from that problem. Broad wording alone did not answer the question without the whole lease and the nature of the transactions. (C&M printed 565, PDF 258.)
