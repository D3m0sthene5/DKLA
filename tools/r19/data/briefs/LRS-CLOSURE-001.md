# Federal Election Commission v. NRA Political Victory Fund
6 F.3d 821 (D.C. Cir. 1993), as amended October 25, 1993; 513 U.S. 88 (1994) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Two distinct judgments

The D.C. Circuit’s October 22, 1993 decision held that the Federal Election Commission’s composition violated separation of powers because congressional agents served as nonvoting members. Judge Silberman wrote the opinion; Judge Wald participated, and Judge Ruth Bader Ginsburg, who had heard argument before joining the Supreme Court, did not participate in the decision. No dissent appears. The court reversed the district court’s enforcement judgment. (6 F.3d at 821–822 & designation note, 828.)

The Supreme Court granted review but dismissed the petition on December 6, 1994 for want of jurisdiction. Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote for himself and Justices O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Breyer. Justice Stevens dissented; Justice Ginsburg did not participate. The 7–1 decision concerned the FEC’s authority to petition and the Solicitor General’s late authorization. It did not affirm the constitutional reasoning on its merits. (513 U.S. at 88–90, 99–105.)

## Transactions and enforcement proceedings

In March and July 1988, the NRA Political Victory Fund sent fundraising letters to NRA members. The mailings cost $415,744.72, paid by the National Rifle Association Institute for Legislative Action. The Fund reimbursed that amount on August 1, but subsequently faced a shortfall. On October 20 the Institute returned the reimbursement to the Fund. The Fund’s resources, including those funds, financed independent expenditures supporting candidates and direct campaign contributions before the November elections. (6 F.3d at 822.)

The FEC accepted the legality of the first two transactions. FECA allowed a corporation to pay its political committee’s member-solicitation expenses, and it did not restrict the committee’s payment back to the parent corporation. The FEC treated the third transfer as a prohibited corporate contribution. The defendants argued that it should be considered with the lawful earlier transactions rather than in isolation. After unsuccessful statutory negotiations, the FEC sued the Institute for making the contribution, the Fund for accepting it, and its treasurer, Grant Wills, for facilitating it. (822.)

Both sides sought summary judgment. The defendants raised statutory interpretation, First Amendment protection, and separation-of-powers objections. The district court held that the October transfer violated the statute, deemed the structural objections nonjusticiable, rejected the First Amendment defense, imposed a civil penalty measured by investigation and litigation costs, and enjoined similar transfers. The appeal renewed those objections. The D.C. Circuit resolved the structural issue without deciding whether the contribution was prohibited or constitutionally protected. Challenges to a tribunal’s constitutional composition could precede decision of the underlying merits, and the statutory and First Amendment issues were intertwined. (823 & nn.1–2.)

## The reconstituted Commission and standing

After Buckley v. Valeo invalidated the original voting-member appointment system, Congress provided for six voting members appointed by the President with Senate consent, no more than three from one party. The Secretary of the Senate and Clerk of the House, or their designees, remained nonvoting ex officio members. Buckley had mentioned those officials without deciding whether their participation was constitutional. The defendants challenged the partisan limit, insufficient presidential control over civil enforcement, and the presence of congressional agents. (823–824.)

The FEC argued that defendants could not trace their injury to these structural features without proving that a differently constituted Commission would have treated them better. The court rejected that requirement for the independence and congressional-agent challenges. Civil sanctions supplied injury, vacating them supplied redress, and an enforcement action directly subjected the defendants to governmental authority. A litigant need not prove the unknowable counterfactual outcome of a lawful agency process. (824.)

The partisan-limit challenge was different. Nothing showed that a President wanted to appoint a fourth same-party commissioner or that the statutory limit had altered any particular nomination. The Senate could refuse confirmation for the same partisan reason. Declaring all appointments tainted would require unsupported assumptions about the President’s choices. The court therefore found this challenge nonjusticiable, leaving open a dispute concerning an actual constrained nomination or confirmed fourth same-party member. (824–825 & nn.3–4.)

## Presidential control and congressional membership

The court rejected the independent-enforcement objection in light of Morrison v. Olson and Humphrey’s Executor. Statutory silence about removal did not prove that the President lacked removal power. The court thought the FEC’s proposed implied good-cause limit likely correct but needed only the assumption that good-cause removal was available at minimum. It did not finally adjudicate the exact removal protection. (826.)

Congressional membership supplied the decisive defect. The FEC emphasized that its nonvoting officials could not chair, convene or adjourn meetings, or count toward a quorum. The court nevertheless concluded that membership could influence deliberations. Legislative statements contemplated representation of Congress’s viewpoint; even silent congressional agents could convey a tacit message. The absence of a formal vote did not erase the structural concern, just as nonvoting alternate jurors could improperly affect deliberations. (826–827.)

The court applied the anti-encroachment reasoning of Metropolitan Washington Airports Authority and Bowsher while recognizing that their congressional actors had possessed express decisionmaking powers. Congress retained ordinary channels of oversight, appropriations, authorization, and communication. The Constitution did not demand complete isolation of branches, but placing congressional agents on an executive entity crossed the legislative boundary. Note 5 also cautioned that congressional questioning about a specific pending case could itself raise due-process concerns. (827 & n.5.)

## Severability and relief to these defendants

FECA’s express severability clause allowed removal of the ex officio membership provision without destroying the remaining regulatory system or requiring new legislation. The court rejected the FEC’s de facto officer argument for withholding relief. Buckley’s prospective declaratory and injunctive posture differed from defendants raising an unconstitutional composition as a defense to this enforcement action. Constitutional invalidity could not be declared while leaving these challengers’ sanctions intact. Note 6 rejected the circular claim that the supposed inability to give relief made the issue nonjusticiable. The court reversed the district court’s judgment; it did not decide the contribution and First Amendment merits. (827–828 & n.6.)

## Supreme Court: timely filing without timely authority

The FEC filed its certiorari petition in its own name on January 18, 1994, two days before the ninety-day period expired. It neither sought nor obtained the Solicitor General’s approval before filing. After the Court requested the United States’ views on authority, the Solicitor General authorized the petition by a May 26 letter. That was more than 120 days after the January 20 deadline. The FEC maintained that its statute already supplied independent authority; the United States disagreed but relied on the later authorization. (513 U.S. at 90–91, 98.)

Under 28 U.S.C. § 518(a) and the implementing regulation, the Solicitor General ordinarily controlled Supreme Court litigation involving the United States, unless Congress supplied an exemption. The FEC’s general power to initiate, defend, or “appeal” civil enforcement actions did not expressly authorize certiorari. By contrast, the presidential public-funding statutes expressly authorized both appeals and Supreme Court certiorari petitions. One of those provisions and the general enforcement provision were enacted in the same 1974 legislation. The majority treated that contrast as intentional. The FEC’s authority in district and appellate courts was undisputed; note 1 distinguished that statutory exception from authority in the Supreme Court. (91–95 & nn.1–2.)

The majority accepted that institutional independence might justify broader litigation authority but found competing congressional policy favoring centralized Supreme Court litigation. Prior FEC appearances had not presented the jurisdictional question and did not settle it silently. Nor did the agency’s substantive enforcement expertise entitle its interpretation of its litigation authority to controlling weight. (95–97.)

## Ratification and the Supreme Court’s exact disposition

The late approval could not relate back. Under the agency-law principles applied, ratification cannot defeat intervening rights after the time for the act has expired: the principal must be able to perform the act both when initially done and when ratified. By May 26, the Solicitor General could no longer file a timely petition himself. Allowing retroactive approval would let him extend the mandatory jurisdictional ninety-day period without congressional authorization. The Court dismissed for want of jurisdiction, leaving the lower judgment in place without passing on its constitutional merits. (98–99.)

## Stevens’s dissent

Stevens read “appeal” in its natural broader sense to include discretionary appellate review. Section 518 itself used that term broadly. The FEC had once been able to invoke mandatory Supreme Court jurisdiction under FECA; he found it anomalous to deny discretionary review while permitting that route. The legislative response to Watergate, including the Solicitor General’s role in the Saturday Night Massacre, reinforced the aim of independent enforcement. Long unchallenged practice supported that understanding. (100–103 & nn.1–3.)

He viewed the public-funding provisions as copied language, insufficient evidence that Congress intentionally narrowed the general provision. Both statutes could involve presidential campaigns, weakening the majority’s policy distinction. Note 7 questioned whether the majority’s narrow approach even clearly authorized opposition briefs under the public-funding provisions. His dissent rested on independent statutory authority and did not separately endorse the majority’s ratification analysis. (103–105 & nn.4–7.)
