# Redgrave v. Boston Symphony Orchestra, Inc. — District Court
602 F. Supp. 1189 (D. Mass. 1985) | Contracts

## Court and procedural stage
Judge Robert E. Keeton issued this post-trial opinion on February 13, 1985, after a sixteen-day jury trial. Vanessa Redgrave and Vanessa Redgrave Enterprises, Ltd. sued over cancellation of Boston and New York performances of Stravinsky’s Oedipus Rex in which Redgrave was to narrate. Both plaintiffs asserted breach of contract; Redgrave individually asserted a Massachusetts Civil Rights Act claim. Several other theories had been rejected before trial in a separate 1983 opinion. This decision addresses cross-motions for judgment notwithstanding the verdict and the judgment to be entered on the tried claims. (1189, 1191.)

## Dispute and jury findings
Redgrave alleged that BSO cancelled in retaliation for her public political statements, particularly support for the Palestine Liberation Organization. BSO maintained that physical-security risks and possible disruption would impair the performances’ artistic integrity. The jury rejected the proposition that political disagreement was the primary or only reason for cancellation. It also rejected the proposition that a BSO agent had urged cancellation because of personal disagreement with her views. Those answers left further retaliatory-purpose questions unanswered under the verdict form’s instructions. They were not findings that no public controversy existed or that no harm followed cancellation. (1191–1192; Appendix 1, 1203–1204.)

The jury separately found that cancellation was not caused by circumstances beyond BSO’s reasonable control, that it caused consequential harm to Redgrave’s career, and that such harm was foreseeable within the parties’ contemplation. It assessed $100,000 for that harm. It answered no to an additional question asking whether cancellation and the press release implied that BSO’s agents held a specified opinion about the risks of presenting her. Consequently, questions asking whether they actually held that opinion and what damage a false statement of that opinion caused were not answered. (1195, 1203–1205.)

## Civil-rights claim
The Massachusetts statute required interference, or attempted interference, with protected rights through threats, intimidation, or coercion. Keeton rejected Redgrave’s retaliatory-purpose theory because its necessary factual premises had been rejected. He also held that merely acquiescing in others’ public pressure, without the relevant conduct and intent, did not constitute the statutory threats, intimidation, or coercion. Cancellation might breach a contract without satisfying that civil-rights standard. (1192.)

The judge did not decide the disputed entitlement to a jury trial on the state claim. If the jury controlled, its findings were sufficiently supported; if the judge had to find facts independently, or could treat the verdict as advisory, he made the same findings against Redgrave. He referred to a separately filed Rule 52(a) memorandum for fuller factual findings; that separate memorandum is not reproduced in this published opinion. Fundraising concerns suggested by a trustee’s testimony did not require decision because neither side had advanced that motivation as its litigated theory. Judgment was directed for BSO on the civil-rights claim. (1192–1193.)

## Contract liability and the fee
A contractual clause excused nonperformance caused by events beyond a party’s reasonable control. BSO invoked that clause. The jury’s negative answer to the relevant interrogatory defeated the excuse, and the judge found that answer supported by evidence. Once it contracted, BSO could not simply invoke its freedom to choose artists to avoid the contractual consequences of cancellation. The agreed performance fee was $31,000; after expenses Redgrave would have borne, the parties stipulated to $27,500. That sum was recoverable for breach regardless of the dispute about further damages. (1193–1195.)

The appended instructions explain the cancellation issue more fully. They required actual events rendering performance impossible or impracticable, rather than speculation alone, but permitted reasonable apprehension about future risks grounded in events that had occurred. They distinguished ordinary foreseeable protest from an unexpectedly extreme reaction and also discussed severe, faultless frustration of the parties’ mutually shared objectives. These were the instructions submitted to this jury, not findings that the excuse had been established. (1208–1210.)

## Foreseeability, causation, and sufficient evidence
The consequential-damage instructions permitted recovery for harm discussed during negotiations or otherwise sufficiently likely that a reasonably prudent party would have considered it in deciding whether to contract. The test was objective and took account of what BSO’s agents knew or should have known through reasonable care at contracting. Plaintiffs had to prove foreseeability by a preponderance and could not ask the jury to speculate. The casebook reproduces this portion of Appendix 2. (1195–1196; 1212.)

Causation required an incremental loss attributable to BSO’s cancellation. Political views might independently diminish Redgrave’s opportunities; damages could not compensate losses that would have occurred anyway. The cancellation had to influence another employer’s decision and be a but-for cause of the additional loss. Direct or circumstantial evidence could establish that connection. The instructions also barred punitive additions and required reasonable mitigation. (1196; 1210–1213.)

Keeton rejected BSO’s attacks on the evidentiary sufficiency of foreseeability and the $100,000 figure. Although the amount issue was close, Massachusetts law did not demand mathematical precision. Theodore Mann specifically described deciding against hiring Redgrave for Heartbreak House after BSO’s cancellation: if a leading arts institution could not take the perceived risk, his organization thought it should not either. The judge regarded the evidence as sufficient to show additional career harm and reasonable compensation. He nonetheless denied that compensation on a different legal ground. (1196–1198 & n. 1.)

## The communicative-link and defamation analogy
In this record, the claimed career loss necessarily depended on people learning of BSO’s cancellation, deriving a message from it, and declining to employ Redgrave because of that message. Keeton treated that communicative link as raising expression protections analogous to those in defamation law. Judicial enforcement of a state damages rule could itself constitute state action subject to constitutional constraints, even though BSO’s own private cancellation was not governmental action against Redgrave. Her interests in expression were significant, but that did not make the competing private speaker’s conduct automatically actionable. (1197–1200.)

The court predicted that both state and federal expression protections would require the plaintiff to identify an unprivileged communication within the causal chain and distinguish harm from that communication from harm caused by protected expression. It reasoned that truthful factual statements and genuinely held opinions could not supply the necessary actionable link. For a public figure such as Redgrave, it predicted that a materially false factual implication would require knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard of truth. The opinion framed these issues as unsettled predictions and analogies, not as an existing Massachusetts decision directly controlling every contract involving career loss. (1199–1201.)

Plaintiffs proposed five formulations of implied messages, including that Redgrave’s engagement had wrecked the concerts or made performance risks unacceptable. Keeton concluded that these formulations mixed opinions with factual assertions and did not isolate a materially false, actionable fact supported by evidence. The court therefore had not submitted them as a basis for damages. It left unresolved the broader possibility of liability for falsely professing to hold an opinion, because the plaintiffs had not supplied a sufficiently distinct theory and the jury had rejected the particular implied-opinion premise actually submitted in question 11A. On this record, the $100,000 findings became legally irrelevant despite their evidentiary support. (1201–1203.)

## Exact disposition and later-stage boundary
Keeton directed judgment for plaintiffs for $27,500 on the contract claim and for BSO on all other claims. Because BSO had offered $31,000 under Rule 68 on November 16, 1982, and the proposed recovery was not more favorable, costs were to be assessed under that rule. Counsel had ten days to report whether they agreed on the amount. The clerk was expressly directed to withhold final judgment pending their report. Thus this opinion directed the judgment and rejected the consequential-damage award; it did not itself document completion of the subsequent final-judgment entry. (1203.)
