# Skidmore v. Swift & Co.

**323 U.S. 134 (1944) — Supreme Court of the United States, December 4, 1944.** Justice Jackson delivered the Court’s single opinion; the complete report contains no separate writing or dissent notation. Assigned in LRS November-a, physical PDF 150–152, printed 930–932.

## Facts and procedural path

Seven employees at Swift’s Fort Worth packing plant sought approximately $77,000 in overtime compensation, liquidated damages, and attorneys’ fees under the Fair Labor Standards Act. Their ordinary daytime work was undisputedly covered working time. Two maintained the fire hall and firefighting equipment; others operated elevators or provided relief for fire duties. They worked five days weekly from 7 a.m. to 3:30 p.m., excluding a half-hour lunch, and received weekly salaries. Their additional nighttime arrangement created the controversy. Under an oral agreement, they stayed in the company fire hall or within hailing distance three and a half to four nights weekly, ready to answer alarms. No fires occurred during the relevant period. Alarms were rare, generally required no more than an hour, and produced a separate payment of fifty cents, later sixty-four cents. (135–136.)

Swift furnished heated and air-conditioned accommodations, sleeping quarters, games, and a radio. The employees could sleep or amuse themselves but could not freely leave the vicinity. The stipulated facts described no required task during these periods beyond responding to an alarm. The district court accepted those facts without making a distinct factual finding about whether the arrangement made any nighttime waiting compensable. Instead, it classified the time as nonwork as a conclusion of law, reasoning that pleasurable occupations and personal chores did not constitute work. It denied the entire claim, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed. The Administrator of the Wage and Hour Division appeared as amicus in the Supreme Court urging reversal. (134–136.)

The competing positions favored broad answers about inactive time. The parties urged all-in or all-out rules, while the Administrator proposed separating sleeping and eating time from other on-call time. The case therefore presented both an employment question and a question about what weight courts should give the Administrator’s interpretation. Those questions were connected: the lower courts’ legal assumption had narrowed the factual inquiry, and administrative experience could help conduct the inquiry properly.

## Issues and holdings

Waiting is not categorically excluded from working time simply because an employee can pursue personal activities. Whether this particular arrangement constituted working time, in whole or part, required factual findings concerning the agreement and circumstances. The Court rejected the lower court’s categorical legal premise but did not decide that every nighttime hour was compensable. (136–137, 140.)

The Administrator’s rulings, interpretations, and opinions were not legally controlling on the courts. They nevertheless constituted relevant experience and informed judgment. Their persuasive weight depended on the care of consideration, soundness of reasoning, consistency with earlier and later positions, and other circumstances that made the position persuasive. An agency interpretation could deserve substantial respect without exercising authoritative control over judicial decision. (139–140.)

## Why the Court required a different inquiry

The Court’s companion decision in Armour established that the statute and judicial decisions did not categorically prevent waiting time from being working time. Skidmore explained why that conclusion did not supply an automatic formula for every job involving inactivity. The factfinder had to examine the parties’ agreement, how their conduct implemented it, the nature of the service, its relationship to the waiting, and the surrounding circumstances. An employee might have been hired to wait or might instead have waited for employment to begin. Compensation might cover waiting and active tasks together or only the tasks. Lodging could facilitate the service or form part of compensation. The inquiry was into the actual employment arrangement, including matters the parties had not expressly anticipated. (136–137.)

The recreational facilities consequently did not settle the statutory question. They made the restrictions less unpleasant but did not remove the restriction on the employees’ freedom. Conversely, being on call did not itself establish that every hour counted. The separately agreed payment for answering alarms was one circumstance distinguishing this case from Armour and requiring its own assessment. The Court emphasized that each case had to stand on its facts. (136–137, 140.)

## The Administrator’s role and proposed solution

Congress had assigned courts responsibility for determining coverage in individual suits rather than creating an administrative tribunal to decide those facts initially. At the same time, the Administrator had enforcement responsibilities, investigative powers, and experience with industry practices. Interpretative Bulletin No. 13 and informal rulings gave employers and employees practical guidance about how the public enforcement office would apply the statute. Their significance came from that accumulated experience and official responsibility. (137–138.)

The Bulletin treated inactive-duty arrangements flexibly. Its examples included workers who ordinarily had ample uninterrupted sleep and private time despite being continuously on call. Relevant considerations included freedom to pursue personal activities and the number of consecutive idle hours. Applying those general considerations here, the Administrator would exclude sleeping and eating time because the evidence showed rare interruption and adequate opportunities for activities the men would ordinarily perform privately. He would include the remaining on-call time because the record did not show that its enjoyable uses were those the employees would have chosen without the employment restriction. That was the Administrator’s proposed application, not a Supreme Court wage award or a binding division of the employees’ hours. (138–139.)

The interpretations were not products of adversarial administrative factfinding and did not bind district courts as a superior court’s pronouncement would. Nevertheless, lack of adversarial procedure did not deprive them of all value. Specialized investigations could provide a broader base of information than an individual lawsuit. The Court also considered coordination between public enforcement and private-rights litigation desirable: divergent standards required good reasons. Its resulting approach made persuasion depend on the interpretation’s actual qualities rather than either disregarding agency expertise or treating official status alone as decisive. (139–140.)

## Judgment and assigned significance

The Court reversed the Fifth Circuit and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. Although the district court had mentioned the Bulletin, its legal assumption that waiting could not be work apparently restricted the inquiry. Reversal removed that assumption and permitted the necessary findings; the Court did not itself calculate compensation, damages, or attorneys’ fees. (140.)

The assignment reproduces the administrative-weight discussion and explains the facts but omits much of the Court’s earlier factual-inquiry analysis and final remedy. Reading those portions restores the distinction between an agency’s recommendation and the judicial disposition. Skidmore was decided before the APA’s 1946 enactment; the textbook’s subsequent APA and Pittston discussion is editorial context rather than reasoning from this 1944 opinion.
