# National Petroleum Refiners Association v. Federal Trade Commission

**Course:** Legislation, Regulation & the State

**Court and date:** United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, June 27, 1973; rehearing denied August 6, 1973  
**Opinion:** Judge J. Skelly Wright for the court, with Chief Judge Bazelon and Judge Robinson; no separate opinion appears.

**Brief ID:** LRS-MAX-PETROLEUM

## Case orientation

The court held that § 6(g) of the Federal Trade Commission Act authorized the FTC to adopt substantive trade regulation rules defining the statutory prohibitions it enforced under § 5. Such rules could resolve common issues before individual enforcement proceedings while leaving complaint, hearing, findings, cease-and-desist orders, and judicial review intact. The court decided authority to promulgate rules, rather than the ultimate validity of the particular gasoline rule challenged here.

## Facts and procedural posture

The FTC adopted a rule treating failure to display octane ratings on covered gasoline pumps as an unfair method of competition and an unfair or deceptive act or practice. The requirement applied to refiners or other distributors selling to retailers when those refiners or distributors owned or leased the pumps through which the public bought motor gasoline. It required clear, conspicuous, permanent disclosure of the minimum octane rating. The rule made allowances for legally practical compliance with existing leases and excluded aviation gasoline. It specified an octane-rating methodology. (Opening discussion and note 1.)

Two trade associations and thirty-four gasoline refining companies challenged the rule. They disputed the FTC's statutory rulemaking power and raised constitutional, procedural, evidentiary, and arbitrary-and-capricious objections. The district court ruled solely on statutory power, holding that the FTC lacked authority to make such substantive rules. On the FTC's appeal, the D.C. Circuit reviewed only that ground, reversed, and remanded for consideration of the remaining challenge to the proceedings producing the rule. The appeal did not involve an adjudicated violation by one refiner, damages, or a civil penalty. (Introduction; note 2.)

## Question and holding

Did the Act's general grant of power to make rules and regulations extend to substantive rules defining unfair competitive methods and unfair or deceptive practices, or was it confined to procedural and investigatory rules? The court held that it extended to substantive rules. Sections 5 and 6(g), read together with the statute's purposes and the relevant history, permitted the FTC to clarify the statutory standard through rulemaking and then enforce it through the adjudicatory sequence that § 5 required. (Parts I and VII.)

## Statutory framework and the companies' position

Section 5 directed the FTC to prevent specified unfair practices and provided for complaints, hearings, factual findings, and cease-and-desist orders. Section 6(g) empowered it to make rules and regulations to carry out the Act's provisions, including § 5. Other parts of § 6 addressed corporate investigations and reports. The companies argued that those neighboring provisions confined § 6(g) to implementing the agency's investigatory functions. They also contended that § 5's express adjudicatory machinery excluded a separate method for defining prohibited conduct.

Historical practice reinforced their argument. For decades the FTC had not claimed binding substantive rulemaking authority, and some officials had expressly disclaimed it. Congress had later granted express rulemaking powers in particular product-labeling and consumer-protection statutes. The companies argued that those specific grants would have been unnecessary if the 1914 Act already conferred a general power. The district court also found legislative statements describing the FTC as adjudicatory rather than legislative significant. These were substantial interpretive arguments, not a dispute solely about administrative convenience. (Parts IV and VI.)

## Reasoning: integrated text and continued adjudication

The court began with statutory authorization: useful policy alone could not create powers Congress had withheld. Nevertheless, § 6(g)'s words were broad enough to support substantive rulemaking. Section 5 specified how the FTC would obtain an enforceable order; it did not expressly make individualized adjudication the exclusive method for developing the legal standard applied in that proceeding. The companies' inference from the enumeration of adjudicatory procedures depended on assuming that Congress considered and rejected every supplemental technique, an assumption the court found unreliable here.

The FTC was not bypassing those procedures. A rule would narrow the later inquiry, but the agency would still have to issue a complaint, hold a hearing, make findings, and obtain a cease-and-desist order against a particular respondent. For the octane rule, a principal later factual question would be whether the covered pumps displayed the required ratings. The common premise that nondisclosure violated the statutory standard could be resolved in rulemaking rather than repeatedly litigated against every respondent. That difference was consequential even though the formal enforcement sequence remained. (Part I; notes 6–8.)

Supreme Court decisions such as *Morton Salt* instructed courts to read the Act as an integrated whole rather than walling off § 6's investigatory powers from § 5's enforcement functions. Other decisions supported ancillary powers necessary to carry out the statutory mandate. The court recognized that these FTC precedents alone might support only procedural powers, but found no basis for imposing that restriction after considering analogous rulemaking decisions and the broader statutory background.

## Reasoning: analogous agency powers and practical fairness

Decisions involving the FCC, Federal Power Commission, and other agencies demonstrated that general rulemaking grants could authorize substantive standards even where statutes contemplated individual hearings. In *Storer Broadcasting*, for example, a general rule could narrow the issues requiring individualized adjudication, subject to an opportunity to establish grounds for waiver. The court treated these decisions as interpretive analogies, rather than as interchangeable grants of authority to every agency.

The companies tried to distinguish the FTC as a prosecutorial agency lacking the licensing and rate-setting powers of traditional regulators. The court rejected the distinction. Its broad power to influence competitive and consumer practices had a substantial regulatory effect even when exercised through adjudication. The similar enforcement structure of the NLRB, which also possessed substantive rulemaking authority, further weakened the proposed dichotomy. (Parts II and III; note 13 identifies statutory exemptions from FTC coverage.)

Rulemaking could collect information and arguments from a wider group of affected parties, provide advance notice, avoid singling out one company for the first imposition of an industry-wide obligation, and protect complying firms from competitors profiting through delayed enforcement. Octane disclosure required consideration of engine needs, consumer buying habits, costs, and alternative forms of disclosure. A general proceeding could address those common questions more efficiently and fairly than successive adjudications. But the court expressly distinguished the issue before it—whether rulemaking was authorized—from whether an agency must choose rulemaking in a particular situation. Its discussion of contemporary *Chenery*, *Wyman-Gordon*, and circuit *Bell Aerospace* decisions did not impose a general mandatory-rulemaking rule. (Parts II and IV.)

## Reasoning: legislative history, nonuse, and later statutes

The opinion's substantial appendix traced the 1914 proposals and debates. The early House bill contemplated a relatively weak investigatory commission and included rulemaking authority linked to its limited functions. After President Wilson changed his position, the Senate pursued a stronger commission with independent enforcement authority but no express rulemaking provision. The conference combined an enforcement standard with the general rulemaking provision. The court concluded that neither early bill, viewed alone, conclusively established the meaning of the final combination.

Statements disavowing legislative powers had to be understood in context. In particular, some compared the FTC with the Interstate Commerce Commission's power to prescribe future rates. The court distinguished immediately operative rate orders from the FTC's proposed rules, which would require subsequent adjudicatory enforcement and judicial review. Under the court's reading, the statements did not clearly reject using rules to clarify a statutory prohibition. The appendix recognized evidence cutting both ways and declined to treat ambiguous floor exchanges as an unmistakable exclusion of substantive rulemaking. (Part IV and Appendix, Parts I–III; all appendix notes.)

The broad concerns shared by the statute's proponents—judicial delay, inadequate expertise, uncertain standards, and inefficient enforcement—favored the court's reading when combined with the text. This was an argument about carrying out the statutory design, rather than creating a power solely because judges considered it desirable.

Long nonuse did not forfeit a power Congress had granted. Earlier official disclaimers deserved consideration but were not controlling, particularly because the question involved legal and historical interpretation within judicial competence rather than specialized economic expertise. The court undertook its own statutory construction instead of simply deferring to either the former or current FTC position. Specific later grants also did not conclusively ratify the narrow view: Congress could legislate out of caution or to end uncertainty. The opinion examined evidence that the question remained disputed during consideration of the Fair Packaging and Labeling Act. Note 40 likewise treated pending, unenacted bills as evidence of continued study, rather than completed statutory changes. (Part VI; notes 26–40.)

## Limits, safeguards, and judgment

The power recognized was bounded by the FTC Act's legal standards and subject to review of rulemaking procedures, reasoning, and statutory scope. The court also said that respondents must have some opportunity to show that unusual circumstances justified waiving a rule—for example, where its rationale did not apply or a compelling hardship existed. The appendix described this as a necessary safety valve while expressly reserving the precise circumstances and procedures for waiver. The court did not decide that a particular company was entitled to one. (Part V; Appendix III.)

The judgment reversed the district court's categorical rejection of substantive rulemaking authority and remanded for further proceedings. It did not resolve all constitutional and procedural objections, establish that the factual record supported the octane rule, or impose a penalty for its violation. The authority holding should therefore be separated from complete validation of that rule.

## Exam use and historical scope

Use this case to analyze the interaction between a general rulemaking clause and a statute's specific enforcement procedures. Ask whether a rule supplements adjudication or eliminates procedures Congress required; then examine text, statutory structure, analogous powers, historical context, and preserved opportunities for exceptional treatment. Distinguish legally binding rules from advisory announcements: both may encourage voluntary compliance, but a valid substantive rule can narrow what the agency must establish in later enforcement.

This brief states the 1973 holding. It does not certify current FTC rulemaking powers after later legislation or subsequent litigation. The assigned editorial Notes discuss later developments, but those developments are not holdings of this opinion.
