# Perez v. Mortgage Bankers Association

575 U.S. 92 (2015) | Legislation and the Regulatory State | Supreme Court of the United States | March 9, 2015

## Assigned passage and decision

The assigned paragraph at November casebook PDF 81, printed 656, describes Perez's rejection of the D.C. Circuit's Paralyzed Veterans doctrine. That doctrine required notice and comment when an agency significantly revised a definitive interpretation of its regulation. Perez held that the APA's exemption for interpretive rules also applies when those rules change. The paragraph follows discussion of Paralyzed Veterans and precedes a distinct explanation that an interpretation must remain within the underlying legislative rule. Those are separate constraints: exemption from notice and comment does not authorize an agency to rewrite a binding regulation through guidance.

Justice Sotomayor wrote the Court's opinion. Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer, and Kagan joined throughout; Justice Alito joined except Part III–B. Alito separately concurred in part and in the judgment. Scalia and Thomas each concurred in the judgment. All nine agreed to reverse, but the Court did not adopt their proposals to abandon deference to agencies' interpretations of their own regulations. 94, 107–108, 108–112, 112–133.

## Facts and chronology

The Fair Labor Standards Act generally requires overtime pay, but exempts employees employed in a bona fide administrative capacity as defined by Department of Labor regulations. The regulation contained an example concerning employees in the financial-services industry and treated their day-to-day work as relevant to the exemption. Mortgage-loan officers therefore presented a question about their actual primary work, rather than their title alone. 97–98.

The Department's Wage and Hour Division issued 1999 and 2001 opinion letters concluding that mortgage-loan officers were not within the administrative exemption. After notice and comment, the Department adopted revised regulations in 2004. Its financial-services example generally included employees in that industry depending on their day-to-day work, while expressly excluding employees whose primary duty was selling financial products. Mortgage Bankers Association requested an interpretation of the revised regulation. In 2006 the Division issued a letter concluding that mortgage-loan officers qualified for the exemption. Neither the initial letters nor that letter underwent notice and comment. 98–99.

In 2010 the Department changed position again. An Administrator's Interpretation determined that mortgage-loan officers primarily sold products and did not qualify. It withdrew the 2006 letter, criticizing its selective analysis and misleading assumptions about officers' work. The Department again did not use notice and comment. These descriptions identify the competing agency interpretations; the Supreme Court did not decide which classification of loan officers was substantively correct. 99.

## Proceedings and parties' positions

MBA challenged the 2010 interpretation under the APA on procedural and arbitrary-and-capricious grounds. Three former mortgage-loan officers, Buck, Henry, and Nickols, intervened for the Department. The district court granted summary judgment to the Department. Applying circuit precedent, it concluded that MBA had not demonstrated the substantial and justifiable reliance necessary to invoke the Paralyzed Veterans doctrine. It also rejected MBA's substantive APA challenge; MBA did not appeal that merits ruling. 99 & nn.2–3.

The D.C. Circuit reversed. It held that a significant change to a definitive regulatory interpretation required notice and comment even without a showing of reliance, noting the Department's concession that a prior, conflicting interpretation existed. It consequently vacated the 2010 interpretation. The Supreme Court granted the Department's petition and the intervenors' companion petition, consolidated them, and heard argument together. 92 n.*, 100.

The Department maintained that the APA expressly exempts interpretive rules, including their amendment or repeal. MBA argued that revising an interpretation effectively amended the legislative regulation, requiring notice and comment; it also invoked deference doctrine, earlier decisions, and concerns about disruptive changes in agency policy. 100–107.

## Holding and statutory reasoning

The Paralyzed Veterans doctrine conflicts with the APA. Section 551 defines rulemaking to include formulating, amending, and repealing rules. Section 553 generally prescribes notice and comment but expressly exempts interpretive rules, general statements of policy, and rules of agency organization, procedure, or practice, subject to its exception for notice or hearing required by another statute. Nothing in that exemption turns on whether an interpretive rule is the agency's first interpretation or replaces a prior one. The same procedures ordinarily apply to issuing and amending a particular type of rule. 95–97, 100–101.

Legislative rules have the force and effect of law; interpretive rules advise the public of the agency's construction of the statutes and rules it administers and lack that independent legal force. The Court acknowledged debate over the boundary but did not settle its general contours. The dispute came to the Court on the premise that the 2010 interpretation was interpretive. 96–97.

The APA's procedures are the maximum procedural requirements courts may impose upon agencies under Vermont Yankee. Agencies may voluntarily adopt additional procedures, and Congress may impose others, but courts cannot create an extra notice-and-comment requirement because they consider it good administrative practice. The D.C. Circuit had added just such a requirement for changes to otherwise exempt rules. 102–103.

## MBA's arguments and their rejection

MBA's amendment theory equated changing an agency's interpretation with amending the regulation's text. But an interpretation explains the agency's understanding of a regulation; it does not amend that regulation merely by interpreting it differently. Were interpretation itself amendment, an initial interpretation would also require notice and comment, despite the statutory exemption. MBA's distinction between first and subsequent interpretations lacked a basis in the APA. 103–104.

Deference under Auer did not change the statutory procedural requirement. The Court emphasized that courts ultimately decide whether an interpretation is correct and that an interpretation conflicting with the regulation receives no deference. The majority did not resolve the broader criticism of deference advanced by the separate opinions. 104 & n.4.

MBA also misread Christensen and Guernsey Memorial Hospital. Christensen concerned an interpretation inconsistent with an unambiguous regulation, rather than a procedural duty triggered by a change from prior guidance. Guernsey's statement that interpretive rules could not be used to change existing regulations likewise concerned the underlying legislative regulation, not a prior interpretive rule. The agency must remain consistent with its binding regulation, but need not retain every previous explanation of it. 104–105.

Part III–B addressed policy concerns about unpredictable changes. A change resting on contrary factual findings or disregarding serious reliance interests may require a more detailed justification than an initial policy. Those substantive review safeguards do not add notice and comment to an exempt category. The FLSA also protects employers who act in good-faith conformity with and reliance on qualifying written agency guidance, even when the guidance is later rescinded. The Court did not decide the application of that protection to particular employers or employees, and expressly reserved the more general question whether the 2010 interpretation could be applied retroactively. Alito did not join this policy discussion. 105–106 & n.5.

Finally, MBA suggested that the 2010 interpretation was actually legislative. Even assuming the argument had not been waived below, MBA had omitted it from its opposition to certiorari, contrary to the Court's Rule 15.2. The Court treated the argument as waived rather than deciding that classification on the merits. 107.

## Separate opinions

Alito joined the opinion except Part III–B and agreed that the doctrine was incompatible with the APA. He expressed concern that deference could give interpretive rules effects approaching those of legislative rules, while the boundary between the categories remained unclear. He would consider the propriety of Seminole Rock and Auer deference in a future case with full briefing and argument. His concurrence did not establish a new deference rule. 107–108.

Scalia agreed that the APA forbade the D.C. Circuit's procedural workaround. He argued that §706 assigns statutory and regulatory interpretation to courts and that courts should decide whether an agency’s interpretation of its regulation is correct without deferring to the agency. Deference could encourage agencies to issue vague binding regulations and later determine their practical meaning through exempt guidance. The courts of appeals nevertheless had to follow Supreme Court precedent rather than counteract it by disregarding §553's express exemption. Scalia proposed abandoning Auer; the Court did not do so in this decision. 108–112.

Thomas likewise agreed with the APA holding while presenting a broader constitutional critique of Seminole Rock deference. He traced its origins and expansion beyond its original context, questioned shifting judicial interpretive responsibility to executive officials, and stressed Article III's independent judgment and the judiciary's checking function. He argued that agency expertise and authorship of a regulation did not justify binding judicial acceptance of the agency's interpretation. His discussion also addressed notice, separation of powers, and the limits of congressional authority to transfer judicial power. These were grounds for reconsideration in an appropriate case, not holdings of the Court. 112–133.

Thomas's six notes qualify that argument. He reserved other considerations, including stare decisis and interpretive methods, for a future case; recognized decisions denying deference in particular circumstances; discussed historical concerns about combined powers; and reserved the constitutional relationship between lawmaking and interpretation. His remaining notes addressed administrative procedures and the intellectual history underlying expertise-based administration. The concurrence ended by calling for reconsideration while joining the statutory result. 114 n.1, 115 n.2, 117 n.3, 123 n.4, 128 n.5, 129–130 n.6, 133.

## Exact disposition and scope

The Supreme Court **reversed the D.C. Circuit's judgment**. 107. The final disposition does not expressly add a remand command. It rejected the procedural doctrine that had supported vacatur of the 2010 interpretation; it did not adjudicate loan officers' actual entitlement to overtime, the unappealed arbitrary-and-capricious challenge, or the waived claim that the interpretation was legislative.

The assigned passage should be read as the later statutory rejection of Paralyzed Veterans' notice-and-comment requirement for changed interpretations. It does not abolish the legislative/interpretive distinction, authorize contradiction of a binding regulation, or eliminate review of reasons and reliance interests. This is an account of the 2015 decision; its discussion of deference describes the framework then addressed, rather than certifying the present status of subsequent doctrine.
