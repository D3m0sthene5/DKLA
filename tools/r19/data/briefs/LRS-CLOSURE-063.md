# Gregory v. Ashcroft
501 U.S. 452 (1991), decided June 20, 1991 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and procedural history

Missouri’s constitution required all judges other than municipal judges to retire at seventy. Ellis Gregory, an associate circuit judge, and Anthony Nugent, a court of appeals judge, were appointed by the governor under the Missouri Non-Partisan Court Plan. Each subsequently remained in office through an unopposed retention election in which voters voted yes or no. Together with two other judges, they sued Governor John Ashcroft in the Eastern District of Missouri, alleging that compulsory retirement violated the Age Discrimination in Employment Act and the Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause. (455–456.)

The district court dismissed. It held that the appointed judges fell outside the ADEA’s definition of employee as appointees on the policymaking level, and that the retirement classification had a rational basis. The Eighth Circuit affirmed on both grounds. The Supreme Court granted certiorari on the statutory and constitutional questions and affirmed. (456, 473.)

## Statutory dispute

The ADEA prohibited covered employers from discharging persons at least forty because of age. Congress expressly extended the definition of employer to states and political subdivisions in 1974. But the employee definition excluded elected public officials, their chosen personal staffs, appointees on the policymaking level, and immediate advisers concerning the office’s constitutional or legal powers. Ashcroft maintained that judges were policymaking appointees and alternatively that retention elections made them elected officials. (456–457, 464–467.)

The judges argued that adjudication resolved disputes and applied law rather than making policy. They also invoked noscitur a sociis: because the policymaking exception sat between personal-staff and immediate-adviser exceptions, it should likewise require a close working relationship with the elected official. Independent judges did not work at the governor’s behest. The governor answered that Missouri common-law judges made policy, supervised inferior courts, administered procedural rules, and regulated the bar. (465–466.)

## Federalism and the plain-statement requirement

Justice O’Connor’s five-Justice statutory majority treated the qualification of important state officials as a particularly fundamental sovereign choice, beyond ordinary regulation in a field historically occupied by states. A state defined its government through its structure and the character of its officials. Federalism’s divided authority could promote responsiveness and experimentation and check abuses of power. Congress could override state policy when acting within its constitutional powers, but the Court would not infer that it had upset the usual constitutional balance from uncertain language. Its intent had to be unmistakably clear in the statute. (457–461.)

The majority drew on plain-statement principles used in sovereign-immunity and preemption decisions and on cases recognizing state authority over positions at the heart of representative government. Political-function cases allowed less demanding equal-protection scrutiny for some citizenship requirements for important public positions. O’Connor reasoned that those cases recognized constitutionally significant state authority and reinforced caution before construing a federal employment statute to displace qualifications for state judges. The Court did not hold that all such qualifications were immune from the Fourteenth Amendment; it acknowledged constitutional constraints. (461–464; 463 n. *.)

Garcia had left protection against Commerce Clause regulation primarily to the national political process. The majority viewed its interpretive rule as ensuring that Congress had actually made the state-displacing choice through that process. It also suggested that applying the rule could avoid a potential constitutional problem about federal regulation of state official qualifications. It did not adjudicate the ultimate constitutional limit on Congress’s power or overrule Garcia. (464.)

## Applying the rule to appointed judges

The majority did not require proof that judges made policy exactly as legislators or executive officers did. The statutory phrase concerned appointees at the policymaking level, and a position involving discretion on publicly important issues could satisfy it. Although that was an unusual way to refer to judges, the decisive inquiry was whether Congress had clearly included them, rather than clearly excluded them. In a statute expressly exempting most important state officials, the phrase was at least ambiguous as applied to appointed judges. That ambiguity prevented the necessary plain statement of coverage. (466–467.)

The Act did not have to name judges expressly: where an employee unambiguously fell outside every exception, the general coverage provision was sufficiently clear. Here the possible policymaking exception created the uncertainty. Having resolved the case on that basis, the Court left undecided whether a retention election made an initially appointed judge an elected official under the separate exception. (467.)

The Court also considered possible Fourteenth Amendment § 5 authority. Earlier precedent had upheld the state-employer extension under the Commerce Clause while reserving whether Congress also acted validly under § 5. The majority acknowledged that the Reconstruction Amendments expanded federal power and intruded on state sovereignty. Nonetheless, it used the political-function cases and Pennhurst’s caution against inferring an unstated § 5 purpose to conclude that this statutory ambiguity should not be resolved as an intrusion into state governmental functions under either possible source of power. It did not decide that Congress lacked power to enact unmistakable coverage. (467–470.)

## Equal protection

Seven Justices joined Parts I and III, including the equal-protection analysis. Age was not a suspect classification, and the judges claimed no fundamental right to judicial office, so rational-basis review applied. Missouri could rationally seek a competent judiciary, avoid repeated difficult individualized assessments of older judges, promote orderly opportunities for other qualified judges, and administer predictable retirement arrangements. (470–472.)

The Court did not find that every or even most judges became incapable at seventy. Rather, Missouri could reasonably address the risk of deterioration through a general classification. Voluntary retirement and impeachment could be inadequate; judges’ relatively long terms, limited public visibility, and infrequent unopposed retention elections provided less accountability than ordinary elections for other officials. Other employees’ deficiencies could be more readily observed and addressed. These differences rationally explained both the age line and the distinction between judges and other state officials or employees. Imperfect generalization did not establish irrationality. (472–473.)

## White and Stevens: same judgment, different statutory method

Justice White, joined by Justice Stevens, agreed with the result and joined the facts and equal-protection discussion, but rejected the plain-statement reasoning. Congress had already expressly subjected states to the ADEA; the remaining issue was the precise employee exception, not whether the statute reached states at all. Where valid federal law actually conflicted with state law, relative state importance could not defeat supremacy. He viewed the majority’s new requirement as inconsistent with Garcia’s political-process protection, unsupported by alienage scrutiny cases, vague in scope, and likely to burden Congress with detailed lists of covered state functions. He also objected to extending that requirement to § 5 legislation designed to overcome state sovereignty. (474–481 & n. 1.)

Ordinary interpretation, in his view, readily excluded the judges as policymakers. Common-law adjudication selected among alternatives and developed rules for future cases, and judges occupied a level comparable to other high officials. The common link among the statutory exceptions was connection to elected officials, not necessarily a close working relationship. Requiring closeness for every policymaking appointee would make that exception largely redundant. Title VII’s identical language and its legislative history supported a separate category of high-level policymakers, including judges. (481–485 & n. 2.)

White also rejected deference to the EEOC’s contrary view. He characterized it as a litigation position rather than a formal agency interpretation and considered it inconsistent with the statutory text. His note examined the administrative decision on which the dissent relied and found that it did not establish the proposed close-relationship limitation. That was his separate rationale; it should not be attributed to O’Connor’s opinion as a Court holding about the EEOC. (485–486 n. 3.)

## Blackmun and Marshall: statutory coverage and deference

Justice Blackmun, joined by Justice Marshall, agreed with White’s criticism of the plain-statement rule but disagreed about the employee exception. Statutory context tied policymakers to personal staffs and immediate advisers: appointed judges’ independence placed them outside that grouping. Legislative history, particularly the sequence of proposed amendments and the conferees’ instruction to construe the exception narrowly, supported a category like cabinet officials working closely with the appointing official rather than the judiciary. He disputed White’s reading of isolated comments about judges. (486–493 & nn. 1–3.)

Alternatively, Blackmun would have deferred under then-governing Chevron doctrine to the EEOC’s reasonable, consistent construction of the ambiguous phrase. He distinguished an agency’s convenient litigation position from the EEOC’s interpretation, which he found supported by an administrative ruling and a consistent enforcement position. He would have held the mandatory-retirement rule invalid under the ADEA and therefore did not reach the judges’ alternative equal-protection claim. These deference and coverage conclusions were dissenting positions. (493–495 & nn. 4–5.)

## Judgment and assigned scope

The Court affirmed 7–2. O’Connor’s full opinion was joined by Rehnquist, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter; White and Stevens joined Parts I and III and the judgment; Blackmun and Marshall dissented. All three writings and their judicial notes were read: the majority’s unnumbered note, White’s notes 1–3, and Blackmun’s notes 1–5, including cross-page continuations. The report’s advocacy asterisk is separate prefatory material. (454, 473–495.)
