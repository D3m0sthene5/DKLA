# LASA Per L'Industria Del Marmo Societa Per Azioni v. Alexander

**414 F.2d 143 (6th Cir. 1969) · Civil Procedure · CP-079**

The Sixth Circuit reinstated related claims arising from marble problems during construction of Memphis City Hall. The majority treated the supplier's payment claim, the subcontractor's claims against the prime contractor and architect, and the prime contractor's claim against the subcontractor as logically connected parts of the same construction controversy. Judge McAllister dissented, viewing the disputed tort claims as different in proof and legal theory from the original marble-supply contract dispute. The court reversed and remanded the dismissal orders; it did not decide which participant actually caused the problems or award damages.

The contractual chain began with the City of Memphis and prime contractor Southern Builders, whose performance and payment obligations were bonded by Continental Casualty. Southern Builders subcontracted marble supply and installation to Alexander Marble and Tile. Alexander in turn contracted with LASA, an Italian corporation, for marble. LASA alleged that it had supplied the marble as agreed and that a $127,240.80 balance remained unpaid. It sued Alexander, the prime contractor, its surety, and the city. The assigned introductory editorial account additionally identifies Marble International among the parties and gives competing asserted contract prices; those editorial details are not needed to resolve the appellate reasoning and are not treated as judicial factual findings here. (Full opinion, majority opening; dissent factual recital.)

Alexander disputed LASA's performance, alleging delayed, damaged, incorrect, and incomplete shipments and price increases obtained through pressure. It counterclaimed for overpayment and damages. Southern Builders also disputed LASA's right to payment and counterclaimed for failure to deliver marble as promised. These original claims and counterclaims remained in the district court. The appeal concerned additional claims between defendants and against the architect, rather than the mere right to litigate LASA's supply contract. (Majority discussion of surviving pleadings; dissent recital of initial pleadings.)

Alexander's crossclaim demanded $158,061.75 allegedly due under its subcontract with Southern Builders. It also sought actual and punitive damages, asserting inadequate concrete bases and metal supports, demands to install marble in cold and rainy conditions contrary to specifications, wrongful termination, replacement by a more costly subcontractor at Alexander's expense, and public blame damaging its reputation. Against architect A. L. Aydelott and his firm, Alexander alleged improper specifications and supervision, failure to secure proper prime-contractor performance, refusal to approve work estimates, pressure to terminate the subcontract, preferential modified specifications for a replacement installer, and reputational injury. The dissent's detailed recital identifies a $750,000 treble-damages demand against the architect. Each description concerns pleaded wrongdoing, not proved misconduct. (Majority descriptions of dismissed claims; dissent pleading recital.)

Southern Builders also asserted unliquidated damages against Alexander for delay, defective materials and workmanship, and failure to meet specifications. That claim was dismissed, while its claim for indemnity if LASA recovered against it survived. The architect claim, originally styled a third-party complaint, was treated by the district court as a crossclaim involving an added party under Rule 13(h); the prime contractor's claim against Alexander was likewise treated as a crossclaim. The district court dismissed the disputed additional claims because it found that they did not arise from the transaction or occurrence underlying the original action or its counterclaims. This procedural treatment matters: the appellate result should not be simplified into a holding that any independent claim against an outsider qualifies for derivative impleader under Rule 14. (Majority opening and pleading descriptions.)

Rule 13(g) permitted a crossclaim against a coparty arising from the transaction or occurrence underlying the original action or a counterclaim, or relating to property that was its subject. Rule 13(h) allowed additional parties through Rules 19 and 20. The majority read these remedial provisions broadly to facilitate decisions on the merits and avoid successive litigation over one core of facts. Its ancillary-jurisdiction discussion treated a sufficiently connected crossclaim or third-party claim as supported by jurisdiction over the original subject matter without another independent jurisdictional ground. Judicial footnote 2 reproduces the governing crossclaim and additional-party provisions; this brief addresses their historical wording and application. (Majority rules and ancillary-jurisdiction analysis; n.2.)

Applying the flexible logical-relationship meaning of transaction, the majority identified a concrete common question: who was responsible for the marble problems? The supplier, installer, prime contractor, and architect were each blamed for aspects of the same project. Different subcontracts and specifications did not prevent overlap in factual and legal issues, and some of the same evidence would be needed across the original claims, counterclaims, and crossclaims. That connection supported resolving the controversy in one action. The decision therefore rests on related marble-performance problems and overlapping proof, rather than merely on all parties having worked somewhere on the same building. (Majority logical-relationship application.)

The prospect of a confusing jury trial did not require dismissing the claims. Rule 42(b), reproduced in judicial footnote 3, authorized separate trials of claims or issues when convenience, prejudice, expedition, or economy warranted them, while preserving jury-trial rights. The court distinguished allowing claims within one action from insisting that every issue be presented simultaneously to one jury. Case management could address complexity without excluding related claims from the proceeding. (Majority final discussion; n.3.)

McAllister would have affirmed dismissal. He emphasized that Rule 13(h)'s additional-party route incorporated Rule 20's requirements of a qualifying transaction or series and a common question of law or fact. For him, LASA's claim and the counterclaims against it concerned marble delivery and payment under the supply agreement. Alexander's claims against Southern Builders and Aydelott instead concerned obstruction, termination, malicious injury to reputation, and interference with another contract. He viewed those claims as torts requiring different evidence and legal analysis from the contractual performance issues. The common construction setting did not supply the necessary connection, and the evidence resolving the original contract dispute would not resolve the separate alleged malicious conduct. (Dissent Rule 20 and proof-comparison analysis.)

The majority reversed and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. Judge Phillips wrote, joined by Judge Celebrezze; Senior Judge McAllister dissented. The complete majority and full dissent, together with all three judicial footnotes, were read. Footnote 1 supplies the source for the opening observation about ancillary-jurisdiction confusion; it creates no additional rule. The entire assigned principal passage and all three scans were checked, including the distinction between bracketed editorial narrative and actual opinion. The case teaches a broad, concrete connection test for crossclaims and added parties, while preserving the separate questions of claim joinder, jurisdiction, and trial management. It is not a merits judgment on the competing accusations, nor a current-law certification of every historical ancillary-jurisdiction formulation.
