# Goodyear Tire & Rubber Co. v. Haeger

**Civil Procedure — Compensatory limits on inherent-power fee sanctions.** Supreme Court of the United States, April 18, 2017. **581 U.S. 101; 137 S. Ct. 1178.** Justice Kagan wrote for all eight participating Justices; Justice Gorsuch did not participate. The assigned account is Note 4 in the Civil Procedure casebook, physical PDF pages 499–500, printed pages 976–977.

## The accident, discovery, and settlement

Leroy, Donna, Barry, and Suzanne Haeger sued Goodyear after their motorhome swerved off the road and overturned. They alleged that a Goodyear G159 tire failed because it was not designed to withstand the heat generated by motorhome use at highway speeds. They also sued the motorhome and chassis manufacturers. The accident’s alleged cause was the subject of litigation, not an established finding in the Supreme Court’s sanctions decision. 581 U.S. at 104 & n.1.

The Haegers repeatedly requested Goodyear’s internal G159 testing data. Discovery continued for years, with slow and uninformative responses and disputes requiring judicial intervention. The parties eventually settled shortly before trial for an undisclosed sum. Months later, the Haegers’ lawyer learned from a newspaper account that Goodyear had disclosed relevant heat tests in another lawsuit. Those tests showed unusual heating between 55 and 65 miles per hour. Goodyear then acknowledged that it had withheld them here despite repeated requests for all testing data. Id. at 104–105.

The Haegers sought sanctions for discovery fraud. The district court found a years-long course of bad-faith conduct by Goodyear and its lawyers intended to frustrate resolution on the merits. Goodyear did not contest that assessment in the Supreme Court. Because the underlying action had settled, the court could not enter the default judgment it otherwise wanted to impose. It instead used its inherent authority to award litigation fees and costs. Id. at 105 & n.2.

## The two awards and the appeal

The district court awarded approximately **$2.7 million**, covering all fees and costs from Goodyear’s first dishonest discovery response onward. It recognized that fees normally must be caused by the misconduct but believed particularly egregious behavior justified recovery even of expenses lacking that connection. It also thought timely disclosure probably would have produced a substantially earlier settlement, though it expressed uncertainty. Id. at 105–106.

The court made a contingent **$2 million** award in case appellate review required a causal link. Using estimates supplied by Goodyear, it subtracted approximately $700,000 for expenses related to other defendants and the Haegers’ medical damages. The Ninth Circuit affirmed the larger award, reasoning that fees incurred while Goodyear acted in bad faith could be shifted. Judge Watford dissented: temporal overlap did not establish causation, and Goodyear retained plausible defenses even if the tests had been disclosed. Id. at 106–107.

The Supreme Court reviewed whether a federal court imposing a fee sanction through civil procedures could shift expenses beyond those caused by the misconduct. The parties largely agreed on the correct general rule; their principal dispute concerned whether the lower courts had applied it and whether either award could nonetheless stand. Id. at 111–112.

## Rule: compensation requires but-for causation

Federal courts have inherent authority to manage litigation and sanction bad-faith abuse of the judicial process. Reimbursement of the opposing party’s legal expenses is one permissible sanction. But when a court uses ordinary civil procedures, that award must compensate the injured litigant rather than punish the offender. Punishment requires the procedural protections applicable to criminal proceedings, including an appropriate standard of proof. Coercive sanctions intended to secure compliance were not at issue. Id. at 107–108 & n.4.

Compensation therefore follows the injury actually caused: the innocent party may recover fees it would **not have incurred but for** the misconduct. If the same deposition, motion, or other work would have been necessary even with proper discovery, shifting its cost would add punishment rather than repair incremental harm. The Court drew this framework from *Fox v. Vice* and noted that rule-based and statutory sanctions also connect recoverable expenses to the offending conduct. Id. at 108–110 & n.5.

This test does not require perfect accounting. A trial judge may estimate time, allocate percentages, or treat a category of expenditures as caused entirely by misconduct, using familiarity with the litigation. Appellate review owes substantial deference to those informed judgments. But practical flexibility concerns calculation of causally attributable losses; it does not dispense with causation. Id. at 109–110.

An award of every fee can satisfy the test in an exceptional case. If the whole lawsuit is initiated in bad faith, all defense expenses may be attributable to it. If a defendant’s entire course of conduct is a scheme to defeat a valid claim, all litigation expenses may result from that scheme. And if the court finds that proper disclosure would have produced settlement at a specific point, fees after that point may all be caused by withholding the evidence. These are applications of the same but-for rule, not exceptions allowing punishment for extreme behavior. Id. at 110–111.

## Why the $2.7 million award could not stand

The district court explicitly allowed severity to replace causation. The Ninth Circuit likewise used the period during which Goodyear acted in bad faith as its limiting principle. Neither court found that each shifted expense occurred because of, and solely because of, the misconduct. Concurrent timing was not enough. Id. at 112–113.

The Haegers argued that the record nevertheless supported all fees because timely disclosure would have ended the suit. The Supreme Court declined to supply that finding. The district court had said only that settlement probably would have occurred much earlier, not immediately upon the required disclosure. Moreover, Goodyear could still argue that this tire, after more than 40,000 miles of use, failed from striking road debris. In another G159 case, it disclosed the tests yet proceeded to trial. These were plausible defenses and litigation choices, not Supreme Court findings that road debris actually caused this accident. Id. at 113–114.

Nor did withholding the tests necessarily cause every subsequent expense. The district court’s own alternative award recognized that roughly $700,000 concerned other defendants and medical damages that the plaintiffs would have litigated regardless of Goodyear’s discovery conduct. The Haegers supplied no concrete reason to reject that distinction. At least those expenses could not be included in the full award. Id. at 114.

## The unresolved $2 million award and judgment

Without a waiver issue, the Supreme Court would require a fresh calculation of the smaller award too: the district court’s sparse discussion did not establish that it had used the correct but-for standard. But the Haegers argued that Goodyear had waived further challenge by submitting that only about $700,000 would have been incurred irrespective of its behavior. The Ninth Circuit had not decided that argument, and the Supreme Court declined to decide it first. Id. at 114–115.

The Court **reversed the Ninth Circuit’s judgment and remanded**. The first task below was to decide waiver. If waiver existed, the contingent award could end this dispute; otherwise, the district court had to reassess fees under the proper causation requirement. The Court did not itself uphold $2 million, eliminate every sanction, or decide tire-defect liability. There were no separate Supreme Court writings. Id. at 115.

## Assigned context and source distinctions

The assigned account correctly identifies the compensatory limit and the rejected comprehensive award. Its statement that Rule 37 sanctions were unavailable because the suit had settled is editorial explanation, not the precise proposition stated in the Supreme Court’s note 2. That note recounts the district court’s view that Rule 11 and § 1927 did not reach all offending conduct; the main text explains why settlement foreclosed a default judgment. This decision should not become a categorical holding that settlement always disables Rule 37.

The textbook’s final sentence describes a separate 2018 district-court remand decision finding waiver and vicarious liability. That later stage is separately briefed and independently reviewed against its recovered complete eleven-page court order; it is not the 2017 Supreme Court holding. The central lesson of the assigned Supreme Court decision is that egregious misconduct does not, through civil fee shifting alone, authorize punishment beyond causally attributable expense.
