# Hall v. Hall

**Civil Procedure — 138 S. Ct. 1118 (2018), No. 16–1150, 584 U.S. ___ in the official slip opinion. Supreme Court of the United States, March 27, 2018. Chief Justice Roberts wrote for a unanimous Court, 9–0. Pinpoints below refer to the Court’s eighteen-page opinion, separately paginated after the three-page syllabus.**

## Two lawsuits within a family dispute

Ethlyn Hall owned property in the United States Virgin Islands. Her son Samuel, a lawyer, acted as her caretaker and legal adviser. After a disagreement about his management of her real estate, she created an inter vivos trust, transferred her property to it, and named her daughter Elsa as successor trustee. She then moved to Miami to live with Elsa; the parties disputed the circumstances of that move. In May 2011, Ethlyn sued Samuel and his law firm in federal district court, both individually and as trustee. Her claims alleged breach of fiduciary duty, legal malpractice, conversion, fraud, and unjust enrichment. This became the “trust case.” Slip op. 1–2.

After Ethlyn died, Elsa replaced her as trustee and plaintiff. Samuel counterclaimed against Elsa in both her individual and representative capacities, alleging that she had exploited their mother’s mental frailty to turn her against him. The counterclaims included intentional infliction of emotional distress, fraud, breach of fiduciary duty, conversion, and tortious interference. But Elsa had entered the trust case only in a representative capacity. To pursue his claims against her individually, Samuel filed another complaint in the same district court—the “individual case”—asserting the same claims. Id. at 2.

The two cases initially followed separate tracks. On Samuel’s motion, the district court consolidated them under Rule 42(a), directing that submissions be filed on the trust case’s docket. Shortly before trial, it dismissed Samuel’s counterclaims from the trust case; those claims remained in his individual action. Both cases were then tried together before one jury. Id. at 2–3.

## Different judgments created the appeal problem

In the individual case, the jury found for Samuel on intentional infliction of emotional distress and awarded $500,000 in compensatory damages and $1.5 million in punitive damages. The court entered judgment but then granted Elsa a new trial, reopening that judgment and leaving the individual case pending. In the trust case, the jury rejected Elsa’s claims against Samuel and the law firm. The clerk entered judgment that she recover nothing and that the action be dismissed on the merits. That completely resolved the trust case. Id. at 3–4.

Elsa appealed the trust judgment. Samuel and his law firm argued that it was not a final decision while the individual case remained pending because consolidation had effectively made the two suits a single case. The Third Circuit agreed. It employed a case-by-case approach to finality in fully consolidated litigation and emphasized that these claims had been scheduled and tried together. It dismissed the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. The Supreme Court reviewed that dismissal—not the jury’s evaluation of the family dispute or the order granting a new trial. Id. at 3–5.

## Holding: a completed constituent case is immediately appealable

When a district court finally resolves one of several cases consolidated under Rule 42(a), the losing party may immediately appeal that decision even though another consolidated case remains pending. Consolidation does not eliminate a constituent action’s separate identity for this purpose. The Supreme Court **reversed the Third Circuit’s dismissal and remanded for further proceedings**. It did not decide that Elsa should win her underlying claims. Id. at 17–18.

Section 1291 grants courts of appeals authority over final district-court decisions. A final decision ends the litigation on the merits and leaves only execution of judgment. Without consolidation, Elsa’s trust judgment plainly qualified: it resolved all claims in her action. The question was whether Rule 42(a) changed that conclusion merely because another action had been tried with hers. Id. at 4–5.

## The Court resolved ambiguity through consolidation’s legal history

Samuel argued that ordinary definitions of “consolidate” meant joining actions into one whole. Roberts rejected the premise that ordinary usage supplied a single clear answer. The word can describe a complete merger, but it can also describe bringing things together without eliminating their distinct identities. The controlling question therefore concerned consolidation’s established legal meaning. Id. at 5–6.

Rule 42(a), adopted in 1938, was based on a federal consolidation statute dating to 1813. For 125 years, decisions and treatises had understood consolidation as a management device that preserved the identities of the cases and the rights of their separate parties. The Court explained several precedents rather than simply invoking historical usage in the abstract. Id. at 6–12.

In *Rich v. Lambert*, consolidated admiralty claims could not be aggregated to satisfy the amount required for appellate jurisdiction; each constituent case had to qualify independently. The Court also treated each decree as separately appealable. In *Hillmon*, insurance cases tried together remained sufficiently distinct that each defendant retained its own peremptory challenges. In *Stone*, an appellant could not use an appeal from one consolidated case to complain about rights in another. And *Johnson v. Manhattan Railway* reiterated that consolidation did not merge suits or automatically make a party to one a party to the other. Hall’s single asterisk footnote at opinion page 10 acknowledges isolated federal departures and differing state practices rather than describing a literally universal historical rule. Id. at 7–11 & 10 n.*.

The Court drew several connected inferences from that history. Separate appellate-jurisdiction analysis would be unnecessary if consolidation created a single action. Separate judgments ordinarily triggered separate appeal rights. Parties to one action should not lose those rights because of proceedings involving other parties. And making a party await the conclusion of another case could impair the right to appeal a final decision in the party’s own completed action. Id. at 12.

## Rule 42’s different tools did not silently create a merger

Samuel argued that the Rule’s structure changed the old meaning. Rule 42(a)(1) allowed the court to join matters for hearing or trial; subsection (a)(2) allowed it to consolidate actions; and subsection (a)(3) authorized other orders avoiding unnecessary expense or delay. He reasoned that the first provision supplied limited consolidation, so the second must add complete merger or become redundant. Id. at 13–14.

The Court rejected that division of labor. Subsection (a)(1) concerned hearings and trials, but litigation also included discovery and motion practice. Authority to consolidate for those limited purposes had to come from subsection (a)(2). That subsection therefore had independent work to do without turning constituent actions into a single undifferentiated case. Id. at 14.

More fundamentally, the Rule borrowed a settled legal term without defining a new meaning. The careful rulemaking process and Advisory Committee materials gave no indication of a dramatic departure. The Committee said the Rule was based on the statute; unlike its notes explaining some other changes to prior practice, it did not identify a transformation of consolidation’s consequences. Contemporary commentary and later Supreme Court decisions continued to recognize separate case identities. Id. at 12–17.

## Scope and practical consequence

Hall did not forbid consolidation for all purposes. District judges retained substantial discretion over whether and how extensively to coordinate proceedings. Nor did the Court decide every consequence of consolidation before a final decision. Its express limit was that the constituent case retained separate identity at least when a final decision resolved it and the losing party sought appellate review. Id. at 17.

The result supplied a clear point at which the appeal right arose. Samuel’s concerns about practical complications could be considered through the authorized rulemaking process rather than by withholding an appeal from an otherwise final judgment. Elsa could therefore pursue review of the completed trust action while the individual action awaited retrial. That appeal opportunity did not erase the trust verdict, revive the reopened individual judgment, or resolve either sibling’s substantive entitlement. Id. at 17–18.
