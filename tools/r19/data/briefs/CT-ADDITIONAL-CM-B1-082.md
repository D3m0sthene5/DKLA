# Ardente v. Horan
117 R.I. 254, 366 A.2d 162 (1976) | Contracts

## Facts and proceedings
William and Katherine Horan offered their Newport residential property for sale in August 1975. Ernest Ardente bid $250,000 through their attorney. After informing Ardente that the price was acceptable, the attorney prepared a purchase-and-sale agreement at the Horans’ direction and sent it to Ardente’s attorney for signature. Ardente investigated conditions of title and signed. His attorney returned the agreement with a $20,000 deposit check and a September 8, 1975 letter seeking confirmation that a dining-room set and tapestry wall covering, fireplace fixtures, and sun-parlor furniture were included. The letter described replacing these items as difficult. The Horans declined to include them, never signed the agreement, returned both the document and deposit, and refused the sale. (117 R.I. at 255–256.)

Ardente sought specific performance. The Horans answered and moved for summary judgment under Superior Court Rule 56. After affidavits from both sides and a hearing, the trial justice held that the accompanying letter made Ardente’s response a counteroffer. Because the sellers never accepted it, no contract formed. Ardente appealed, challenging both the propriety of summary judgment and the court’s interpretation of his response. Justice Doris wrote the December 2, 1976 decision. Chief Justice Bevilacqua and Justices Joslin, Kelleher, and Doris were listed as present; Justice Paolino did not participate. There is no separate opinion or express numerical vote. (Id. at 254–258, 261.)

## The claimed factual dispute
The court acknowledged that summary judgment is a drastic remedy requiring caution, but appropriate when no genuine material factual dispute exists and the movant is legally entitled to judgment. The appellate court applied the same rules as the trial court. Ardente argued in his appellate brief that a factual question existed about whether the preliminary oral agreement created an immediately binding sale contract before the written document was prepared. (Id. at 256–257.)

That argument did not defeat the judgment. Ardente had not presented the trial court, through his opposing affidavit or otherwise, specific facts establishing that dispute. At the hearing he argued only the interpretation of his September 8 letter, which the court treated as a legal question. A party opposing summary judgment cannot rely merely on pleading allegations or denials; he must identify specific facts requiring trial. Footnote 2 adds that Ardente did not press the supposed factual issue at appellate oral argument and expressly conceded in a later appellate memorandum that no factual dispute existed, while maintaining that judgment had been entered for the wrong party. This disposition therefore does not establish that every disputed question of contractual intention is necessarily a question of law. It addresses the record and arguments actually supplied. (Id. at 257–258 & n.2.)

## Offer, communicated assent, and additional requests
For the acceptance analysis, the court assumed, favorably to Ardente as the nonmovant, that delivery of the purchase-and-sale document constituted the Horans’ offer. It did not conclusively decide that premise. Footnote 3 notes that the document lacked the sellers’ signatures and that Ardente said he needed to investigate title conditions. If delivery was not an offer, his signed return could itself be only an offer that the Horans had never accepted. (Id. at 258 & n.3.)

On the assumed-offer premise, formation required an overt communication of acceptance, rather than Ardente’s private intention. The only communicated response was the signed agreement accompanied by the September 8 letter. The court consequently interpreted those documents together. Signing and returning the purchase agreement alone would have accepted the assumed offer, but the accompanying language could qualify that apparent assent. An effective acceptance had to be definite and unequivocal; a conditional or limited acceptance was a counteroffer requiring the original offeror’s acceptance. (Id. at 258–260.)

The court nevertheless rejected a mechanical rule under which every request for a change defeats formation. An offeree can unequivocally accept while also suggesting an addition or modification, provided the acceptance clearly stands whether or not the request is granted. Williston and Corbin supported that distinction. The question was whether the furnishings language was a condition of assent or an independent collateral inquiry, determined through ordinary interpretation of the response. (Id. at 260.)

## Application and exact result
Ardente’s letter sought confirmation that the listed items were part of the transaction; it did not state that he would purchase without them. That formulation incorporated the personal property into the real-estate bargain rather than requesting a gratuitous extra after accepting it. Emphasis on the difficulty of replacing the furnishings reinforced their significance to his proposed transaction. Cases sustaining acceptance despite an accompanying request generally contained a more definite expression of unconditional assent. Reading these documents together, the court held the response conditional: it rejected the Horans’ offer and created no contractual obligation. (Id. at 260–261.)

The exact disposition denied and dismissed Ardente’s appeal, affirmed the judgment, and remanded the case to the Superior Court. The remand accompanied affirmance; it was not an order to retry formation or grant specific performance. A separate constraint in footnote 1 is also important: although the transaction appeared within the statute of frauds, the Horans raised no such defense below or on appeal. The court would not interpose that defense on their behalf. The actual judgment rests on the response and the summary-judgment record, not a holding that the sale failed the statute of frauds. (Id. at 256 n.1, 261.)
