# Insurance Co. of North America v. S/S Hellenic Challenger

**88 F.R.D. 545 (S.D.N.Y. 1980) — August 27, 1980. Civil Procedure.** District Judge Motley issued the memorandum opinion. Assigned passage: CivPro, physical pages 156–157, printed pages 263–265, ending before Notes and Questions. The defendant moving for relief was Hellenic Lines Limited, the vessel operator. No separate writing is reported.

## Facts and procedural posture

Insurance Company of North America sued on an admiralty and maritime claim arising from pickled sheepskins shipped from Port Sudan to New York aboard the Hellenic Challenger. It alleged nondelivery, shortages, loss, and damage, claiming $33,352.02. On May 29, 1979, a United States Marshal delivered the summons and complaint at Hellenic’s office to Mr. Syed, a claims adjuster who assisted the claims manager, Mr. Diamond. Diamond was absent because of illness. The company expressly authorized its titled officers and claims manager to accept process; Syed had no such express authorization. He nonetheless had accepted summonses and complaints for the company on at least two previous occasions as part of receiving and investigating claims. [547–548; preserved complete opinion ¶¶3–8.]

Syed misplaced the papers. They never reached the company personnel who should have handled the litigation. A default judgment entered on February 14, 1980, awarded the insurer the claimed $33,352.02, with interest and costs. Hellenic first learned about the suit on March 10 when its bank reported that the insurer had attached its account. The writ of execution was for $36,392.06. These figures describe different stages—the pleaded cargo claim, the default award, and execution including added amounts—and should not be treated as competing descriptions of the original claim. [¶¶2–5.]

Hellenic moved to set aside the judgment. It argued first that defective service prevented personal jurisdiction; second, that the misplaced papers constituted mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect warranting relief under Rule 60(b). Alternatively, it asked the court to vacate the damages award and hold an inquest to determine the proper amount. Those alternatives mattered: the court ultimately preserved the default while reopening the amount of damages. [¶2.]

## Service issue and governing historical rules

The service question was whether delivery to this claims adjuster satisfied the federal rules despite his lack of express designation. Historical Rule 4(d)(3) allowed delivery to an officer, managing or general agent, or another agent authorized by appointment or law. Rule 4(d)(7) also allowed service by a method prescribed by applicable federal statute or the law of the state where the district court sat. The court stated that service was proper under these provisions and developed its analysis principally through Rule 4(d)(3). It did not supply a separate, extended New York statutory analysis that should be invented in a brief. The textbook situates the case within its discussion of later-numbered Rule 4(h). [547; ¶¶6–7.]

Hellenic’s argument treated its formal authorization policy as decisive: only the listed officers and claims manager were eligible recipients. The court instead construed the corporate-service provision functionally. A person’s relationship to the organization could make implied authority to receive process reasonable even without an official title or express appointment. Relying on earlier district decisions, it asked whether the recipient was sufficiently integrated into the company to understand the papers and know how to direct them. The purpose was a method reasonably calculated to alert the defendant, rather than compliance with titles alone. [547–548; ¶¶7–8.]

Syed’s ordinary responsibilities, prior acceptance of process, and working relationship with Diamond supplied that connection. The court inferred easy access to the authorized claims manager because only Diamond’s glass-walled office separated the two men. It found Syed familiar with summonses and complaints and capable of routing them properly. Consequently, delivery to him was valid even though his later mishandling defeated actual internal notice. The holding depends on this recipient’s established role and experience; it does not make every employee at a corporate office a proper recipient. [548; ¶8.]

## Lost papers and relief from default

The company next invoked Rule 60(b)(1), arguing that Syed’s loss of the summons and complaint was excusable neglect. The court distinguished cases affording relief for failures by attorneys or other representatives from a client’s own faulty internal handling. Here the loss could not be attributed to defense counsel. Receiving and routing legal papers was part of the company’s internal business procedure, and the adjuster’s mistake occurred within that procedure. The court refused relief on that ground. This is the opinion’s historical application of Rule 60(b)(1), rather than a certification that every corporate mistake must receive the same treatment under later doctrine. [548; ¶¶9–11.]

The sole judicial footnote also rejects Rule 60(b)(6) relief: Hellenic had failed to establish good cause for that alternative ground. The opinion gives that issue a short disposition with a precedent citation, not a separate factual analysis. Its service holding and neglect holding therefore did not depend on showing that the papers actually reached management. The failure occurred after valid delivery to a sufficiently integrated recipient. [548 n.1; the reproduction places the note after ¶15 as ¶16.]

## Why the damages award was reopened

Hellenic succeeded on its alternative damages argument. Rule 55(b)(1)’s sum-certain mechanism required an amount susceptible to reliable computation. The insurer’s surveyor had visually examined the sheepskins and assigned heat-damage percentages to individual batches. Those estimates were opinions about the extent and value of damage, rather than a binding calculation fixing the amount due. Putting a precise dollar figure in the complaint or surveyor’s report did not turn the underlying disputed valuation into a liquidated sum. [548–549; ¶¶12–14.]

The court relied on Ace Grain’s treatment of surveyor estimates: even a surveyor retained by the defendant did not conclusively bind it to an assessment for default-judgment purposes. A judicial determination of the damages, either by the court or through an appropriate reference under Rule 55(b)(2), remained necessary. Thus valid service and the surviving default did not automatically establish the insurer’s chosen monetary valuation. [549; ¶13.]

## Exact order and assigned significance

The court **denied relief based on defective service and Rules 60(b)(1) and (6), vacated the default judgment to the extent of the damages award, and granted an inquest to determine damages**. It neither dismissed the action nor vacated the entire default. No amount was fixed at the new inquest by this opinion. [549; ¶¶14–15.]

The assigned passage accurately develops the service reasoning and internal-handling explanation but omits the full opinion’s reopening of damages. Its questions about managing-agent terminology and appointment should be distinguished from the court’s actual grounds. Its additional caution that actual notice does not inevitably cure defective service is compatible with the narrower point here: service was valid because of Syed’s organizational role, although he then lost the papers.
