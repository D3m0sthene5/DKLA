# Lisk v. Lumber One Wood Preserving, LLC

**792 F.3d 1331 (11th Cir. 2015) — July 10, 2015.** District Judge Robert L. Hinkle, sitting by designation, wrote the panel opinion with Circuit Judges Marcus and Jill Pryor. Assigned source: CivPro physical page 276, printed page 515, Note 1, with later editorial context in Note 5 through physical page 277, printed page 516.

## Alleged fence defects and the dismissal

Robert Lisk hired Clean Cut Fence Company to install a fence at his home. The contract required treated wood and stated that manufacturers supplied the warranties on fencing materials. Clean Cut bought wood through Capitol Wholesale Fence Company, a distributor supplied by Lumber One. Lisk alleged that Lumber One’s website, advertising, and labels represented that the wood was pressure-treated using licensed MCA technology, which would resist rot, fungal decay, and termites for at least fifteen years. His posts allegedly rotted within three years; Clean Cut said other customers had similar problems. These were complaint allegations accepted for evaluating dismissal, not facts established after trial. (1333.)

Lisk asserted claims under Alabama’s Deceptive Trade Practices Act and for breach of express warranty, seeking to represent a nationwide class. His individual claim did not exceed $75,000, so although he and Lumber One were citizens of different states, he invoked the Class Action Fairness Act. For purposes of the appeal, the court accepted the parties’ assumption that Alabama substantive law governed. It did not conduct a choice-of-law determination establishing that assumption for every future issue. (1333.)

Lumber One argued that Alabama forbade private ADTPA class actions, that no sufficiently pleaded warranty ran to this remote purchaser, and that eliminating the class claims would leave no jurisdictional basis for the small individual ADTPA claim. The district court dismissed the complaint. The circuit court reviewed the failure-to-state-a-claim decision de novo, accepting factual allegations but requiring plausible entitlement to relief rather than labels or conclusions. (1333–1334.)

## First holding: Rule 23 governs the proposed class claim

The ADTPA prohibited the alleged misrepresentations and authorized consumer relief, including actual or minimum damages, possible enhanced damages, and attorney’s fees. It nevertheless reserved representative actions to the Alabama Attorney General or district attorneys and prohibited private class actions. Rule 23, by contrast, permits representative litigation when its conditions are met, without excluding this type of claim. The complaint alleged those conditions, which the court assumed for the dismissal appeal. It **did not certify the class**. (1334.)

The Rules Enabling Act permits federal procedural rules but prohibits abridging, enlarging, or modifying substantive rights. A valid Federal Rule displaces conflicting state provisions in federal litigation. The panel held that applying Rule 23 here changed the mode of litigating existing ADTPA rights without changing the rules governing liability or the underlying substantive obligations. Lumber One remained obligated to represent its product accurately; buyers retained the same right to redress for misrepresentation. The disputed difference was whether those rights could be pursued together by consumers rather than only separately or through public officials. (1335–1338.)

## Why the fractured Shady Grove opinions did not require choosing a single test

Lumber One and Lisk debated whether to follow Justice Scalia’s plurality approach or Justice Stevens’s concurrence in *Shady Grove*. The panel emphasized that Stevens had joined Scalia’s Parts I and II-A, giving those portions five votes, and that all five justices supporting the result agreed Rule 23 governed the New York statutory-penalty class claim there. Whatever broader rationale controlled, that holding remained binding. (1335.)

The panel found no meaningful distinction between the New York statutory penalty and the Alabama deceptive-practice claim. Both were state-created rights with individual recovery permitted but private collective recovery restricted. Alabama even allowed public officials to bring representative actions, making the claim that all class treatment necessarily changed substantive rights harder to sustain. (1335–1336.)

Lumber One relied on the Alabama ban’s placement within the statute creating the claim, unlike the New York prohibition’s location in a general procedural code. The court rejected statutory placement as controlling: reorganizing a code could not itself change whether applying a Federal Rule altered substantive rights. It also rejected narrowing *Shady Grove* to statutes that facially reached claims under other jurisdictions’ laws, because the actual claim there arose under New York law. (1336.)

The panel concluded that the result was consistent with both Scalia’s approach and Stevens’s approach. It therefore **expressly left unresolved which broader opinion supplied the binding rule**. It explained that determining the narrowest grounds under *Marks* can be difficult and that its own circuit had articulated differing approaches to fragmented decisions. Resolving that problem should await a case in which the choice affects the outcome. This opinion consequently is not a holding that Scalia’s entire plurality or Stevens’s concurrence invariably controls. (1336–1338.)

## Second holding: the remote buyer plausibly pleaded an express-warranty claim

The district court had correctly recognized a pleaded express warranty from Lumber One to its distributor, a point not disputed on appeal. The contested question was whether Lisk plausibly alleged intended third-party-beneficiary status. Alabama required an intent at contract formation to confer a direct benefit, status as an intended beneficiary, and breach. Lisk alleged each element, including intent to protect subsequent purchasers and end users. (1338.)

Rule 9(b) permits general allegations of intent, but plausibility still mattered. The court found it plausible that a manufacturer would warrant its product to end users to increase demand. Alabama’s *Harris Moran* seed case supported the analogy: defective wood and seeds might appear sound until substantial installation or cultivation expenses reveal the problem, and remote purchasers could receive warranty benefits. Foreseeable harm and the distribution circumstances supported Lisk’s pleaded status. (1338–1339.)

The court preserved an important limit. Unlike the seed case, the complaint did not describe similar provisions in the manufacturer-distributor agreement. If that agreement expressly excluded end-user warranties, it could support Lumber One and might ultimately establish a defense. Its terms were not yet known, so their effect belonged to later proceedings. Allowing the claim to proceed was not a final finding that every manufacturer warranty automatically benefits remote buyers. (1339.)

## Judgment and assignment

The court **reversed and remanded** the dismissal. Both the ADTPA class-claim theory and the express-warranty theory could proceed; neither consumer liability nor class certification was adjudicated. The only reported note identifies Hinkle’s designation. There are no judicial substantive footnotes, separate writings, or appendices. (1332–1333 designation note, 1339.)

The textbook accurately emphasizes the refusal to choose between the broader *Shady Grove* rationales. Its later Note 5 describes Alabama’s 2016 legislative response labeling the class restriction substantive and later district-court treatment. Those are subsequent editorial accounts, not developments known or decided in this 2015 opinion.
