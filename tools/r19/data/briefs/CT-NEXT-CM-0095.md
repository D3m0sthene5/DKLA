# Fairchild Industries v. Maritime Air Service, Ltd.
274 Md. 181, 333 A.2d 313 (1975) | Contracts

## Court and certified-question posture

The Maryland Court of Appeals answered a question certified by the federal district court on March 10, 1975. Judge Levine wrote the majority opinion. Judge Singley dissented, joined by Judges Smith and O’Donnell; the remaining judges on the seven-member panel were Chief Judge Murphy and Judges Digges and Eldridge, producing a four-to-three decision. The majority held that a written “as is” exclusion under UCC §2-316(3)(a) must satisfy the conspicuousness requirement in subsection (2). It answered the first certified question and did not need to answer the second. It did not finally adjudicate helicopter defects, award damages, or expressly find the particular clause inconspicuous. 274 Md. at 182–184, 191–192.

## The lease, purchase, and underlying lawsuit

Fairchild initially leased a helicopter to Maritime on May 9, 1969, for one year, with options extending the lease two additional years and allowing purchase during the lease. Maritime exercised the purchase option. On March 19, 1970, the parties signed a Fairchild printed form containing typewritten additions. One addition sold the aircraft as is, denied express and implied warranties except title, and acknowledged that the buyer had examined the aircraft as fully as desired. Maritime subsequently sued for alleged express, merchantability, and particular-purpose warranty breaches. The case began in Baltimore’s Superior Court and was removed to federal court. Fairchild’s motion to dismiss invoked the contractual exclusion, prompting certification of the state-law question. Id. at 182–183.

The first certified question asked whether subsection (2)’s requirement of conspicuous written exclusions applies to subsection (3)(a). The second, contingent on conspicuousness sometimes but not invariably being required, asked whether business dealings, circumstances of the transaction, prior examination or use, and relative commercial skill determine its necessity. The certified record thus presented an interpretive issue, not a completed merits trial establishing breach or the physical condition of the helicopter. Id. at 183–184.

## The statutory routes and Fairchild’s argument

Section 2-316(2), made subject to subsection (3), requires a merchantability exclusion to mention merchantability and, when written, be conspicuous. A fitness exclusion must be written and conspicuous. Subsection (3), introduced as operating notwithstanding subsection (2), permits expressions such as “as is” or “with all faults,” unless circumstances indicate otherwise, because their common understanding signals that no implied warranty is made. Other provisions address defects that a precontract examination should reveal and exclusions through dealing, performance, or trade usage. Id. at 183–184.

Fairchild read those introductory clauses as creating independent alternatives. Under its reading, ordinary disclaimer language faced subsection (2)’s vocabulary and presentation rules, but an as-is provision independently excluded the implied warranties without conspicuousness. It emphasized the natural effect of the statutory words and argued that the drafters’ use of lowercase expressions rather than capitalized examples supported that distinction. Id. at 184–185, 187.

## The majority’s reasoning

The majority agreed that the as-is route is an exception to some requirements but found its extent ambiguous. In particular, using those words can dispense with expressly naming merchantability; that does not necessarily dispense with conspicuousness when the disclaimer is written. Similarly, although subsection (2) demands both writing and conspicuousness for fitness, the majority did not regard subsection (3)(a)’s introductory words as clearly eliminating every associated protection. It therefore examined legislative purpose, statutory context, and consequences rather than resolving the case by the introductory phrase alone. Id. at 185–186.

Official Comment 1 identifies protection against unexpected, unbargained disclaimer language as a central purpose. Comment 6 describes the subsection (3) exceptions as factual situations in which the circumstances themselves alert the buyer that implied warranties are excluded. For the majority, comprehensible language is useful only when the buyer’s attention is called to it. An as-is expression buried in a lengthy document would otherwise defeat protection that an explicit merchantability exclusion in the same location would have to satisfy. The majority considered that disparity an anomalous result inconsistent with the section’s purpose. Id. at 186–187.

The typography of the Code’s own examples did not establish that lowercase printed contractual language is invariably sufficient: the statute likewise gives subsection (2) examples in lowercase, although their required conspicuousness was undisputed. Nor did Comment 7’s discussion of as-is language as ordinary commercial usage eliminate the threshold need to bring the disclaimer to attention. That comment explained the expression’s commercial meaning; it did not license hidden contract terms. Id. at 187–188.

The majority found Gindy Manufacturing particularly persuasive. Gindy treated as-is expressions as additional permissible wording, while carrying the conspicuousness protection over to a written exclusion. Woodruff and Osborne supported the same protection against surprise. The majority distinguished Smith v. Sharpensteen, where the buyer had actual knowledge of a disclaimer but the document used neither merchantability nor the as-is vocabulary. It declined to derive a general contrary rule from an unexplained assertion that subsection (3) was satisfied in that case. Id. at 188–190. Its own note 1 supplies the source for Professor Hawkland’s view that these expressions should be conspicuous even though subsection (3)(a) does not expressly say so. Id. at 190–191 & majority n.1.

The resulting rule concerns written as-is and similar exclusions: they satisfy the language requirement through commonly understood words, but must remain conspicuous within subsection (2)’s meaning. The majority did not decide the alternative examination or usage routes under subsection (3)(b)–(c), despite their appearance in the statutory quotation and the purchase document’s examination acknowledgment. The second certified question’s proposed contextual exceptions became unnecessary under its affirmative answer to the first. Id. at 191.

## The three-judge dissent

Singley saw no ambiguity. The introductory “notwithstanding” language meant that the subsection (3) alternatives operated despite subsection (2)’s requirements. The extensive deliberation surrounding the Code made it especially inappropriate, in his view, to supply a requirement its drafters could easily have stated. Hawkland’s concession that the subsection did not expressly demand conspicuousness supported, rather than overcame, this textual objection. Singley would answer the first question negatively. Smith and O’Donnell joined his reasoning. Id. at 191–192.

The dissent’s three notes identify the literary aphorism used to introduce its interpretive stance, define notwithstanding as a preposition, and identify Hawkland’s discussion. They contain no distinct factual finding about how this buyer actually read the clause. The disagreement therefore turns on whether the statutory purpose justifies carrying over a presentation safeguard that the alternative subsection does not expressly repeat, not on competing findings about the helicopter’s defects or the buyer’s actual notice.

## Exact result and limits

The court answered the first question affirmatively, declined the second as unnecessary, and directed that costs abide the ultimate result. Id. at 191. The federal litigation still had to apply the state-law answer; the certified decision itself neither granted nor denied the underlying dismissal motion.
