Parties
J. W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. was an importer contesting customs duties on barium dioxide brought into New
York. It was the petitioner in the Supreme Court. The United States defended the assessment made by the
collector under a presidential proclamation. The importer challenged the legal authority for two of the six cents
per pound collected: Congress had specified a base rate of four cents, and the proclamation raised that rate to
six. 276 U.S. at 400, 404.
Material Facts
Section 315 of the Tariff Act of 1922 established a flexible-tariff mechanism. Its policy was to equalize the
difference between domestic production costs and those in the principal competing foreign country. The
President was to investigate the relevant differences and proclaim changes in classifications or increases or
decreases in duties necessary to give effect to that policy. The total rate change could not exceed fifty percent
of the rate Congress specified. Changes took effect thirty days after proclamation. Id. at 401–402.
The statute identified factors for the inquiry, including wages and materials, wholesale selling prices, foreign
governmental or private advantages, and other competitive advantages or disadvantages. The Tariff
Commission had to investigate before any proclamation and give interested parties reasonable public notice, an
opportunity to produce evidence, and a hearing. Its role was investigatory and advisory: the President made the
rate determination. The statute required modification or termination when the underlying differences changed
or disappeared. Id. at 402, 405.
Additional limits barred moving an article between the free and dutiable lists or changing the form of duty.
Applicable statutory ad valorem ceilings also remained binding. Thus the President could adjust within an
enacted tariff framework, rather than decide without statutory restriction which articles should be taxed or what
type of charge should apply. Id. at 402.
President Coolidge's May 19, 1924, proclamation recited that the Commission had investigated barium dioxide
after notice and hearing, that Germany was the principal competing country, and that the existing duty did not
equalize the production-cost difference. It raised the rate from four to six cents per pound, the maximum
fifty-percent increase. Hampton's assessment used that increased rate. The controversy before the Court
challenged the statute's constitutionality, rather than asking the Court to calculate the production-cost
difference anew. Id. at 400, 403–404.
Procedural History
Hampton protested the assessment and pursued the prescribed customs review. A majority of the United States
Customs Court upheld the statute. The Court of Customs Appeals affirmed, and the Supreme Court granted
certiorari. The Attorney General had certified the case's importance while it was pending in the customs
appellate process. Id. at 400–401.
The importer advanced two constitutional objections. First, § 315 allegedly transferred Congress's legislative
power to impose duties to the President. Second, the protective purpose of the tariff allegedly exceeded a

Issue
Could Congress authorize the President, aided by a mandatory Tariff Commission investigation, to adjust
customs duties within defined limits to equalize domestic and foreign production costs? Did that authorization
impermissibly delegate legislative power under Article I? Separately, did Congress's purpose of encouraging
domestic industry invalidate a tariff act that also raised revenue? 276 U.S. at 404–405, 409–413.
Rule and Holding
Congress may authorize execution of its legislative policy when it supplies an intelligible principle to which
the authorized official or body must conform. Section 315 satisfied that requirement: Congress selected the
policy of equalizing specified cost differences, identified the relevant inquiry, prescribed procedures, and
confined the permitted adjustments. The President's role in implementing that plan did not constitute a
forbidden transfer of legislative power. Id. at 404–410.
The Court also rejected the contention that customs duties were unconstitutional because Congress used them
to protect domestic production. A revenue measure did not become invalid because Congress also sought to
encourage American industries. The flexible-tariff provision and the challenged assessment were sustained. Id.
at 411–413.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Customs Appeals. The assessment at six cents per pound remained
effective, and Hampton's constitutional challenge supplied no basis to recover the additional two cents per
pound. The Court did not replace the President's rate with its own assessment of the appropriate tariff. Id. at
400, 404, 413.
Reasoning
Taft began with the actual congressional policy. Congress sought duties that would equalize the cost of
producing and selling comparable domestic and foreign goods in the American market. Calculating the
difference could be difficult, but difficulty of measurement did not make the objective unintelligible. Congress
had decided what relationship the duties should bear to production conditions, leaving investigation and
adjustment to the executive. Id. at 404–405.
Changing conditions supplied a practical reason for the arrangement. Congress might lack the information
required for accurate initial adjustment, and a rate accurate at enactment might cease to equalize costs as
conditions changed. The President could receive continuing assistance from investigators and make the
required changes. The Commission could not itself fix the duties; investigation, notice, and opportunity to be
heard preceded the President's decision. The allocation therefore combined an enacted standard with an
institutional means of finding the facts needed to implement it. Id. at 405.
The separation of powers prohibited Congress from surrendering legislative power or taking over another
branch's constitutional field. It did not require the branches to operate without assistance from one another. The
permissible extent of assistance depended on the character of the task and the practical needs of coordinated
government. Congress had long used executive discretion within defined limits to administer laws, including
rules whose violation carried legislatively authorized penalties. Id. at 406–407.
The opinion distinguished discretion about what the law should be from discretion exercised under an existing
law. Conditional legislation illustrated the distinction: Congress could make an enacted policy take effect upon
later circumstances rather than itself determine those circumstances in advance. The official ascertaining or
implementing the condition did not necessarily make the underlying legislative choice. Taft connected this
analysis to earlier decisions, including Grimaud and Field. Id. at 406–407, 410–411.

Rate regulation supplied another analogy. Congress could not practicably prescribe every rate for every kind of
interstate carriage. It could require just, reasonable, nondiscriminatory rates and entrust a commission to
investigate particular circumstances and apply that standard. The need for recurring, fact-sensitive adjustments
supported a similar division of labor in customs duties. The Court rejected the importer's proposed categorical
distinction between executive assistance in regulation and assistance in administering tariffs. Id. at 407–409.
The intelligible-principle formulation expresses the required connection between the statute and the delegated
task. Congress had to prescribe a principle that directed the decisionmaker's action; the mere existence of an
official or investigatory body was not the stated test. Here the cost-equalization rule supplied the goal, while
the fifty-percent ceiling and other statutory restrictions bounded the available response. The Court upheld this
particular combination rather than declaring all presidential changes to tariff rates valid. Id. at 401–402, 409.
Field further supported the result. Its reciprocity arrangement had left the President to ascertain when the
contingency for specified duties existed. Hampton involved continuing numerical adjustment, with an advisory
commission, but the Court treated both as execution of congressional policy. It did not rest the proclamation on
an independent presidential power to tax imports. The legal authority for the disputed increase remained § 315.
Id. at 404, 409–411.
The protective-purpose objection concerned Congress's own substantive authority and required a separate
answer. Taft reviewed tariff legislation beginning with the 1789 act, which expressly referred to
encouragement and protection of manufactures. He treated long-standing practice, including enactments by
legislators familiar with the Constitution's framing, as evidence against the proposed revenue-only restriction.
This reasoning addressed constitutional power; the Court expressly separated that inquiry from whether
protection was wise policy. Id. at 411–412.
Finally, a law securing revenue for the general government could have additional purposes in selecting taxable
articles and rates. The protective purpose did not eliminate the revenue character of this act. The Court
therefore rejected both objections: Congress possessed authority to enact this tariff policy, and its specified
method of executive implementation did not improperly transfer legislative power. Id. at 412–413.
Separate Opinions
No concurrence or dissent is reported. Taft's opinion resolves both the delegation question and the separate
objection to a protective purpose. The Customs Court's divided disposition, mentioned in the procedural
account, should not be mistaken for a division recorded in the Supreme Court's published opinion. Id. at 400,
413.
Notes
A useful explanation of Hampton states the operative standard rather than stopping at the phrase intelligible
principle. The President was to compare domestic and foreign production conditions, use the statutory factors
and mandatory investigation, and adjust within the statutory limits. These features show how Congress's
enacted policy directed the exercise of discretion. Id. at 401–405, 409.
The casebook omits most of the separate revenue-purpose discussion. Its inclusion here comes from the full
opinion and prevents confusing the two constitutional questions: whether Congress could pursue the policy and
whether it could enlist the executive to implement it. This is a brief of the 1928 holding, not a conclusion about
the legality of any later tariff measure under a different statute. Id. at 404, 411–413; casebook pp. 56–60.
