# Overstreet v. Norden Laboratories, Inc.
669 F.2d 1286 (6th Cir. 1982) | Contracts

## Court, result, and division of the panel
The Sixth Circuit reversed and remanded a $40,500 warranty judgment on February 3, 1982. Judge Damon J. Keith wrote the lead opinion. Judges Engel and Cornelia G. Kennedy agreed that the express-warranty instructions omitted reliance and that the evidence did not sustain an implied-warranty recovery, but their joint special concurrence supplied the two-judge position on consequential damages. Keith disagreed with that damages analysis while agreeing to reversal on the express-warranty instruction. The lead opinion initially describes the judgment as vacated, but its final directive is reversal and remand for proceedings consistent with the opinion. (1286, 1288–1289, 1292–1295.)

## Facts and proceedings
Dr. Luel P. Overstreet practiced veterinary medicine and operated a standardbred horse farm in Henderson County, Kentucky. Equine rhinopneumonitis produces cold-like symptoms in horses but can cause pregnant mares to abort. In spring 1973, two mares on his farm aborted, later determined to have been caused by this virus. Around the same time, Norden began marketing Rhinomune, a new vaccine promoted through advertisements, brochures, and sales representatives. A representative spoke to an associate in Overstreet’s office; Overstreet asserted that he read promotional literature and ordered the vaccine because of its representations. He administered it before November 1973, and six inoculated mares aborted their foals in spring 1974. These circumstances supported his warranty allegations; the appeal did not finally establish that the vaccine caused the disease. (1288.)

Footnote 1 distinguishes the surviving trial claim from a broader initial complaint: twenty-three mares were vaccinated between August 10 and November 9, 1973, and partial summary judgment had disposed of some claims. The appealed jury award concerned the six aborted foals. The jury found that Overstreet had not violated his duties in using Rhinomune and that Norden had violated “any of its warranties,” without specifying an express or implied warranty. Norden’s motions for judgment notwithstanding the verdict or a new trial were denied. On appeal it challenged reliance instructions, causation, and the damages measure. (1288 & n. 1, 1289 & n. 2.)

## Warranty instructions and Kentucky-law prediction
Sitting in diversity, the panel had to predict Kentucky law because no Kentucky court had then construed the relevant express-warranty provision. The lead opinion examined statutory language, earlier state decisions, commentary, and other indications of state law rather than announcing a federal warranty rule. Under its interpretation of Kentucky Revised Statutes § 355.2-313, an affirmation, promise, description, or model must become part of the basis of the bargain. Reliance as an inducement to purchase was therefore necessary. The jury instructions and verdict form failed to require that finding, and the nonspecific verdict could not reveal the theory actually used. (1289–1291 & nn. 2–5.)

Formal warranty language or an intention to warrant was unnecessary; an advertisement could create a warranty in context, while mere opinion or sales puffing might not. Substantial compliance with directions limited the warranted use. A buyer had no general duty to investigate a representation but could not ignore facts he knew made it incorrect. Expertise did not necessarily bar reliance on a newly marketed, unique product whose properties were better known to its manufacturer. The panel did not resolve the broader question whether one expert could rely on another expert’s representations within their shared specialty. (1290–1292.)

Merchantability was different. Its implied warranty arose by law under § 355.2-314 and required no reliance. Thus Norden’s reliance objection did not defeat that theory, and the instruction was proper in that respect. Nevertheless, all three judges agreed that the evidence in this record was insufficient to establish breach of an implied warranty. That evidentiary conclusion and the express-warranty instruction prevented affirmance of the existing verdict. (1289, 1295.)

## Controlling consequential-damages analysis
After finding liability, the jury was told to measure the difference between each mare’s value with and without her foal. Engel and Kennedy concluded that this instruction treated the foals’ loss as recoverable automatically upon a warranty breach, leaving only valuation to the jury. They required a separate causal connection between the breach and each consequential loss. Under §§ 355.2-714 and 355.2-715, the buyer could recover the vaccine’s difference in value and appropriate handling or administration expenses; recovery for the aborted foals additionally required proof that the breach caused that particular harm. Foreseeability that ineffective vaccination would leave mares exposed did not itself establish causation. (1295–1296.)

The concurrence identified several possible causal paths: the vaccine might itself cause injury, the delivered batch might be inferior to an effective batch, reliance on the promise might lead the buyer to forgo another effective preventive, or reliance might otherwise induce detrimental conduct. The record supported conflicting evidence about another preventive and possibly about the particular batch; footnote 2 explains that the alleged promise of complete effectiveness could support a finding that this batch was deficient. Those possibilities belonged to the jury. If no effective product or device could have prevented the abortions and no other causal link existed, the promise’s breach had not caused the claimed loss of the foals. (1296 & nn. 1–2.)

The two judges rejected Keith’s characterization of their position as a universal “Alternative Product Rule.” Alternatives mattered only when needed to establish the relevant causal link. They illustrated recoverable reliance losses even without an effective alternative, such as expenses incurred by having mares impregnated in reliance on the promise or liability on contracts for delivery of the expected foals. Their baldness hypothetical similarly distinguished an unavoidable loss of hair from the cost of replacing wigs discarded in reliance on a purported cure. These were explanatory hypotheticals, not facts about Overstreet’s actual conduct. Where an alternatives issue was fairly raised and causally material, as here, the plaintiff bore the burden of proving an effective alternative. (1296–1297 & n. 3.)

## Keith’s competing view and significance
Keith regarded the liability instructions’ causation language as adequate despite its omission from the later damages instruction. He argued that the warranty itself defined the promised preventive performance: a jury could find that a properly administered vaccine failed to prevent a disease predictably producing abortions. He rejected limiting consequential recovery by availability of another product, reasoning that it disadvantaged consumers who lacked information, had allergies to alternatives, or bought a novel product. His discussion warned that the restriction could reduce new-product promises to ineffective guarantees and discourage suit. Engel and Kennedy responded that the governing inquiry concerned causation of each loss, not a blanket entitlement or prohibition based on uniqueness. (1292–1294 & nn. 6–9; 1296–1297 & n. 3.)

The decision illustrates a dispute between enforcing a promised result and separately proving causation of consequential loss. It did not enter judgment for Norden or finally determine that Overstreet had no alternative preventive. The existing judgment was reversed, leaving proceedings on remand under the specified instruction and causation requirements. (1295–1297.)
