# Eastern Rolling Mill Co. v. Simon Michlovitz et al.
157 Md. 51, 145 A. 378 (Md. Mar. 20, 1929) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Maryland’s Court of Appeals affirmed, with costs, a decree specifically enforcing two five-year scrap-output contracts. Judge Parke wrote the court’s opinion after argument before Chief Judge Bond and Judges Urner, Adkins, Offutt, Digges, Parke, and Sloan. No separate opinion appears. The decision resolves challenges to corporate power, the general manager’s authority, fraud and fairness, and the adequacy of damages. Its legal framework includes the then-applicable Sale of Goods Act rather than a holding under the later UCC. (51–52, 56, 65–69.)

## Commercial relationship and breach
Michlovitz & Co.—Simon and Abram Michlovitz and David Furman—sold scrap wholesale from Pennsylvania yards. Eastern manufactured sheet steel in the Baltimore area. Its process produced crop-end scrap from steel bars and hydraulically bundled scrap from sheet ends. These by-products represented only about two percent of its business’s gross money value, but accumulated in large daily quantities requiring prompt removal. Since Eastern began operating in 1920, it sold the scrap exclusively to the buyers. (52–53, 61.)

The parties progressed from short-term arrangements to a thirteen-month contract and then a five-year contract beginning in 1924. In September 1927, they replaced the latter with two new contracts, each covering the complete output of one scrap type from October 1, 1927 through September 30, 1932. The prices for each succeeding quarter were calculated from designated Philadelphia-market quotations in Iron Age, minus $3 per ton. The buyers accepted scrap as it accumulated, loaded by Eastern onto gondola cars at its plant, and paid $5,000 on account at formation, credited against deliveries, with excess purchases paid on the existing terms. (53–54.)

John M. Jones, Eastern’s president and general manager from inception, made these agreements. After his death around November 1, 1927, the new president sought rescission or shortening the duration, while remaining willing to enter a one-year contract on the other terms, including prices. The evidence did not support Eastern’s claim that the buyers had agreed to rescind or modify. Both sides instead fixed quarterly prices and performed through June 1928. Eastern then refused further performance despite demands. It did not question the buyers’ willingness and ability to perform. They sought specific performance, alleging irreparable loss; after open-court proof, the chancellor granted it. Eastern appealed. (54–55.)

## Defenses and corporate authority
Eastern argued that the agreements were ultra vires, outside Jones’s authority, fraudulent or unconscionable, adequately remedied by damages, and more harmful to enforce than beneficial to the buyers. The court first observed that continued performance with knowledge of the asserted objections suggested an election to abide by the contracts. But because election could not supply a corporate power actually absent, it addressed the substantive defenses rather than disposing of the case on ratification alone. (55–56.)

The charter expressly authorized manufacture and sale of sheet steel and its by-products. Neither it nor the governing rules imposed the alleged duration or pricing limits. A possibly unwise five-year term or price therefore did not make this ordinary by-product sale ultra vires. The court did not announce that duration could never exceed corporate power in some exceptional situation. (56–57.)

Jones also acted within his managerial authority. The charter, bylaws, employment agreements, and actual practice put ordinary sales in his control, subject generally to the board. The board had imposed no special restriction, required no advance contract approval, and habitually left duration, price, execution, and modification to him. A successor five-year scrap contract with a market-based price resembled the parties’ previous authorized transactions and did not alert the buyers to an unusual limitation. The corporation could not deny the ordinary authority it had permitted Jones to exercise. (57–60.)

## Fraud, pricing, and equitable fairness
The company claimed that duration and price produced extraordinary profits amounting to constructive fraud. Direct evidence showed no collusion, ulterior interest, or personal benefit to Jones apart from benefits shared as a substantial stockholder. He had managed the business successfully, and the company challenged the agreements only after his death. The five-year duration was familiar from the previous bargain, and the price floated with quarterly market changes rather than freezing one price for all five years. (60–61.)

The $3 deduction also had a commercial explanation. Historically the buyers had paid an average $3.37 below the designated market quotations, so the new formula improved Eastern’s price by 37 cents per ton. The deduction covered freight ordinarily at least $2.27 per ton, handling, overhead, storage and resale risks, and prospective profit. Delivery to Bethlehem Steel at Sparrows Point cost only 90 cents and made the buyers’ trade particularly profitable when Bethlehem resumed purchases. At formation, however, Bethlehem was not taking their scrap; no assurance existed that it would continue as a ready market or take Eastern’s entire output promptly for five years. Jones also sought to avoid dependence on the same steel company as both raw-material supplier and scrap customer. The bargain secured reliable clearance of Eastern’s loaded cars and prompt payment irrespective of the buyers’ resale success. (61–63.)

The court judged those choices when made, not exclusively by the favorable events that later increased the buyers’ profits. Honest disagreement about commercial wisdom did not establish fraud or gross inadequacy shocking the conscience. The buyers faced real storage, market, and freight risks if demand changed. The evidence showed a fair bargain between knowledgeable parties, not oppressive enforcement or unconscionable terms. Eastern remained bound by its agent’s business judgment within the authorized transaction. (63–65.)

## Why damages were inadequate
The seller proved that it owned assets from which damages could be recovered, so relief was not justified merely by inability to collect a judgment. The court recognized the ordinary preference for damages in chattel sales, then considered the actual adequacy of that remedy. Long-term deliveries and future profits could fall within equitable exceptions when a present damages estimate would be little more than conjecture. (65–66.)

Here neither quantity nor price was fixed for the remaining years. Scrap output varied with operations and could fall or cease if the plant stopped. Contract prices changed quarterly with published quotations, and the future market values required to measure loss were likewise uncertain. A jury could not fairly ascertain all future tonnage, contract prices, and resale-market prices through September 1932. Damages would force the buyers to relinquish the contractual opportunity for a speculative estimate. (66–67.)

There were additional practical reasons for this remedy. The particular scrap’s quality and concentrated weight were unavailable in the neighborhood or extensive Philadelphia market; Baltimore delivery was itself commercially valuable. The contracts required delivery of accumulated identifiable scrap, not personal skill in operating a coal mine. The court found no comparable enforcement difficulties and distinguished an authority involving mine operations. Thus the result rested on the actual source, replacement difficulty, long duration, uncertain damages, and manageable performance obligation, not on an automatic rule that every output contract receives specific performance. (66.)

## Specific goods and exact disposition
Section 89 of article 83, the Sale of Goods Act, authorized specific enforcement of a broken agreement to deliver specific or ascertained goods, subject to equitable discretion and appropriate conditions. The output’s future existence did not make it an ordinary interchangeable supply contract. All scrap from this designated manufacturer’s specified plant, produced during the stated period, became identified and appropriated to the agreements when manufactured. Eastern could not satisfy them by supplying other generic scrap; selling the specified output to someone else breached the bargain. (67–69.)

The court held that these were specific or ascertained goods in the special circumstances and that the chancellor had power to decree specific performance. It affirmed the decree with costs. This report contains no damages computation, new remand, or separate writing. Its discussion of crop failure in Howell is an illustration supporting identification of future goods, not a finding that Eastern’s plant had ceased operating. (68–69.)

## Assigned relationship and source scope
The assigned account on printed page 953 uses the first-year breach and speculative future damages to ask about specific performance under later UCC provisions. The actual 1929 reasoning additionally addresses authority, formation-time fairness, the distinctive commercial utility of the scrap, and statutory identification under the Sale of Goods Act. The assignment’s question about goods without a peculiarly available source is not an actual finding that this scrap was freely replaceable.
