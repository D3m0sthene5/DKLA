# Shoshone Mining Co. v. Rutter

**Course:** Civil Procedure

## Citation

Shoshone Mining Co. v. Rutter, 177 U.S. 505–514 (1900). Supreme Court of the United States. Argued March 21, 1900; decided April 30, 1900. Justice Brewer delivered the Court's opinion. Justice McKenna dissented without a reported explanation; Justice White did not hear argument and did not participate. Assigned account: Civil Procedure casebook printed pp. 339–340, repository PDF p. 193, note 4.

## Parties and material facts

Shoshone Mining and the Rutter side asserted competing claims to mining property in proceedings connected with obtaining a federal mineral-land patent. The case concerned an “adverse suit” under Revised Statutes §§ 2325–2326, through which competing claimants could establish the right of possession before the Land Department issued a patent. The Court's opinion concentrates on jurisdiction and does not supply a detailed chronology of discovery, location, or competing ownership claims. It expressly states that diverse citizenship did not exist. 177 U.S. at 505–509, 513–514.

That absence of diversity made the alleged federal character of the adverse suit decisive. The right of possession might turn on federal law, but it might instead turn on local mining rules, state statutes, or ordinary facts such as when a mineral deposit was discovered or where a claim was located. The statutory procedure did not establish that a disputed federal legal question actually appeared in every such action. Id. at 507–509.

## Procedural history

Two similar proceedings were consolidated for trial in the federal Circuit Court in Idaho and then taken to the Ninth Circuit. The Rutter side had commenced one action, numbered 81, in federal court. Shoshone had commenced the other in the Idaho state district court for Shoshone County, and the Rutter side removed it. The official report calls that second action No. 103 in its procedural description on page 513 but No. 102 in its final mandate on page 514. The discrepancy is preserved rather than silently corrected. Id. at 513–514.

The Supreme Court reexamined its recent decision in Blackburn v. Portland Gold Mining Co., 175 U.S. 571, after counsel challenged the conclusion that federal authorization of an adverse suit did not itself confer federal-question jurisdiction. The Court adhered to that conclusion. Shoshone is a separate decision, not a restatement presented as a new ruling in Blackburn. 177 U.S. at 505–506.

## Issue

Does the federal statute authorizing an adverse suit to determine mining possession itself place every such action within federal jurisdiction, despite the absence of diversity and despite the possibility that the dispute involves only state law, local custom, or fact? Id. at 506–509.

## Rule and holding

The mere status of an action as an adverse suit authorized by Congress is insufficient to confer federal-question jurisdiction. Congress directed claimants to a court of competent jurisdiction and left competency to the ordinary jurisdictional rules. Such a suit could sometimes present a genuine federal question, or could qualify through diversity under the then-governing rules; neither possibility made all adverse suits inherently federal. Id. at 506–509, 513.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court reversed the Ninth Circuit and directed a remand to the federal Circuit Court, Northern Division, District of Idaho, with instructions to reverse its decree, dismiss the action originally filed in federal court, and remand the removed action to state court. The jurisdictional disposition did not decide which claimant owned the mining rights or should receive a patent. Id. at 513–514.

## Reasoning

The Court distinguished Congress' constitutional power from the jurisdiction Congress had actually conferred. Congress could provide for federal adjudication of controversies growing out of public-land disposal. But the relevant statute did not specify an exclusively federal forum or establish a new division of state and federal authority. Its direction to use a competent court invoked the existing jurisdictional framework. Id. at 506–507.

The historical general grants could support jurisdiction through diversity or through a genuine federal question, subject to the then-applicable amount exceeding $2,000. That historical amount belongs to the Court's analysis, not to a statement of today's thresholds. A right's original connection to federal land law was insufficient: otherwise, many ordinary land-title disputes in the newer states would automatically become federal cases. Id. at 507.

Congress allowed mining possession to be determined through applicable local rules and customs or state limitations statutes. Recognition of those sources did not transform them into federal law. The Court illustrated the distinction with constitutional reliance on state qualifications for congressional electors and with statutes referring to another government's rules. A legal system can recognize another system's rules without incorporating them as its own laws for jurisdictional purposes. Id. at 508–509.

Counsel invoked federal incorporation cases, including Osborn, on the theory that congressional authorization supplied the indispensable federal ingredient. The Court answered that a statute authorizing an action to establish a right differs from a statute creating the right whose extent must be determined. The mining suit could be resolved without construing the scope or effect of congressional legislation. Id. at 509–510.

The connection between the lawsuit and federal land administration was likewise insufficient. Congress had left cases below the historical jurisdictional amount to state courts and contemplated adjudication near the mining property. That framework undermined the claim that federal adjudication was an essential part of every patent application. Differences between agricultural-land and mineral-land procedures also did not compel a federal forum: the mineral-land action could encompass factual or local-law issues as well as federal questions. Id. at 510–513.

Finally, federal provisions concerning limitations and the form of decrees did not independently establish jurisdiction over every case. The Court expected state tribunals to give effect to federal enactments under the Supremacy Clause. It treated possible future state-court refusal as immaterial to this case, observing that Congress could legislate further if necessary. Id. at 512–513.

## Separate positions and notes

McKenna's dissent is recorded but no dissenting reasoning is published. White's nonparticipation is explicit. The report contains no judicial footnotes or appendix. Do not manufacture an explanation for McKenna or treat White as a vote supporting either side. Id. at 514.

The textbook asks whether a federally created cause of action always produces federal-question jurisdiction and uses Shoshone to qualify a mechanical application of Holmes' formula. The actual opinion emphasizes congressional intent, the competent-court language, and the possible sources of the possession dispute. Its holding leaves open federal jurisdiction in a particular adverse suit presenting the necessary independent jurisdictional basis. Casebook pp. 339–340; 177 U.S. at 506–513.
