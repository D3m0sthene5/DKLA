# Briseno v. ConAgra Foods, Inc.

**Civil Procedure · CP-091**  
844 F.3d 1121 (9th Cir. 2017) · January 3, 2017 · Opinion by Judge Friedland

## Product claims and certification appeal

Consumers alleged that Wesson cooking oils' “100% Natural” label was false or misleading because the products contained genetically modified ingredients. ConAgra manufactured and sold the oils. Consolidated actions asserted state-law claims in eleven states, and the plaintiffs sought statewide classes of residents who bought Wesson oils during the applicable periods. Whether the challenged label was actually unlawful remained a merits question. The appeal addressed certification, not a judicial finding that genetically modified ingredients were unnatural. Slip opinion 4–6.

The district court partly granted certification, establishing eleven damages classes under Rule 23(b)(3). ConAgra argued that purchasers could not reliably identify themselves: consumers generally did not retain grocery receipts and might not remember individual purchases of an inexpensive product. The district court required an objective class definition based on purchases during the class period but declined to demand an additional administratively feasible identification method. ConAgra obtained interlocutory review under Rule 23(f).

## Precise issue and holding

Must class representatives demonstrate an administratively feasible way to identify absent members as a separate prerequisite to certification? The Ninth Circuit held **no**. Rule 23's text and existing requirements addressed the relevant concerns without a freestanding administrative-feasibility hurdle. This was the specific issue decided in the published opinion. The court avoided the label “ascertainability,” which other courts used for different concepts, such as objective definition, vague boundaries, or a class defined by success on the merits. Slip opinion 5–7 & nn.3–4.

Friedland wrote for a panel including William A. Fletcher and Morgan B. Christen. No concurrence or dissent accompanied the published opinion.

## Text and structure of Rule 23

Rule 23(a) enumerated four prerequisites: numerosity, commonality, typicality, and adequacy. It omitted administrative feasibility. The court contrasted that closed list with Rule 23(b)(3)'s expressly inclusive list of matters relevant to predominance and superiority. Moreover, Rule 23(b)(3) already directed consideration of likely management difficulties. An independent identification hurdle would both add to the prescribed prerequisites and displace the rule's existing balancing process. Slip opinion 8–10.

The court relied on *Amchem*'s admonition that judges could not substitute their own certification standard for one produced by the prescribed rulemaking process. It did not excuse plaintiffs from demonstrating the actual Rule 23 requirements. Footnote 4 explains that the Ninth Circuit had handled class-definition problems through those requirements, rather than adopting the additional test ConAgra proposed.

## Three policy concerns and existing safeguards

**Administrative burdens.** Management difficulties belonged within the superiority inquiry, which compared class adjudication with other available methods. Subclasses and issue certification could help. A separate threshold would examine burdens in isolation and could prevent small-value consumer cases even when individual litigation offered no realistic alternative. The rule instead required comparing the costs and benefits of class adjudication. Slip opinion 12–14.

**Absent members and fraudulent claims.** Rule 23 required the best practicable notice, including individual notice to members identifiable through reasonable effort, rather than perfect actual notice to every person. Due process likewise permitted appropriate alternative notice. The court considered the risk that an unidentified person would lose an economically realistic opportunity to sue individually small in these low-value cases. Requiring perfect identification might protect a theoretical opportunity while preventing any recovery. Its discussion of cy pres supported that analysis within the court's existing jurisprudence; it did not approve a particular distribution plan in this case. Slip opinion 15–17.

The court also regarded fraudulent small-value claims and resulting dilution as manageable through auditing, claim administrators, sampling, follow-up communications, and other tailored techniques. Low participation could further limit dilution of actual claimants' recovery. Footnote 8 distinguishes possible effects on residual cy pres funds after legitimate claimants received distributions. These were reasons not to impose a categorical additional prerequisite, rather than a holding that fraud or inadequate notice could never matter. Slip opinion 17–18 & n.8.

**Defendants' due process.** ConAgra remained free to dispute standing, the representatives' credibility and purchases, certification prerequisites, liability elements, and later individual claims for payment. Certification did not alter substantive burdens or require accepting every claimant's assertion as true. Auditing and, in appropriate circumstances, limited absent-member discovery could protect defenses. The right was to a fair opportunity to contest claims, not to the cheapest possible identification procedure at certification. Slip opinion 19–22 & n.10.

The plaintiffs proposed aggregate damages based on a price premium attributable to the label multiplied by units sold. If liability could validly be calculated that way, identifying each recipient would concern allocation rather than the total owed. The court used that proposal to explain why membership verification did not necessarily determine aggregate liability; it did not in this opinion finally validate every aspect of the damages model. Footnote 9 also explains why a clear class definition could support a later preclusion defense even without identifying every member at certification. Slip opinion 19 n.9, 22–23.

## Judgment and scope

The court **affirmed** the district court's refusal to condition certification on the extra identification showing. It joined the Sixth, Seventh, and Eighth Circuits in rejecting the standalone requirement as described in the opinion, while discussing the Third Circuit's different approach and qualifications in other circuits. That description is the judicial landscape reported in 2017, rather than an unchecked survey of current circuit law. Slip opinion 11 n.6, 23–24.
