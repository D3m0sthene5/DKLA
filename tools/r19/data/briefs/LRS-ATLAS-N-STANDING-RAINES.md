# Raines v. Byrd

**521 U.S. 811 (1997) — Supreme Court of the United States — June 26, 1997.** Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote for six Justices; Justice Souter concurred in the judgment, joined by Justice Ginsburg, who also joined the Court's opinion. Justices Stevens and Breyer separately dissented. The judgment was **7–2**. Assigned account: November second casebook, physical PDF pages 123–125, printed pages 1180–1182.

Six legislators lacked Article III standing to challenge the Line Item Veto Act based on its asserted dilution of their legislative power. They alleged an abstract institutional injury, rather than deprivation of a personal entitlement or complete nullification of decisive votes. The Act's express authorization for congressional plaintiffs removed prudential obstacles but could not supply missing constitutional injury. The Court vacated the judgment declaring the statute unconstitutional and required dismissal without deciding its constitutionality. (521 U.S. at 818–830 & n.3.)

## The Act and the lawsuit

Four Senators and two House members who had voted against the Act in the 104th Congress brought this suit. The Senate passed it 69–31 on March 27, 1996, and the House passed the identical bill 232–177 the next day. President Clinton signed it April 4. It took effect January 1, 1997; the legislators sued the Treasury Secretary and OMB Director on January 2. Senator Hatfield had retired at the end of the previous Congress, a qualification noted in the report's description of the six plaintiffs. (814 & n.1.)

The Act allowed the President, after signing a bill into law, to cancel specified discretionary budget authority, new direct spending, or limited tax benefits. He had to determine that cancellation would reduce the deficit without impairing essential governmental functions or harming the national interest and transmit a timely special message to Congress. Congress could enact a disapproval bill to nullify a cancellation, but it would pass through the ordinary presentment process and face a possible presidential veto. (814–816; 825 n.9.)

The legislators alleged that this arrangement changed the effect of their future votes, removed their role in repealing legislation, and shifted the constitutional balance toward the Executive. The statute expressly permitted any member of Congress or adversely affected individual to challenge its constitutionality in the District of Columbia, with expedited direct Supreme Court review. The district court denied dismissal, found standing and ripeness even before the President used the power, and granted the legislators summary judgment. It held that the Act violated the Presentment Clause and unconstitutionally delegated legislative power. Executive officials appealed directly. (815–818.)

## Constitutional standing and the claimed injury

The legislators argued that the immediate change in their lawmaking position constituted a concrete injury. Votes for a package of appropriations no longer ensured that the entire package would become effective if the President signed it: he could sign and then cancel selected projects. This altered both legislative bargaining and the meaning of a vote. The district court accepted that account of unwelcome subservience to presidential cancellation authority. The Executive challenged standing and ripeness, and the Supreme Court addressed standing first. (816–817, 824–826.)

The Court required personal, particularized, concrete, judicially cognizable injury fairly traceable to challenged conduct and likely to be redressed. Rigorous enforcement mattered especially when adjudication would decide whether another branch had acted unconstitutionally. Congress's invitation to litigate could remove prudential barriers and reduce concern about unwanted conflict with Congress, but it could not erase Article III requirements. Convenience and the wish to resolve an important constitutional dispute were insufficient. (818–820 & n.3.)

The legislators' claimed loss was institutional political power shared equally by congressional members. They had not been singled out or deprived of seats, salaries, or a private entitlement. In *Powell v. McCormack*, exclusion from the House affected a member's own entitlement to his elected position and salary. Here the asserted injury attached to the legislative office rather than the individual holding it. That distinction made *Powell* an inadequate precedent for standing. (820–821.)

## Why Coleman did not control

The plaintiffs relied on *Coleman v. Miller*, where twenty Kansas Senators voted against ratifying a constitutional amendment and alleged that a legally unauthorized lieutenant-governor tie-breaking vote nullified their decisive opposition. As *Raines* read it, *Coleman* at most allowed legislators with votes sufficient to enact or defeat a specific measure to challenge complete nullification of those votes. The historical *Coleman* standing majority included Justices dissenting on the merits; the Court explained that distinction in note 5. (821–823 & nn.5–6.)

The federal plaintiffs' votes against the Line Item Veto Act had been counted fully. They lost the vote; it had not been nullified. Nor had their votes been given less weight than their colleagues' votes. They remained able, with sufficient colleagues, to enact or reject appropriations, repeal the Act, or exempt particular legislation from it. An asserted change in the effectiveness of later votes differed from the complete nullification in *Coleman*. Treating those situations alike would extend that precedent substantially. The Court did not decide additional arguments distinguishing *Coleman* because it originated in state court and involved state legislators. (824–826 & nn.7–9.)

Historical practice reinforced judicial restraint. Past conflicts involving presidential removal authority, the legislative veto, appointments, and pocket vetoes did not produce immediate suits by officials merely alleging diminished institutional power. Constitutional review instead arose through plaintiffs with traditional injuries, such as the salary claimant in *Myers*. The Court acknowledged that other constitutional systems might permit direct institutional litigation; it found that American Article III courts had operated within a more restricted model. (826–829.)

## Judgment, qualifications, and separate opinions

The legislators lacked authority to represent either House; congressional amici opposed their suit's merits while expressly taking no position on standing. The Court treated institutional opposition as relevant, not as an independent jurisdictional test. Political remedies remained available, and denying these plaintiffs standing did not foreclose a challenge by someone personally harmed by an actual cancellation. Whether different circumstances would alter the outcome was reserved. Note 11 also questioned whether injury was traceable to these Executive defendants rather than to colleagues who passed the statute. (818 n.2, 829–830 & nn.10–11.)

The Court **vacated the district court judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss the complaint for lack of jurisdiction**. It did not uphold the Act, reject the constitutional arguments on their merits, or decide whether every institutional plaintiff always lacks standing. Rehnquist's opinion was joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Ginsburg. Souter supplied the seventh judgment vote; Stevens and Breyer dissented. (813, 830.)

Souter, joined by Ginsburg, regarded the distinctions between personal and official injury, and between abstract and concrete harm, as debatable under precedent. He nevertheless found the claim insufficiently personal and concrete in light of separation of powers. The dispute would enlist courts in both an interbranch power contest and an internal congressional disagreement. Awaiting a private beneficiary deprived of money by cancellation would distance judicial review from that immediate political contest while preserving an avenue to adjudication. His concurrence did not say official injury can never support standing. (830–835 & nn.1–3.)

Stevens viewed the Act as denying each legislator the constitutional opportunity to vote on the precise truncated law that survived cancellation. That was an immediate impairment of an office-specific right, comparable to voting injuries already recognized in other contexts. He rejected historical absence of suits as conclusive, noting that declaratory-judgment legislation postdated the Johnson example. He would find standing and affirm the constitutional judgment. (835–838 & nn.1–3.)

Breyer likewise found genuine adversaries, focused legal issues, and concrete impairment of legislative work. Official injury was not categorically outside Article III. The systematic threat to the lawmakers' votes was at least as immediate and serious as *Coleman's* isolated ratification controversy. Without overruling *Coleman*, he could not find this suit nonjusticiable. Unlike Stevens, he expressly reserved the constitutional merits for future argument. (838–843.)

Two discrepancies in the assigned account require correction. Printed page 1181 says the majority received five votes, but the official lineup and the Court's dagger note on 813 expressly include Ginsburg, making six; she also joined Souter's concurrence. The same assigned page describes the relevant *Coleman* senators' votes as votes to ratify, whereas the actual *Raines* discussion identifies votes **against** ratification. Later cases described in the assignment are separate decisions, not additional holdings of this 1997 judgment.
