# Dunlop v. Bachowski
421 U.S. 560 (1975) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and assigned setting

The Supreme Court held that the Secretary of Labor’s decision not to challenge a union election was reviewable under the APA, but that review ordinarily had to be confined to the Secretary’s statement of reasons. The reviewing court could not conduct an adversarial trial of the complaining member’s challenge to the underlying factual findings. The Court reversed the Third Circuit’s judgment insofar as it authorized broader proceedings, and directed a new remand consistent with that limited review. It reserved whether a court could ultimately order the Secretary to bring an election challenge. (566, 572–577.)

Justice Brennan wrote for eight Justices. Chief Justice Burger joined and concurred separately to stress the narrow scope of review. Justice Rehnquist concurred in the result in part and dissented in part, accepting a reasons requirement on another statutory ground but rejecting judicial review of the enforcement decision. The assigned material is editorial note 4, beginning near the bottom of physical PDF page 75 and continuing through the opening quotation on page 77 of the November second volume, printed pages 1058–1060. It discusses Dunlop as a statutory qualification to later nonenforcement doctrine; those later cases are editorial context rather than holdings of this 1975 decision. (561–562, 590–598.)

## Election, investigation, and administrative decision

On February 13, 1973, the United Steelworkers held district-officer elections. In District 20, incumbent Kay Kluz received 10,558 votes, Walter Bachowski 9,651, and a third candidate 3,566. Bachowski exhausted internal remedies and timely complained to the Secretary under Title IV of the Labor-Management Reporting and Disclosure Act. Similar complaints concerned five other districts. After investigation, the Secretary sued to set aside elections in two districts but declined to sue concerning District 20. A November 7, 1973 letter said that the investigative findings did not warrant a civil action; the complaint also alleged that an earlier telephone call had communicated the decision on November 5. (562–564 & nn.1–3.)

Under 29 U.S.C. § 482, a qualifying member could complain after exhaustion or the specified period of unsuccessful internal review. The Secretary had to investigate and, upon finding the statutory probable cause for an unremedied violation, bring the authorized action within the statutory period. The challenged election was presumed valid meanwhile. Prior Supreme Court decisions interpreted the enforcement scheme to permit the Secretary to assess both probable violation and probable effect on the election’s outcome. A violation alone therefore did not automatically establish that an election should be challenged. (563 n.2, 568–571.)

The complete report includes the Secretary’s subsequent reasons statement as an appendix. It described a district of approximately 67,419 members in 190 local unions, with eighty locals investigated, including all twenty-seven former District 50 locals. Investigators pursued Bachowski’s identified locations, additional random locations, and unusually high-turnout locations, and interviewed witnesses and reviewed records. The statement found irregularities in seventeen locals, including missing notices, elections not held, compromised secrecy, deficient ballot control, alleged ballot fraud, campaign expenditures, and tally errors. These are the Secretary’s investigative conclusions reproduced for later review, not Supreme Court factual determinations. (578–587.)

The Secretary used deliberately maximized estimates of affected votes. For instance, when a local with 249 members held no election, the estimate assumed that every member would have voted for Bachowski, despite the district’s much lower actual participation rate. Other estimates used the incumbent’s entire local margin or the potentially affected ballots. The statement totaled 884 affected votes against the 907-vote winning margin and concluded that the disclosed violations could not have changed the result. It also mentioned the union’s objection to the way Bachowski pursued internal remedies. The Supreme Court did not adjudicate the accuracy or legal sufficiency of that reasoning; it left the statement’s adequacy initially to the district court. (580–589; 577.)

## Litigation and the issue presented

Bachowski sued the Secretary and the union in the Western District of Pennsylvania on November 7, 1973, seeking a declaration that the refusal was arbitrary and capricious and an order requiring an election challenge. The district court dismissed after a hearing, describing its lack of authority and, in the formal order, lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The Supreme Court regarded the distinction between jurisdiction and reviewability as immaterial to its result. (563–565 & n.4.)

The Third Circuit reversed. It found jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1337, treated the refusal as final agency action with no adequate alternative judicial remedy, and rejected the APA exceptions for statutory preclusion and action committed to discretion. It required a sufficiently specific reasons statement to enable Bachowski to challenge the factual basis of the decision. Its original July 26, 1974 opinion expressly allowed examination of the data and reports; a September 3 amendment substituted the language reproduced in the Supreme Court opinion and acknowledged possible privilege or confidentiality. The Supreme Court nevertheless understood the amended opinion to permit an impermissibly broad factual trial. (564–566 & nn.5–6; 576–577.)

The Secretary argued that the statutory structure, objectives, history, and prosecutorial character of the action foreclosed review. Bachowski relied on the APA and the Secretary’s statutory duty to vindicate union-election rights. The Supreme Court agreed that jurisdiction and review existed but disagreed with the lower court about their scope. This combination explains the limited reversal: reviewability survived, while the authorized remand proceedings changed. (566–568.)

## Majority’s reviewability reasoning

Section 483 made the statutory remedy exclusive for challenging an election already conducted. The Court construed this provision as channeling post-election enforcement through the Secretary’s action, rather than prohibiting review of the Secretary’s refusal. No provision expressly barred review. Under the Court’s then-stated presumption favoring review of final agency action, the Secretary bore a heavy burden of showing clear and convincing evidence of congressional intent to foreclose it. The majority found no indication that Congress had even considered total preclusion and rejected the prosecutorial-discretion argument, incorporating the Third Circuit’s reasons in footnote 7. (566–568; n.7 at 567.)

That rejection did not mean unrestricted judicial intervention. Congress chose a centralized, expert enforcement system to protect unions against frivolous litigation, resolve uncertainty over elected officers promptly, preserve union self-government, and vindicate a public interest extending beyond an individual losing candidate. The Secretary’s resources and discretion were meant to shape investigation and enforcement. Courts therefore could not substitute their assessment of probable violation or electoral effect for the Secretary’s. (568–571 & n.8.)

## Reasons statement and permissible scope of review

Meaningful but limited review required a statement explaining the grounds and essential facts underlying the Secretary’s inferences. The statement informed both the court and the complaining member, encouraged consideration of relevant points, and restrained reliance on irrelevancies. The Secretary’s role in enforcing members’ rights did not give a member the ordinary client’s prerogative to demand litigation. Reasons supplied accountability without transferring enforcement control to the claimant or court. (571–574.)

Ordinarily the court should ask whether the reasons statement itself revealed an irrational, arbitrary, or capricious decision. It should not hear an adversarial factual challenge to the findings that no violations occurred or that violations lacked an effect on the outcome. If the stated basis was rational and defensible, the inquiry ended. Detailed findings were unnecessary, but the initial conclusory letter did not disclose enough to perform the required review. The majority allowed that the letter might satisfy the APA’s brief-denial-notice provision, § 555(e), while failing the more informative reasons requirement it derived from the LMRDA. (572–574 & n.10.)

Rare cases could warrant inquiry beyond the statement: a declared refusal to enforce Title IV at all, constitutionally discriminatory enforcement, or conduct plainly beyond or defiant of the Act were examples. Footnote 9 expressly noted that the present complaint did not raise the broader allegation of departmental hostility to union reformers mentioned by an amicus. The examples therefore defined possible exceptions without finding one established here. (571 n.9; 574.)

If the statement adequately showed a lawful decision, the court should dismiss the member’s suit. If it inadequately disclosed the reasons, the Secretary could supplement it, as in the procedures discussed in footnote 11. The court had to avoid endless litigation over explanatory sufficiency. If the statement instead established an arbitrary refusal, the Court assumed the Secretary would act appropriately after judicial advice. It expressly reserved coercive relief compelling a lawsuit, including the union’s separation-of-powers and Article III adversity objections discussed in footnote 12. (574–576 & nn.11–12.)

## Application, separate writings, and precise result

Bachowski’s allegation that the investigation substantiated violations did not mean that the Secretary had also found an outcome-changing effect. Footnote 13 explains this distinction while acknowledging the pleading posture. The Third Circuit had nevertheless opened the way to a factual trial, contrary to the limited statutory scheme. Meanwhile, the Secretary had furnished the appended reasons statement pursuant to a remand order whose propriety the parties conceded. The district court was to assess that statement initially under the newly specified limits. (576–577 & n.13.)

Burger stressed that this statute called for review narrower than ordinary arbitrary-and-capricious review in most administrative settings. He understood intervention to require a statement showing outcome-affecting irregularities coupled with a refusal to sue that had no rational justification. His concurrence did not authorize a general judicial reconsideration of election evidence. (590–591.)

Rehnquist objected that the Court addressed a reasons obligation that the Secretary no longer contested, while reserving the question the petition actually presented—whether courts could compel an enforcement action. He accepted an explanation requirement under APA § 555(e), independently of judicial review. Although he agreed that § 483 did not expressly preclude the action against the Secretary, he considered the enforcement choice committed to agency discretion under § 701(a)(2). The statute’s exclusive machinery and prior decisions, in his view, deliberately balanced union members’ rights against harassment and left the Secretary in control of post-election litigation. He would reverse the holding that the refusal was judicially reviewable. (591–598 & sole note 591–592.)

The Court reversed only insofar as the Third Circuit directed proceedings under its broader opinion and required a replacement remand conforming to the Supreme Court’s limits. It did not set aside the election, order a new election, require the Secretary to sue, or declare the appended reasons sufficient. The significance lies in the combination of reviewability, a meaningful explanation, and a sharply constrained reviewing role shaped by this particular enforcement statute. (566, 577.)
