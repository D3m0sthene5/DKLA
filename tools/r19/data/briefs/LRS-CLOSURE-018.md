# Allied-Signal, Inc. v. U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission
988 F.2d 146 (D.C. Cir. 1993) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, questions, and mixed remedy

On March 16, 1993, Stephen F. Williams wrote a unanimous D.C. Circuit opinion with Silberman and Douglas Ginsburg in consolidated Nos. 91-1407, 91-1435, 92-1001, and 92-1019. Allied-Signal and Combustion Engineering challenged NRC's generic-fee allocation rule and denials of exemptions. The court remanded two allocation issues for a reasoned explanation without vacating the rule, rejected another allocation challenge, and directly required an exemption for Combustion's duplicative license assessment. No judge wrote separately. The assigned remand-without-vacatur test arose within this concrete fee dispute. (148–154.)

## Statutory recovery program and regulated firms

NRC had long collected fees for particular services under the Independent Offices Appropriation Act. Beginning in 1986, Congress also required recovery of a percentage of its annual budget, allowing fees for generic expenses such as rulemaking and safety research rather than only identifiable individual services. Earlier generic fees fell mainly on nuclear power reactors. The 1990 legislation increased recovery to one hundred percent for 1991–1995, directing fair and equitable allocation among licensees and, to the maximum practicable extent, a reasonable relationship to regulatory costs. After notice and comment, NRC issued its 1991 rule charging virtually all licensees. (148–149 & n. 1.)

Allied converted uranium hexafluoride and competed with foreign converters outside NRC's fee regime. It alleged that bids could turn on one cent per pound while the new fees added nearly five cents, making price increases commercially impossible. Combustion faced a related inability to recoup expenses because of long-term fixed-price contracts. The agency did not dispute those factual assertions. Allied also challenged equal generic charges despite the other domestic converter's much greater regulatory problems. Separate disputes concerned low-level waste disposal costs and Combustion's two licenses for allegedly equivalent aggregate production capacity. (149–154.)

## Pass-through limitations required consistent treatment

Allied argued that Congress's equitable-allocation direction and legislative history required protection for firms unable to pass fees to customers. The court found no unambiguous command to exempt every such class. Earlier managers' statements required taking limited pass-through ability into account, not making it invariably controlling; the later statute left discretion to allocate fees among nonreactor licensees. Under the then-governing Chevron analysis, the remaining question was whether NRC's approach was reasonable. (149–150.)

NRC offered two explanations for ignoring Allied's claim. First, reducing one group's fees would shift expenses to others under the full-recovery mandate. That rationale conflicted with NRC's actual exemption for nonprofit educational institutions. Second, assessing the price elasticities governing pass-through ability for nine thousand licensees was genuinely difficult. That justified rejecting a rigid universal duty to calculate elasticities, but did not excuse declining a reasonably accurate and economical inquiry for particular classes. NRC had found educational institutions unable to pass costs through without explaining why it could not similarly evaluate domestic converters. The inconsistent handling made the denial unreasoned. Notes 2 and 3 examine the scope and possible basis of the educational exemption rather than treating it as automatic precedent for every firm. (149–150 & nn. 2–3.)

## Why remand did not entail vacatur

An inadequately supported rule need not invariably be vacated. The choice depends on the seriousness of its deficiencies, including doubt whether the agency chose correctly, and the disruptive consequences of an interim change that might itself later change. For pass-through treatment, NRC might supply a coherent distinction based on education's unusually large external benefits. The court offered that possibility without deciding that it actually justified the exemption. There was a serious possibility of lawful substantiation on remand. (150–151.)

Vacatur, even confined to converters' exemption treatment, would require refunding collected fees that NRC apparently could not recover under a later retroactive rule. Combining possible justification with disruptive consequences, the court left the rule intact while requiring a reasoned treatment of pass-through exemption claims. NRC also had to address Combustion's fixed-price-contract claim. This was not an order granting Allied an exemption or accepting NRC's current explanation. (151.)

## General within-class allocation was defensible

Allied's demand that generic converter fees track each converter's individual regulatory demands or service-specific fees failed. It did not contest classification of the challenged costs as generic. Even effort initially prompted by one firm could produce research or rules benefiting all firms in its class. Service-specific costs were not a reliable measure of those shared benefits. Group-level allocation had lower administrative costs, while finer allocation created measurement difficulties and ignored spillovers. Note 4 rejected Combustion's parallel general challenge for the same reasons. (151–152; 153 n. 4.)

## Waste-disposal allocation required another explanation

NRC assigned forty percent of its low-level-waste costs to materials licensees based on their share of waste output. It then abandoned that yardstick within the class and charged each large fuel facility $143,500 without a rationale beyond its belief that charges should be identical. If benefits depended on output, that appeared relevant within the class too; if the true benefit was merely disposal availability, that rationale conflicted with allocation between classes. Output data apparently already existed, making the computation straightforward. The court found this distinction inadequately explained. (152.)

Here it gave little weight to the prospect of a persuasive explanation, but again declined vacatur. Total refunds would confer a windfall even on firms advantaged by the old allocation, and the $3.8 million of generic costs apparently could then be recovered from no one. If remand produced usage-based allocation, firms whose correct liability was lower should receive only the difference. The court distinguished that limited backward adjustment, identifying the legally excessive portion of the old fee, from imposing an entirely new retroactive assessment. (152–153.)

## Double licensing required an actual exemption

Combustion operated two separately licensed low-enriched-uranium facilities. It asserted, without dispute, that together they were operationally equivalent to other firms' single facilities and that historical acquisition explained the extra license. The equal-fee-per-license rule itself could reasonably address ordinary cases and reserve unusual circumstances for exemptions. It did not need to incorporate this rare situation into its general formulation. (153.)

But NRC's exemption criteria contemplated significantly disproportionate cost allocations. The second license added $836,500 while NRC identified only essentially minimal additional burdens: mailing a second set of materials and two regional offices handling allegations. Those burdens did not justify the extra assessment. Note 5 treated the listed exemption factors as independent considerations rather than conjunctive requirements. The court found the denial arbitrary and capricious and directed NRC to grant an exemption for fees attributable to double licensing. It did not merely invite reconsideration on this issue. (153–154 & n. 5.)

## Exact closing judgment and scope

The court remanded for reasoned, coherent treatment of pass-through claims and generic waste-cost allocation, and separately ordered Combustion's double-license exemption. Note 6 explained why Allied's separate exemption request did not need additional adjudication: its pass-through and waste components tracked remanded issues, and licensee-specific calibration failed on the merits. The general fee rule was not vacated. (154 & n. 6.)
