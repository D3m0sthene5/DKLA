CONTRACTS: OFFER AND ACCEPTANCE
Wucherpfennig v. Dooley
351 N.W.2d 443 (1984). Supreme Court of North Dakota. Sand, J. Decided July 11, 1984.
The North Dakota Supreme Court held that Donald Wucherpfennig failed to accept Elizabeth Dooley's offer
to sell her interest in inherited farmland before she revoked it. The response sent through the estate's
attorney expressed readiness to proceed and sought further information, but it did not communicate a
definite present commitment to buy on the offered terms. The court affirmed dismissal of Donald's claim for
specific performance, the remedy that would have required Elizabeth to convey her interest. Donald was the
plaintiff and appellant; Elizabeth, his sister, was the defendant and appellee. Their sister Louise Grettum and
persons claiming interests in the land were also named defendants, although the disputed sale concerned
Elizabeth's share. 351 N.W.2d at 443-45.
The siblings inherited a family farm consisting of four quarters of land after their father died in 1964 and
their mother died in 1977. Donald received an undivided two-thirds interest in the quarter containing the
farmstead, while Elizabeth and Louise each received an undivided one-sixth interest in that quarter. Each
sibling received an undivided one-third interest in each of the other three quarters. The unequal fractional
interests matter because the parties were negotiating over Elizabeth's total ownership interest, and a price per
acre had to be translated into the amount due for that interest.
During probate of their mother's estate, Elizabeth indicated that she wanted to sell her share to Donald. On
January 4, 1979, she wrote Robert Case, the attorney handling the probate, offering to sell at $200 per acre if
Donald wanted to buy, provided the transaction was for cash and handled promptly. The parties did not
dispute that this letter was an offer. Case replied on January 13 that Donald was interested in purchasing her
interest. That expression of interest preceded the communication on which Donald ultimately relied as his
acceptance.
On February 17, Case wrote that Donald had arranged financing through the Federal Land Bank and that
they were ready to proceed with the transaction. He then asked Elizabeth to state the exact amount she
expected for her interest in the land. He also asked whether she would sign an agreement relating to Special
Use Valuation and requested a response on both subjects. Elizabeth did not answer. On March 9, she
revoked her offer to sell at $200 per acre. Donald contended that the February 17 letter had already created a
contract and that the revocation therefore came too late. 351 N.W.2d at 443-44.
Donald sought specific performance of the alleged sale contract and, alternatively, partition of the jointly
owned property. Partition would address the co-owners' interests independently of the claimed agreement to
buy Elizabeth's share. By consent, the parties tried the specific-performance claim first. The district court
found that Donald had not accepted Elizabeth's offer and that, even if an acceptance existed, the supposed
contract could not be specifically enforced. It gave four independently sufficient grounds for dismissal. On
appeal, Donald therefore needed to defeat every independent ground to obtain reversal. The Supreme Court
agreed with the lack-of-acceptance ground and expressly declined to decide the remaining issues. Its
affirmance concerned dismissal of specific performance; the reported decision does not resolve the
alternative partition claim. 351 N.W.2d at 444.

Wucherpfennig v. Dooley (continued)
The controlling question was whether the February 17 letter expressed assent that would itself complete the
bargain on Elizabeth's terms, or whether it contemplated further negotiation before either side became
bound. Applying N.D. Century Code § 9-03-21 and its precedents, the court required an absolute,
unequivocal, and unconditional acceptance. The offer and acceptance had to concern the same bargain, and
the response had to express an intention to create the contract through that response itself. The court drew
that formulation from Cooke v. Blood Systems, Inc., Grossman v. McLeish Ranch, Greenberg v. Stewart,
and Minnesota decisions including Markmann v. H.A. Bruntjen Co. and Minar v. Skoog.
The court read the letter as a whole. Arranging financing showed that Donald was preparing to buy, and
readiness to proceed showed continued interest. The immediate request for the amount Elizabeth expected,
however, indicated that a material aspect of the proposed exchange remained open. The court understood the
letter as movement toward a future agreement. Donald had not unequivocally committed himself to the
transaction described in Elizabeth's offer. The letter's additional inquiry concerning Special Use Valuation
formed part of the correspondence, but the court did not announce a separate holding that such an inquiry
automatically defeats acceptance in every transaction. 351 N.W.2d at 444.
Donald's own trial testimony strengthened that reading. When asked why the February 17 letter did not
specify a total amount, he said the amount would have been $37,200 and acknowledged that Elizabeth
would have expected more. He also said that she would not have signed a contract stating the figure he
intended to pay. The court used that testimony as further support for its conclusion that the parties had not
assented to the same terms. The problem was therefore more substantial than leaving out a mechanical
calculation that both parties understood identically. Donald knew that the figure he intended to pay would
not secure Elizabeth's agreement. 351 N.W.2d at 444-45.
The opinion should be applied with care to a response that both accepts and requests information. A
response can accept an offer while also asking for a change or clarification, if assent is complete and does
not depend on the offeror agreeing to the request. The Restatement expresses that distinction in §§ 59 and
61: a response conditioning assent on different terms is a counteroffer, while a completed acceptance may
accompany a nonconditional request. In Wucherpfennig, the court found the threshold commitment missing.
It did not hold that every question following words of acceptance destroys the contract. Nor did it need to
classify the February 17 letter as a definite counteroffer; continued negotiations were enough to explain why
it was ineffective as an acceptance.
The transaction also illustrates the limit of reliance as a formation argument. Donald's financing
arrangements showed preparation, but the court did not treat them as acceptance of Elizabeth's cash-sale
offer. It resolved the case on the correspondence and supporting testimony, without deciding the district
court's other grounds. The reported opinion contains no separate dissent; the other justices concurred. The
full opinion is available at Wucherpfennig v. Dooley, 351 N.W.2d 443.
