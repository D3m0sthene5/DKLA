# Hendricks & Associates, Inc. v. Daewoo Corporation
923 F.2d 209 (1st Cir. 1991) | Contracts

## Court and dispute
The First Circuit decided this diversity appeal on January 10, 1991. Judge Cyr wrote for Chief Judge Breyer and Senior Judge Bownes, with no separate opinion. The court sustained liability for consequential loss caused by defective garments, but distinguished a foreseeable loss of a business relationship from sufficiently proven profits on future business. It affirmed $21,614.73 already withheld from Hendricks and required a reduction of the $375,000 future-profit award to $45,000 or a new trial on that amount. (209, 211, 221.)

## Facts and commercial arrangements
James Hendricks began a consulting firm in 1979 and started advising Champion Products about overseas apparel production in December 1981. His firm, incorporated in Massachusetts in 1983, eventually served eleven other companies. Champion sought competitively priced imports; Hendricks supplied expertise about manufacturers, customs, tariffs, and quotas. Two initial Daewoo orders had minor quality problems that Hendricks helped resolve. Champion then introduced its 1984 Stripe Collection and, with Champion’s concurrence, Hendricks selected Daewoo to manufacture it. (211; n. 1.)

For the Stripe Collection, Hendricks became an intervening buyer and seller, rather than simply Champion’s import agent. Champion ordered from Hendricks, which ordered from Daewoo and resold at a five-percent markup. No contract directly joined Champion and Daewoo. Champion funded irrevocable letters of credit negotiable FOB Korea, so both Hendricks and Daewoo received full payment before the quality dispute was resolved. Daewoo shipped directly to Champion in three installments. The first contained serious defects; Daewoo promised conformity in later shipments, and Hendricks reassured Champion. The second was also defective, and the entire third shipment was ultimately unacceptable. Latent defects in the third shipment’s pants appeared through repeated washing: seams opened and garments came apart. Inspection also found Korean rejection stickers in all three shipments. (211–212, 215 & n. 6.)

Champion issued debit memoranda totaling $213,486.61 for payments and inspection costs. It actually withheld a $21,614.73 credit owed to Hendricks. The record did not show that Hendricks ever became accountable for the remaining $191,871.88. Champion was not a party to this litigation. It also ceased doing business with Hendricks. Its former purchasing manager, Donald Sullivan, testified that their business had been increasing and would have continued increasing absent the Stripe Collection failures. Other contemplated clothing lines went to different import agents. (212, 215–216.)

## Procedure and parties’ positions
Hendricks sought the entire debit-memorandum amount and $150,000 for anticipated future profits. The jury instead awarded $275,000 for the debit memoranda and $375,000 for future profits. The district court denied judgment notwithstanding the verdict and conditionally denied a new trial on the debit damages if Hendricks accepted a reduction to $21,614.73. Hendricks accepted. The $375,000 future-profit award remained intact. Daewoo appealed, contesting governing law, foreseeability, causation, the withheld-credit award, and the evidence supporting future profits. (211–212 & nn. 2–3.)

The court applied Massachusetts substantive law under the forum’s functional choice-of-law approach. Hendricks’s Massachusetts incorporation, negotiations and communications involving Massachusetts, and payment through a Massachusetts bank outweighed the asserted New York delivery connection. Passage of title to Hendricks in Korea also complicated Daewoo’s description of New York delivery under these particular contracts. (212–213 n. 3.)

## Governing rules and review
Massachusetts UCC sections 2-714 and 2-715 allow an accepting buyer that gives seasonable notice to recover appropriate incidental and consequential damages. Lost prospective profits must be a natural and probable consequence of breach, reasonably foreseeable at contracting, and demonstrated with a fair degree of certainty. Section 2-715(2)(a) concerns needs of which the seller had reason to know and losses not reasonably preventable through cover or otherwise. Its consequential-damage rule operates alongside common-law requirements of causation and certainty. (212–214 & n. 4.)

Review of denial of judgment notwithstanding the verdict was de novo under a stringent standard favoring the verdict: the court could not assess credibility, resolve evidentiary conflicts, or substitute its weighing of the evidence. Denial of a new jury trial received additional deference both to the jury and to the trial judge; reversal required an abuse of discretion amounting to a manifest miscarriage of justice. Those standards nevertheless did not permit an award supported only by speculation about its amount. (214, 217.)

## Foreseeability and causation
Daewoo did not need express notice that breach might destroy Hendricks’s relationship with Champion. Foreseeability was objective: what a reasonably prudent seller should contemplate at contracting. Earlier transactions had shown Champion’s insistence on precise specifications, and Daewoo knew it was supplying garments for Champion through Hendricks. A rational jury could find that another delivery of severely and pervasively defective goods would probably end that continuing relationship and deprive Hendricks of later Champion contracts. The court did not announce that every defective resale shipment necessarily makes all future customer business recoverable. It relied on the magnitude of these defects, the prior dealings, and the known continuing commercial relationship. (214–215 & nn. 5–6.)

Causation was also supported. The coordinated Stripe Collection was important to Champion’s reputation and marketing; defects in its constituent garments made virtually the whole collection too risky to sell. Champion could be found to have lost investments in design, manufacture, promotion, and inspection, as well as expected sales profits. Sullivan’s testimony about increasing business and the transfer of future work to other agents supported the inference that the breach caused the relationship’s termination. The jury received an instruction requiring that natural, primary, and probable causal connection. (215–216 & n. 7.)

The $21,614.73 credit presented a concrete loss. Daewoo’s knowledge of the resale arrangements, payment terms, and delivery terms supported foreseeability that Champion would seek recovery from Hendricks when the goods failed. Hendricks’s records supplied a firm basis for the amount actually retained. The balance of the debit memoranda was not similarly shown to be an incurred loss. The court therefore sustained the reduced award and rejected both judgment notwithstanding the verdict and a new trial concerning it. (212 n. 2, 216–217 & n. 9.)

## Why the future-profit award exceeded the evidence
Proving that a relationship would have continued did not establish the profit Hendricks would have earned. Its financial presentation separated revenues from Champion, expenses serving all clients, and profits from the entire firm. Champion revenues were $75,000 in fiscal 1983 and $170,000 in fiscal 1984, while total firm expenses were $188,000 and $350,000. Hendricks allocated fifty to sixty percent of operating costs to Champion. Even allocating only half left Champion-account losses in both years. The firm had never demonstrated an actual profit from Champion; its highest annual profit from all clients combined was only $20,000. Thus its past performance did not support the claimed long-term profit stream. (217–218 & n. 12; 220.)

A pre-breach fiscal 1985 forecast supplied a limited alternative. Hendricks projected maintaining existing Champion business and adding $50,000 in revenue from a contemplated polo-shirt order, producing $220,000 in Champion revenues while total expenses remained $350,000. Allocating fifty to sixty percent of those expenses to Champion produced possible profit of $10,000 to $45,000. The forecast depended precariously on generating the additional revenue without additional expenses. Nevertheless, Hendricks had already priced and sampled the polo-shirt order, and Sullivan said the work had been done. That evidence permitted an inference that much of the relevant cost had already been incurred in fiscal 1984. The court considered the issue close but found enough support for a $45,000 fiscal 1985 profit award. (218–220 & nn. 11, 14–15.)

Nothing established profits beyond fiscal 1985. There were no later revenue or expense projections, no defined time horizon, and no rational calculation method. General testimony that business would have grown did not supply those missing dimensions. Neither foreseeability nor the prospect of increasing revenues allowed the jury to guess at years of net profits sufficient to reach $375,000. The court’s illustrative calculations in note 17 emphasized the gulf between the proven business and the scale of future contracts needed to sustain the award. (219–220 & nn. 13, 16–17.)

## Exact disposition and assigned significance
The court affirmed the future-profit award conditionally on remittitur of everything above $45,000. Otherwise, Daewoo was entitled to a new trial on the amount of those consequential damages. It affirmed the district-court judgment in all other respects and remanded for proceedings consistent with the opinion. This was not an unconditional substituted award or a rejection of all future profits. The separate $21,614.73 award survived. (221.)
