# Hanson v. Denckla

**357 U.S. 235 (1958) · Supreme Court of the United States · June 23, 1958**

## An estate plan with competing Florida and Delaware judgments

Dora Browning Donner established a revocable trust in Delaware in 1935 while domiciled in Pennsylvania. Wilmington Trust Company, a Delaware company, held securities as trustee. Donner kept the income for life, the power to change or revoke the trust, and the power to appoint who would receive the remainder. She also retained substantial control through an adviser whose approval the trustee needed for specified investment decisions. The trust therefore supplied a way to dispose of the property at her death while preserving considerable lifetime control. 357 U.S. at 238–239.

Donner moved to Florida in 1944. On December 3, 1949, she executed a will and an appointment under the trust. The appointment directed $200,000 to each of two trusts established for two of her daughter Elizabeth’s children, along with smaller appointments to a hospital and servants. The balance, worth more than $1 million at Donner’s death, went to her executrix and passed through the will for the benefit of her other daughters, Katherine Denckla and Dorothy Stewart. Donner died on November 20, 1952. Elizabeth Hanson became executrix. Id. at 239–240 & nn.1–2.

The will’s residuary clause included property over which Donner had a power of appointment that she had not effectively exercised. Katherine and Dorothy therefore had a reason to challenge the $400,000 appointments benefiting Elizabeth’s family: if those appointments were ineffective, that property would augment their shares under the will. They brought a Florida declaratory judgment action concerning what passed through the residuary clause. Elizabeth and several of her children were personally served, but Wilmington Trust and other nonresidents received mailed notice and publication. The nonresident trust companies did not appear. Id. at 240–241.

The Florida trial court initially held that it lacked jurisdiction over the nonresidents because they had not been personally served and the trust property was outside Florida. Nevertheless, it entered a January 14, 1955 decree against the parties before it, treating the appointment as testamentary and ineffective under Florida law. The Florida Supreme Court affirmed the result and went further: it held that the original trust was invalid because Donner had retained excessive control and that the appointment lacked the required testamentary formalities. It also reversed the trial court’s jurisdictional ruling, reasoning that its authority to construe the Florida will supplied jurisdiction over the absent defendants. Id. at 242–243.

Meanwhile, Elizabeth had commenced a Delaware action after the Florida litigation began but before its decree. The trust companies and almost all the interested parties appeared there. The Delaware court refused to treat the Florida decree as binding and, on January 13, 1956, sustained the trust and appointment under Delaware law. The Delaware Supreme Court affirmed and rejected full faith and credit to the Florida judgment because Florida lacked jurisdiction over the trustees and the trust property. The U.S. Supreme Court considered the Florida and Delaware decisions together. Id. at 242–243.

## The questions the Court actually reviewed

The central questions were whether Florida had jurisdiction to adjudicate the validity of the trust and whether Delaware had to recognize Florida’s judgment. The Court did not decide a separately proposed question whether Florida had to recognize Delaware’s decree: that claim had not been timely presented to Florida’s courts. Id. at 243–244.

The Florida matter arrived as an appeal, but the Court dismissed that appellate route because the parties had challenged the court’s exercise of jurisdiction rather than properly presenting the statutory constitutional attack required for that route. It treated the papers as a certiorari petition and granted review. The distinction changed the route to Supreme Court review, not the Court’s ability ultimately to decide the jurisdictional controversy. Id. at 244 & n.4.

The legatees also argued that the appearing beneficiaries could not assert the absent trustees’ jurisdictional rights. The Court rejected that objection. If Florida law required the trustee as an indispensable party before the controversy could be decided, the beneficiaries had a direct personal interest in whether jurisdiction over that party had been obtained. The case involved the authority to adjudicate, rather than a failure to give reasonably adequate notice or an opportunity to be heard. Id. at 244–246 & nn.6–10.

## Holding: Florida lacked both asserted bases of jurisdiction

Chief Justice Warren’s majority first rejected jurisdiction over the trust property itself. The securities forming the corpus were held in Delaware; nothing in the record established a Florida situs. The question concerned the validity of the trust and its corpus, rather than merely the location of a beneficiary’s interest. Donner’s Florida domicile and the possibility that invalidating her lifetime disposition would bring additional property into a locally probated estate did not give Florida jurisdiction over that out-of-state corpus. Otherwise, a probate court could use a local will as a basis for adjudicating distant property without the required affiliation. Id. at 246–250 & nn.16–20.

The Court next rejected personal jurisdiction over Wilmington Trust. The trustee had no Florida office, transacted no business there, held or administered no trust assets there, and had not solicited business there. The trust had been made in Delaware with a then-Pennsylvania settlor. Donner’s later move to Florida, the remittance of income to her there, and her acts of trust administration did not have the same relationship to the underlying agreement as the insurer’s California solicitation in *McGee*. Id. at 250–253.

The majority acknowledged the evolution from *Pennoyer* to *International Shoe* and the reduced inconvenience of interstate litigation. But convenience did not exhaust the constitutional inquiry. State judicial authority still required a connection attributable to the defendant. The unilateral activities of another person with a relationship to a nonresident defendant could not alone supply that connection. The defendant had to purposefully avail itself of conducting activities in the state, invoking its legal benefits and protections. Id. at 251–253.

An important premise was contested: the majority understood the litigation to attack the original trust’s validity, rather than merely a later Florida appointment. Florida’s characterization of the appointment as a republication might matter to its choice of governing law, but did not establish jurisdiction over the Delaware trustee. The majority accordingly separated the question of what law a competent court might apply from whether that court had authority over the defendant at all. Florida’s status as the dispute’s convenient center could not replace the requisite acts of the trustee. Id. at 253–254 & n.25.

## Indispensability and the competing judgments

Florida could constitutionally adjudicate the rights of parties over whom it had personal jurisdiction. But the majority read Florida law as requiring the trustee’s presence before adjudicating a challenge to the trust’s validity. Although Florida’s highest court had left that point unresolved in this case after sustaining jurisdiction, its earlier decisions supplied enough authority for the Supreme Court to apply the rule. The jurisdictional defect therefore required reversal even as to the appearing Florida parties. Id. at 254–255.

The same reasoning justified Delaware’s refusal to recognize the Florida decree under 28 U.S.C. § 1738. A judgment invalid in its rendering state for want of constitutionally required jurisdiction did not become binding elsewhere through full faith and credit. The majority also declined to hold the Delaware case while Florida reconsidered indispensability. Delaware had correctly resolved the matter on the available Florida authorities; delaying affirmance to allow the litigation’s sequence to change would depart from reviewing the judgments as entered. Id. at 255–256.

## Black’s and Douglas’s separate dissents

Justice Black, joined by Justices Burton and Brennan, viewed the relevant transaction as the appointment made in Florida by a Florida domiciliary, substantially benefiting Florida residents. In his reading, Florida had invalidated that appointment as a testamentary disposition lacking formalities, rather than the original Delaware trust. Donner retained effective control of the assets, and the trustee had maintained communications and business relations with her in Florida for eight years. Those circumstances, adequate notice, Florida’s responsibility for administering the estate, and the absence of a disproportionate litigation burden supported jurisdiction. He distinguished jurisdiction from choice of law but considered their connections closely related here. Id. at 256–261 & dissent nn.1–5.

Black separately objected to the disposition even assuming Florida lacked jurisdiction over the trustee. He would have held the Delaware case while Florida decided whether the trustee was indispensable in this particular proceeding. Immediate affirmance of Delaware, he reasoned, effectively destroyed the Florida judgment against parties over whom Florida concededly had jurisdiction and prevented Florida from answering its own unresolved joinder question. Id. at 261–262.

Justice Douglas dissented separately. He emphasized that the action sought to determine interests in intangible property, rather than to impose personal liability on the absent trustee. Because the trustee functioned as a stakeholder or agent closely identified with Donner and obliged to distribute as she directed, Douglas considered it in privity with her. Florida could permit the decedent’s representative to stand in judgment for that shared interest through a fair procedure. Florida’s strong connection to the decedent, will, and beneficiaries therefore supported the controlling determination. Id. at 262–264.

## Judgment and the assigned reading

The decision was 5–4. Warren wrote for the Court; Black’s dissent had Burton and Brennan, and Douglas wrote separately. The Court reversed the Florida Supreme Court’s judgment and remanded, while affirming the Delaware Supreme Court’s judgment. Thus the Delaware ruling sustaining the appointments survived; the Court did not itself formulate a new estate distribution. Id. at 256–264.

The assigned material, printed pages 103–106, presents an editorial account and extracts from Warren and Black. Its notes continue through printed page 107. The complete opinion supplies the omitted appellate-route and standing issues, the full analysis of the trust corpus, the competing positions on indispensability and judgment priority, and Douglas’s separate dissent. The historical holding requires attention to the defendant’s own forum connection; the disagreement also shows how characterizing the disputed transaction and the trustee’s role affected that analysis.
