# Raffles v. Wichelhaus
2 H. & C. 906, 159 Eng. Rep. 375 (Exch. 1864) | Contracts | Latent ambiguity and differing referents

## Facts and procedural posture

Raffles's declaration alleged a Liverpool agreement under which he would sell, and Wichelhaus and the other defendants would buy, 125 bales of Surat cotton, guaranteed middling fair merchant's Dhollorah, to arrive from Bombay aboard the Peerless. The buyers were to take the cotton from the quay and pay 17¼ pence per pound within the agreed period after its arrival in England. Raffles alleged that the goods arrived at Liverpool, that he was ready and offered to deliver them, and that the defendants refused to accept or pay. These are allegations in a pleading, rather than facts found after trial. The complete reproduced report sets out the declaration on PDF page 1.

The buyers' plea identified a different referent for the ship's name. They meant the Peerless that sailed from Bombay in October. Raffles did not offer goods arriving on that ship; he offered cotton on another ship also called Peerless that sailed in December. Raffles demurred to that plea, and the parties joined issue on the demurrer. The court therefore decided whether the asserted misunderstanding supplied a legally sufficient answer to the seller's claim. The report does not describe a jury verdict, damages assessment, or evidentiary trial establishing either party's actual intention. Reproduced report, PDF pp. 1–2.

## Issue and arguments

The question was whether an apparently intelligible written description—cotton to arrive aboard Peerless from Bombay—required the buyers to accept cotton from the December ship when their plea identified the October ship as the vessel they intended.

Milward, for the seller, argued that the agreement concerned cotton of a specified quality and quantity, rather than purchase of the ship itself. The seller was prepared to deliver cotton of that description aboard a ship bearing the agreed name. In his view, identifying Peerless merely allocated the risk of loss during the voyage: if the vessel were lost, the contract would end. Nothing in the writing specified a sailing date. The defendants alleged neither fraud nor misrepresentation, and their undisclosed intention should not contradict a written contract that appeared valid on its face. Reproduced report, PDF pp. 1–2.

Mellish, with Cohen, answered that the existence of two ships with the same name created a latent ambiguity. External evidence could identify the different ships meant by the two parties. If the buyer meant one and the seller another, they had not agreed on the same transaction. The court stopped Mellish at that point and gave judgment for the defendants. His explanation is counsel's argument recorded in the report; the judges did not issue a developed written opinion adopting each sentence of it. Reproduced report, PDF p. 2.

## Holding and judicial reasoning

The plea was a good answer on demurrer, and judgment was for the defendants. The per curiam announcement contains no extended reasons. The reported exchanges during argument nevertheless show why merely matching the ship's name did not settle the matter.

Chief Baron Pollock said that whether both parties meant the same Peerless would be a jury question. He then treated the carrying vessel as identifying the particular cotton purchased. Delivery of goods of the same description from warehouse B would not necessarily satisfy an agreement for goods in warehouse A. Similarly, naming a wine-producing estate would not make wine from another estate with the same name interchangeable. Baron Martin objected that the seller's position would impose a different contract on the buyers. Pollock also noted the October and December departures in response to the seller's effort to treat the two ships as equivalent. Reproduced report, PDF pp. 1–2.

Together, those exchanges and the disposition support the use of Raffles as a latent-ambiguity and mutual-misunderstanding case. The ambiguity emerged from the external fact that two vessels fit the written name, rather than from a facially obscure word. On the pleaded transaction, a shared name did not establish a shared referent. The decision should not be enlarged into a rule that every uncommunicated subjective preference defeats an otherwise established agreement; the report supplies neither that formulation nor the modern rules allocating the risk when one party knows the other's meaning.

## Disposition, court, and assigned context

The exact disposition was judgment for the defendants on the seller's demurrer. The report attributes the ruling to the court per curiam. BAILII's reproduction includes a report note identifying Pollock C.B., Martin B., and Pigott B.; it reports no separate writing or individual vote. The brief uses the year 1864 because the electronic header and the appended report date do not give a consistent precise decision date.

The casebook separates the very short judicial report from later commentary. Gilmore questioned whether the carrying ship mattered economically; Simpson's historical account describes cotton shortages, volatile prices, and the role of named ships in estimating delivery. Those competing historical explanations are editorial material, not factual findings or reasons stated by the court. Holmes's analysis and the later comparisons with Frigaliment and the CISG likewise provide interpretive questions, not additional holdings of Raffles. C&M assigned printed pp. 600–603.
