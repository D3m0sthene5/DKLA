# Czarnikow-Rionda Company v. Federal Sugar Refining Company
255 N.Y. 33, 173 N.E. 913 (1930) | Contracts

## Court and question
The New York Court of Appeals decided these consolidated actions on November 18, 1930. Judge Kellogg wrote the majority opinion, joined by Chief Judge Cardozo and Judges Pound, Lehman, and O’Brien. Judge Crane dissented in an opinion, and Judge Hubbs also dissented without a separate writing. The five-to-two court reversed the judgments below and ordered a new trial because the proof did not justify passing the wholesaler’s customer-settlement losses through to the refiner as special damages. (33–34, 50, 55.)

## Facts and contractual chronology
Czarnikow and Federal made contracts on September 15, 1919, March 8, 1920, and April 21, 1920, covering approximately 75,000 tons of fine granulated sugar to be delivered in installments from April through August 1920. Their arrangements linked deliveries of raw sugar to Federal with deliveries of refined sugar to Czarnikow. The first two sets of writings expressed sales in each direction; the third expressly required refining for a fee. Federal characterized all the arrangements as tolling contracts under which it was only a manufacturing bailee. The court doubted that characterization of the sale writings but left the issue undecided because it regarded the damage rule as the same either way. (37–39.)

Czarnikow had previously imported raw sugar and exported refined sugar. The September contract required export, but Federal later offered to permit domestic sales, with a payment adjustment reflecting the lost export drawback. Czarnikow accepted that modification by giving directions for domestic shipment. The March contract expressly permitted domestic shipments; the April contract did not specify export or domestic destination. Federal therefore contemplated possible domestic resales. The majority found, however, that it did not learn until May 18 that domestic resale contracts had actually been made, and it did not learn their precise terms until this litigation. (39–41.)

Beginning March 27, Czarnikow contracted with customers across the United States. All but one resale contract specified Eastern cane fine granulated sugar bearing Federal’s brand, rather than fine granulated sugar generically. Federal shipped directly to those customers under Czarnikow’s directions. Of approximately 1,670,000 hundred-pound bags, 29,691 contained discolored sugar rather than the promised quality. Customers rescinded and demanded advance payments back or sought damages. Czarnikow ultimately paid or refunded more than $310,000, incurred more than $30,000 defending suits, and recovered $442,484.03 from Federal including interest. (37–38.)

## Procedure and arguments
The trial court awarded the customer payments and litigation expenses as the measure of Federal’s breach damages, and the Appellate Division sustained that judgment. Federal argued that recoverable damages should instead reflect the difference between the value of conforming sugar and the sugar actually delivered at its refinery. Knowledge that a buyer might resell in the ordinary course, it contended, did not make it answerable for unknown resale terms, exceptional prices, and avoidable customer liabilities. Czarnikow relied on the known resale arrangement, Federal’s direct shipments, the claimed impossibility of substitution, and Federal’s later involvement in customer settlements. It also disputed whether Federal had preserved its objection to special damages. (35–38, 49–50.)

## Majority rule
The majority treated replacement through the general market as central. Ordinarily, a buyer that could obtain substitute goods could fulfill its resale obligations and recover general damages from the original seller. Special customer liabilities and associated expenses could instead supply the measure when circumstances known at contracting made it reasonably foreseeable that the seller’s breach would cause the buyer itself to breach a resale agreement. A resale contract need not already exist; a contemplated resale could suffice if the seller also knew circumstances preventing substitute procurement. Mere knowledge that the buyer was a wholesaler or intended resale did not alone establish the necessary special circumstances. Notice after the original contract was made did not enlarge the seller’s contractual damage exposure. (41–44.)

The majority did not resolve whether special-damage liability rested on an implied promise or was imposed by law. It discussed criticisms of describing damages as a fictitious second contract to pay for breach, then concluded that either explanation required the relevant knowledge of special circumstances. Its examples distinguished coal whose defects became apparent aboard ships at sea, uniquely branded salmon destined for foreign customers, and petroleum in special cans sent to Calcutta: their sellers knew why ordinary substitution would be unavailable. (42–46.)

## Application to the sugar transactions
Federal’s undertaking concerned fine granulated sugar, a standard commodity. The proof did not establish that it knew other conforming sugar would be unavailable, or that the general market lacked enough sugar to replace the defective bags. Czarnikow’s own undisclosed resale specifications narrowed replacement to Federal’s brand. If that restriction increased its losses, the majority would not charge Federal with the additional consequence without advance knowledge. It also observed that Federal’s refinery capacity was 30,000 bags per day, suggesting even branded replacement might have been obtainable. This observation was part of the court’s assessment of the proof, rather than a finding that timely replacement actually occurred. (46–47.)

The timing of the resale contracts presented a further difficulty. Both levels called for delivery by August, yet shipments to distant western customers could take more than twenty-one days. A shipment after August 9 might not be inspected and rejected until after September 1, when replacement within the agreed month was impossible. The majority regarded that exposure as an undisclosed consequence of the way Czarnikow had framed its own subcontracts. Federal could reasonably expect the buyer to leave room to obtain substitutes if an initial delivery failed. Thus, either undisclosed special circumstances prevented replacement, in which case the resulting special loss was not chargeable to Federal, or replacement was possible, in which case the buyer was limited to general damages. (47–48.)

Federal had preserved this issue by objecting when the first witness testified about a customer payment, specifying that damages should be the difference between what Czarnikow should have received at Yonkers and what it actually received. The trial court overruled the objection, and Federal took an exception. The litigation below had substantively addressed that choice of damage measure. The appellate court therefore rejected Czarnikow’s claim that Federal had effectively conceded special-damage liability. (49.)

Federal’s later settlement negotiations did not establish liability for these unpaid claims. Czarnikow’s payments now in suit were not made until 1922, whereas Federal’s participation ended in 1920. The majority found no conduct or promise inducing Czarnikow to postpone market replacement until it became impossible. Substantially all the negotiations occurred in September or later, after the August replacement opportunity had already expired. Settlement of different customer claims did not itself impose liability for those now before the court. (49–50.)

## Crane’s dissent
Crane viewed the circumstances and Federal’s conduct differently. Federal knew it was supplying immediate domestic resale customers directly, selected shipment routing, knew the sugar market, and had the customers’ identities. The wholesaler had no opportunity to inspect before delivery. In Crane’s view, these facts supported the trial court’s finding that the resulting customer losses were within the parties’ contemplation. He did not regard exact knowledge of resale prices and terms as invariably necessary; Federal was itself a source of market information and could foresee the relevant price conditions. (50–51, 53–55.)

Crane also stressed agent Lowry’s authorized attempts to replace defective sugar or settle customer claims. An August 27 letter supplied a requested list of defective shipments for replacement before September 1. That course of conduct both evidenced Federal’s understanding of its obligations and, in the dissent’s view, estopped it from arguing that Czarnikow should independently have bought substitutes. Crane agreed that the legal objections had been preserved, but would have affirmed the trial court’s judgment on the merits. Hubbs joined the dissenting disposition; the report does not supply an additional Hubbs opinion. (51–55.)

## Exact disposition and assigned account
The court reversed both the Appellate Division and Trial Term judgments, granted a new trial, and directed costs to abide the event. It did not fix a replacement general-damage award, reject the existence of breach, or affirm reimbursement of the $442,484.03. (50, 55.)
