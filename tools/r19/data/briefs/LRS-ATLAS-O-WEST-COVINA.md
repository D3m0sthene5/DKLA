# City of West Covina v. Perkins
525 U.S. 234 (1999) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and vote

On January 13, 1999, the Supreme Court reversed the Ninth Circuit and remanded for further proceedings. Justice Kennedy’s seven-Justice opinion held that officers lawfully seizing property for a criminal investigation need not provide individualized advice about remedies for its return when those remedies are established in published, generally available statutes and decisions. The Court distinguished notice that a seizure has occurred from notice explaining the legal procedures available afterward. Justice Thomas, joined by Justice Scalia, concurred in the judgment, making the result unanimous while disputing the majority’s due-process basis for notice of the seizure itself. (235–236, 240–248.)

The Court did not decide the legality of the initial search or whether the State could permanently retain the property. Those issues were not contested at this stage. Nor did it decide a challenge to the adequacy of California’s return remedies as such. The dispute concerned instructions about those remedies and information allegedly needed to invoke them. (240.)

## Search, inventory, and unsuccessful inquiries

Police searched Lawrence Perkins’s home on May 21, 1993, under a valid warrant in a homicide investigation of a former boarder, Marcus Marsh. Neither Perkins nor his family was the suspect; they had not known of Marsh’s later involvement in the investigation. Officers seized photographs of Marsh, an address book, a shotgun, a starter pistol, ammunition, and $2,629. They left an itemized inventory and a notice identifying the search date, agency, date of the warrant, issuing judge and court, and officers to contact with telephone numbers. They omitted the warrant number because the warrant was sealed to protect the continuing investigation. A public court index nevertheless recorded issuance by the address searched and warrant number. (236–237.)

Perkins contacted one of the listed detectives and was told that return of the property required a court order. About a month after the search, he went to the issuing court, where the judge was on vacation. Another judge could not locate a matter under Perkins’s name. Rather than filing a written request, pursuing further inquiries, or returning later, the family brought a federal suit. That sequence supplied the factual basis for their claim that the remedy was inaccessible without further information from the City. (237–238.)

## Federal litigation and the Ninth Circuit’s notice rule

The original complaint alleged a search without probable cause, action outside the warrant’s scope, and a municipal policy allowing unlawful searches. The District Court granted summary judgment to the City and officers, but invited briefing on a due-process issue the plaintiffs had not raised: adequacy of remedies for return of seized property. The court initially did not decide that issue. On appeal concerning the Fourth Amendment claims, the Ninth Circuit remanded for resolution of due process. The District Court then found California’s remedies adequate and again entered summary judgment for the City. (238.)

The Ninth Circuit reversed. It accepted that a postdeprivation remedy sufficed and that California’s statutory and decisional remedies met due process. It nevertheless inferred from Memphis Light that officials must give notice of those procedures and the information necessary to use them. Its required notice went beyond the existing search particulars and contact information: it would explain how to contest retention, the need for a written motion giving reasons for return, and the warrant number or a means of identifying the court file. The Supreme Court reviewed that expanded instruction requirement, not a holding that the State lacked a legally adequate return process. (238–240.)

## Notice of the event versus published legal remedies

Kennedy explained that a meaningful opportunity to be heard generally requires knowledge that the matter affecting one’s property is pending. In this setting, reasonable steps to inform an owner that officers have taken property are necessary because the owner otherwise may have no reasonable way to learn who caused the loss. The family conceded receipt of the notice and inventory, so the Court did not determine precisely how detailed such seizure notice must be or when it must be delivered. (240–241.)

The rationale did not extend to individualized legal advice about procedures already publicly accessible. Once an owner knows the government has seized the property, public statutes and case law permit inquiry into remedies. California law allowed a motion under Penal Code section 1536 or section 1540, and the owners raised no independent challenge to the adequacy of those mechanisms. The City therefore did not have to add instructions about them to the seizure notice. (240–241.)

Memphis Light involved a materially different access problem. A utility customer facing cutoff had to be informed about an internal billing-review procedure whose contours were not described in publicly available documents. The opportunity depended on word-of-mouth referral. That decision could require information about arcane procedures unavailable in public sources, but did not impose a general duty to advise people of published remedies for protecting property. West Covina thus distinguished Memphis Light rather than overruling its holding or denying that procedure-related information can sometimes be required. (241–242.)

The Court also examined settled federal and state practice. Federal Criminal Rule 41(d) required a warrant copy and receipt, but no instruction on pursuing return of property. The Appendix collected federal, state, and District of Columbia laws concerning execution and return procedures. Kennedy found the Ninth Circuit’s expanded rule unsupported by that longstanding practice across jurisdictions. The collection was evidence for the procedural analysis in 1999, not an independent holding concerning every listed jurisdiction’s particular statute. (242–245.)

## The alternative warrant-number argument

The owners urged a narrower basis for affirmance: even without a duty to give legal advice, the City should have supplied the warrant number needed to obtain release. The District Court had expressly found no evidence that the number was indispensable. The plaintiffs possessed the date, agency, officers, issuing court and judge, and could identify the property. They had asked the court to assume that an actual motion would be rejected without a warrant number, but supplied no supporting evidence. That finding defeated the asserted factual predicate, so the Supreme Court did not further decide the narrower constitutional question. The opinion should not be read as holding that government may always withhold information necessary to invoke a remedy. (243–244.)

## Thomas’s concurrence, joined by Scalia

Thomas agreed that detailed notice of state-law postdeprivation remedies was unnecessary. He rejected the majority’s suggestion that procedural due process supplies the obligation to notify owners of property seized under a criminal search warrant, describing it as dictum and an unwarranted extension of civil procedural principles into an area governed by the Fourth Amendment. If constitutional notice were necessary, his view was that it would be part of whether executing the warrant was reasonable. (246.)

The concurrence examined common-law warrants for stolen goods, which required an inventory, and the criticisms of general warrants in Wilkes and Entick. That history made Thomas open to considering a Fourth Amendment notice requirement in an appropriate case. He did not finally hold that the Fourth Amendment required the particular notice here, or that it could never require notice. His first note acknowledged lower-court views against treating Rule 41(d)’s requirements as constitutionally compelled; the other two notes supplied the historical authorities. (246–248 & concurrence nn.1–3.)

## Judgment and assigned scope

The final order reversed and remanded. Seven Justices joined Kennedy’s reasoning; Thomas and Scalia agreed with the result and its rejection of legal-remedy instructions, but not the majority’s due-process foundation for seizure notice.
