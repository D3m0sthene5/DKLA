# Bollinger v. Central Pennsylvania Quarry Stripping and Construction Co.
425 Pa. 430, 229 A.2d 741 (Pa. 1967) | Contracts | Mutual mistake and reformation of a writing

## The missing topsoil promise

Mahlon and Vinetta Bollinger allowed the construction company to deposit waste on their property while working on the nearby Pennsylvania Turnpike. They maintained that the bargain included a protective sequence: remove the topsoil, deposit the waste on the exposed ground, and then replace the topsoil over it. They signed without reading the writing because they assumed that the agreed requirement had been included. It had not. Their case therefore concerned an alleged failure of the document to record an agreement already reached, rather than a request to escape an accurately recorded term merely because they had not read it. (425 Pa. at 431.)

The company's initial performance supported their account. It removed and set aside topsoil, placed waste underneath, and replaced the soil. After a period, it stopped following that sequence. When the Bollingers protested, the company relied on the writing's omission. The superintendent also explained that the relevant equipment had been taken away, and responded to a reminder of the original understanding that he could not help that. Similar topsoil treatment of neighbor Beltzner's property furnished further corroboration. (Id. at 431–432.)

The Bollingers brought an equity action asking the court to reform the writing to include the omitted paragraph and enforce the corrected agreement. After testimony, the chancellor granted relief, finding that they had met the heavy burden of proving the mutual understanding through undisputed corroborating conduct. The company appealed the decree. The opinion does not specify a purchase price, waste quantity, monetary damages award, or precise dimensions of the property, and none should be inferred. (Id. at 431–432.)

## Proof of a real mutual mistake justified reformation

Justice Musmanno explained that equity may reform the written evidence of a contract to make it correspond to the parties' actual understanding. The mistake must be mutual. A party's subsequent denial does not, by itself, prevent a court from finding that both parties' agreement was mistakenly omitted from the document. Otherwise the party benefiting from the defective writing could defeat correction merely by denying the defect. (Id. at 432.)

The court also preserved the ordinary force of a signed writing. A signer cannot escape merely by asserting failure to understand what was signed. The exception required proper evidence of an actual shared mistake, rather than a hypothetical or contrived claim. Here the decisive evidence was not the landowners' failure to read. It was the company's own conduct: it initially performed the very topsoil sequence the Bollingers said had been agreed and followed the same practice on the neighboring property. The chancellor reasonably inferred that the company would not have undertaken that work without agreeing to it. (Id.)

This reasoning explains the case's placement beside parol-evidence decisions. The court was correcting an instrument that failed to reflect the bargain, not enforcing an additional oral promise after deciding the writing accurately embodied the entire bargain. The assigned editorial notes discuss the broader treatment of challenges to a writing's validity; those statements should be distinguished from the narrower mutual-mistake holding in this opinion.

## The rehearing request and final disposition

The full opinion adds a procedural point omitted from the assigned excerpt. After the decree nisi, the company requested rehearing based on purportedly after-discovered evidence. The Supreme Court assumed, without deciding, that the evidence met that description, but concluded that it was not shown to be sufficiently material or relevant to alter the result. It was not inconsistent with the chancellor's findings. This was not a categorical rejection of all after-discovered-evidence motions. (Id. at 432–433.)

The decree was affirmed, with costs on the appellant. Musmanno wrote the opinion. Chief Justice Bell dissented without a separate written opinion; Justice Cohen did not participate. The listed panel also included Jones, Eagen, O'Brien, and Roberts, yielding the reported five-to-one alignment with one nonparticipant. The decision issued May 24, 1967. (Id. at 430–433.)
