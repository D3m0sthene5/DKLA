# Collatz v. Fox Wisconsin Amusement Corporation
239 Wis. 156, 300 N.W. 162 (1941) | Contracts

## Court, report dates, and result
Justice Fowler wrote the Wisconsin Supreme Court’s opinion filed October 7, 1941. The report’s heading lists September 9–December 2, while its closing notice specifically records denial of rehearing on December 2 with $25 costs. The merits judgment reversed the circuit court and directed dismissal of the complaint. No separate opinion or numerical voting lineup appears. The contestant could not recover half the automobile’s value merely because an unfinished contest left two unsuccessful finalists. (156, 159.)

## Contest and established facts
The theater publicly advertised a promotion in which the fifteen persons who most closely guessed the number of beans in a displayed jar could participate in a quiz. The quiz was to continue until all but one had been eliminated, with a new Chevrolet awarded to the winner. Collatz qualified, notified the theater that he would participate, was accepted, and competed. Those facts came from complaint allegations not denied by the answer. The case reached the Supreme Court without a bill of exceptions, so review rested on the trial judge’s findings and admitted allegations, rather than a fresh evaluation of the trial testimony. (156–157.)

The judge found that nine contestants actually participated. They drew numbers to establish questioning order, and Collatz drew the first position. Three contestants were eliminated in the first round, two in the second, and two in the third, leaving Collatz and one other person. Both failed to answer their respective fourth-round questions. Before the contest, the manager had announced that each contestant had fifteen seconds to answer or be automatically eliminated and that the car would go to the person last eliminated. After both finalists failed, he ended the contest and awarded the car to the other finalist, who had been asked the fourth question later. (157–158.)

The judge valued the car at $782 and found that the promotion’s intended winner was the person whose superior ability allowed survival through the intellectual contest. He interpreted the last-eliminated formulation to require a sole successful survivor, rather than a prize determined solely by the sequence of questioning. Because both finalists had failed and neither had become that survivor, he treated ending the contest as a breach and awarded Collatz half the car’s value. (158.)

## Claim and damages issue
Collatz’s theory was that the theater’s contractual obligation required additional rounds until one finalist correctly answered and the other failed in the same round. Ending the contest deprived him of an opportunity to win. The Supreme Court considered whether that asserted breach entitled him to ownership of the car, a half interest in it, or monetary recovery for the claimed half interest. It did not require the theater to establish that the other finalist had legitimately won. Instead, it asked whether the plaintiff had established a compensable entitlement of his own. (158–159.)

## Why the claimed half interest failed
Even assuming the contract was breached by not continuing, Collatz had not won the contest, won anything, or become entitled to any part of the prize. The court reasoned that it could neither assume nor prove that he would have won if the quiz had continued properly. The existence of two finalists did not, in its analysis, convert the winner-take-all promise into a promise that each already owned half the car. The theater’s wrongful selection of someone else likewise did not establish that Collatz himself suffered the claimed loss. (158–159.)

The court added that even if the chosen finalist was not entitled to the car as a prize, the defendant could give its property to whomever it wished; that transfer did not itself give this plaintiff an enforceable half interest. This reasoning resolved the damages claim presented. The opinion supplies no probability valuation, no alternative recovery of participation expenses, and no nominal-damages analysis. Its language rejects the claimed injury rather than pricing an independently recoverable lost chance. It should not be rewritten as an award of fifty percent of the prize or as an appellate finding that the selected contestant actually satisfied the advertised rules. (158–159.)

## Disposition and assigned comparison
The circuit-court judgment was reversed with directions to dismiss, and rehearing was later denied. C&M Book 4 physical pages 27–28, printed pages 1034–1035, recount the two-finalist dispute and quote the court’s inability to assume or prove that Collatz would have won. The quotation continues across the page boundary before the distinct Chaplin comparison. That editorial comparison frames a different approach to loss of chance; it is not a separate analysis supplied by the Wisconsin court. (159; assigned 1034–1035.)
