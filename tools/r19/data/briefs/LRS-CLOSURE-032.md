# Decker v. Northwest Environmental Defense Center
568 U.S. 597 (2013) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and procedural posture

On March 20, 2013, the Supreme Court reversed the Ninth Circuit and remanded, holding that the EPA’s permissible interpretation of its preamendment Industrial Stormwater Rule exempted the logging-road runoff at issue from federal discharge-permit requirements. Kennedy wrote for Roberts, Thomas, Ginsburg, Alito, Sotomayor, and Kagan. Scalia joined the factual and jurisdictional Parts I and II but dissented from the merits, making the judgment 7–1. Roberts also concurred separately, joined by Alito. Breyer did not participate. Scalia’s assigned criticism of *Auer* deference is his separate opinion, not the Court’s holding. (597, 599, 615–616, 626.)

## Facts and regulatory setting

Georgia-Pacific West and other companies harvested timber under contracts with Oregon in the Tillamook State Forest. Rain falling on two logging roads flowed through ditches, culverts, and channels into the South Fork Trask and Little South Fork Kilchis Rivers. The runoff carried sediment, including dirt and crushed gravel, with evidence of potential harm to fish and other aquatic organisms. In September 2006, Northwest Environmental Defense Center sued the companies and state and local governments and officials, including the Oregon state forester, for discharging without National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System permits. It invoked the Clean Water Act’s citizen-suit provision. (606.)

The Act generally requires permits for discharges of pollutants from point sources into navigable waters. Its point-source definition includes pipes, ditches, channels, and other discrete conveyances. The EPA’s Silvicultural Rule distinguished particular logging-related point sources from nonpoint activities involving natural runoff. A separate statutory exemption generally covered discharges composed entirely of stormwater, but not those associated with industrial activity. The EPA’s Industrial Stormwater Rule defined that category through conveyances directly related to manufacturing, processing, or raw-material storage areas at an industrial plant, and listed industrial classifications including lumber and wood products. (602–604.)

## Litigation and the amended rule

The district court dismissed for failure to state a claim, reasoning that the conveyances were not point sources under the Act and Silvicultural Rule. The Ninth Circuit reversed. It found jurisdiction to hear the citizen suit, construed the conveyances as point sources, and held that the industrial-classification reference unambiguously brought their discharges within the Industrial Stormwater Rule despite the EPA’s contrary interpretation. Permits therefore were required under its analysis. (606–607.)

Three days before Supreme Court argument, the EPA issued a final amendment specifying that covered logging operations were rock crushing, gravel washing, log sorting, and log storage facilities. That change was meant to clarify the scope of the rule going forward. The parties disputed both its effect on the pending litigation and whether the prior regulation already excluded the disputed runoff. The Supreme Court resolved the earlier regulation’s application without interpreting the new version. (604–605, 609–610.)

## Jurisdiction and continuing controversy

Defendants argued that § 1369(b), which channels challenges to specified EPA actions into the courts of appeals and imposes a filing period, barred this district-court suit. The Court disagreed. NEDC sued alleged violators to enforce what was at least a permissible construction of the Silvicultural Rule, rather than seeking invalidation of an EPA regulation. The natural-runoff language could be read as excluding unchanneled runoff while treating engineered conveyances as point sources. Whether that was ultimately the required reading did not have to be decided to establish citizen-suit jurisdiction. (607–609.)

The amendment did not moot the cases. The earlier rule governed past discharges, and proven violations could potentially support penalties, fees, or requested remediation and injunctive relief even if future discharges no longer required permits. The possibility that NEDC would lose on the merits or obtain no relief did not extinguish jurisdiction. Scalia joined these determinations. (609–610, 616.)

## Majority’s interpretation and deference

The Court first rejected NEDC’s claim that the statutory phrase associated with industrial activity unambiguously included the runoff. Industry could mean economic activity generally or narrower manufacturing and processing in factories. Congress had not foreclosed a more specific agency definition. (611.)

NEDC’s regulatory argument was more substantial: the rule listed the industrial classification containing logging and included access roads used to transport raw materials. The EPA nevertheless read the rule as addressing traditional industrial facilities, such as sawmills, rather than temporary outdoor harvesting operations. Its references to facilities, establishments, manufacturing, processing, storage, and industrial plants supported that construction. A road carrying harvested materials did not necessarily have the required direct relationship to the specified operations at an industrial plant. (611–613.)

Other covered outdoor activities, including mining, landfills, and construction, did not compel coverage of every logging discharge. The EPA could regard those activities as more fixed and connected to industrial sites, and the regulation’s general requirement still limited their scope. Under the then-applicable *Auer* rule, the agency’s interpretation needed to be permissible, not the only or best reading. It was not plainly erroneous or inconsistent with the regulation. (613–614.)

The Court also found the interpretation consistent with prior practice rather than a changed position or litigation-driven justification. Oregon already had detailed practices governing road surfaces, filtration, and cessation of operations when runoff visibly increased turbidity. The EPA could reasonably regard additional federal regulation as duplicative or counterproductive, particularly given Congress’s direction to consult state and local officials on stormwater management. (614–615.)

The Court concluded that these discharges were exempt under the preamendment Industrial Stormwater Rule. It consequently did not decide the alternative contention that the conveyances were not statutory point sources. The final decree reversed and remanded; it imposed no civil penalties and announced no constitutional invalidation of regulatory deference. (615.)

## Roberts’s concurrence

Roberts considered Scalia’s objections to *Seminole Rock* and *Auer* serious and potentially suitable for reconsideration. But the parties had addressed reconsideration only briefly in footnotes, and most amici had not discussed it. He would await a case properly presenting and arguing that basic administrative-law issue. Here, he agreed with deciding under the existing precedent. Alito joined this view. (615–616.)

## Scalia’s separate opinion

Scalia accepted jurisdiction and the continuing controversy but rejected the majority’s merits analysis. He would abandon *Auer* and determine the fairest meaning of the regulation through ordinary interpretation. An agency’s unexpressed drafting intention was not the enacted rule, and expertise in making policy did not establish authority to control the interpretation of language already promulgated. (616–619.)

His central constitutional objection distinguished statutory from regulatory deference. Whatever the rationale for *Chevron*, Congress’s ambiguous statute would be elaborated by another institution. Under *Auer*, the rulemaker also controlled its ambiguities, encouraging vague drafting followed by potentially retroactive clarification without notice and comment. That combination, he argued, offended separation-of-powers principles. The practical benefit of nationwide interpretive uniformity did not overcome the objection: an agency could amend its own rule much more readily than obtain a statutory amendment, as the new logging rule demonstrated. These propositions were his criticism of existing precedent. (619–621.)

On the merits, Scalia found the runoff discharged through expressly enumerated point sources. Treating engineered channels as natural runoff would contradict the Act, so he would read the Silvicultural Rule to preserve its validity. He also read the industrial classification as expressly covering logging and the roads transporting its raw materials. The last-antecedent rule supported applying the industrial-plant qualifier to storage areas rather than to all manufacturing and processing. Other listed outdoor activities and a narrowly specified classification exclusion reinforced that reading. He rejected the agency’s reliance on a general industrial gestalt or unexpressed intent. He would affirm the Ninth Circuit. (622–626.)
