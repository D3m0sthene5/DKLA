# Edmond v. United States
520 U.S. 651 (1997), No. 96–262 and five consolidated cases (May 19, 1997) | Legislation and the Regulatory State | Supervision and inferior-officer status

## Civilian appellate judges required constitutional appointments

The Coast Guard's intermediate military appellate court included two civilians, Chief Judge Joseph Baum and Judge Alfred Bridgman. The Department of Transportation's General Counsel, who served as the Coast Guard Judge Advocate General, originally assigned them to that court. Unlike commissioned military officers, the civilians could not rely on an existing military commission as their constitutional appointment. In anticipation of challenges, the Secretary of Transportation issued a January 15, 1993 memorandum adopting their assignments as his own judicial appointments. 520 U.S. at 653–654.

Ryder had previously obtained relief from a conviction affirmed before the Secretary's memorandum by a panel including the improperly appointed civilians. That decision required a properly appointed appellate panel but did not decide whether the Secretary's later appointments were valid. Edmond and five other petitioners presented the later question: their courts-martial convictions and sentences had been affirmed, in whole or part, after the Secretary's appointments. Baum participated in all six decisions and Bridgman in two. The Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces affirmed, relying on its post-remand Ryder decision upholding the new appointments. Id. at 654–655.

The Supreme Court unanimously affirmed the judgments for all six petitioners. Justice Scalia wrote an opinion joined entirely by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Souter joined Parts I and II on the history and statutory authorization, and concurred in the judgment while separately qualifying Part III's constitutional analysis. The constitutional rationale therefore commanded eight votes, with all nine agreeing that these appointments were valid. Id. at 652, 666–669.

## Statutory appointment authority was distinct from assignment

The petitioners first argued that the Secretary lacked statutory authority to appoint judges. Under 49 U.S.C. § 323(a), however, the Secretary could appoint officers and employees of his department and prescribe their duties. The judges were department officers, so the provision's ordinary language covered them even without specifically naming the Coast Guard appellate court. Id. at 655–656.

The petitioners invoked the more specific UCMJ Article 66(a), arguing that it gave the Judge Advocate General exclusive appointment power and displaced the Secretary's general authority. The Court rejected that reading because Article 66 described assignment, not appointment. As Weiss had explained, selecting a validly appointed officer for a particular judicial duty differs from making the constitutional appointment itself. A specific provision would control over a conflicting general one, but the asserted conflict depended on treating assignment as appointment. Id. at 656–658.

Constitutional avoidance supported the distinction. A Judge Advocate General was neither the President, a department head, nor a court of law, and Congress could not authorize that official to appoint even inferior officers. Reading Article 66 as an exclusive appointment provision would therefore make it unconstitutional. Reading § 323(a) to permit the Secretary's appointments still required deciding whether the judges were inferior officers, but that constitutional inquiry appropriately tested whether the congressional authorization could be preserved. Id. at 658.

## Significant authority identified an officer, not necessarily a principal officer

The petitioners' second objection was that the civilian judges were principal officers who required presidential appointment and Senate confirmation. Their adjudicative powers were substantial: they reviewed serious criminal sentences, resolved constitutional questions, and independently weighed facts and witness credibility rather than simply deferring to courts-martial. The Court accepted that importance but explained that significant federal authority separates officers from employees; it does not itself separate principal from inferior officers. Id. at 661–662.

Morrison's limited duties, jurisdiction, tenure, and removability factors did not constitute an exclusive test. These military appellate judges lacked the independent counsel's narrowly confined jurisdiction and task-limited tenure, but Morrison had not purported to establish a definitive boundary. Generally, the Court explained, inferior officers' work is directed and supervised at some level by officers appointed through presidential nomination and Senate consent. A superior must exercise a relevant supervisory relationship; mere higher rank or greater responsibilities elsewhere in government do not suffice. Id. at 661–663.

The structural concern was accountability. Appointment through the President and Senate was the default for principal and inferior officers alike. Congress could choose specified alternatives only for inferior officers, achieving administrative convenience without abandoning responsibility for the selection and supervision of those exercising public power. Id. at 659–660, 663–664.

## Divided supervision made these judges inferior

The Judge Advocate General prescribed procedural rules, helped formulate court-martial review policy, and could remove a judge from the judicial assignment without cause. But that official could not use removal threats to influence particular cases and could not reverse the appellate court's decisions. A second executive institution, the Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces, supplied decisional review. It reviewed death-sentence cases, cases certified by the Judge Advocate General, and cases accepted on an accused person's petition. Id. at 664–665.

That higher court did not freely reweigh facts when competent record evidence supported every element beyond reasonable doubt. Nevertheless, the limited scope of factual review did not make the intermediate judges principal officers. Their decisions could not become final on behalf of the United States without permission, through the review arrangement, of other executive officers. The combination of administrative oversight, assignment removal, and higher executive review established inferior status. Id. at 664–666 & n.2.

Freytag did not compel the opposite result. It had classified Tax Court special trial judges, not definitively classified regular Tax Court judges as principal officers. Regular Tax Court judges also lacked analogous executive supervision: their decisions went to Article III appellate courts, and no Judge Advocate General controlled their procedural rules, reassignment, or submission of decisions for executive review. Id. at 665–666.

The Secretary's appointments were consequently authorized by § 323(a) and constitutional appointments of inferior officers by a department head. The Court affirmed each petitioner's judgment rather than granting another hearing or invalidating the post-appointment decisions. Id. at 666.

## Souter agreed on the result while rejecting supervision as sufficient by itself

Souter considered an official superior necessary for inferior status, but did not regard the mere existence of supervision and control as sufficient. Some officers subordinate to department heads could still be principal officers; he used the Solicitor General as an example. He would examine the judges' powers, duties, tenure, jurisdiction, and removal arrangements together. Reaffirming his Weiss analysis, he found classification uncertain enough to defer to the rational determination of the political branches. That broader analysis led him to agree that these judges were inferior officers. Id. at 666–669.

This separate view should remain distinct from Scalia's controlling reasoning. Conversely, the majority's direction-and-supervision formulation should not be enlarged into a claim that any nominal supervisor conclusively makes every officer inferior; the Court expressly acknowledged that its cases had not supplied an exclusive criterion, and it examined the concrete combination of controls here. Id. at 661–666.
