# Shamrock Oil & Gas Corp. v. Sheets

**Civil Procedure · 313 U.S. 100 (1941) · Supreme Court of the United States · April 28, 1941**

Justice Stone delivered the Court’s opinion. The assigned account describes the decision as unanimous; the complete official report records no dissent, concurrence, or nonparticipation. The case concerns the original state-court plaintiff’s statutory eligibility to remove after the defendant asserts an independent counterclaim. The Court affirmed the Fifth Circuit’s decision requiring remand to state court. Its holding rests on the removal statute then in force, Judicial Code § 28, rather than an adjudication of the contract dispute.

## Facts and procedural history

Shamrock, a noncitizen of Texas, brought an action in a Texas state court to recover an alleged indebtedness. The Texas defendant asserted a counterclaim, also described as a cross-action, demanding more than $3,000 for breach of a contract separate and distinct from the indebtedness Shamrock sought to collect. Shamrock therefore faced an affirmative demand for damages despite having initiated the lawsuit. The Supreme Court’s opinion expressly identifies the asserted basis for removal as diversity of citizenship. It does not describe the counterclaim as a federal cause of action. **313 U.S. at 102–103.**

Shamrock removed the case to the federal district court for Northern Texas. The district court denied the defendant’s motion to remand and proceeded to a trial on the merits. It entered judgment for Shamrock on both Shamrock’s original claim and the defendant’s counterclaim. The Fifth Circuit reversed and ordered the case remanded to the state court, reasoning that an original plaintiff was not a defendant within the meaning of § 28. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve disagreement among federal courts about that statutory question. **Id. at 103.**

The Court assumed that removal had been timely and procedurally regular if Shamrock was eligible to remove. It also assumed that the counterclaim constituted an independent cause of action and exceeded the jurisdictional amount. Those assumptions removed procedural timing, the counterclaim’s independence, and the amount in controversy from the issues decided. The question was whether Congress had given this original plaintiff a right of removal at all. **Id. at 103–104.**

## Issue and holding

Does the statutory authorization permitting removal by the defendant or defendants include an original plaintiff who must defend against an independent counterclaim?

No. For the ordinary diversity removal sought here, Shamrock remained the original plaintiff and could not acquire removal eligibility merely because the state defendant asserted a counterclaim against it. Congress’s statutory language and deliberate narrowing of the earlier removal law excluded that route. **Id. at 104–108.**

## The competing arguments

Shamrock argued that its position should be judged by the substance of the counterclaim. Under Texas procedure, the counterclaim could operate as an independent suit and produce an affirmative judgment against Shamrock. From that perspective, Shamrock was defending a claim, even though the original pleadings designated it as the plaintiff. It relied on federal decisions allowing removal by a party defending a cross-action and argued that the older restriction recognized in *West v. Aurora City* should not control the later statutory scheme. **Id. at 104, 108; see also the separately printed counsel argument at 100–102.**

The defendant sought remand on the ground accepted by the Fifth Circuit: Congress had authorized removal by the defendant in the original suit, and Shamrock’s decision to bring that suit in state court did not become a statutory removal right when the defendant counterclaimed. The Supreme Court adopted that construction. The complete report prints Shamrock’s counsel argument separately from Stone’s opinion; factual assertions and characterizations in that argument should not be mistaken for additional findings by the Court.

## Why the Court rejected removal

First, federal law supplied the governing classification. Texas might characterize a counterclaim as a separate suit and its target as a defendant, but the federal removal statute operated nationwide. Congress intended its eligibility criteria to apply uniformly rather than change with each state’s procedural terminology. The Court therefore interpreted the federal statute on its own terms. **Id. at 104.**

Second, the statutory history showed a deliberate restriction. The Judiciary Act of 1789 had allowed a defendant to remove when entering an appearance. In *West v. Aurora City*, the Court had held that an original plaintiff facing a counterclaim could not use that authorization. The plaintiff had voluntarily submitted to the state court’s jurisdiction, whereas the removal right belonged to a defendant brought before that court. **Id. at 105–106.**

Congress subsequently broadened removal in 1875. The statute allowed either party to remove and permitted removal before trial. In 1887, Congress changed the authorization back to the defendant or defendants and replaced the broader timing provision with a requirement tied to the defendant’s obligation to plead. Stone treated these coordinated changes as evidence of an intended return to the narrower rule, rather than as accidental edits. Congress was presumed to know the earlier statutory history and the Court’s interpretation of the 1789 provision. **Id. at 105–107.**

The legislative material reinforced that inference. Footnote 2 reproduces the House committee’s explanation that a plaintiff ordinarily should abide by the forum it selected. The committee also described plaintiffs’ use of removal to extract concessions from defendants unable to bear the costs of federal litigation away from home. If Congress had intended to preserve ordinary removal for an original plaintiff confronted with a counterclaim, the Court reasoned, it could have supplied appropriate language. It did not. **Id. at 106–107 & n.2.**

The opinion nevertheless identifies a distinct historical provision concerning prejudice and local influence. Its treatment of that provision and the committee report demonstrates deliberate differentiation among statutory removal routes. The decision should therefore be understood as construing the ordinary diversity removal asserted by Shamrock, rather than as declaring that no statute could ever permit removal by a plaintiff under any circumstances. The relevant statutory text is reproduced in footnote 1. **Id. at 104–105 & n.1, 107–108.**

Third, Shamrock’s waiver argument confused possession of a right with loss of a right. Some lower courts had suggested that a plaintiff suing for less than the jurisdictional amount had not waived a later right to remove upon receiving a substantial counterclaim. Shamrock urged that the same reasoning applied even though its own demand exceeded the jurisdictional amount. The Court held the amount of the original demand immaterial. A party cannot acquire a statutory right merely by showing that it never waived one. Congress first must confer the right, and this provision did not confer it on the original plaintiff. **Id. at 108.**

Finally, the Court connected this reading to Congress’s policy of restricting removal jurisdiction. Federal courts must respect the independence of state courts by keeping their own jurisdiction within the limits Congress prescribed. A counterclaim’s practical importance could not expand those statutory limits. The Court also clarified that its earlier disposition in *Wichita Royalty* had not decided the removal question now presented, and it reserved consideration of factors present there but absent here. **Id. at 108–109.**

## Judgment and assigned significance

The Supreme Court **affirmed the Fifth Circuit**, whose decision had reversed the federal district court and required remand to state court. It did not affirm Shamrock’s favorable federal judgment on the debt and contract claims. The underlying dispute belonged back in the state proceeding because Shamrock lacked the asserted removal right. **Id. at 109.**
