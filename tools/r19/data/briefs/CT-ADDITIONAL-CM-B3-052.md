# Dzinglski v. Weirton Steel Corporation
191 W. Va. 278, 445 S.E.2d 219 (1994) | Contracts

## Court, issue, and result

On May 26, 1994, the Supreme Court of Appeals of West Virginia reversed a $500,000 judgment for intentional infliction of emotional distress arising from an employer's investigation and discharge of a manager. Justice Neely wrote the opinion. The reproduced report contains no separate writing or numerical voting statement. The employer's audit, paid suspension, limited internal communications, and private opportunities to answer the accusations did not constitute outrageous conduct. The employee's distress arose from the discharge itself rather than an outrageous manner of carrying it out. The final disposition is “Reversed”; it does not expressly order a new trial or dismissal with prejudice. (191 W. Va. at 278, 281, 286–288.)

## Employee ownership and the investigation

Andrew Dzinglski had worked for National Steel for twenty-five years when its Weirton operation became employee-owned in January 1984. Employees accepted substantial reductions in pay and benefits in exchange for stock. During the negotiations, their union proposed removing management employees considered detrimental to the new company and eliminating outside contractors whose work company employees could perform more cheaply. Valley Systems, an industrial cleaner with which Dzinglski dealt daily as a general foreman, was accordingly discontinued as a contractor. (Id. at 281.)

Security supervisor Richard McKittrick then received anonymous accusations that Dzinglski had taken kickbacks from Valley Systems. The caller eventually identified himself as Tim Lawson, a foreman who had worked under Dzinglski. Lawson alleged approval of payments for salaried supervisors who had performed no actual work, acceptance of food, fixtures, meals and travel, and permission to use Weirton supplies. He named former Valley employees William Baxter and Charles Fraizer as corroborating witnesses. Vice president William Doepken considered the charges sufficiently serious to investigate. Dzinglski denied wrongdoing when confronted in late May and was suspended with pay under the company's policy for investigations of management employees. (Id. at 281–282.)

The internal audit examined company records, while McKittrick interviewed the identified witnesses and prepared memoranda. According to him, the memoranda went to only four company officials. Baxter described deliveries of bacon, a bathroom vanity, bolt cutters and a camera, altered invoices, meals and access to a motel room. Some items had possible work-related explanations, including using the bolt cutters to clean former employees' lockers. Fraizer described the camera, use of company supplies and lunches. Valley's owner, Eugene Valentine, acknowledged gifts but characterized them as business goodwill; he said the camera was for work and the vanity for Valley's trailer office. These were accusations, testimony and competing explanations, not judicial findings that Dzinglski had committed crimes. (Id. at 282.)

Doepken reviewed the allegations with Dzinglski again in July, and Dzinglski again denied wrongdoing. Although witnesses confirmed some accusations and the audit uncovered some billing irregularities, Lawson's charges were never fully substantiated and there was no evidence of criminal wrongdoing by Dzinglski. The company nevertheless considered the accusations damaging to his effectiveness as a labor manager and honorably discharged him on October 31, 1984. (Id. at 282–283.)

## Pleadings, directed verdicts, and jury awards

Dzinglski sued in October 1985. An October 1987 amended complaint asserted wrongful discharge, promissory estoppel, breach of good faith and fair dealing, defamation, negligent investigation, violation of public policy, and punitive damages. At the May 13, 1992 pretrial conference, two weeks before trial, he added an outrage or intentional-distress claim. (Id. at 283.)

The trial judge directed a verdict for Weirton on wrongful discharge, estoppel and good faith because Dzinglski was an at-will employee and his discharge violated no public policy. The defamation and negligent-investigation claims also failed: the investigation and internal memoranda were qualifiedly privileged, with no evidence of publication beyond the privilege; defamation was additionally time-barred. The judge allowed the outrage claim to reach the jury, which awarded $500,000 compensatory and $150,000 punitive damages. On a post-trial objection, the judge struck the punitive award. (Id.)

Weirton appealed the refusal to remove the outrage claim from the jury and challenged both the late amendment and its timeliness. Dzinglski cross-assigned error to the striking of punitive damages. The Supreme Court reversed on the insufficiency of outrageous conduct. It explicitly acknowledged that this made the remaining issues unnecessary to the judgment, but decided them to clarify the law for the bar. Those additional rulings should therefore be distinguished from the ground requiring reversal. (Id. at 283, 287.)

## Discharge and the manner of discharge

The tort required intentional or reckless causation of severe distress through conduct sufficiently extreme to exceed acceptable bounds of decency. The court recognized the difficulty of defining such conduct and, drawing on the Restatement, noted that abuse of a relationship of authority could contribute to its outrageous character. Its sole footnote credited James Bleeke's article for the employment analysis. But the Restatement also protected insistence on one's legal rights in a permissible manner, even when distress was foreseeable. Neither an employment relationship nor a lawful discharge mechanically answered the separate question of how the employer acted. (Id. at 283–284 & n. 1.)

The court distinguished decisions permitting a separate distress claim for abusive conduct surrounding a discharge from decisions rejecting attempts to relabel the emotional consequences of termination. Farmer illustrated how distinct discriminatory and outrageous conduct could support separate causes of action. Magnuson and Viestenz, by contrast, involved distress attributable to discharge itself. West Virginia's Harless likewise recognized that an employee might have an outrage claim even without a retaliatory-discharge claim, while rejecting duplication where the separate tort's elements were not present. The operative distinction was between distress from the fact of termination, such as embarrassment and financial loss, and distress from an outrageous manner of implementing it. (Id. at 284–285.)

## Application and qualified privilege

Weirton had both a right and an obligation to investigate reported improprieties by a manager. Its record audit and witness interviews, paid suspension, internal memoranda, and private meetings allowing Dzinglski to answer the charges were appropriate measures. The court found the manner of discharge far from outrageous and the company's motivations proper. It accepted that Dzinglski suffered distress but attributed that distress to losing his job rather than to the investigatory process. The lack of fully substantiated accusations therefore did not, on this record, turn the investigation into the tort. (Id. at 286.)

The court also agreed that a qualified privilege supported the employer. Such privilege protected good-faith communications concerning a legitimate interest or duty, limited to people with a legitimate interest in the subject. Bad motive could defeat it. Here, the communications concerned the company's management interests and had not gone beyond the privilege's scope. The court upheld that finding as an additional reason the investigation could not support outrage. This was a qualified protection applied to the proven scope and circumstances of this investigation, rather than unconditional immunity for every employer investigation. (Id. at 286–287.)

## Amendment, limitations, and punitive damages

The court rejected Weirton's procedural challenges. Rule 15 favored a merits adjudication where the amendment caused no unfair prejudice and the opponent could meet the issue. The distress claim arose from the same investigation facts already at issue, and Weirton had not sought a continuance to conduct additional discovery. The trial court therefore did not err by allowing it shortly before trial. The amendment also related back to the original complaint: it added a theory concerning the same conduct rather than a new claim founded on different facts. It was accordingly not barred by the limitations period. (Id. at 287–288.)

Dzinglski also could not restore the additional $150,000 punitive award. Although the jury's special answer characterized the employer's acts as wanton, oppressive and reckless, the court drew on earlier decisions explaining that distress awards without physical trauma, arising from intentionally outrageous conduct, could themselves perform a punitive and deterrent function. Here the additional punitive verdict stacked punishment for the same acts on top of the $500,000 award. Striking it avoided that double recovery. The court addressed this question even though the underlying compensatory judgment failed for insufficient outrageous conduct. (Id. at 288.)
