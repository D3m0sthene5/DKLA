Citation
Securities & Exchange Commission v. Chenery Corp. (Chenery I), 318 U.S. 80 (1943). Supreme Court of the
United States, decided February 1, 1943. Justice Frankfurter wrote the Court's opinion. Justice Black dissented,
joined by Justices Reed and Murphy; Justice Douglas did not participate. Full judicial text: Full judicial
opinion . Assigned casebook: printed pages 536–544, October second scan, PDF pages 60–68. The later 1947
Chenery decision receives its own brief.
Parties
The Securities and Exchange Commission sought to defend its approval of a utility holding company's
reorganization on terms that treated management's recently purchased preferred shares differently from other
preferred shares. The respondents were officers, directors, and controlling shareholders of Federal Water
Service Corporation, including interests associated with the Chenery group. They challenged the restriction on
participation attached to the shares they had purchased during the reorganization process. The controversy
concerned the SEC's administrative order, rather than a private damages action for securities fraud. 318 U.S. at
81–85.
Material Facts
Federal was a Delaware public utility holding company whose assets included securities of operating
subsidiaries in thirteen states and one foreign country. Respondents controlled Federal through Utility
Operators Company, which owned all of Federal's controlling Class B common stock. Federal registered under
the Public Utility Holding Company Act in November 1937 and submitted a reorganization plan. Three
successive plans preserving participation for Class B shareholders proved unacceptable to the SEC and were
withdrawn. 318 U.S. at 82–84.
A fourth plan, submitted in March 1940, proposed a merger and cancellation of Class B stock. Preferred stock
and Class A common stock would become common stock of the reorganized corporation. Apart from 5.3
percent allocated to Class A holders, the new equity would go to preferred shareholders. Meanwhile, from
November 1937 through June 1940, the respondents had bought 12,407 preferred shares out of 159,269
outstanding, through brokers in the over-the-counter market. Those purchases, intended to protect their
interests, would give them more than ten percent of the new common stock. The proposed shares' book value
exceeded their purchase prices, although the Court expressly distinguished book value from actual market
value. Id. at 84–86.
The SEC refused to let the management-acquired preferred stock participate on the same terms as other
preferred stock. It reasoned that reorganization managers were fiduciaries bound by equitable principles that
prohibited such trading. The plan was amended so that respondents' shares would be surrendered at cost plus
four percent interest, rather than converted into new equity, and the Commission approved it over their
objection. Id. at 85.
The majority emphasized what the SEC had not found: fraud, concealment, trading on inside knowledge, a
price advantage attributable to managerial position, or actual prejudice to the corporation or its other
shareholders. It understood the Commission to accept that the transactions were honest, disclosed, and at fair
prices, yet to believe settled judicial fiduciary doctrine compelled adverse treatment solely because of the
purchasers' managerial role. Black's dissent read the administrative decision differently and disputed that
characterization of its legal and factual basis. Id. at 85–90; id. at 95–100 (Black, J., dissenting).

SEC v. Chenery Corp. (Chenery I)
Procedural History
The SEC approved the amended reorganization plan by order of September 24, 1941. Respondents sought
review under § 24(a) of the Holding Company Act. A divided Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia set
the Commission's order aside. The Supreme Court granted certiorari because of the question's importance to
administration of the Act. 318 U.S. at 81.
The Supreme Court concluded that the order could not be sustained on the rationale the agency had actually
adopted. Its mandate sent the cause to the Court of Appeals with directions to remand to the Commission for
appropriate further proceedings consistent with the opinion. This allowed further administrative consideration;
it did not itself approve a plan giving management the disputed equity. Id. at 94–95.
Issue
Could the SEC's restriction on management's preferred-stock participation be sustained by the settled judicial
equitable principles it had invoked? If those principles did not justify the order, could a reviewing court
nevertheless uphold it on the agency's broad statutory power, specialized experience, or policy considerations
urged during judicial review but not adopted as the basis of the administrative decision? The linked question
was which institution had to make the discretionary public-interest judgment needed to support such a
restriction. 318 U.S. at 87–94.
Rule and Holding
A reviewing court must judge an administrative order on the grounds the record shows the agency actually
relied upon. Where sustaining the order requires a policy choice or administrative judgment entrusted to the
agency, a court cannot supply that unmade judgment—even to affirm an outcome that might have been lawful
on a different basis. The agency's grounds must be sufficiently disclosed and supported to permit review. The
requirement concerns responsible decisionmaking, rather than a prescribed verbal form. 318 U.S. at 87–88,
94–95.
The SEC had treated existing judicial fiduciary principles as controlling, but those principles did not
categorically forbid officers and directors to buy corporate stock merely because they held those offices. On the
majority's understanding of the findings, the precedents did not support this order. Although the statute granted
broad protective authority and did not freeze the SEC to existing equitable doctrine, the Commission had not
exercised that authority through the administrative judgment now offered in defense. Its order therefore could
not stand as issued. Id. at 88–94.
Judgment
The cause was remanded to the Court of Appeals with directions to remand to the SEC for further proceedings
consistent with the Supreme Court's opinion. The challenged order was not sustained, but the SEC remained
able to consider the matter again within the statute and the Court's legal ruling. Black, Reed, and Murphy
would have sustained the Commission's approach; Douglas took no part. 318 U.S. at 95–100.
Reasoning
1. Calling managers fiduciaries identifies a relationship, not its complete legal consequences. The Court agreed
that reorganization managers occupied positions of trust and owed demanding obligations. It nevertheless
required identification of the beneficiary, the particular duty, the breach, and its consequences. Without that
work, the general label could not establish that buying stock on ordinary market terms automatically forfeited
the right to participate in a reorganization. 318 U.S. at 85–87.
2. The Court identified the decision's actual premise by reading the SEC's explanation. The Commission had
analogized management to trustees dealing in trust property and invoked familiar equity decisions. As the

majority read the explanation, it professed to apply a rule already established by courts; it had not announced
an independent policy judgment derived from administering the Holding Company Act. This characterization
was decisive because it defined the basis the Court would review. It was also a central point of disagreement
with the dissent. Id. at 87–90; id. at 97–99 (Black, J., dissenting).
3. The cited equity precedents did not establish the asserted categorical prohibition. Pepper v. Litton concerned
a fraudulent scheme disadvantaging creditors. Woods concerned a bankruptcy court's control over
compensation to representatives with conflicting interests. Michoud and Magruder concerned duties of express
trustees. The majority considered those settings materially different from openly purchasing corporate stock
without the specific misuse the Commission had disavowed finding. The conclusion was about the precedents'
inability to supply this order's stated rule, not a general approval of insider self-dealing. Id. at 88–90.
4. The usual rule permitting affirmance of a lower court on a different legal ground did not authorize judicial
policymaking for an agency. An appellate court can sometimes affirm a judicial judgment on an alternative
ground it has authority to decide. It cannot, however, replace an unmade jury finding. The Court used that
institutional distinction to explain administrative review: a policy determination committed to the agency must
come from the agency. Courts would invade the delegated field by making it themselves to uphold an order,
just as they would by substituting their own preferred policy to overturn one. Id. at 88.
5. The statute conferred substantial power that the Court did not deny. Sections 7 and 11 permitted scrutiny of
whether a reorganization was fair and equitable and consistent with the interests of investors, consumers, and
the public. The statutory history supported protection against the power of favored insiders. Management's
capacity to initiate, amend, withdraw, and consent to a reorganization gave it a strategically important role.
Section 17's separate regulation of short-swing profits did not exhaust the SEC's authority to address other
reorganization abuses. Id. at 90–92.
6. Broad authority did not cure the missing administrative judgment. In court the Commission emphasized the
dynamics of reorganization, potential subtle abuses, and its specialized experience. Those considerations could
support a prophylactic policy even where individual transactions appeared fair. The difficulty was that the
agency had not made that policy choice as the basis for the order being reviewed. The Court could neither infer
a responsible finding that had not been made nor adopt the alternative reasoning in the SEC's place. Id. at
92–94.
7. The form of relief further exposed the weakness of the asserted equity basis. The SEC had not undone the
stock purchases or directed restitution to selling shareholders supposedly disadvantaged by them. It confirmed
the purchases but required surrender to the corporation at cost plus interest. That remedy reinforced the
majority's understanding that management was being subjected to a positive rule of conduct rather than redress
for a found injury to particular sellers. The existence and source of that rule therefore mattered. Id. at 93.
8. Reason-giving makes both review and deference possible. Courts cannot assess legality without knowing
whether an agency is applying a judicial legal rule or exercising delegated administrative judgment. Legal
misunderstanding permits correction; an authorized policy choice receives the review appropriate to that kind
of action. Requiring clear grounds respects the agency's authority by ensuring that the agency, rather than the
reviewing court or litigation counsel, exercises the discretion Congress assigned. The Court disclaimed
demands for artistic drafting or ritual language. Id. at 94–95.
9. The remand preserved room for lawful agency development. The majority expressly acknowledged that
standards of fairness could evolve and that the Commission was not limited to pre-1935 judicial doctrine.
References to a possible general rule illustrated an avenue the agency had not used; the judgment should not be
converted into an unconditional holding that all new policy must originate through rulemaking. The later
Chenery litigation directly addressed how the Commission could proceed after this remand. Id. at 89, 92–95.

SEC v. Chenery Corp. (Chenery I)
Separate Opinions
Justice Black, joined by Justices Reed and Murphy, dissented. He agreed about management's fiduciary
position and the Commission's broad statutory responsibility, but believed the SEC had already exercised the
authority the majority said was missing. He quoted its findings that management's participation would make
the securities issuance detrimental to investors and the plan unfair and inequitable. For him, its reasoning was
ascertainable and adequate without a redrafted explanation. 318 U.S. at 95–99 (Black, J., dissenting).
Black treated management's role in shaping the proposed plan and the large potential increase in the purchased
shares' book value as grounds for a protective rule. He believed the cited equitable decisions supported that rule
by analogy, even though their facts were not identical. He also rejected the inference that invoking judicial
decisions excluded reliance on administrative experience. His description of insiders' informational advantage
and unconscionable profit differs from the majority's account of what the SEC had and had not found; it must
not be silently imported into the majority's factual premise. Id. at 96–99.
The dissent warned that demanding elaborate findings could impede administration and provide courts an
unobtrusive route into policy review. It expressly defended case-by-case development of standards: an
adjudication can announce a principle to the industry, and a protective rule may address a tendency toward
abuse without proof of injury in each transaction. Black favored approving the rule and order, rather than
requiring the Commission to restate its rationale. Id. at 99–100. No concurrence is reported, and Douglas did
not participate. Id. at 95.
