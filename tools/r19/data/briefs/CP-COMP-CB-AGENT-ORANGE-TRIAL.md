# In re “Agent Orange” Product Liability Litigation — 1979 district decision

**506 F. Supp. 737 (E.D.N.Y. 1979) · November 20, 1979 · Civil Procedure**

District Judge George C. Pratt decided whether the veterans’ product-liability claims could proceed under federal common law. The assigned account is Boyle Note 3, CivPro physical PDF 287, printed 538–539. This brief concerns the November 1979 ruling; the separately assigned 1980 appeal and 2008 contractor-defense decision are distinct stages.

## Facts and litigation posture

Vietnam veterans and members of their families alleged injuries from the military’s use of Agent Orange, a defoliant manufactured and sold to the government by private chemical companies. The court described anticipated claims numbering in the thousands or more, including alleged genetic harm to veterans’ children, and potential liabilities reaching millions or billions of dollars. These were allegations and estimates supporting the jurisdictional analysis, not findings that exposure caused each claimed injury. The litigation was proceeding as MDL No. 381. The textbook’s later retrospective account mentions consolidation with 600 similar cases; the 1979 opinion itself describes the developing litigation without establishing that retrospective count. (740, 747–748.)

After oral argument on defendants’ motion against the second amended verified complaint, plaintiffs filed a third amended verified complaint on October 22, 1979. Although the court criticized their filing practices, defendants accepted service and consented to treating the existing motion as directed at the new complaint without more briefing. The court therefore reached the jurisdictional objections and the requests to dismiss or strike particular allegations. Note 1 limits the opinion’s use of “plaintiffs,” excluding claims filed on or after October 3, 1979 and Kahler v. Dow. (740 & n.1.)

Plaintiffs invoked both an asserted equitable jurisdiction and §1331 federal-question jurisdiction. They sought a private federal remedy under pesticide and consumer-safety legislation, alternatively relying on federal common law. Defendants argued that ordinary state tort law governed claims between private parties and that federal regulation, military procurement, and the size of the litigation did not supply federal jurisdiction. (740–744.)

## Questions and results

The court rejected an independent equitable basis for federal jurisdiction and dismissed the asserted implied statutory claims. It nevertheless held that federal common law could govern the negligence and strict product-liability claims, supplying a basis for §1331 jurisdiction. Accordingly, the action survived the motion to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. That procedural ruling did not establish defendants’ liability or grant the requested remedies. (740–742, 749–750.)

## Why the statutory theories failed

Federal courts can grant equitable relief only after a valid basis for jurisdiction exists; requesting equity cannot create jurisdiction by itself. The court therefore turned to plaintiffs’ asserted federal rights. It treated FEPCA as an amendment to FIFRA rather than a separate source of recovery. Because the applicable statutory definitions excluded FIFRA pesticides from the Toxic Substances Control Act and Consumer Product Safety Act, those latter statutes did not provide the claimed remedy for this product. (740–741 & n.2.)

For FIFRA, the court used Cort v. Ash’s inquiry into protected beneficiaries, legislative intent, consistency with the statutory scheme, and matters traditionally governed by state law. Although plaintiffs belonged to the class the statute sought to protect, the court concluded that Congress had withheld private enforcement actions. It relied on the legislation’s enforcement provisions and prior decisions examining rejection of citizen-suit proposals. A unique tragedy or a large number of claims could not substitute for congressional intent to authorize private recovery. Note 3 also distinguishes a state negligence-per-se action using FIFRA’s labeling requirements from a private cause of action created by FIFRA itself. (741–743 & n.3.)

## Why Pratt nevertheless applied federal common law

Plaintiffs urged a broad reading of Clearfield Trust, emphasizing national military activity, veterans’ benefits, procurement, and unequal treatment under different state laws. Pratt accepted defendants’ important qualification: for a suit between private parties, Miree and Wallis supplied the relevant limits. A connection to a federal program did not automatically justify federal law, and legislative authority to regulate an area did not itself authorize judicial displacement of state law. Federal common law remained exceptional. (743–745.)

The court organized the analysis around three considerations: the substantial federal interests implicated, the effect of applying state law on those interests, and the effect of displacing state law on state interests. First, it relied on the distinctive relationship between the United States and its soldiers. Veterans’ benefits were limited and did not compensate spouses and children for their alleged injuries. Note 4 expressly leaves service-related eligibility to separate litigation. The court also considered how substantial contractor liability could affect future military procurement. Note 5 identifies possible price increases, supply conditions, and broader financial effects as speculative federal interests, rather than established consequences of this lawsuit. (746–747 & nn.4–5.)

Second, Pratt thought the scale and common origin of the alleged injuries made varying state rules especially problematic. Soldiers exposed while serving together could face different limitations periods and different compensation systems because of where they lived before or after service. The court regarded that uncertainty and inequality as burdens on federal interests in this particular litigation. Notes 6 and 7 connect the argument to the veterans’ and contractors’ identities as well as the number and size of the claims. The court distinguished isolated defective-aircraft or explosives suits in which state law had previously governed. (747–748 & nn.6–7.)

Third, the court acknowledged the traditional state role in tort law, but believed this combination—federally regulated toxic chemicals, military use abroad, extensive exposure, and war contractors’ duties to soldiers—had not been developed in state law. In its view, federal common law would not displace an established state framework tailored to these claims. That conclusion supported allowing the negligence and strict-liability theories to proceed. Note 8 reserves whether the additional warranty, intentional-tort, nuisance, and equitable theories should also be federal or instead pendent state claims. Thus, the opinion does not federalize every theory in the complaint. (748–749 & n.8.)

## Relief, disposition, and limits

The court refused to strike requests for declaratory relief and a trust fund merely because such relief might ultimately be unavailable. Keeping the requests in the complaint imposed no obligation to admit or deny them and prejudiced defendants neither through that pleading burden nor through an actual award. Other challenged allegations concerning phenoxy herbicides and corporate history had already been omitted, making those objections moot. (749.)

The concluding order dismissed the implied statutory claims and excused defendants from answering the corresponding paragraph. It retained the federal-common-law claims and denied dismissal for lack of jurisdiction. Note 9 confirms that a claim governed by federal common law can support §1331 jurisdiction. Defendants were directed to answer within 20 days; the court set a December 21 pretrial conference to address class-certification and merits-discovery schedules. No class was certified by this ruling, and no damages or trust fund was awarded. (749–750 & n.9.)

Pratt is the sole author; this district decision contains nine judicial footnotes and no separate writing or numerical vote. Its importance is the reasoning used to retain federal jurisdiction despite rejecting statutory causes of action. The assigned note reports that the Second Circuit later reversed this federal-common-law ruling; that reversal must be studied as a separate decision rather than treating this 1979 analysis as an enduring rule.
