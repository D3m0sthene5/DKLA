# Garcia v. Hilton Hotels International, Inc.

**Civil Procedure · CP-070 · 97 F. Supp. 5 (D.P.R. 1951), May 8, 1951, No. 6155.** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, PDF pp. 349–351, printed pp. 664–669. District Judge Roberts decided motions addressing the sufficiency and form of a defamation complaint.

## The allegations and the three requested remedies

Garcia, a Puerto Rico citizen and resident, sued Hilton, a Delaware corporation, for defamation in the local District Court of Puerto Rico. Hilton removed to federal court. The complaint contained two causes of action with identical paragraphs pertinent to the motions. The federal judge applied his rulings to both. This was a pleading decision, not a trial determining whether the charges were true or damages were owed. (Opening and final judicial paragraphs.)

Paragraph 4 alleged that Hilton discharged Garcia on August 22, 1950 while falsely accusing him of bringing women into hotel rooms for prostitution. Later paragraphs alleged that Garcia sought severance pay and overtime through the Puerto Rico Labor Department, that Hilton repeated the accusation at the department's hearing, and that his compensation claim was later compromised. The court analyzed the discharge accusation and the hearing accusation separately because different privileges could apply to those occasions. (Paragraphs beginning “In support of its motion” and “It is alleged in these paragraphs.”)

Hilton sought dismissal for failure to state a claim, arguing that the complaint did not allege publication and that the accusation was conditionally privileged. Alternatively, it sought to strike paragraphs 5–8 and obtain a more definite statement. The parties also agreed at the hearing to strike paragraph 9. Those requests had distinct functions: terminate the pleaded action, remove particular material, or require clarification before an answer. (Opening two paragraphs; paragraph beginning “The parties have agreed.”)

## Why the court denied dismissal

Roberts applied the liberal pleading approach of the time. Rule 8(a) required a short and plain statement showing entitlement to relief, rather than the former formula demanding facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. Reading the complaint favorably and accepting its allegations, the court asked whether evidence within its framework could establish a claim. It relied on Dioguardi and other judicial authorities favoring testing the merits after an answer and proof rather than terminating an imperfectly expressed claim. This is the court's historical 1951 approach, not an independently checked statement of the present pleading standard. (Paragraphs beginning “The motion to dismiss,” “It is clear,” and “The controlling question.”)

Paragraph 4 did not expressly say that the alleged slander was published to another person. Nevertheless, its description of a discharge accompanied by a false and slanderous accusation showed an intended publication claim that Hilton could reasonably understand. Garcia could potentially introduce evidence of publication at trial. The omission therefore made the claim imperfectly stated but did not require dismissal of the action. The court did not find that publication had actually occurred. (Paragraphs beginning “In the instant case” and “While in a technical sense.”)

Hilton's claimed conditional privilege also did not conclusively defeat the pleading. The court distinguished absolute privilege, which could decisively bar an action when disclosed by the complaint, from a conditional privilege that could be lost through abuse or actual malice. Even assuming a conditional privilege applied to the discharge communication, factual questions remained about whether Hilton abused it or acted maliciously. The allegations could permit evidence negating that defense. Hilton therefore needed to assert the conditional privilege as an affirmative defense rather than obtain dismissal merely because it might be available. (Paragraphs beginning “The conclusiveness of privilege” through “And, clearly.”)

## Why it struck the hearing allegations

The Labor Department communication had a different legal status. Puerto Rico's 1902 defamation statute protected communications in legislative or judicial proceedings and other proceedings authorized by law. Roberts understood this protection as absolute. The department's hearing on Garcia's labor-compensation claim was authorized by the 1949 labor-enforcement statute, which charged the commissioner with investigating complaints and provided compulsory processes for obtaining evidence. (Paragraphs beginning “Section 4” and “It appears that the hearing.”)

The full opinion explains the policy in greater detail than the excerpt. Effective labor-law enforcement required participants to supply information freely, without fearing liability based on their testimony's alleged motive or falsity. Courts elsewhere similarly protected statements to bodies exercising quasi-judicial powers. Both that policy and the Puerto Rico statutory phrase covering proceedings authorized by law supported absolute privilege for this hearing communication. Under the court's analysis, alleged malice could not remove that protection as it could remove a conditional privilege. (Paragraphs beginning “It appears, upon examination” through “And this conviction.”)

Because the hearing accusation was absolutely privileged, Roberts struck paragraphs 5–8 as redundant, including the surrounding account of the labor claim and compromise. He separately struck paragraph 9 by the parties' agreement. The ruling did not treat the original discharge accusation as having occurred in the protected hearing merely because it was later repeated there. (Paragraphs beginning “Clearly, then” and “The parties have agreed.”)

## Why clarification was still required

Surviving dismissal did not mean paragraph 4 was sufficiently definite for an answer. Its account left unclear the substantial words of the allegedly slanderous statement and the facts establishing publication. Those details mattered to preparing a response asserting conditional privilege. The court granted the Rule 12(e) request for a more definite statement requiring that information. Its reasoning distinguishes a complaint that could support relief from one sufficiently clear to require the opposing party to answer immediately. (Final reasoning paragraphs.)

**The motion to dismiss was denied; the motion to strike paragraphs 5–8 was granted; paragraph 9 was stricken by agreement; and a more definite statement concerning paragraph 4 was required. All rulings applied to the second cause of action as well.** Neither side received a final defamation damages judgment. (Final judicial paragraph.)
