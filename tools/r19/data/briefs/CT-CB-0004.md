Bayliner Marine Corp. v. Crow

Citation, court, and assigned scope
The Supreme Court of Virginia decided this appeal on January 8, 1999. Justice Barbara
Milano Keenan wrote the opinion; all the justices were present. The casebook prints pages
4–7 and expressly notes that the implied-warranty discussion is omitted. This brief covers all
three warranty theories. Pinpoints identified as “slip” refer to the court's twelve-page
opinion, Record No. 980392, rather than to the course scan.
Parties and their objectives
John R. Crow was the purchaser of a sport-fishing boat and the prevailing plaintiff at trial.
Bayliner Marine Corporation manufactured the boat and appealed the warranty judgment.
Tidewater Yacht Agency was the authorized local dealer whose representative supplied the
product information; Crow nonsuited his claim against Tidewater before trial. Brunswick, the
diesel-engine manufacturer, had also been sued, but the final trial order dismissed the
negligence claim against it. This appeal concerns Bayliner's warranty liability, not a
judgment adjudicating all defendants' responsibility alike. (Slip pp. 1, 4 & n.1.)
Material facts
In the summer of 1989, Crow took a short ride on a Bayliner 3486 Trophy Convertible offered
through Tidewater. He asked the dealer's representative, John Atherton, about maximum
speed. Atherton lacked personal experience or customer information sufficient to answer, so
he furnished two manufacturer “prop matrixes” from the dealer's manual. The matrices listed
propeller sizes, gearing, engines, and maximum speeds. The relevant thirty-mile-per-hour
figures concerned boats with propellers different from the 20-by-17 propeller on Crow's boat.
The data also specified testing conditions including approximately 600 pounds of passengers
and gear. (Slip pp. 1–2.)
Atherton also showed a sales brochure promoting the model's performance for reaching
offshore fishing grounds. Crow purchased the boat for $120,000 before taxes. The
transaction included installation of approximately 2,000 pounds of equipment, such as
navigation equipment, a generator, fishing outriggers, and heating and cooling systems. He
did not conduct a further test drive after those additions and before delivery. These
differences in configuration matter because the asserted precise speed representation had to
relate to the boat actually purchased or one with substantially similar characteristics. (Slip
pp. 2–3, 6–7.)
Upon delivery in September 1989, Crow measured a maximum speed of thirteen miles per
hour. After repeated repairs and adjustments the boat generally reached seventeen, with a
temporary improvement to approximately twenty-four after an engine modification. In July
1990, Bayliner wrote that the performance representations made at purchase had been
incorrect and identified twenty-three to twenty-five miles per hour as the boat's maximum
capability. Crow testified that he would not have bought it even at that capability because

Procedural history and standard of review
Following a bench trial, the circuit court found breaches of an express warranty, the implied
warranty of merchantability, and the implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose. It
awarded $135,000 plus prejudgment interest: the $120,000 purchase price and
approximately $15,000 toward the claimed costs of storing, maintaining, insuring, and
financing the boat. Bayliner challenged liability and damages. (Slip pp. 4–6 & n.2.)
The Supreme Court viewed the evidence in the light most favorable to Crow, the prevailing
party. It stated that a trial judgment would be upheld unless plainly wrong or without
evidentiary support. The resulting reversal thus was not based on treating every warranty
issue as exclusively a question of law. The court acknowledged the ordinarily factual nature
of several questions but held the evidence insufficient to sustain the findings here. Its
disposition on liability made it unnecessary to decide Bayliner's separate assignments
concerning the amount of damages. (Slip pp. 5–6 & n.2, 10–12.)
Issues
Did the numerical matrices or general brochure language create a thirty-mile-per-hour
express warranty for Crow's boat? Did failure to reach that speed establish
unmerchantability in the offshore-fishing-boat trade or unfitness for the boat's ordinary
purpose? Finally, did the seller have the required knowledge of Crow's particular
thirty-mile-per-hour requirement to support the separate implied fitness warranty? (Slip pp.
6–12.)
Rules and holdings
Under Virginia's enacted version of UCC § 2-313, an affirmation, promise, or description
relating to the goods and forming part of the basis of the bargain can create an express
warranty. The matrices did not warrant the performance of Crow's differently configured
boat. The brochure's general praise of offshore performance was commendation and opinion,
rather than a description promising a specific speed. The evidence therefore did not support
express-warranty liability. (Slip pp. 6–8.)
Under § 2-314, the trade-acceptability and ordinary-fitness components of merchantability
require evidence of the appropriate standard and the goods' failure to meet it. Crow did not
establish a relevant trade standard or show that this boat's speed made it generally
unacceptable or unfit for ordinary offshore fishing. Under § 2-315, a buyer must establish the
seller's reason to know the particular purpose and the buyer's reliance on the seller's skill or

Actual judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the trial judgment and entered final judgment for Bayliner. It
did not remand to give Crow another opportunity to prove the missing elements. It also did
not approve the circuit court's damages methodology: those issues were unnecessary once
each warranty ground for liability failed. (Slip pp. 6 n.2, 12.)
Reasoning
Numerical specificity is insufficient unless the statement concerns the purchased
goods
The thirty-mile-per-hour data were definite numbers, so dismissing the entire dispute as sales
puffery would obscure the court's analysis. The problem was their referent. They described
different propeller configurations and materially different equipment weight. A precise
assertion about a different product or materially different setup does not automatically
become a warranty about the purchaser's actual boat. The court contrasted an earlier jewelry
case in which an appraisal described the very diamonds being sold. (Slip pp. 6–7.)
The testing qualification reinforced that contextual limitation. The opinion did not rest on a
broad proposition that any disclaimer eliminates every express warranty, nor did it say that
sellers must use the word “warranty.” Instead, it examined what the statements described
and whether they supplied the claimed promise for the specific transaction. The brief
therefore preserves the matrix analysis separately from the treatment of the brochure. (Slip
pp. 2, 6–8.)
General praise did not promise the particular performance claimed
The brochure described the boat as capable of getting customers to desirable offshore
grounds. It did not identify a specific speed or other measurable performance characteristic
corresponding to Crow's claim. Section 2-313(2) preserves the distinction between an
affirmation or description and an opinion commending the goods. On this record the general
promotional sentence fell on the commendation side. That conclusion does not establish that
every advertising statement is nonpromissory; it concerns this statement's content. (Slip pp.
7–8.)
Merchantability requires a standard beyond the buyer's own disappointment
The court considered two statutory aspects independently. For acceptance in the trade, Crow
had supplied no evidence of the offshore-boat trade's merchantability standard or of a
substantial segment of buyers' rejection of boats with this capability. Testimony that travel to
the Gulf Stream took too long for his fishing preferences did not fill that evidentiary gap. It
described his desired use and inconvenience without establishing the relevant general
benchmark. (Slip pp. 8–9.)
For fitness for ordinary purposes, the evidence of actual offshore fishing and substantial
engine hours undermined the inference that the vessel was generally unfit for that category
of use. Crow's explanation that many hours concerned repairs did not itself prove general

A particular-purpose claim turns on information conveyed at the sale
Crow identified the relevant particular purpose as offshore fishing in a boat capable of thirty
miles per hour. He had discussed offshore fishing and speed, but the record did not establish
that Atherton knew a slower boat was unacceptable to him. A buyer's privately decisive
condition does not necessarily become a term or trigger a particular-purpose warranty
merely because the broader intended activity is discussed. The lack of proof of this
knowledge defeated the claim without a need to resolve every other § 2-315 element. (Slip
pp. 10–12.)
Separate opinions
The judicial opinion records all the justices as present and contains no separate concurrence
or dissent. The online host's annotation labels the author as Morrison, but the opinion's first
page identifies Keenan as the author and Morrison as the circuit judge. This brief follows the
judicial document, not that annotation. (Slip p. 1.)
Use in class and limits of the decision
Analyze the warranty theories separately. An express warranty concerns the bargain's
particular representation; merchantability supplies an ordinary-use and trade standard;
fitness for a particular purpose depends on knowledge of the buyer's special requirement and
reliance. An unsuccessful claim under one does not conceptually dispose of the others, which
explains why the court had to address all three before entering final judgment.
The assigned excerpt primarily teaches the boundary of express warranties. The full opinion
supplies the $135,000 trial award, the treatment of the other defendants, the evidentiary
standard of review, and the two implied-warranty analyses. Those additions explain the
actual judgment rather than broadening it. This historical brief does not claim to resolve
present-day warranty law, consumer statutes, or theories not adjudicated in the opinion.
