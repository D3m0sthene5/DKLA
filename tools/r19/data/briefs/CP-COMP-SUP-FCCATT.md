# Federal Communications Commission v. AT&T, Inc.

**608 U.S. 531 (2026) • Supreme Court of the United States • June 4, 2026**

**Assigned source:** Civil Procedure Supplement, printed p. 521 (PDF 349). Consolidated cases: No. 25–406, FCC v. AT&T, from the Fifth Circuit, and No. 25–567, Verizon Communications v. FCC, from the Second Circuit. Chief Justice Roberts wrote for eight Justices; Justice Thomas dissented. The 8–1 split concerns the judgment and treatment of these paid penalties. Thomas agreed with the Court's statutory interpretation for future proceedings but would give these carriers an opportunity for relief.

## Enforcement framework

The Communications Act supplies two forfeiture routes. Section 503(b)(3) provides formal administrative adjudication with limited appellate review; the Court described that route as unused in practice. **The route at issue was § 503(b)(4)**: the FCC issues a notice of apparent liability stating the factual and legal basis, accepts a written response, and then determines liability and assesses a penalty. The holding should not be expanded automatically to the different formal route. 536–537.

A recipient can pursue Hobbs Act appellate review on the agency record, or decline to pay and await possible enforcement. Under § 504(a), the Department of Justice may bring a civil collection suit, which proceeds de novo. Until voluntary payment or a judicial payment order, § 504(c) prevents use of the unresolved notice or forfeiture proceeding to prejudice the regulated party in later FCC proceedings. The FCC cannot collect the penalty directly through execution. 537, 541–543.

## Facts and procedural history

AT&T and Verizon sold customer location information through intermediaries for location-based services. Reports in 2018 identified security breaches, including access obtained with irrelevant material instead of meaningful legal authorization. The FCC investigated whether the carriers had taken reasonable confidentiality safeguards, issued notices, considered responses, and imposed approximately $57 million against AT&T and $47 million against Verizon in 2024. These were agency findings; the Supreme Court did not independently try the alleged privacy violations. 538–539.

The carriers paid and sought review. The Fifth Circuit accepted AT&T's Seventh Amendment challenge and vacated its forfeiture order: the FCC had found facts and assessed punishment without a jury. The Second Circuit rejected Verizon's challenge because the order did not itself compel payment, and the Government needed a collection action before payment could be forced. The Supreme Court granted both petitions to resolve the conflict. 539–540. Thomas emphasized that both carriers had paid under protest, preserved their objections, and sought return of their payments. 549–552.

## Issue and holding

Does the FCC's § 503(b)(4) process violate the Seventh Amendment by making initial findings and issuing a forfeiture order without a jury?

**No.** Those orders do not conclusively establish legal obligations or bind the later factfinder. Before the Government can compel payment, the regulated party can insist on a jury's ultimate determination in a de novo enforcement action. Preliminary nonjury findings therefore do not violate the jury right. 540–543.

This holding depends on the Court's interpretation of the particular enforcement scheme. It is not a general authorization for agencies to collect every civil penalty without a jury.

## Majority reasoning

**The jury must have the ultimate factual determination, not necessarily the first procedural step.** Meeker upheld an agency damages report that could be enforced only after a jury trial; Ex parte Peterson upheld a preliminary auditor's report where the jury retained ultimate control. Neither arrangement removed factual issues from the jury or cut off defenses. The FCC process satisfied the same principle. 540–541.

The statute gave the FCC no power to seize assets or obtain liens under these forfeiture orders, imposed no nonpayment penalty or accruing interest on the assessed sum, and protected against adverse use of the unresolved order. A de novo collection action treated the case as if no prior trial had occurred. Both factual and legal conclusions could be contested anew; the Court expressly confirmed the legal component in note 1. 541–543 & n.1. Note 2 declined speculation about the Government choosing a circuit with contrary historical precedent, particularly where the carriers had not made that argument. 543 n.2.

**Mandatory-sounding words did not make the order immediately binding.** Terms such as determining, assessing, and imposing had to be read within the complete scheme. The statute's liability language created a right of action, rather than an immediate duty to pay the agency's requested amount. 543–544 & n.3.

**Jarkesy was different.** The SEC penalties there were immediately enforceable, and later enforcement did not provide a jury to redetermine the underlying violation. Here the FCC's findings were preliminary and the Government needed to establish its case anew. The difference concerned enforceability and ultimate adjudication, not simply the fact that both agencies used administrative processes. 544.

The order's role as a prerequisite to suit did not itself settle private legal rights. Nor did possible reputational harm from preliminary accusations create a Seventh Amendment right to a jury at that stage. 545–546.

**No unconstitutional condition coerced abandonment of the jury right.** If the Government never brought a collection action, there would be no suit requiring a jury. The FCC could not use the unresolved order or nonpayment against the carrier. It could examine relevant underlying facts again in a later proceeding, but without preclusion or special weight and with a fresh opportunity to contest them. The uncertain risk of reputation harm did not impose an unconstitutional cost on awaiting a jury. 546–548.

Two limits are important. The parties litigated on the premise that appellate review required payment first, but the Court expressly declined to endorse that premise. 546 n.4. It also reserved the claim that the particular orders had misled the carriers into paying, including any available refund, remedy, or proper proceeding. 548 n.5.

## Thomas dissent

Thomas agreed that the statutory interpretation announced by the Court should govern future proceedings, with nonbinding FCC findings and a full de novo judicial action before compelled payment. His objection was to withholding relief from the carriers before it. 549, 552.

The actual orders used mandatory payment language and required payment within thirty days. The FCC had asserted authority to impose penalties without Article III adjudication, and historically some courts excluded review of legal conclusions or limited challenges to the amount. Given nationwide enforcement venue, the carriers could not confidently expect the complete new trial the Court described. Their payments under protest preserved rather than abandoned constitutional objections. Thomas would permit them to proceed under the correct understanding instead of treating their earlier payments as unquestionably voluntary. 550–554 & dissent n.* at 553.

## Exact dispositions and course significance

In **No. 25–406, the Fifth Circuit's judgment was reversed and the case remanded**. In **No. 25–567, the Second Circuit's judgment was affirmed**. 548. The two consolidated cases therefore have different dispositions despite a common governing rule. The Court did not order refunds and expressly left the misleading-order argument open.
