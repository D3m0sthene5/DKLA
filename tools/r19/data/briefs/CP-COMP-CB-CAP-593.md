# Southland Corp. v. Keating

**465 U.S. 1 (1984)** — January 23, 1984. Chief Justice Burger wrote for Justices Brennan, White, Marshall, Blackmun, and Powell. Justice Stevens concurred in part and dissented in part; Justice O’Connor dissented, joined by Justice Rehnquist. Civil Procedure. Assigned: CivPro PDF 705, printed 1400, own franchise-law account before Rodriguez de Quijas.

Southland owned and franchised 7-Eleven convenience stores. Its standard agreements licensed trademarks, supplied store premises, financed inventory, and assisted with advertising and merchandising. Franchisees operated the stores, furnished bookkeeping information, and paid a percentage of gross profits. The agreements required arbitration of controversies or claims arising from or relating to the agreement or its breach. Several franchisees sued in California between 1975 and 1977, alleging fraud, misrepresentation, breach of contract and fiduciary duty, and violations of the California Franchise Investment Law’s disclosure requirements. Keating later brought a class action asserting substantially the same claims on behalf of approximately eight hundred California franchisees. The suits were consolidated; Southland sought arbitration, and franchisees sought class certification. The Supreme Court decided forum enforceability, not whether these alleged violations occurred. (3–5.)

The Superior Court ordered arbitration of all claims **except** those based on the Franchise Investment Law and did not rule on class certification. The California Court of Appeal reversed that exception: it interpreted both the agreement and state statute to permit arbitration, alternatively concluding that a contrary state restriction would conflict with the Federal Arbitration Act. It also directed class-certification proceedings. The California Supreme Court, by a 4–2 vote, held the statutory claims nonarbitrable. It construed the state law’s prohibition on waiving statutory compliance to require a judicial forum, found no federal conflict, and remanded for consideration of classwide arbitration. The assigned account’s statement that the trial court ordered arbitration of all claims omits the statutory exception; the original opinion expressly includes it. (4–6 and n.1.)

Two questions reached the U.S. Supreme Court: whether federal law displaced the state requirement of a judicial forum for the franchise-law claims, and whether federal law prohibited imposing class procedures on arbitration. The Court reached only the first on the merits. Its then-applicable appellate jurisdiction under §1257(2) ordinarily required a final state judgment sustaining a statute against a federal challenge. Although state proceedings remained, the Court applied Cox Broadcasting’s practical finality analysis: delaying review until judicial litigation concluded could destroy the benefit of the very arbitration agreement in dispute. Immediate review was necessary to prevent erosion of federal policy and would terminate litigation of the arbitrable merits in court. (6–8.)

The class-arbitration issue had a different preservation problem. Southland had argued that state law and the agreement did not authorize class procedures and that the Federal Rules did not govern the state proceedings. It had not properly presented the claim that the FAA preempted state-authorized class arbitration. Nor had the state supreme court decided that federal issue. Because the state law’s validity on that ground had not affirmatively been drawn into question, the U.S. Supreme Court lacked jurisdiction to decide it under §1257(2). The Court therefore did **not** decide that the FAA either authorized or prohibited class arbitration. References in the lower proceedings to analogous federal consolidation cases did not cure the missing federal challenge. (8–9 and nn.2–4.)

On the preserved merits, §2 made written arbitration provisions in maritime transactions and contracts involving commerce enforceable, subject to grounds applicable at law or equity for revoking any contract. The majority understood this as substantive federal law grounded in Congress’s commerce power and applicable in state as well as federal courts. California’s statute, as its highest court interpreted it, removed a class of agreed disputes from arbitration and required judicial adjudication. That restriction conflicted with §2 and violated the Supremacy Clause. The agreement’s broad language covered the statutory franchise claims. (10–11, 15 n.7.)

The majority relied on Prima Paint’s recognition of federal power over interstate commercial and maritime arbitration, and Moses H. Cone’s description of federal substantive arbitration law. It also examined legislative history addressing resistance to enforcing arbitration agreements. Congress sought to overcome both entrenched common-law hostility and inadequate state enforcement. For the majority, limiting enforcement to federal litigation would defeat that purpose, especially because most litigation occurred in state courts. The express limitation to transactions involving commerce made sense as a qualification on substantive legislation extending to state courts, rather than an unexplained restriction on a merely federal procedural rule. Contrary state treatment would also make enforcement depend on the chosen forum. (11–16.)

The holding retained important boundaries. Section 2’s saving clause allowed general contract defenses, such as fraud; the majority rejected this particular state defense because it invalidated arbitration provisions in franchise contracts rather than supplying a ground for revoking any contract. A state could not recreate the old arbitration-specific hostility by describing it as franchise protection. The majority distinguished Congress’s own ability to create exceptions through other federal enactments. It also expressly stated that the FAA supplied no independent federal-question jurisdiction: an otherwise sufficient jurisdictional basis was still needed to invoke federal court. And note 10 states that the Court was **not** holding §§3 and 4 applicable to state-court proceedings; for example, the Federal Rules did not govern those proceedings. These limits matter when evaluating the dissent’s criticism of the decision’s practical procedural implications. (15–17 and nn.9–11.)

Stevens agreed with the jurisdictional holdings and with the general conclusion that federal arbitration law applied equally in both forums. He accepted O’Connor’s historical account that Congress originally viewed the Act as procedural, but thought intervening legal developments supported its substantive reach. He nevertheless would affirm the California judgment concerning this agreement. In his view, the saving clause left room for legitimate state substantive policy, particularly protections for franchisees with unequal bargaining power. Federal law aimed to place arbitration agreements on an equal footing with contracts, not above all regulatory limits. He thought courts could incorporate relevant state policies through federal common-law judgment without reinstating a general ban on arbitration, and he objected to treating comparable federal protective policy more favorably than state policy. He therefore did not join the six-Justice majority’s decision invalidating the California restriction. (17–21.)

O’Connor and Rehnquist rejected the foundational extension to state courts. The 1925 legislative history, drafting materials, and enforcement provisions’ express references to federal courts showed a statute intended to regulate federal procedure. Erie and Bernhardt later changed the legal background, while Prima Paint upheld applying the Act in federal diversity litigation through commerce and admiralty powers; those developments did not establish its application in state court. O’Connor considered Moses H. Cone’s state-court language unnecessary dictum, rather than a prior resolution. Her dissent examined the historical sources extensively, including the Act’s relationship to coordinated state legislation. (21–30 and nn.1–19.)

She also argued in the alternative that, even if §2 created a right binding state courts, those courts should initially choose reasonable enforcement procedures instead of necessarily compelling arbitration through federal-style specific enforcement. Damages, cost awards, or other remedies might recognize contractual validity. The majority’s explicit disclaimer about §§3 and 4 did not eliminate her concern about the practical mandate. Finally, she rejected its forum-shopping justification: parties with complete diversity had equal access to federal enforcement, while those without it lacked such access on either side. Her objections concerned congressional intent and federalism, not a categorical claim that arbitration lacked value. (31–36 and nn.20–29.)

The Court **reversed in part, dismissed the appeal in part, and remanded**. The six-Justice majority reversed the refusal to enforce arbitration of the franchise-law claims; Stevens agreed that substantive federal arbitration law extended to state courts but dissented on this enforcement result. The separate class-arbitration federal issue remained undecided.
