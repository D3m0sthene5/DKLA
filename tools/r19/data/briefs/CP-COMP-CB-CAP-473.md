# Hartford Accident and Indemnity Company v. Ace American Reinsurance Company

**103 Conn. App. 319, 930 A.2d 701 (2007)** — Connecticut Appellate Court, August 14, 2007; argued May 30; AC 25661. Judge Peters wrote for Judges DiPentima, Lavine, and Peters; the other judges concurred. Assigned: CivPro physical PDF page 601, printed 1187–1188, Note and Questions.

## Court, posture, and result

The court reversed the denial of prepleading security and remanded. Service on the reinsurers' contractually designated agents satisfied the governing service requirement. The industrial-insured exclusion did not defeat these reinsurance plaintiffs' access to security. But the amount required an evidentiary hearing at which both sides could present evidence. The court awarded neither contract damages nor a default judgment, and fixed no security amount. This is the 2007 merits appeal; the 2006 Supreme Court decision reopening appellate review was a separate stage. (321–325, 331–337.)

## Dispute and procedural history

Hartford and thirteen affiliates filed an amended fifty-seven-count complaint on April 1, 2004, alleging breaches of numerous reinsurance contracts. The original defendants numbered approximately 225, including about 150 Lloyd's underwriting syndicates. Some received service through the insurance commissioner and were outside this appeal. Others received service through their designated contractual agents in New York; intervening settlements left only five defendants in this appeal. These are pleading and litigation facts, not findings that any defendant breached a contract. (322 & nn. 1–2.)

The defendants answered and asserted eighteen special defenses on May 4. Hartford moved on May 29 to compel prepleading security or strike the answer and defenses. The defendants argued that the relevant security provision required substituted service through the insurance commissioner. After argument on July 19, the trial judge denied the motion orally, treating the alternative-service subsection as a rule of construction rather than an additional method triggering security. (322–324.)

Hartford appealed on July 26. The Appellate Court dismissed for lack of a final judgment on October 27, 2004. The Connecticut Supreme Court granted certification, reversed that dismissal, and remanded for consideration of the merits in 2006. The present opinion decides those merits, not the original appealability issue afresh. Statutory construction received plenary review. (324, 326.)

## First holding: contractual-agent service permitted access to security

Section 38a-27 generally required an unauthorized insurer, before pleading in the covered proceeding, either to provide security sufficient for a possible final judgment or to obtain authorization to conduct insurance business in Connecticut. Security could consist of cash, securities, or an approved bond; the quoted provision also permitted discretionary dispensation upon an adequate showing of available in-state assets. Authorization was a separate statutory alternative, not merely an exercise of discretion to waive a bond. (322–323 n. 3, 327.)

Section 38a-25 supplied substituted service through the commissioner, but subsection (e) preserved service in another legally permitted manner. Reading it alone to exclude a designated agent would deprive that subsection of work. The harder question arose because section 38a-27 also expressly cross-referenced sections 38a-26 and 38a-273: an entirely broad reading could make those references redundant. The court acknowledged that textual difficulty rather than claiming the answer followed from one isolated phrase. (325–328.)

The defendants invoked the principle favoring specific provisions over general language. The court instead read the connected provisions as remedial legislation intended to protect Connecticut insureds from the difficulty of pursuing unauthorized insurers in distant forums. Liberal construction and a sensible reconciliation of the provisions supported contractual-agent service. It would be illogical to exclude a contractually authorized method likely to reach the insurers promptly. The court therefore held that the plaintiffs' actual service qualified, entitling them to judicial determination of security. It did not dispense with legally sufficient service altogether. (328–331.)

Several notes qualify this interpretation. Note 4 applies the same construction to the comparable alternative-service language in section 38a-273. Note 5 rejects reliance on a revised statutory heading because the heading change was not part of the legislative enactment. Note 6 rejects the defendants' attempt to escape the statutory definition of unauthorized insurer: they conceded absence of a certificate of authority. Note 7 reproduces authority to postpone proceedings to afford a reasonable compliance opportunity. Note 8 reports the court's comparative research; the Oregon contrary authority discussed in the body concerned attorney's fees, and should not be silently converted into an identical prepleading-security holding. (325 n. 4, 326–327 nn. 5–6, 329 n. 7, 330–331 & n. 8.)

## Second holding: the industrial-insured objection failed

The plaintiffs did not deny industrial-insured status. The defendants relied on section 38a-271(c)(5), excluding certain industrial-insured transactions from section 38a-27, and on the treatment of reinsurance contracts as insurance contracts. But subsection (b)(2) excluded lawful reinsurance transactions from the application of sections 38a-271 through 38a-278. Reading the statute together, that sweeping exclusion prevented the defendants from applying the industrial-insured exception to these reinsurance contracts. The court did not hold that industrial-insured status could never affect an ordinary insurance transaction. Notes 9–11 reproduce the definition and statutory structure. (331–333 & nn. 9–11.)

## Third holding: due process required evidence before the amount was fixed

Hartford proposed setting security from the damages alleged in its prima facie complaint. The defendants demanded an evidentiary hearing. Although section 38a-27 did not expressly say “hearing,” its requirements of court approval and a court-fixed amount were construed to include one, avoiding the constitutional danger of a literal reading. (333–334.)

Doehr's prejudgment-remedy analysis considered the affected private interest, error risk and useful safeguards, and principally the requesting party's interest with attention to ancillary governmental interests. Prepleading security impaired defendants' use of property before liability was determined, much as prejudgment attachment did. The opinion also relied on Connecticut's legislative response to Doehr and decisions emphasizing adversarial hearings. (334–336.)

The court treated the New York statute and its judicial treatment as persuasive support because of their close similarity, including supervision and an opportunity to be heard. British International and Curiale had upheld that procedure with safeguards. Note 12 reproduces the New York provision; note 13 adds Texas authority involving notice and a full evidentiary hearing. The constitutional response here was to construe Connecticut's provision to afford a hearing, not to strike down the entire security statute. (336–337 & nn. 12–13.)

## Mandate, assignment, and source comparison

**Reversed and remanded for further proceedings:** the plaintiffs could obtain a security determination, and both parties could present evidence concerning its amount. No final dollar figure, breach finding, default, or merits recovery resulted. (337.)

The textbook correctly centers the hearing requirement but omits the two statutory holdings necessary to reach it. Its statement that Hartford “distinguished” Connecticut's statute from New York's needs qualification: the actual opinion emphasizes their near identity and uses the New York safeguards to support a saving construction. Its compressed waiver/authorization description should likewise be read with section 38a-27's distinct alternatives. (Assigned 601; actual 322–323 n. 3, 336–337.)
