# Aldinger v. Howard

**Civil Procedure · CP-ATLAS-ALDINGER · 427 U.S. 1 (1976) · June 24, 1976**

Aldinger held that the federal court could not use pendent-party jurisdiction to hear the plaintiff's state-law claim against Spokane County alongside her § 1983 claims against county officials. Under the interpretation controlling in 1976, municipalities were excluded from § 1983 liability. The Court read that exclusion together with the associated jurisdictional statute as an implied congressional limit on bringing the county into this federal action. It **affirmed the Ninth Circuit's judgment sustaining dismissal as to the county**, without deciding the merits of the employee's constitutional or state-law claims. Justice Rehnquist wrote for six Justices; Justice Brennan dissented with Justices Marshall and Blackmun. (427 U.S. at 2–5, 16–19.)

## The dismissal and the attempted combined lawsuit

The complaint alleged that county treasurer Howard hired the plaintiff for clerical work in 1971, then notified her two months later that her performance was excellent but she would be discharged because she was allegedly living with her boyfriend. He relied on a Washington statute allowing the appointing officer to revoke appointments at pleasure. The plaintiff requested a hearing, but alleged that she received none either before or after her dismissal. These were allegations considered at the pleading stage, not Supreme Court findings that the officials had violated her rights. (Id. at 3 & n.1.)

Her second amended complaint sought an injunction restraining the discharge and compensation for lost salary against Howard, his wife, county commissioners, and the county. The federal claims invoked § 1983 and the First, Ninth, and Fourteenth Amendments, including procedural due process. The state claim against the county relied on state provisions waiving sovereign immunity and imposing vicarious liability for officials' tortious conduct. All parties were Washington citizens, so diversity furnished no alternative jurisdiction. (Id. at 3–5 & nn.2–4.)

The district court concluded that the county was not a suable person under the then-governing construction of § 1983 and that it consequently lacked power to hear the pendent state claim against it. A Rule 54(b) judgment allowed an immediate appeal of that dismissal. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, adhering to its position against pendent-party jurisdiction despite contrary approaches elsewhere. The district court had not alternatively decided whether it would decline jurisdiction as a matter of discretion. The Supreme Court therefore addressed the existence of statutory power rather than reviewing a discretionary refusal to exercise established power. (Id. at 5–6.)

Note 3 preserves another procedural limit. Although the complaint also mentioned § 1331 and the then-applicable amount threshold, the plaintiff had consistently relied on § 1983, § 1343(3), and pendent jurisdiction in pursuing the county. Neither lower court resolved a proposed direct Fourteenth Amendment cause of action under § 1331, and the certiorari petition did not present it. Aldinger did not decide that alternative route. (Id. at 4 n.3.)

## Constitutional relationship did not end the inquiry

The plaintiff argued from Gibbs's common-nucleus-of-operative-fact test and from ancillary jurisdiction, which had sometimes permitted additional parties without an independent jurisdictional basis. The same alleged discharge generated both her federal claims against officials and her state claim against the county. Trying them together would avoid duplicated proceedings. The Federal Rules encouraged combining related claims and parties, and she contended that the distinctions between pendent and ancillary jurisdiction did not justify excluding the county. (Id. at 6–13.)

The Court reviewed the development of both doctrines. The pendent-claim cases concerned nonfederal questions or claims connected to a proper federal dispute between parties already before the court. The ancillary cases included claims to property or funds under federal control, where excluding other claimants could deprive them of an effective forum or undermine the court's judgment. Those examples showed that additional-party jurisdiction existed in some settings; they did not establish an unrestricted power whenever claims shared facts. The Court declined to create an all-encompassing account of every variation. (Id. at 7–13 & nn.5–8.)

The decisive distinction was statutory. Gibbs had considered related state claims against a defendant already properly present on a substantial federal claim. Here the plaintiff sought to add a different defendant solely through a state-law claim. Article III's permissible scope and the convenience of a combined trial could not answer whether Congress had authorized that particular extension of federal power. Federal courts had limited jurisdiction, and their statutory grants had to be examined along with the claim and party alignment. (Id. at 13–16.)

## The particular congressional limit

Section 1343(3) extended jurisdiction over civil actions authorized to redress specified deprivations of rights. Section 1983 supplied the relevant cause of action, but the Court's existing precedents excluded counties from its covered persons. The majority inferred that the jurisdictional grant should not be read to restore that excluded defendant merely because state law imposed a related obligation. It read the two provisions together and found Congress had implicitly declined this form of pendent-party jurisdiction. (Id. at 16–19 & nn.9–12.)

That holding was limited to the statutes and alignment before the Court. It did not disturb independent diversity jurisdiction over municipal corporations. It did not decide that every state-law claim against an additional party was constitutionally forbidden. Other statutory language or alignments could produce a different answer. The Court specifically observed that exclusive federal jurisdiction over a tort claim against the United States could strengthen the argument for trying related claims together because only the federal forum might accommodate all of them. That observation reserved a question rather than resolving an actual Federal Tort Claims Act joinder dispute. (Id. at 17–18 & nn.12–13.)

The general inquiry was consequently twofold: Article III had to permit the jurisdiction, and Congress must not have expressly or implicitly negated it in the relevant statutes. Judicial economy mattered within those limits; it could not itself erase the county's statutory obstacle. The affirmance left the dismissal of the county intact without adjudicating liability for the discharge. (Id. at 18–19.)

## Brennan's dissent

Brennan would have applied Gibbs to related parties as well as related claims. Adding a defendant could affect convenience, fairness, or the difficulty of state-law issues, but those considerations belonged to the discretionary decision whether to exercise jurisdiction, not to a categorical absence of power. Duplicating the same factual litigation in state and federal courts could waste professional and judicial resources. (Id. at 19–22 & nn.1–2.)

He accepted that Congress could forbid pendent-party jurisdiction, but disputed the majority's inference that it had done so. Section 1343's reference to civil actions did not address the joinder of defendants on state claims. Excluding municipalities from substantive federal liability did not necessarily exclude a federal forum for an obligation created by state law. His extended legislative-history analysis distinguished objections to Congress imposing a new municipal obligation from acceptance of federal enforcement of existing municipal contracts or duties. Majority note 12 read those historical statements more narrowly as recognition of established diversity jurisdiction. (Id. at 22–30 & nn.3–12; majority at 17–18 n.12.)

Brennan also emphasized Congress's purpose of providing an independent federal forum to protect civil rights. Requiring an employee to choose between splitting one factual controversy and taking everything to state court could deter use of that federal remedy. The availability of a combined state proceeding was therefore not neutral convenience in his assessment. He would have preserved the federal court's power, subject to Gibbs's discretionary safeguards. (Id. at 30–37 & nn.13–17.)
