# Islamic Republic of Iran v. Pahlavi

**62 N.Y.2d 474, 467 N.E.2d 245, 478 N.Y.S.2d 597 (1984). New York Court of Appeals, July 5, 1984.** Simons wrote for a five-judge majority; Chief Judge Cooke and Judges Jasen, Jones, and Wachtler concurred. Judge Meyer dissented from the disposition concerning the Empress. Judge Kaye did not participate. Assigned account: CivPro PDF 236, printed 430–431, Note 2.

## Facts and procedural course

The Islamic Republic of Iran alleged that the former Shah and Empress had accepted bribes and misappropriated approximately $35 billion during the Shah’s thirty-eight-year reign, violating duties owed to Iran. It sought recovery of the funds, $20 billion in exemplary damages, an accounting, and a constructive trust over assets throughout the world. These were allegations, rather than adjudicated findings of wrongdoing or asset ownership. In November 1979 the Shah, undergoing cancer treatment at New York Hospital, received substituted service. The Empress was personally served at the New York residence of the Shah’s sister, Princess Ashraf. The defendants challenged justiciability, service or personal jurisdiction, and the suitability of New York as the forum. The trial court dismissed on forum non conveniens grounds; a divided Appellate Division affirmed. (477–478.)

Two procedural qualifications matter. The Shah died while the dismissal motion was pending. Because nobody substituted a representative, the Court of Appeals dismissed Iran’s appeal concerning him under CPLR 1021, without reaching the forum issue as to his estate. Separately, the court had already dismissed the Empress’s cross-appeal challenging the New York contacts necessary for personal jurisdiction because she was not aggrieved by the judgment dismissing the action. That earlier dismissal did not decide the jurisdictional argument on its merits. The present substantive review concerned Iran’s appeal from dismissal as against the Empress. (477–478 nn.1–2; 493–494.)

## Question, rule, and holding

Must a New York court retain a foreign controversy whenever the plaintiff may lack an alternative forum? The majority held that an available alternative forum is an important consideration, but is not an absolute prerequisite to dismissal under New York law. The lower courts had not abused their discretion as a matter of law in dismissing this action. (478–484.)

CPLR 327 authorizes a court, on a party’s motion, to stay or dismiss an action on just conditions when substantial justice calls for its resolution elsewhere. The defendant bears the burden of establishing the relevant public and private interests. Considerations include the burden on New York courts, hardship to the defendant, availability of another forum, the parties’ nonresidence, and the location of the underlying transaction. No single factor controls. The doctrine assumes an otherwise jurisdictionally sound action and asks whether exercising that jurisdiction is sensible in the particular circumstances. (478–479 & n.3.)

## Why the majority permitted dismissal

The litigation concerned decades of Iranian governmental management, foreign witnesses, and assets in numerous countries. Iranian law would probably govern important questions. The parties’ brief presence in New York supplied little connection between the state and the transactions being challenged. Iran’s lists of property extended well beyond New York; the asserted existence of relevant New York assets had not been established. A New York court’s ability to impose an effective constructive trust on foreign property was uncertain, and much relevant evidence was in Iran, under the plaintiff government’s control and beyond New York’s compulsory process. The inquiry therefore concerned practical access to proof and effective relief, as well as the cost of adjudicating an extensive foreign controversy. (479–480, 482–483.)

Iran relied on decisions describing another available forum as indispensable to forum non conveniens. The majority treated the relevant language in Gulf Oil as dictum and read New York precedent as identifying availability as one pertinent factor. An absolute requirement would compel New York to adjudicate foreign-based actions unrelated to the state whenever another forum refused or could not accept them. The majority also distinguished federal transfer decisions: New York could not transfer this case to a foreign court in the manner authorized for transfers within the federal system. (480–483.)

The court did not make a factual finding that every other forum was unavailable. It said Iran had failed to establish that proposition, then explained that dismissal was permissible even assuming it. Iran was a sovereign government with control over its legal system and relevant evidence, rather than a private claimant helplessly excluded from courts by a regime it could not influence. New York did not acquire responsibility for remedying defects in that system merely because Iran selected New York as a preferable forum. On this record the state’s limited connection and the substantial foreign burdens justified an unconditional dismissal, without requiring the Empress to accept process elsewhere. (482–484.)

## The Algerian Accords argument

Iran also contended that the January 1981 agreements resolving the hostage crisis obligated American courts to entertain the action. The United States had agreed to take specified steps concerning the former Shah’s assets, including asset restraints and informing courts that certain immunity defenses were unavailable, and to guarantee enforcement of final judgments within the agreement’s terms. The majority distinguished those undertakings from a promise that a New York court would adjudicate the underlying claim. Language requiring the United States to “facilitate” recovery did not displace ordinary forum rules. The agreements were negotiated with knowledge that this litigation was pending, yet did not expressly guarantee this forum. (484–487.)

The opinion raised constitutional concerns about compelling state courts to adjudicate through an executive agreement, but did not announce a categorical holding invalidating such agreements. It distinguished decisions concerning settlements of international claims from this dispute between Iran and its own former nationals. Nor did its forum ruling resolve the defendants’ broader political-question defenses or establish liability for the alleged misappropriation. (485–487.)

## Meyer’s dissent and the judgment

Meyer would have reversed as to the Empress. He regarded an available alternative forum as a threshold condition, supported by the statutory reference to another forum, legislative history, the Restatement, and federal forum non conveniens decisions. In his view the defendant should establish availability; the ordinary discretion to balance convenience did not authorize closing the courthouse when no suitable alternative existed. New York precedent retaining litigation involving foreign assets also undercut the majority’s practical objections. If an alternative emerged, a conditional stay could protect both sides without denying Iran any forum. Meyer additionally disagreed with the majority’s treatment of the Accords, although his developed analysis focused on forum non conveniens. (487–493.)

The court **affirmed the order concerning the Empress, with costs, and dismissed the appeal concerning the Shah, without costs**. It neither adjudicated the alleged theft nor ordered asset recovery. (493–494.) The assigned account compresses these two dispositions into an affirmance of dismissal concerning the couple and presents the absence of an alternative forum more definitively than the actual opinion. The distinctions above follow the complete report. This is a historical New York CPLR decision; its state-law holding should not be substituted for the federal adequate-alternative-forum inquiry.
