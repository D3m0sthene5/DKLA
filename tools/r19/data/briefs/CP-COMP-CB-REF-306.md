# Spectacor Management Group v. Brown

**131 F.3d 120 (3d Cir. 1997). Decided November 24, 1997. No. 96-1969.** Judge McKee wrote the majority opinion, joined by Judge Greenberg. Senior Sixth Circuit Judge Wellford, sitting by designation, dissented. Assigned account: Civil Procedure casebook, physical PDF page 383, printed page 733, Note 5 on compulsory counterclaims and the jurisdictional amount.

## Employment dispute and jurisdictional allegations

Spectacor, a Pennsylvania joint venture managing stadiums, arenas, and other public facilities, sued its former executive vice-president, Matthew Brown, a New Jersey citizen, over payments made following his termination. It alleged payment of $42,500 in benefits and $4,921.04 for medical insurance, totaling $47,421.04—less than the $50,000 diversity threshold applicable when the suit was filed. It added $3,287.21 in payroll taxes, claiming $50,708.25 exclusive of interest and costs. **131 F.3d at 121.**

Brown argued that the tax component was a contrivance to obtain federal jurisdiction and prevent his intended state-court suit. He also argued that Spectacor’s own concessions showed it owed him more than it sought to recover. Spectacor maintained that the payments were recoverable advances against a settlement that never was concluded and that its claimed tax amount was asserted in good faith. The opinion describes Spectacor’s stipulated obligations of $50,000 in benefits and $15,692 in vacation pay and its position that money retained could be set off against Brown’s repayment obligation. These were the litigants’ positions in the employment dispute; the appellate jurisdictional holding did not establish that every disputed payroll-tax component was recoverable. **Id. at 121–123.**

Brown did not first move to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(1). He answered, expressly denying subject-matter jurisdiction, and asserted compulsory counterclaims for $135,000 in severance, $837,000 in commissions, $70,720 in bonus, and $17,550 in unused vacation and unreimbursed expenses. Those demands exceeded the threshold. Following a bench trial, the district court entered judgment for Brown, but for substantially less than he demanded. He appealed. **Id. at 120–122.**

## Issue and narrow holding

Could the amount demanded in Brown’s compulsory counterclaim be considered toward the diversity amount when he answered and counterclaimed without first obtaining consideration of a dismissal motion, even though his answer disputed jurisdiction?

The majority held that it could. By bringing a compulsory counterclaim before the court in these circumstances, Brown placed that demand into the controversy whose amount the court measured. It therefore did not decide whether the payroll taxes properly counted in Spectacor’s own demand. The majority also affirmed the merits judgment without extended discussion because it found no clear error in the district court’s evidentiary assessment. The report does not give the exact amount awarded to Brown. **Id. at 120–122, 126.**

## Majority reasoning: the whole controversy

The majority began with the usual rule measuring the amount from good-faith allegations in the complaint, subject to a legal-certainty challenge. But the complaint was not necessarily the entire controversy. Brown’s compulsory counterclaim arose from the same transactions: the parties disputed their obligations under the employment arrangement and the payments resulting from termination. A compulsory counterclaim represented part of what actually was at stake, not an unrelated additional lawsuit merely attached at the defendant’s preference. **Id. at 122–123.**

The majority drew support from Home Life, Roberts Mining, Fenton, and Motorists Mutual. It distinguished Home Life’s failure of jurisdiction where the counterclaim only reduced the original amount through a setoff, rather than independently demanding sufficient affirmative recovery. It also acknowledged the historical equity context of some earlier precedents rather than assuming they arose under the modern unified procedural rules. **Id. at 123–125 & n.5.**

One practical consideration was that a sufficiently large compulsory counterclaim could have supported an independently filed suit with the parties reversed. Measuring the actual connected controversy avoided making jurisdiction turn solely on which litigant reached federal court first. That consideration did not establish that Brown had voluntarily chosen this forum: footnote 7 acknowledged his assertion that Spectacor raced to federal court and his preference for state court. It also noted that Spectacor, as a Pennsylvania citizen, could not have removed a Pennsylvania state-court suit in the circumstances discussed there. **Id. at 125–126 & n.7.**

## No consent or waiver theory

The majority expressly rejected the idea that litigants could confer federal subject-matter jurisdiction by agreement or waiver. Its explanation instead concerned which claims had become part of the controversy presented to the court. Brown’s jurisdictional denial remained a challenge, but it did not prevent his counterclaim from being before the court for measurement. A Rule 12 dismissal motion could have been filed before an answer and counterclaim; he did not take that course. A later Rule 11 sanctions request, noted in footnote 8, did not change the analysis. **Id. at 125–126 & n.8.**

The holding was confined to compulsory counterclaims. Footnotes 2 and 4 expressly declined to extend it to permissive counterclaims or decide whether a permissive claim might ever have a sufficient connection to affect the jurisdictional amount. **Id. at 121–123 & nn.2, 4.**

## Removal and Horton qualifications

The majority distinguished removal cases that excluded counterclaim amounts. Removal involved a separate statutory framework, a policy of narrow construction protecting the state forum, and potential dependence on state definitions of compulsory claims. This action was filed originally in federal court. The opinion therefore did not hold that Brown’s counterclaim could make an otherwise insufficient state action removable. **Id. at 125–126.**

The majority discussed Horton v. Liberty Mutual extensively in footnote 9 but expressly did not rely on it to decide this case. Horton involved the particular Texas workers’ compensation review system and a complaint anticipating a larger claim. Although it could support the result, the majority acknowledged difficulties in generalizing its reasoning and used it for completeness rather than as an indispensable premise. **Id. at 126–127 n.9.**

## Wellford’s dissent

Wellford would remand for a fuller determination of jurisdiction. He stressed that jurisdiction ordinarily is assessed when the action is filed, the invoking party must establish it when challenged, and Brown expressly objected in his answer. In his view, filing a counterclaim that the rules required should not strip Brown of that objection simply because he did not use a separate dismissal motion. He distinguished cases where defendants had not initially challenged jurisdiction and read Motorists Mutual as protecting the objecting defendant’s choice. **Id. at 127–129.**

The dissent also questioned the adequacy of the payroll-tax allegation and the district court’s good-faith explanation. It asked whether the taxes were actually incurred, merely bookkeeping entries, part of the advance, or amounts Brown personally had to repay. It distinguished Savarese’s offset setting and rejected the majority’s reliance on portions of Home Life. Its two footnotes further addressed the amount claimed after an offset and the limits of counting an insurance loan or unearned premiums. **Id. at 127–129 & dissenting nn.1–2.**

The dissent stated that Spectacor could have sought removal had Brown sued in state court. The majority’s footnote 7 expressly identified the Pennsylvania forum-defendant obstacle in the contemplated setting. These are differing statements in the two writings, not a unified ruling that removal was available. The majority’s narrower holding governed.

## Judgment and assigned significance

The Third Circuit **affirmed the district court’s judgment**, with Wellford dissenting in favor of remand for jurisdictional inquiry. The majority’s chosen ground left the payroll-tax recovery question unresolved; it did not hold that parties could waive jurisdiction, that all counterclaims counted, or that the same reasoning governed removal. **Id. at 127, 129.**

The assigned note accurately states the majority’s answer-and-compulsory-counterclaim holding and asks separately about removal. It does not reproduce Brown’s express jurisdictional objection, the partial-payment background, or Wellford’s dissent. The full opinion supplies those features and shows why describing Brown simply as having accepted federal jurisdiction would be misleading. The $50,000 threshold is the historical threshold in this litigation; the majority’s footnote 3 itself noted the subsequent increase to $75,000.
