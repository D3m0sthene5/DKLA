Justice Kennedy wrote the majority opinion, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Scalia, Souter, and
Thomas. Justice Stevens dissented with Justice Breyer. Justice Ginsburg dissented with Justices Stevens,

Parties
In the first action, Allapattah and approximately 10,000 Exxon dealers were plaintiffs claiming that Exxon
systematically overcharged them for fuel. Exxon, the defendant below, sought Supreme Court review of the
decision allowing supplemental jurisdiction over class members with claims below the diversity amount
threshold. In the second action, an injured child and members of her family sued Star-Kist. The family
members sought review after the First Circuit rejected jurisdiction over their smaller claims. These remained
distinct underlying lawsuits; consolidation brought their common statutory question before the Supreme Court.
Majority, Part I; CB 365–366.
Material Facts
The Exxon dealers alleged an intentional scheme to inflate the price of fuel they bought from Exxon. Their
action proceeded as a diversity class action. At least one class representative had a claim satisfying the
jurisdictional amount, but some class members did not. The jurisdictional question concerned whether those
smaller, related claims could accompany a qualifying claim in the same action. It did not turn on adding
unrelated losses across different people until the class collectively crossed the ordinary diversity threshold.
Majority, Parts I and II.B; CB 365–368.
In Ortega, a nine-year-old girl suffered unusually severe injuries when she cut her finger on a tuna can. Her
family joined under Rule 20, asserting claims for emotional distress and medical expenses associated with her
injury. The First Circuit determined that her allegations satisfied the amount requirement while the family
members’ allegations did not. The related claims thus presented the same supplemental-jurisdiction problem
outside the class-action setting. Majority, Part I; CB 366.
Procedural History
The dealers filed in the Northern District of Florida in 1991. After a unanimous jury verdict for the plaintiffs,
the district court certified for interlocutory appeal the question whether it had properly exercised supplemental
jurisdiction over class members whose individual claims lacked the required amount. The Eleventh Circuit
upheld that jurisdiction. The Supreme Court’s review concerned that jurisdictional ruling, rather than an
independent reconsideration of whether Exxon had overcharged the dealers. Majority, Part I; CB 366.
The Ortega family sued in the District of Puerto Rico. The district court granted summary judgment for
Star-Kist on the ground that none of the plaintiffs satisfied the amount requirement. The First Circuit disagreed
about the child’s allegations but held that § 1367 did not authorize supplemental jurisdiction over the other
family members. The Supreme Court consolidated the cases to resolve the disagreement among the circuits
about § 1367’s effect. Majority, Part I; CB 366; full-opinion supplement concerning the First Circuit’s statutory
rationale.

Issue
When complete diversity and the other applicable jurisdictional requirements are present, and at least one
named plaintiff has a claim satisfying § 1332’s amount-in-controversy requirement, does § 1367 authorize
jurisdiction over related claims of additional Rule 20 plaintiffs or Rule 23 class members whose claims fall
below that amount? The disputed threshold was whether such a suit is a civil action within the district court’s
original jurisdiction for purposes of § 1367(a). Majority, Part II.B; CB 367–368.
Rule and Holding
Yes. Section 1367(a) supplies a broad grant over claims forming the same Article III case or controversy as a
claim within the court’s original jurisdiction, expressly including claims involving additional parties. Once at
least one named plaintiff’s claim qualifies and other relevant jurisdictional requirements are satisfied, the
smaller related claims do not destroy the necessary original-jurisdiction foundation. Section 1367(b) does not
exclude the additional Rule 20 plaintiffs or Rule 23 class members involved here. Majority, Part II.B; CB
367–371.
The holding preserves the complete-diversity requirement applicable to these ordinary diversity actions. It
distinguishes a defect in a particular claim’s amount from the presence of opposing parties whose citizenship
destroys complete diversity. The statute displaced Clark and Zahn’s requirement that every relevant plaintiff
independently satisfy the jurisdictional amount, within the circumstances and statutory limits the Court
identified. Majority, Parts II.A–B; CB 366–371.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the Eleventh Circuit in Exxon. It reversed the First Circuit in Ortega and
remanded that case for proceedings consistent with the opinion. The family members’ smaller claims were not
excluded on the First Circuit’s theory that each plaintiff necessarily had to satisfy the amount requirement.
Majority, concluding disposition.
Reasoning
The Court began with the need for statutory authority. Gibbs’s common-fact conception of a constitutional case
identifies the outer relationship among claims, but federal district courts still need authorization from Congress.
Earlier cases treated additional claims between existing parties more generously than claims involving
additional parties. Finley refused to infer the latter authority from a statute conferring jurisdiction over claims
against a particular defendant. Congress responded in 1990 by enacting § 1367. Agreement that Congress
reversed Finley did not establish that the statute accomplished only that result. Majority, Parts II.A–B; CB
366–367.
The decisive language in subsection (a) refers to civil actions within original jurisdiction and then grants
supplemental jurisdiction over all other sufficiently related claims, including claims involving additional
parties. The majority read the first requirement as satisfied by an otherwise qualifying claim within the action.
Requiring original jurisdiction over every claim before supplemental jurisdiction could attach would make the
supplemental grant largely self-defeating: its function is precisely to reach some claims that do not
independently qualify. Majority, Part II.B; CB 367–369.
Subsection (b) reinforced that reading. In actions founded solely on diversity, it withdraws supplemental
jurisdiction in specified circumstances involving claims by plaintiffs against parties added under Rules 14, 19,
20, or 24, and claims of persons proposed to join as plaintiffs under Rule 19 or to intervene as plaintiffs under
Rule 24, where jurisdiction would be inconsistent with § 1332. The provision does not make the corresponding

exclusion for plaintiffs permissively joined under Rule 20 or class members under Rule 23. The distinction
between claims against Rule 20 defendants and claims by Rule 20 plaintiffs is therefore consequential.
Majority, Part II.B, reproducing § 1367(b); CB 368.
The majority rejected an indivisibility theory under which every claim in a complaint must stand or fall as one
inseparable jurisdictional unit. Federal practice permits defective parties or claims to be dismissed while
retaining properly supported ones. Moreover, Gibbs itself would become difficult to explain if a related state
claim deprived the entire civil action of original jurisdiction. The statutory language gave the Court no basis for
imposing that all-or-nothing conception only in diversity cases. Majority, Part II.B; CB 368–369.
It separately addressed the argument that one jurisdictional defect contaminates the entire case. Complete
diversity serves the purpose of supplying a neutral federal forum where the relevant opposing parties come
from different states. A nondiverse opposing party undermines that jurisdictional premise. By contrast, the
amount requirement screens out disputes too small to warrant federal attention. Adding a smaller related claim
does not make the already qualifying claim less substantial. The Court thus explained why preserving complete
diversity did not require preserving the same rule for each additional plaintiff’s amount. Majority, Part II.B; CB
369–371.
The different treatment of Rule 19 and Rule 20 plaintiffs was admittedly awkward. The majority considered
possible explanations but did not purport to establish Congress’s actual motive. It reasoned that a possible
drafting omission did not authorize a court to add an exception absent from the text. The alternative
interpretation also created a difficulty: if subsection (a) already excluded every plaintiff with a deficient
amount, some of subsection (b)’s express exclusions would become unnecessary. These competing structural
consequences supported the majority’s reading of the enacted provision. Majority, Part II.B; CB 369–371.
The majority regarded the text as unambiguous and rejected reliance on legislative history to preserve Zahn. It
additionally explained that the history contained conflicting indications: a House Report suggested
preservation of pre-Finley limitations, while materials associated with the drafting process contemplated
displacement of Zahn. Kennedy viewed a report as incapable of revising the legal operation of the enacted text.
The brief distinguishes this judicial assessment of legislative materials from an independent finding about
legislators’ motives. Majority, Part II.C; CB 371; detailed discussion of the competing drafting materials is a
full-opinion supplement.
The Court also rejected the suggestion that the recently enacted Class Action Fairness Act resolved these cases.
That Act was not retroactive and concerned a different jurisdictional route; it did not determine the meaning of
§ 1367 enacted in 1990. The Court therefore decided the supplemental-jurisdiction issue on its own statutory
basis. Majority, Part II.D. [Full-opinion supplement; this discussion is expressly omitted from the assigned
excerpt.]
Separate Opinions
Justice Ginsburg accepted that the majority’s reading was plausible but favored continuity with the preexisting
construction of § 1332. In her view, subsection (a)’s reference to original jurisdiction incorporated both
complete diversity and the traditional requirement that each plaintiff independently meet the amount threshold.
Plaintiffs failing that initial condition never reached subsection (b), so Congress had no need to exclude them
there. She understood subsection (b) as guarding against later efforts to evade the ordinary diversity limits
through claims historically associated with ancillary jurisdiction. She would have affirmed in Ortega and
reversed in Exxon. Ginsburg dissent, Parts I–II and conclusion; CB 371–373.

Justice Stevens, joined by Justice Breyer, emphasized interpretive method. He disagreed that the majority’s

Notes
The decision permits a smaller related claim to accompany an independently qualifying claim; it does not
authorize combining separate small claims to manufacture the first qualifying ordinary diversity claim. Nor
does omission from subsection (b) alone suffice: the original-jurisdiction foundation and same-case
relationship under subsection (a) must first exist. The analysis then requires checking the applicable exceptions
rather than treating Gibbs as a free-standing authorization. Majority, Parts II.A–B; CB 366–371.
A precise answer should identify who asserts the additional claim and how that person entered the litigation.
Rule 20 appears in subsection (b), but the statutory wording distinguishes claims by added plaintiffs from
claims against added defendants. The narrow result concerns amount-deficient Rule 20 plaintiffs and Rule 23
class members in otherwise qualifying cases; the broader reasoning interprets how an original-jurisdiction
claim can support a larger civil action. Majority, Part II.B; CB 367–371.
