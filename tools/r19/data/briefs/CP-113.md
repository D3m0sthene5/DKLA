# Flowers v. Flowers

**Civil Procedure · CP-113**  
397 S.W.2d 121 (Tex. Civ. App.—Amarillo 1965) · November 8, 1965 · Justice Chapman  
Rehearing denied December 6, 1965, as recorded in the report

## Custody dispute and juror challenge

Billie Charlene Flowers filed for divorce in January 1964. She and her husband, R.A. Flowers, disputed custody of three daughters, then aged two to ten. The case was tried in a small community where several prospective jurors had heard rumors about the dispute. The parties agreed, with the judge's approval, to present only custody evidence and issues to the jury, leaving divorce, support, and property matters to the court. 397 S.W.2d at 122, 125.

On voir dire, the mother's lawyer disclosed that the evidence would show social drinking and one or two occasions of excessive consumption. Prospective juror Mrs. Schmidt initially said that she knew the family through their church but had formed no opinion. Further questioning revealed categorical opposition to drinking and a willingness to hold any drinking against a parent's fitness. When the judge asked whether she would deny either parent custody if that parent drank, she answered affirmatively. Later leading questions elicited more equivocal assurances. The court overruled the mother's challenge for cause. 397 S.W.2d at 122–23.

An affidavit from another prospective juror, Mrs. Philpot, described Schmidt's preselection statements expressing sympathy for the father and stating that the mother had previously run off and left him. The judge refused to hear Philpot's tendered testimony on motions for mistrial and a new trial. The mother's counsel also testified that eleven panel members were undesirable from her perspective and that fuller disclosure of Schmidt's views would have changed the allocation of peremptory challenges. A marked jury list supported that testimony.

## Legal issue and rule

Did Schmidt's expressed prejudgment require disqualification despite her responses to the judge's rehabilitative questions? The appellate court held that it did. Texas's jury guarantee required impartiality, and Article 2134 disqualified a person biased or prejudiced for or against either party. Disqualifying bias could concern the litigation's subject matter, rather than personal hostility alone. A preference rose to disqualifying bias when it naturally implied that the juror would not act impartially; prejudice meant prejudgment. Once statutory disqualification was established, the issue was one of law rather than trial-court discretion. 397 S.W.2d at 123–24.

## Application and alternative rationale

Schmidt's categorical views about drinking, coupled with her statements about the family, demonstrated that she had prejudged matters central to parental fitness. The court concluded that the record established statutory disqualification as a matter of law. Even if bias had instead remained a factual question for the trial judge, her established views could not be erased by agreeing to a leading assurance that she could decide on the evidence. On that alternative approach, retaining her was an abuse of discretion. 397 S.W.2d at 124.

The full opinion's single footnote explains that the cited law required disregarding a biased juror's assurance of being able to put bias aside. The court also stated that a complainant need not independently establish probable injury from juror disqualification before obtaining a new trial. It nevertheless found supporting harm here in the evidence about peremptory-challenge choices. The assigned excerpt principally emphasizes the bias ruling and omits these additional discussions.

## Other errors addressed in the complete opinion

The mother carried the burden on the submitted question whether she was a fit custodian. Although she presented evidence first, the court denied her counsel the right to open and close argument. Rules 266 and 269 ordinarily attached that valuable right to the party bearing the burden, and the father had made none of the admissions permitting a different arrangement. The appellate court considered the preserved refusal another ground for reversal, particularly in a lengthy, contested record. 397 S.W.2d at 124–25.

The judge also refused to hear divorce evidence after the custody verdict, despite having reserved that issue for judicial determination and earlier excluding such evidence from the jury phase. The parties were entitled to be heard on that remaining controversy. The opinion viewed the accumulated errors as requiring a new trial. It further emphasized that the custody verdict was binding under the statute then applicable, making an impartial jury especially consequential. These are the historical Texas procedural rules the court applied, not a claim that every later custody proceeding follows the same arrangement.

## Judgment, limits, and sources

The court **reversed and remanded for a new trial**. It did not itself award custody to either parent or make the drinking allegations an automatic rule of parental unfitness. Chapman wrote the sole reported opinion; no separate writing or recorded vote count appears. The report notes denial of rehearing without reproducing a separate rehearing opinion.

The lesson is that a juror's demonstrated prejudgment may survive a formulaic promise to be fair. Voir dire must reveal how the prospective juror would evaluate the contested issue, and appellate review must attend to concrete answers rather than the final assurance alone.
