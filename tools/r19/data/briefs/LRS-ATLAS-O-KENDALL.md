# Kendall v. United States ex rel. Stokes
37 U.S. (12 Pet.) 524 (1838) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and precise relief

The Supreme Court affirmed with costs the Circuit Court for the District of Columbia’s judgment awarding a peremptory mandamus and remanded for further proceedings. The writ required Postmaster General Amos Kendall to credit mail contractors with the entire amount that the Solicitor of the Treasury had awarded under a special act of Congress. It compelled a definite bookkeeping act that Congress made nondiscretionary. It did not command the withdrawal of public money or resolve a general power of courts to supervise every executive policy choice. Justice Thompson delivered the Court’s opinion. Chief Justice Taney and Justice Barbour wrote separately in dissent, and Justice Catron concurred in both dissenting opinions. The recorded dissenters agreed that the duty was ministerial and that Congress could constitutionally authorize judicial enforcement; their disagreement concerned whether Congress had given this particular court the required jurisdiction. (608–626, 626–627, 641–642, 653.)

The assigned account emphasizes that the President’s obligation to execute the laws does not authorize dispensing with them. Its introduction requires two corrections from the full opinion. The judgment compelled credit, rather than Treasury payment, and the Court expressly stated that the President had neither forbidden nor advised Kendall to refrain from the credit. Counsel invoked presidential control as a legal argument; the Court did not find an express presidential order directing the refusal. (613–614; assigned PDF 21–22/printed 265–266.)

## Mail contracts and the special settlement statute

William B. Stokes, Richard C. Stockton, Lucius W. Stockton, and Daniel Moore had contracts with Postmaster General William T. Barry for transporting the mail. Barry approved allowances and credits, and some amounts were paid. After Kendall succeeded him in 1835, Kendall reexamined the contracts, withdrew allowances and credits, and recharged the contractors for payments previously made. Unable to resolve the dispute administratively, the contractors petitioned Congress. The ultimate controversy was over their claims against the United States, presented through the Post Office Department. (527–528, 608–611.)

The special act of July 2, 1836 directed the Solicitor of the Treasury to investigate and adjust claims for extra services, determine their equity, and make allowances supported by the evidence and principles of equity. It separately directed the Postmaster General to credit whatever amounts the Solicitor decided were due. Congress prescribed exclusions for specified disputed services and routes; the statute did not simply authorize unrestricted payment of anything the contractors claimed. It required the Solicitor to report the law and facts underlying his decision to Congress, but supplied no appeal allowing Kendall to revise the award. (528–529, 609, 611.)

Solicitor Virgil Maxcy investigated and made awards that were communicated to Kendall. The contractors’ petition identified an adjusted total of $161,563.89, a credited or paid amount of $122,101.46, and an uncredited residue of $39,462.43. Those figures appear together in the detailed account at page 530. The official report varies certain numbers when retelling them at pages 609 and 611; the latter includes an inconsistent residual amount. This brief uses the petition’s internally consistent figures and does not silently treat every printed restatement as identical. The holding turns on the statutory duty to enter the full award, not on choosing between those typographical variants. (529–531, 609, 611.)

## Executive and congressional steps before litigation

Kendall argued that the Solicitor had exceeded the statutory submission. The contractors asked the President to cause the act to be executed. After receiving Kendall’s explanation, the President referred the disagreement to Congress, describing that body as best placed to explain the intended extent of its own reference. The contractors therefore renewed their legislative request. The Senate Judiciary Committee concluded that the award was intended to be final and that no further legislation was needed; it recommended full compliance. The Senate unanimously adopted the resolution. On reviewing further submissions transmitted by the President, the committee adhered to its position. This sequence did not constitute enactment of a new payment law by the Senate alone. It supplied context for enforcing the existing act. (531–533, 612–613.)

Thompson took care to distinguish the President’s conduct from counsel’s constitutional theory. The President had not claimed a dispensing power and did not forbid or advise nonexecution. The committee’s second report likewise described his transmission of the competing materials without an opinion or recommendation on the merits. That finding is incompatible with the assignment’s shorthand that Kendall invoked an express presidential direction to refuse. (613.)

## Proceedings and opposing positions

On May 26, 1837, the contractors petitioned the Circuit Court for the District of Columbia, sitting for Washington County. Their petition sought both entry of the statutory credit and payment of the resulting balance with interest after proper charges. The subsequent mandamus concerned the credit. Following a rule to show cause, the court issued a mandamus nisi on June 7 requiring compliance or a showing of cause. Kendall appeared through counsel and filed his response on June 24. On July 13 the court ordered a peremptory mandamus. Kendall brought a writ of error to the Supreme Court. This was appellate review of the Circuit Court’s jurisdiction and writ, unlike Marbury’s direct original Supreme Court application. (527, 533–535, 608–609.)

Kendall’s response questioned whether the Constitution allowed judicial control of executive functions, whether Congress had conferred mandamus authority on the D.C. court, whether this dispute was a proper case for the writ even if general authority existed, and whether the command required a legal impossibility. An Attorney General’s opinion supporting his position accompanied the response. Key and Attorney General Butler represented him, while Coxe and Johnson represented the contractors. The recorded argument framed presidential responsibility for faithful execution as requiring authority to supervise how subordinate officers construed and implemented the statute. It also disputed whether a general jurisdictional grant supplied the special supervisory mandamus power. The Court’s opinion answered these distinct constitutional, remedial, and statutory-jurisdiction questions separately. (534–543, 608–626.)

## A ministerial duty enforceable by mandamus

The majority rejected the premise that enforcing this credit invaded the President’s constitutional executive power. It did not attempt to draw every boundary between judicial and executive authority. Certain political duties of officers operate under presidential direction, but Congress may impose constitutionally permissible statutory duties that bind officers by law. Here Congress assigned decision-making to the Solicitor and left Kendall no discretion to control or review his determination. Neither Kendall nor the President could deny the required ministerial act. No fraud or misconduct by the Solicitor was alleged. The majority questioned whether Congress itself could subsequently deprive the contractors of their vested award in those circumstances, but expressly declined to decide how far further legislation could have gone. (610–612.)

The Take Care obligation could not imply a presidential authority to forbid executing the law. Thompson characterized that proposed authority as a dispensing power that would permit control of congressional legislation and obstruct the administration of justice. But the actual act commanded here was narrowly defined: enter the award as a credit in the departmental accounts. Kendall did not need to secure funds or decide whether the account ultimately yielded a payable balance. If the credit exceeded the debit, money could not be taken from the Treasury without further legislative provision. The book entry was comparable to a clerk entering a court’s order in its minutes. (612–614.)

Mandamus was appropriate because the right and corresponding duty were fixed and no adequate specific remedy remained. The inherited common-law writ required performance of an official duty where ordinary remedies were insufficient. Another application to Congress had already failed to produce further action. Removing Kendall would not guarantee that his successor entered the credit, and was not an ordinary legal remedy superseding the writ. Repeated damages actions would leave a continuing denial uncorrected; even an action measuring damages by the award might be ineffective if the amount exceeded the defendant’s ability to pay. The majority treated the mandamus proceeding as a judicial suit asserting an enforceable right. (614–615.)

## Why this D.C. court had the necessary authority

That conclusion did not end the jurisdictional inquiry. McIntire v. Wood and McCluny v. Silliman had established that ordinary federal circuit courts in the states lacked the authority for comparable original mandamus against federal officers. The constitutional judicial power could embrace such a proceeding, but Congress had not delegated all of that power to those courts. A state court likewise could not exercise the federal supervisory writ, and Marbury barred an original Supreme Court application. The majority therefore needed a positive basis for distinguishing the D.C. Circuit Court. (615–618.)

Thompson examined the District’s unusual organization. Congress exercised exclusive legislative authority there, without the ordinary division between federal and state governments, and had created its highest court of original jurisdiction. Section 1 of the February 27, 1801 act preserved Maryland law on the Maryland side of the Potomac. That included the common-law mandamus remedy exemplified by Maryland’s decision in Runkle v. Winemiller. The majority discussed the historical King’s Bench supervisory writ, while recognizing that legislatures could allocate the power differently. It distinguished original common-law mandamus from federal writs under section 14 of the Judiciary Act used to aid an already existing jurisdiction, such as requiring a lower court to proceed to judgment so appellate review became possible. (618–622.)

Sections 3 and 5 supplied further support. Section 3 gave the D.C. court the powers vested by law in federal circuit courts; section 5 gave it cognizance of all cases in law and equity between parties resident or found within the District. The majority refused to make the terminology of powers and cognizance a reason to deny jurisdiction when the provisions together supplied both. The resident-or-found restriction concerned personal jurisdiction and service, which could be waived by appearance; no timely personal objection was made here. The subject-matter language encompassed this judicial demand to enforce a specific statutory right. (622–624.)

Most decisively, the Court construed section 3 as incorporating the powers of the circuit courts existing when the D.C. act was passed: those established by the February 13, 1801 act. That law broadly granted cognizance of cases arising under the Constitution, federal statutes, and treaties. Its later repeal did not automatically repeal powers separately incorporated into the D.C. enactment. Adoption by reference brought the existing provision into the adopting law rather than prospectively incorporating every later alteration. The incorporated federal-question grant supplied the delegation missing in the earlier circuit-court mandamus decisions. (624–626.)

## Taney, Barbour, and Catron

Taney agreed that Congress could regulate a statutory office and subject it to judicial supervision, that the July 1836 act required the credit, and that Kendall’s duty was nondiscretionary. He nevertheless found no statutory grant authorizing this D.C. court to issue the original writ. At common law, the prerogative remedy belonged to a court exercising sovereign, general supervisory authority, such as King’s Bench or Maryland’s General Court, rather than to an ordinary geographically limited tribunal. Preserving Maryland substantive law did not confer all the General Court’s powers. Later Maryland legislation giving county courts mandamus authority postdated the District’s adoption and could not establish the power here. Taney’s historical footnote identifies his manuscript source for the Lord Proprietary’s personal participation in the Provincial Court; it is evidence supporting that institutional history, not a separate holding. (626–633 & note at 631.)

He further reasoned that all cases at law did not independently confer a prerogative remedy that ordinary circuit courts lacked. He read section 3’s reference dynamically, preserving uniformity with the current circuit-court system after repeal of the February 13 act. Alternatively, powers meant procedural means, distinct from the cases over which a court had cognizance; incorporating procedural powers did not incorporate the other act’s broad subject-matter jurisdiction. Finally, the earlier insurance-company mandamus appeal did not impliedly establish trial jurisdiction merely because the Supreme Court considered whether the value supported appellate review. Appellate authority includes correction of an erroneous assumption of trial jurisdiction. Taney would reverse. (633–641.)

Barbour likewise accepted the constitutional availability of mandamus and the ministerial character of the credit. He compared the jurisdictional language of the ordinary circuit courts with the D.C. grant, arguing that suits at law and cases at law were equivalent. The absence of ordinary party and amount restrictions did not by itself create original mandamus power. He distinguished auxiliary writ authority from authority to acquire a new case through a writ; section 14 allowed the former. Congress’s express assignment of mandamus to the Supreme Court supported an inference against granting it to inferior courts by implication, even though Marbury later invalidated part of that intended original grant. Maryland law could not give the D.C. court supervisory power over a federal officer that Maryland courts themselves lacked. He would reverse. Catron expressly joined the opinions of Taney and Barbour without a separate writing. (641–653.)

## Judgment and assigned scope

The majority affirmed with costs and remanded for further proceedings, preserving the command to enter the full credit. The three recorded dissenters challenged the statutory allocation of jurisdiction, rather than endorsing an executive right to disregard the settlement law. That distinction is essential to understanding both the agreement and the division within the Court. (626–627, 641–642, 653.)
