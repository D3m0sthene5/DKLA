# Cotnam v. Wisdom

83 Ark. 601, 104 S.W. 164 (1907)

## Court, author, and source scope
The Supreme Court of Arkansas considered compensation for emergency medical services rendered to an unconscious accident victim. Chief Justice Hill delivered the opinion. Justices Battle and Wood agreed with important portions of the decision but disagreed with exclusion of the patient's wealth from the fee calculation. The assigned excerpt is at printed pages 146–149. The complete judicial opinion has three numbered discussions; citations to those numbered parts below identify the court's own divisions because the retrieved full-text version does not display reliable internal reporter-page breaks.

## Parties and material facts
The defendant was Cotnam, administrator of A. M. Harrison's estate. The plaintiffs were physicians who responded after Harrison was thrown from a streetcar and suffered serious injuries. Bystanders summoned them. They performed a difficult operation in an effort to save him, but he died without regaining consciousness. Harrison never had an opportunity to request their services, agree to a price, approve their selection, or assent to treatment. Opinion pt. 1; casebook 146–147.

The physicians sought payment from the estate for professional services. Evidence indicated a professional custom of adjusting fees to a patient's ability to pay. The jury was also told that Harrison was a bachelor whose estate would pass to nieces and nephews. These facts concerned the amount of a fee, not whether he had actually consented to a contract. The trial instruction allowed the jury to consider the operation's character and importance, the surgeon's responsibility, experience and training, and the patient's ability to pay. Opinion pt. 3; casebook 146, 148–149.

The unsuccessful outcome was independently important. The estate argued that recovery required proof of benefit to the patient, even though the physicians' attempt to save his life failed. The complete opinion answers that argument separately from the issues of assent and the patient's wealth. A brief confined to the edited excerpt could miss this second part of the court's analysis. Opinion pt. 2.

## Procedural history
The estate appealed from a judgment awarding the physicians compensation. The disputed jury instructions authorized a reasonable fee for emergency treatment and permitted wealth to influence the amount. The Arkansas Supreme Court sustained the legal basis for recovery without consent, but held that irrelevant and prejudicial financial evidence had been used in determining the award. It reversed the judgment and remanded. Opinion pts. 1–3.

The reversal therefore did not establish that the physicians had no claim. It required reconsideration of compensation under the proper legal standard. The court's approval of restitutionary liability and its rejection of the particular judgment can coexist; merging them into either a simple plaintiff victory or a complete defense victory loses the procedural result.

## Issues
First, may physicians recover the reasonable value of necessary emergency services from the estate of an unconscious patient who never assented to treatment? Second, must they prove that the operation actually benefited or saved the patient? Third, may the patient's wealth and the fact that collateral heirs will receive his estate be considered in determining reasonable compensation? Opinion pts. 1–3.

## Holdings
The law imposes an obligation to pay reasonable compensation for necessary professional services furnished in good faith to a person rendered helpless by injury. The obligation does not depend on proof of an actual agreement, express or inferred from conduct. It is a quasi-contractual duty imposed by law. Opinion pt. 1.

The physicians were not required to prove that the operation succeeded. Subject to the ordinary requirement that professional services be performed with appropriate skill and care, the law does not make a physician's compensation depend upon a cure or recovery in the absence of an express undertaking to that effect. Opinion pt. 2.

In these circumstances, wealth was not a proper factor in calculating the fee. There was no actual transaction in which patient and physician could have contemplated a wealth-adjusted charge. Nor was the identity of the patient's heirs relevant. Admission of that evidence and the corresponding instruction required reversal. Opinion pt. 3.

## Actual judgment
The judgment was reversed and the cause remanded. The physicians' entitlement to reasonable compensation for the emergency services survived. The amount previously fixed did not. The court could not treat the evidentiary error as harmless because the verdict, although within the upper end of testimony, substantially exceeded other evidence of a reasonable fee. Opinion pt. 3; casebook 149.

## Reasoning
### An obligation implied by law is different from an actual promise
The administrator correctly insisted that Harrison could not have assented. The court accepted that premise but rejected the conclusion that no remedy was possible. Drawing on Sceva v. True, it explained the longstanding distinction between a contract implied in fact and an obligation implied in law. An implied-in-fact contract is an actual agreement demonstrated by conduct or circumstances. A quasi-contract supplies a legal duty even when no actual promise or meeting of minds occurred. Opinion pt. 1.

That distinction avoids the false factual claim that an unconscious person secretly consented. The law instead recognizes an obligation to compensate those who furnish necessary services in a helpless person's interest. The court compared emergency treatment with necessary services furnished to persons otherwise incapable of contracting. It characterized this as an established remedy rather than an innovation requiring invention of an actual exchange. Id.

The setting also distinguishes emergency professional intervention from an unwanted service imposed on a capable person who could have been asked in advance. Harrison's inability to make arrangements and the urgency of the attempt to preserve life support the legal intervention. The opinion does not establish a general license to supply any unsolicited service and demand payment afterward. Id.; casebook 145–146, 149–150.

### Reasonable compensation does not depend on success
The estate's objection that no benefit had been proved would have made payment turn on survival. The court rejected that implication. A physician ordinarily undertakes to apply professional care and skill, rather than warrant a cure; failure to save a critically injured patient does not by itself mean the professional services had no compensable value. The complete opinion addresses this issue as its second numbered discussion. Opinion pt. 2.

This is especially important when using restitution vocabulary. Describing the remedy as compensation for a benefit should not silently convert it into a contract guaranteeing a successful result. In this emergency setting the legally recognized service is the reasonable professional attempt, not the eventual fact of recovery. A special guarantee or negligent performance would present other issues; the opinion does not use either to deny recovery here. Id.

### The fee cannot be enlarged by an agreement that never existed
The court distinguished a professional custom that may help interpret an actual agreement from a custom that supplies an unconsented pricing term in a purely legal obligation. If physician and patient actually contract, their circumstances and shared understanding may bear on what compensation was contemplated. Here the physicians were summoned by bystanders to treat an unconscious stranger. The patient could not have contemplated a fee based on his estate. Opinion pt. 3.

The legal fiction is limited to the remedy it justifies: fair payment for time, service, and skill. It does not authorize a court to hypothesize every advantageous term a physician might have negotiated. The services do not become more valuable merely because the patient is affluent. Consequently, the evidence of a custom of graduating fees according to means did not justify the instruction in this case. Id.; casebook 148–149.

The collateral-heir evidence was more plainly irrelevant. The absence of a spouse or children did not increase the value of the treatment or create a reason to transfer more of the estate to the physicians. It could instead invite the jury to regard the estate as money that no especially deserving relative needed. The court held that such information should not have gone to the jury. Opinion pt. 3.

### Error affected the judgment even though some testimony supported the amount
The range of testimony mattered to harmless error. The award did not exceed every expert estimate, but it was higher than other evidence would support. The improper factors could therefore have influenced the selection of an amount within the range. The court could not affirm simply by locating one witness whose estimate supported the verdict. Opinion pt. 3.

## Separate judicial position
Battle and Wood concurred in sustaining a right to compensation and in excluding the fact that collateral heirs would receive the estate. They did not agree that evidence of the estate's value, and an instruction permitting consideration of that value, were erroneous. The report records that disagreement but does not supply a lengthy separate explanation. Their position should neither be omitted nor expanded into an invented rationale. Opinion following pt. 3; casebook 149.

## Study analysis and boundaries
Cotnam distinguishes three propositions often conflated in restitution problems: no actual contract exists; nevertheless the law may impose an obligation; and the measure of that obligation remains constrained. The same absence of assent that makes a conventional contract impossible helps explain why the physicians cannot obtain a wealth-based contractual price.

The casebook's discussion of professional rescuers and gratuitous heroic bystanders is an editorial extension. The court decided a claim for emergency medical services, not a claim by every person who performs a rescue. Likewise, the opinion does not establish a nationwide contemporary medical-billing rule. Its useful doctrinal contribution is the separation of actual agreement, legally imposed compensation, successful outcome, and permissible evidence of reasonable value.
