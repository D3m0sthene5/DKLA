# Gillispie v. Goodyear Service Stores

**Civil Procedure · CP-ATLAS-GILLISPIE · 258 N.C. 487, 128 S.E.2d 762 (1963) · January 11, 1963**

Gillispie applies North Carolina’s then-existing code pleading requirement to a complaint that labels the defendants’ conduct assault, trespass, and malicious confinement without supplying the underlying circumstances. The Supreme Court affirmed four judgments sustaining demurrers for failure to state a cause of action. It rejected misjoinder as a ground and preserved the trial court’s permission to amend. The decision concerns pleading sufficiency, rather than a finding that the alleged misconduct never occurred. (258 N.C. at 488–491.)

Hilda Calahan Gillispie sued Goodyear Service Stores, its corporate parent, and four individuals. Her complaint identified the individuals as Alamance County residents and described the company’s Burlington store and its division. It alleged that on or about May 5 and 6, 1959, the defendants maliciously entered her residence, used threatening language and physical force, assaulted and frightened her, exposed her to public ridicule, and caused her seizure and confinement in jail. She sought $25,000 in actual damages and $10,000 in punitive damages. These were allegations, not established events. The reported complaint gives dates, but does not explain the incident or assign concrete acts and relationships to particular defendants. (Id. at 487–488.)

Four separate demurrers challenged both the sufficiency of the factual allegations and the joinder of parties and causes of action. Judge Mintz entered a separate judgment sustaining each demurrer and gave Gillispie thirty days to amend. The judgments did not state which objection supplied the ground. Gillispie appealed each judgment. (Id. at 488.)

Justice Bobbitt first addressed that ambiguity. A judgment sustaining a demurrer for misjoinder would require dismissal of the action. The permission to amend therefore suggested that the trial court had instead relied on failure to state a cause of action. Independently, the allegations did not establish misjoinder: even if they described several causes of action, they asserted participation by every defendant in all of them. The Supreme Court consequently did not affirm a dismissal for improper joinder. (Id. at 488–489.)

The controlling statute required a plain and concise statement of the facts constituting the cause of action. The court understood that requirement to demand the material, essential, ultimate facts showing the right to relief, rather than the pleader’s legal conclusions. A demurrer admits pleaded facts for this purpose, but not conclusions. The standard also had a forgiving aspect: the complaint must be fatally defective to be rejected, and it stands if any part supplies facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Id. at 489.)

That distinction governed the tort allegations. A complaint must supply facts showing the relevant legal duty, its violation, and resulting injury. The opinion’s debt and negligence examples explain the point: saying that a defendant owes money does not describe how the obligation arose; calling an injurious event negligence does not identify the conduct or causal connection. The court also emphasized the trial judge’s need to identify the asserted right or wrong, because that identification controls admissible evidence, the issues, and the charge. These examples support the pleading rule; they do not convert Gillispie’s claim into a negligence or debt action. (Id. at 489–490.)

Applying the rule, the court regarded the single sentence charging trespass, assault, and confinement as conclusions without an adequate factual basis. It criticized the failure to describe the occasion, who did what, and the relationships among the parties. Its statement that the complaint did not disclose when the events occurred must be read alongside the dates reproduced in the report; the brief does not erase those dates or invent an explanation for the court’s wording. The concrete deficiency the opinion identifies is the missing account of the conduct and circumstances underlying the legal labels. (Id. at 487, 490.)

The court concluded that even the most favorable reading did not furnish facts to which the law could be applied. It compared decisions treating an unexplained assertion of assault as a legal conclusion and stressed that recovery must rest on the case made by the pleadings. The four judgments sustaining the demurrers were **affirmed for failure to state a cause of action**. The court said it would be appropriate for Gillispie to amend under the leave already granted. It did not award damages, resolve the truth of her allegations, or require dismissal for misjoinder. (Id. at 490–491.)

Bobbitt wrote the sole reported opinion. The complete report supplies no judicial footnotes, separate writings, or numerical voting statement, so no unsupported vote count is assigned.
