# Trade-Winds Environmental Restoration, Inc. v. Stewart — 2008 partial-summary-judgment order
2008 WL 236891, No. 06-3299, ECF No. 107 (E.D. La.; signed Jan. 24, filed Jan. 28, 2008) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
Judge Ivan L. R. Lemelle granted Stewart Development, LLC, Stirling Properties, Inc., and Travelers Indemnity Company of Connecticut’s motion for partial summary judgment. The January 2008 order dismissed Trade-Winds’ breach-of-contract claim against Stewart Development and its claims against Stirling and Travelers. It left Trade-Winds’ claims against Stewart Development limited to unjust enrichment for the actual cost of materials, services, and labor. It did not award a particular restitution amount, resolve every remaining issue in the litigation, or direct repayment of the money already paid. The complete filed order is eighteen pages, contains no numbered judicial footnotes or separate writings, and bears Lemelle’s signature and January 24 date; the docket header records January 28 filing. References below use the order’s printed page numbers. (1, 14–18.)

## Facts and competing accounts
Heritage Plaza in Metairie sustained water damage from Hurricane Katrina, followed by a significant mold problem. Stewart Development owned the building, Stirling managed leasing and acted as its agent, and Travelers insured the property. In September 2005, Trade-Winds contacted Stirling employee David Kopp about remediation work. Stirling and Trade-Winds entered into a September 22 agreement priced on an open-ended time-and-materials basis using Trade-Winds’ rate sheet. Trade-Winds then held neither a Louisiana general-contractor license nor a mold-remediation license. It applied for a remediation license on September 28 and received it February 16, 2006. Trade-Winds alleged that it completed the work March 11, after the license issued; the court nevertheless examined the licensing status when the contract was made. (1–3, 7.)

The defendants alleged that Trade-Winds originally estimated the work at $750,000 but ultimately billed more than $9 million because of questionable billing practices. They further alleged that they repeatedly requested supporting documentation and refused further payment after paying $7.5 million without receiving that documentation. Those accusations are the parties’ allegations in the order, not findings that Trade-Winds committed billing fraud or that every claimed charge was unjustified. Trade-Winds filed suit June 27, 2006 for the asserted unpaid balance. The defendants filed the present motion December 11, 2007. (2–3.)

Stewart Development argued that the contract was invalid because Trade-Winds lacked the required licenses at formation. Stirling claimed protection as the disclosed agent of its principal; Trade-Winds responded that knowingly contracting with an unlicensed entity exceeded the agent’s authority. Travelers argued that Trade-Winds was not an insured under the property policy and had no claim against it. Trade-Winds asserted an insurance-payment scheme and suggested that an indemnification agreement might benefit it as a third party. The order examined each defendant separately rather than treating licensing invalidity as the only issue. (3–4, 14–18.)

## Licensing and the claimed emergency exception
The court applied the then-governing Louisiana statutes requiring an active contractor license and a separate mold-remediation license. The contractor definition included specified construction and repair services costing at least $50,000 for commercial property. Mold remediation included removal, cleaning, sanitizing, demolition, and treatment of mold or mold-contaminated matter. Trade-Winds did not dispute that its work fell within the statutory definitions or that it lacked either license when the September 22 contract was executed. Louisiana decisions treated contracting without the required license as null and void, while allowing recovery of actual materials, services, and labor costs through unjust enrichment. (5–7.)

Trade-Winds relied on an affidavit from Charles Marceaux, executive director of the Louisiana State Licensing Board for Contractors. It described earlier gubernatorial suspensions of debris-removal licensing laws after other hurricanes and the Board’s decision following Katrina to delay aggressive enforcement for ninety days, September 1 through December 1, 2005. The Board still publicized licensing requirements and retained authority to act when contractors appeared detrimental to the public. It had taken no such action against Trade-Winds. Governor Blanco, however, had issued no comparable order suspending the licensing statutes after Katrina. (7–8, 13.)

The court considered two earlier disputes involving Trade-Winds that had reached different preliminary conclusions. Biomedical Applications had denied summary judgment because questions remained about suspension and the legal effect of the Board’s informal policy. St. Tammany Park had concluded that relaxed enforcement did not eliminate the statutory prohibition. These are earlier decisions discussed as authorities, not additional dispositions made in this eighteen-page order. Here, the parties did not dispute the enforcement policy itself. Their disagreement concerned its legal effect, so the court could decide that issue on summary judgment despite the complexity of the question. (9–10.)

## Reasoning on contract enforcement and restitution
The central distinction was between an agency’s discretion about enforcement and the continuing force of the statute it administers. Administrative bodies may decide how vigorously to enforce a law, but exercising that discretion does not repeal or suspend the legal requirements for entering a valid contract. The Board’s restrained policy therefore left Trade-Winds prohibited from undertaking the unlicensed contracting work. The court held the September 22 agreement null and void. (10–11.)

Trade-Winds argued that denying enforcement would discourage specialists from helping Louisiana recover from disasters. The court answered that the legislature had expressly chosen competing protections: contractor licensing guarded against incompetent, inexperienced, unlawful, and fraudulent conduct, while mold-remediation qualifications protected people from injury or misleading practices. The judge would not replace those legislative choices with a different emergency policy. Louisiana law did permit the Governor to suspend certain regulatory statutes in response to emergencies, but the necessary executive order had not been issued. Marceaux’s statement that the Board retained selective enforcement also contradicted the claim that the licensing obligations had disappeared. (11–13.)

Trade-Winds additionally argued that Stirling’s awareness of its licensing status should estop the defendants from avoiding the agreement. The court rejected this route because private agreement could not set aside Louisiana’s licensing rules. Even knowing acceptance of unlicensed work would not validate the prohibited contract. That conclusion barred contractual enforcement without extinguishing every possibility of recovery: the court expressly preserved actual-cost unjust enrichment against Stewart Development. It neither calculated those costs nor decided that Trade-Winds’ entire invoice was recoverable under a different label. (13–14, 18.)

## Why the agent and insurer claims failed
Louisiana Civil Code article 3016 generally protects a mandatary who contracts in the principal’s name within its authority; article 3019 can impose personal liability when the mandatary exceeds its authority, subject to stated exceptions. Trade-Winds invoked article 3019 and Kopp’s deposition testimony that he knew in late September that a license had been sought but not received. The court reasoned that those provisions concerned execution of a contract, whereas no valid contract had been executed here. With the principal’s exposure limited to unjust enrichment, Trade-Winds offered no authority allowing it to recover the otherwise unavailable invoice amounts, fees, and costs from the agent. The claims against Stirling in its capacity as Stewart Development’s agent were dismissed. This is the court’s reasoning in this licensing dispute, not a general holding that agents can never be liable for independent misconduct. (14–16.)

Against Travelers, Trade-Winds alleged that the insurer and property owner were falsely asserting offsets to deprive it of payment. It knew that an indemnification agreement existed but did not know its terms. It also relied on the Louisiana Attorney General’s allegations in another suit accusing insurers of a general scheme to suppress property-damage payments. A stipulation pour autrui—a contractual benefit enforceable by a third party—cannot be presumed: the benefit must be manifestly clear. An indemnification agreement does not necessarily create one, and proof of its existence without its contents did not create a genuine factual dispute about a benefit to Trade-Winds. General allegations in a separate suit likewise did not supply specific evidence of the alleged scheme in this case. Travelers therefore obtained dismissal. The court did not find that a beneficiary clause existed and then deny its enforcement; the evidentiary showing was insufficient to establish the necessary issue for trial. (16–18.)

## Significance and assigned-source comparison
The decision illustrates how protective licensing statutes can prevent enforcement of a bargain even after extensive performance and payment, while preserving a narrower actual-cost restitution claim. It also shows why nonenforcement, legal suspension, and private waiver are different matters. The casebook’s licensing account appears at C&M physical pages 105–106, printed 762–763, followed by a restitution note. Its shortened account focuses on Stewart and the public-policy dispute. The actual order adds the license-application and issuance chronology, the agency-affidavit analysis, and the separate agent and insurer rulings. The assignment states a $9 million invoice; the court reports defendants’ allegation that the final amount exceeded $9 million. The brief preserves that distinction and does not supply a judicial answer to the editor’s question why so much had already been paid. (Assigned 762–763; order 2–3, 7–18.)
