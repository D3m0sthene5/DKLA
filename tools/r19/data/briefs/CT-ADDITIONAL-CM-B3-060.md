# Giunto v. Florida Coca-Cola Bottling Company
745 So. 2d 1020 (Fla. Dist. Ct. App. 1999) | Contracts

## Court, issue, and result

On October 13, 1999, Florida's Third District Court of Appeal held that the sweepstakes sponsors were entitled to judgment as a matter of law against Randy Giunto's claim to a $1 million hockey-shot prize. Judge Cope wrote, joined by Judge Jorgenson; Chief Judge Schwartz dissented. The majority enforced the contestant's agreement to accept the contest judges' decisions, finding neither pleading nor proof of fraud, gross mistake or lack of good faith. It reversed on the sponsors' cross-appeal and directed judgment for them, rendering Giunto's appeal from a new-trial order moot. The disposition was reversed and remanded with directions. (745 So. 2d at 1020–1023.)

## Two rule documents and the shot

Giunto entered a March 1994 sweepstakes sponsored by Coca-Cola, Blockbuster and the Florida Panthers. The application rules selected ten people for tickets to different home games and a chance to shoot a puck across the ice into and through a special small goal. They also provided that entrants would be bound by the official rules and judges' decisions, final in all sweepstakes matters. Giunto was chosen. Immediately before his March 18 attempt, he signed a Spectator Agreement to Participate specifying that the puck had to pass completely through the target. (Id. at 1021–1022.)

The target was plywood placed in front of the hockey goal, with a bottom slot somewhat larger than a puck. From 118 feet away, Giunto shot the puck into the corner of the slot; it rebounded side to side and stopped just slightly inside. The parties' concessions were important: the sponsors acknowledged that it crossed the opening's plane, while Giunto acknowledged that it did not pass completely through. The contest judge declared the attempt unsuccessful. (Id. at 1021 & n. 1.)

Giunto sued for breach, contending that the initial into-and-through language controlled, the later completely-through requirement was a nullity, and entry of any portion of the puck satisfied the original wording. A jury found for him, but the trial judge granted a new trial. Giunto appealed that order; the sponsors cross-appealed the denials of directed verdict and judgment notwithstanding the verdict. The majority decided the cross-appeal first. (Id. at 1021–1022.)

## Majority: finality and advance interpretation

Submission of the application bound Giunto to the final decisions clause. Drawing on other contest decisions, the majority recognized that agreed tournament or award decisions remained binding absent fraud, gross mistake or lack of good faith. Its second footnote likened the provision to arbitration's function of resolving disputes outside court. The judge had to apply the rules to a puck that entered but did not pass fully through; because no disqualifying misconduct or error had been pleaded or proved, his decision bound the contestant. (Id. at 1022 & n. 2.)

The signed spectator agreement also notified Giunto before the shot that the judges would read the original language to require complete passage through the template. The majority was inclined to regard that as the ordinary meaning anyway, but explained that any interpretation was better given before rather than after the attempt. Its third note recorded video of five other contests using the same target, with winning pucks passing entirely through. It therefore directed judgment for the sponsors rather than further jury consideration. The majority did not adjudicate the reasons for the new-trial order because its disposition made that appeal moot. (Id. at 1022 & n. 3.)

## Schwartz: conflicting terms and an interested judge

Schwartz treated the accepted unilateral offers as adhesion contracts, requiring ambiguities and conflicts to be resolved against their drafters. Different expressions ordinarily carried different meanings. In his view, the less demanding original into-and-through language controlled over the later completely-through language, and the evidence supported the jury's conclusion that Giunto satisfied it. These were the dissent's contractual interpretations, not the majority's governing conclusions. (Id. at 1023 & n. 4.)

He extended that reasoning to the finality clause: because the later spectator agreement lacked such a provision, he would disregard the earlier one. Independently, he considered it unenforceable because the judge was Declan Bolger, the Panthers' director of promotions. A sponsor could not act as judge of its own responsibility; Schwartz regarded that principle as applying even to the majority's arbitration analogy. Bolger had testified, and Schwartz thought the jury could find his account inconsistent with the video and physical facts, satisfying even the majority's fraud, gross-mistake or bad-faith exception. The majority's contrary statement about pleading and proof must remain distinct from this dissenting assessment of the record. (Id. at 1023.)

Schwartz also would reverse the new-trial order on Giunto's appeal. The first stated ground, improper insertion of unpleaded fraud or misconduct, did not show that an unpleaded issue actually produced the plaintiff's verdict. The second, an alleged request to punish the wealthy and influential sponsors in closing argument, lacked record justification. He would affirm the denial of judgment for the sponsors and remand for entry of judgment on the jury verdict for Giunto. That was the losing proposed disposition; the court instead required judgment for the sponsors. (Id.)
