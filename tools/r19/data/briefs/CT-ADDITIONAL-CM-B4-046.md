# Buccini v. Paterno Construction Co.
253 N.Y. 256, 170 N.E. 910 (1930) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
The New York Court of Appeals decided the case on March 18, 1930, after submission on February 10. Chief Judge Cardozo wrote for a unanimous court; Judges Pound, Crane, Lehman, Kellogg, O’Brien, and Hubbs concurred. The executrix sought arbitration of compensation for work performed before the contractor’s death. Special Term granted her petition, and the Appellate Division denied it. The Court of Appeals reversed the Appellate Division and affirmed Special Term, with costs in both appellate courts. Its order compelled arbitration; it did not calculate or enter a monetary restitution award. (253 N.Y. at 256, 258, 260.)

## Contract, death, and dispute
Alberto Buccini contracted to decorate the ballroom, banquet hall, and swimming pool at Paterno’s Castle, a residence on Riverside Drive in New York City. The work required artistic skill. The contract expressly required Buccini to do the decorative figured work personally while allowing mechanics to perform plain work. It also required arbitration, at either party’s election, of all questions arising under the contract and in performance of the work. (Id. at 257.)

Buccini died while work was in progress. His death excused the duty to continue this personal performance, but left the owner liable for benefits already received. The parties disputed their value, and executrix Flora Buccini demanded arbitration. The company argued that the claim was not within the contractual arbitration provision, that a progress-payment clause left valuation to the owner, and that an executrix was not one of the contracting parties entitled to elect arbitration. (Id. at 257–259.)

## Excused performance did not erase the contractual framework
The law treats sickness or death as an implied excuse in a personal-service undertaking. But discharge of the future performance duty did not nullify the entire agreement or liberate a compensation claim from its conditions. The controversy over work already done arose under that contract and its performance, whether compensation upon excused interruption was expressly fixed by the parties or supplied by quasi-contract and equitable principles. The broad arbitration clause therefore reached it. Cardozo compared it with completed work under an agreement silent about price, where reasonable value could still be a dispute originating in the contract. (Id. at 258.)

The opinion explained why the agreement remained important to the extent of recovery. An estate could not obtain compensation for a willful, unexcused substitution merely because the substitute work was better than the plans required. Nor could the proportionately allocated value permit recovery beyond the contract price or its rate for the part performed. The relevant benefit was advancement of the owner’s contractual purposes, rather than the work’s value viewed independently of the bargain. These principles framed the claim that remained arbitrable; the court did not find a specific value, fraction completed, or balance due. (Id. at 258–259.)

## The owner’s additional objections
The contract required payments during progress at seventy-five percent of the installed work’s value and permitted the owner to determine the amount when such payments were requested. That provision addressed continuing performance and interim payment applications. It did not control compensation after death or disability had ended further performance without either party’s fault. It consequently did not displace arbitration of the interrupted contract’s benefits. (Id. at 259.)

The executrix also could exercise the deceased’s arbitration choice. In the absence of contrary express words, contracting parties were presumed to bind their personal representatives as well as themselves. Arbitration was the agreed means of resolving the differences, and succession to the underlying dispute included succession to that means. The personal character of the artistic work excused its future production; it did not make the dispute-resolution mechanism inaccessible to the estate. (Id. at 259–260.)
