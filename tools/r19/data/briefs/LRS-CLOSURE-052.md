# Campaign Legal Center and Democracy 21 v. Federal Election Commission
952 F.3d 352 (D.C. Cir. 2020) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Complaints, agency explanations, and proceedings

Between August 2011 and April 2015, the two campaign-finance organizations filed five FEC complaints alleging that individuals used closely held corporations and LLCs as straw donors to make Super PAC contributions without accurate disclosure of the true contributors. Four complaints also alleged failures to register and report as political committees. FECA’s straw-donor provision prohibits making contributions in another’s name and knowing participation in that arrangement; the political-committee provisions impose separate registration and reporting obligations. These were alleged violations, not judicial findings that the entities illegally concealed donors. (Official Court opinion 2–4 & n. 1.)

The General Counsel recommended reason-to-believe findings on four straw-donor complaints but no action on their political-committee allegations; she recommended no reason-to-believe finding on the fifth complaint. In February 2016 the six commissioners divided 3–3 on investigation, then unanimously dismissed all five. The three commissioners who had opposed investigation supplied the controlling statement of reasons. They invoked prosecutorial discretion and stressed uncertain guidance after Citizens United and SpeechNow changed the legal landscape for corporate political spending. Past agency decisions treated money in corporate accounts as corporate funds even when an owner could legally convert it to personal funds, and the LLC attribution regulations expressly addressed some but not all corporate forms. The commissioners thought enforcement without adequate notice would be unfair. (4–5, 8–10.)

The District Court dismissed challenges involving two administrative matters for lack of standing; as to the remaining three, it granted summary judgment to the FEC because the dismissals were not contrary to law. Only that summary judgment was appealed here. On March 13, 2020, Tatel, Garland, and Edwards unanimously **affirmed** in a per curiam opinion. Edwards concurred separately to contest the FEC’s claim of nonreviewability. The Court reviewed summary judgment de novo, but evaluated the underlying agency decision deferentially. (1–2, 5, 8, 11.)

## Informational standing despite inability to vote

The FEC argued that nonprofit organizations unable to vote, lacking voting members, and not conducting partisan activity had no personal injury from missing campaign information. The Court rejected that restriction. The organizations’ educational, litigation, regulatory, and advocacy activities could benefit from contributor and political-committee disclosures. On their reading of FECA, they were entitled to information that had not been supplied, and the record gave no reason to doubt its usefulness to their work. The absence of a personal ballot or partisan affiliation did not negate that informational injury. The deprivation was traceable to the administrative dismissals and likely redressable by favorable judicial action. (6–7.)

This standing holding did not establish that the plaintiffs were entitled to prevail on the administrative complaints. Standing and reasonableness of the FEC’s dismissal remained separate inquiries. Nor did it reverse the unappealed standing dismissals concerning the other two matters.

## Reviewability avoided; agency grounds sustained

The FEC offered two grounds for affirmance: circuit precedent treating exercises of prosecutorial discretion as unreviewable, and the reasonableness of these particular dismissals. The Court characterized the first issue as complicated and nonjurisdictional and proceeded directly to the latter. It therefore did not adopt the agency’s broad immunity theory or Edwards’s opposing construction as a majority holding. Under FECA’s contrary-to-law test, a dismissal fails if based on an impermissible statutory interpretation, or if arbitrary, capricious, or abusive even under a permissible interpretation. The question here was whether the agency’s reasons were sufficiently reasonable to accept. (7–8.)

The controlling commissioners did not deny that § 30122 applied to closely held corporations and corporate LLCs; the Court agreed that it did. Their reason for declining investigation was inadequate notice in the altered legal environment. The Court accepted the possible confusion from earlier decisions and regulations, deferred to the agency’s understanding of its precedents, and emphasized the undisputed absence of directly applicable post-Citizens United precedent. It consequently upheld dismissal of the straw-donor allegations, rather than declaring such corporations exempt from the statute. (8–10.)

The commissioners had suggested an intent-to-evade standard for future straw-donor matters. The Court declined to judge that prospective standard because a statement supported by only three commissioners was not binding precedent for future cases; official Commission action required four affirmative votes. This reservation matters: affirming these dismissals did not validate every future enforcement policy proposed in their explanation. (10 n. 2.)

For the political-committee allegations, the commissioners reasonably relied on the General Counsel’s conclusion that the alleged entities were conduits rather than political committees. The plaintiffs also accepted the proposition that an entity could be one or the other but not both in this setting. Given that analysis and the stated reasons for not investigating under the straw-donor provision, the Court upheld the separate political-committee dismissals as well. (10–11.)

## Edwards’s reviewability concurrence

Edwards joined the reasons for affirmance but rejected the FEC’s contention that a label of prosecutorial discretion eliminated judicial review. He viewed FECA’s express aggrieved-party review provision and Akins as displacing Chaney’s usual nonenforcement presumption in this setting. Review could be deferential and sustain an enforcement refusal; that did not mean there was no review. He thought the later CREW nonreviewability rationale inconsistent with Supreme Court authority and earlier circuit decisions, which should control under the circuit’s rule for conflicting panel decisions. (Edwards concurrence 1–6, 9–10; physical PDF 12–17, 20–21.)

He also documented the agency’s change in litigation position. In CREW the FEC had argued that prosecutorial discretion remained subject to judicial checking for legal errors or unreasonable grounds, with a possible private action if it failed to conform to a contrary-to-law declaration. In this case it used CREW to seek categorical immunity without explaining that reversal. Edwards found the new position inconsistent with FECA and prior circuit law, while still agreeing that these dismissals survived merits review. His rejection of immunity is a separate concurrence; the per curiam opinion intentionally did not resolve that dispute. (Concurrence 6–10; physical PDF 17–21.)
