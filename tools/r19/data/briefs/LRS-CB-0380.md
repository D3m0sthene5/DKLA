Parties
The United States and the Interstate Commerce Commission defended the Commission's nationwide incentive
charges for a railroad's use of freight cars owned by others. Florida East Coast Railway and Seaboard Coast
Line Railroad were the appellee railroads challenging that order. Their particular operational and financial
objections supplied the asserted reasons for requiring an oral evidentiary hearing. This was judicial review of
an agency rulemaking, rather than an action to recover a particular railroad's unpaid charges. 410 U.S. at
225–229, 233–234.
Material Facts
A chronic national freight-car shortage prompted Congress in 1966 to enlarge the Commission's power under §
1(14)(a) of the Interstate Commerce Act. The amendment permitted an incentive component in compensation
paid for use of another owner's freight cars. Congress sought efficient use and return of cars and investment in
an adequate car supply; it directed the Commission to consider national ownership levels and other factors
bearing on adequacy. The existing authority permitted the Commission to act “after hearing.” The amendment
did not add a requirement that the rule be made on a hearing record. 410 U.S. at 225–230, 234–235.
An investigation begun in 1966 did not produce sufficient information for an incentive system. In December
1967 the Commission initiated the proceeding at issue and required Class I and Class II line-haul railroads to
collect detailed demand-and-supply information at sample stations during twelve four-week periods beginning
January 29, 1968. A staff conference in April 1968 addressed reporting methods and proposed adjustments.
Twenty railroads, including Seaboard, attended. The conference left participants expecting later hearings. At a
congressional hearing in May 1969, members criticized the Commission's slow implementation of its enlarged
authority. These events help explain the expectation of oral proceedings, but the Court did not treat that
expectation as itself establishing a statutory entitlement. Id. at 230–233, 236–237 n.6.
In December 1969 the Commission published an interim report and a proposed incentive-rate order for
standard boxcars. It allowed sixty days for verified factual statements, briefs, objections, and other pertinent
submissions. A request for an oral hearing had to identify the need and the evidence to be presented. Both
appellees submitted objections and requested oral hearings. In April 1970 the Commission modified parts of its
proposal, rejected the hearing requests, and issued its final order. Id. at 232–234.
Florida East Coast challenged the statistical basis for finding a shortage and asserted that terminating railroads
had surplus standard boxcars warranting different treatment. Seaboard objected to the shift in emphasis from
specialized cars to standard boxcars and claimed serious financial injury. Douglas's dissent specifically
recounted Seaboard's asserted $1.8 million loss. These were the railroads' contentions, not judicial findings that
the statistical study was invalid or that the alleged loss was proved. The incentive system nevertheless applied
prospectively across the regulated railroads rather than resolving a charge against a particular carrier on its
individual facts. Id. at 245–246, 249–253 (Douglas, J., dissenting).

Procedural History
The railroads sought review before a three-judge District Court in the Middle District of Florida. That court
concluded that § 1(14)(a) required compliance with the APA's formal-proceeding provisions and that receiving
only written submissions prejudiced the railroads within § 556(d). It set the order aside on that procedural
ground, leaving other objections to the order unresolved. 410 U.S. at 225–229, 233–234.
The Government appealed. After United States v. Allegheny-Ludlum Steel Corp. had interpreted the same
organic statute's hearing language, the Supreme Court considered whether APA §§ 556 and 557 applied and
whether the Interstate Commerce Act independently demanded oral procedures. The Court rejected the ground
on which the District Court had invalidated the order and remanded for consideration of the objections that
court had not decided. Id. at 234–238, 246.
Issue
First, did the statutory authority to act “after hearing,” combined with the 1966 directive to consider specified
freight-car-supply factors, require this rulemaking to be conducted on the record and therefore trigger APA §§
556 and 557? Second, even if those formal APA provisions did not apply, did the Interstate Commerce Act's
hearing requirement itself require oral testimony, cross-examination, or oral argument? Third, did the railroads'
individualized objections and alleged losses change the general, prospective rulemaking into a proceeding
requiring those procedures? The railroads asserted no constitutional deprivation claim; constitutional hearing
decisions entered the analysis as useful comparisons. 410 U.S. at 234–246.
Rule and Holding
APA § 553(c) substitutes the formal procedures of §§ 556 and 557 for ordinary notice-and-comment
procedures when a statute requires rules to be made on the record after an opportunity for an agency hearing.
Equivalent statutory language can suffice: Congress need not use a talismanic formula. But the phrase “after
hearing” in this statute was not equivalent, and Congress's instruction to consider specified substantive factors
did not prescribe an oral or trial-type method of gathering information. Accordingly, §§ 556 and 557 did not
govern this rulemaking. 410 U.S. at 234–238.
The organic statute still had to be satisfied independently. Its hearing requirement, interpreted in this
rulemaking context, did not require oral evidence, cross-examination, or oral argument. Publication of the
proposed order, a sixty-day opportunity to submit evidence and objections in writing, and the Commission's
consideration of those submissions satisfied the requirement. A general prospective incentive rule did not
become an individualized adjudication simply because it affected some carriers more adversely than others. Id.
at 238–246.
The holding concerns the procedural grounds actually decided. It neither holds that every statutory reference to
a hearing permits written submissions alone nor resolves the substantive validity of every feature of the
incentive system. Id. at 239–240, 245–246.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the District Court's judgment and remanded for consideration of the railroads'
other contentions that the District Court had not reached. It did not order a new oral agency hearing and did not
finally adjudicate every challenge to the Commission's rates. Justice Douglas, joined by Justice Stewart, would
have affirmed the District Court. Justice Powell took no part. 410 U.S. at 246, 256.

Reasoning
1. Identify the trigger before applying formal-hearing safeguards. The District Court had focused on whether
written evidence prejudiced the railroads under § 556(d). The logically prior question was whether the
proceeding came within §§ 556 and 557 at all. Allegheny-Ludlum had already held that the same statute's
authority to act “after hearing” did not trigger those provisions. The 1966 amendment changed the
considerations governing incentive compensation, without adding a new hearing requirement. The Court
therefore rejected the premise for the District Court's prejudice inquiry. 410 U.S. at 234–238.
2. A direction about what to consider is different from a direction about how to receive it. The railroads
emphasized Congress's instruction that the Commission consider national ownership and supply factors before
fixing compensation. The Court treated those factors as necessary components of the agency's substantive
judgment. An agency could consider them using written evidence and argument. Reading a trial-type hearing
requirement into that substantive instruction would add a procedural command Congress had not supplied. Id.
at 234–235.
3. The APA trigger has substantive force without demanding exact words. The Court acknowledged that
statutory language equivalent to an on-the-record hearing requirement could activate formal rulemaking. It
nevertheless enforced the distinction Congress drew between ordinary rulemaking and the narrower class
requiring formal procedures. Existing statutes expressly requiring record hearings demonstrated that the
provision had real work to do. The opinion accordingly rejects both an automatic formal-hearing rule for every
“hearing” reference and an inflexible rule requiring Congress to recite one exact phrase. Id. at 237–238.
4. An organic statute can require more than the APA's default. Finding §§ 556 and 557 inapplicable did not end
the inquiry. The railroads separately invoked § 1(14)(a), and the Court addressed the meaning of its own
hearing requirement. In interpreting a substantive grant enlarged after the APA, the Court used the APA's
differentiated treatment of hearings as guidance. Section 553 expressly contemplates written participation with
or without oral presentation. Even § 556(d), when applicable, permits some written evidentiary procedures
where the statutory conditions are satisfied. Thus the word “hearing” cannot invariably mean oral testimony
and cross-examination. Id. at 238–242.
5. The concrete opportunity to participate mattered. The Commission disclosed a proposed order and its factual
basis, invited verified evidence and legal submissions for sixty days, and considered the appellees' positions in
the final order. It also left the system open to later modification in light of experience. The Court contrasted
these procedures with decisions involving an undisclosed basis for decision or inadequate knowledge of the
proposal to be met. Its holding rests on the procedures actually furnished; it does not authorize an agency to
substitute a nominal invitation for meaningful notice and consideration. Id. at 241–244.
6. The character of the decision explained why the cited hearing cases did not control. Londoner involved a
small group exceptionally affected upon individualized grounds, whereas Bi-Metallic involved a general
valuation adjustment. Other decisions likewise distinguished policy-type standards from determinations of
particular disputed facts. Here the Commission established a national, prospective compensation rule. No
railroad was singled out on its peculiar circumstances. Florida East Coast's complaint that terminating carriers
should be treated separately did not change the character of the rule the Commission actually adopted. Nor did
reliance on factual inferences automatically make a general policy determination adjudicatory. Id. at 244–246.
7. Expectations and agency characterizations did not determine the APA question. Commission personnel had
earlier expected or described further hearings. The Court explained that an agency may choose procedures
beyond the statutory minimum, and its earlier understanding did not control the Court's interpretation of the
APA. That point should not be enlarged into a holding about every enforceable agency procedural promise: the
question decided was whether these statutes required the additional hearing procedures. Id. at 236–237 n.6.

8. The remedy followed the limited scope of the decision. Once the Court rejected the procedural premise for
invalidation, it returned the case to the District Court to consider the other challenges. That disposition
preserves the distinction between rejecting one legal ground for setting an order aside and declaring the entire
order substantively lawful. Id. at 246.
Separate Opinions
Justice Douglas, joined by Justice Stewart, dissented. He regarded the incentive charges as an exercise of broad
agency power imposing concrete and potentially severe financial liabilities. In his view, the combination of that
impact, the hearing language, and the contested factual predicates required the fuller safeguards of APA §§
553, 556, and 557. He would have affirmed the District Court's decision setting the order aside. 410 U.S. at
246–256 (Douglas, J., dissenting).
Douglas emphasized the specificity of these railroads' evidentiary objections. Florida East Coast questioned the
study's statistical validity and the treatment of terminating carriers; Seaboard challenged the shift in the type of
cars for which incentives would be paid. These were not merely unexplained requests for an oral appearance.
For the dissent, the dispute over the factual basis for a shortage, coupled with the financial burden, prevented
reliance on written procedures as a harmless substitute for a full hearing. Id. at 249–253.
The dissent described ratemaking as capable of combining legislative and adjudicative characteristics. Its
prospective scope did not eliminate the need to establish the statutory facts supporting coercive monetary
obligations. Douglas relied on the history of hearing requirements, earlier administrative-hearing decisions,
statements accompanying the 1966 legislation, and the Commission's own initial understanding. Broad
discretion and deferential judicial review made a fair agency hearing especially important, rather than less
important. Id. at 253–256.
This disagreement concerned the procedures the governing statutes required and the significance of the rule's
factual and financial characteristics. The dissent's asserted need for cross-examination and its treatment of the
disputed statistics are not holdings of the Court. No separate concurrence is reported; Powell did not
participate. Id. at 246–256.

and reasoning, rather than certifying the present operation of the historical Interstate Commerce Act provision.
