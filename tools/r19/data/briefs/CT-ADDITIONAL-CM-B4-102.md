# Baxter Healthcare Corporation v. O.R. Concepts, Inc.
69 F.3d 785 (7th Cir. 1995) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

The Seventh Circuit decided the case on November 2, 1995, with rehearing and rehearing en banc denied on November 28. Judge Cummings wrote for the panel with Judges Manion and Kanne; there was no separate writing. The court affirmed the Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal of Baxter’s declaratory-action claims for breach of contract, violation of UCC § 2-210, and breach of the implied covenant of good faith, assessing costs against Baxter. It reviewed dismissal de novo, accepting pleaded facts and reasonable inferences favorably to Baxter and examining the sixteen-page agreement attached to its pleading. The opinion’s recited no-set-of-facts pleading formulation is historical, not a certification of the current federal pleading standard. (785–787, 792.)

## Agreement, stock acquisition, and termination

In January 1992 Baxter agreed to purchase at least $3 million of Thermadrape, a heat-retention surgical drape used to prevent hypothermia, from O.R. over twenty-seven months. Baxter distributed health-care products, while O.R. manufactured and sold them. Baxter knew that O.R. already sold Thermadrape through other independent distributors. The agreement did not grant exclusive rights to existing Thermadrape products or bar O.R. from competing with Baxter; a separate provision gave Baxter a right of first refusal for new Thermadrape products. (786–787, 789–790.)

In November 1992 O.R.’s president and majority shareholder, Jim Elder, told Baxter that he and other shareholders were selling ninety-five percent of O.R.’s stock to Baxter’s competitor, Vital Signs. Elder retained control until resigning as president in June 1993. He later advised that Vital Signs’ 120-person sales force would sell Thermadrape alongside Baxter and that O.R. would move its headquarters to Minnesota. O.R. nevertheless remained a distinct, independent, functioning corporation and continued making and selling its products. (787.)

Baxter’s June 18, 1993 letter claimed that the stock sale constituted a prohibited assignment. Its May 10, 1994 letter terminated the agreement, refusing to purchase the remaining $1 million of product, and it filed the declaratory suit that day. Baxter relied on the requirement that O.R. obtain its prior written consent before transferring or assigning interests in the agreement, alleged exclusivity and noncompetition obligations, UCC restrictions on assignment and delegation, and good faith. The dismissal being reviewed did not award O.R. that unpaid $1 million or resolve a separately pleaded seller counterclaim. (787–788, 792.)

## Stock ownership versus assignment of contractual interests

Under the agreement’s Illinois choice of law, the anti-assignment clause did not prohibit shareholders from selling their stock. O.R.’s continued separate identity meant that its contractual interests and obligations remained its own; changing its owners did not transfer those interests to Vital Signs. Baxter’s own choice to sue O.R. rather than the shareholders reinforced the distinction between the contracting corporation and its owners. (787–788.)

Baxter’s reliance on Rural Electric was misplaced. That case concerned a corporate merger and a specific statute governing transfer of property and interests; it did not establish Baxter’s proposed general distinction between transfers to corporate insiders and outsiders. Sally Beauty was also different: it involved an exclusive distributorship and a merger through which the contracting distributor lost its independent identity. Here there was a stock sale, O.R. continued to exist and perform, and existing-product distribution was nonexclusive. The court accordingly rejected breach of the consent clause without holding that every merger or every change-of-control provision must receive the same treatment. (788–789 & nn. 1–3.)

## Why exclusivity could not be supplied through interpretation

Baxter conceded the absence of an express existing-product exclusivity or noncompetition term but asserted intrinsic and extrinsic ambiguity. The court distinguished unclear written language from apparently clear language whose real-world context makes its meaning uncertain. Although the court’s account of Illinois law permitted contextual evidence, such evidence could not manufacture a conflict wholly apart from the contract: there had to be relevant ambiguous language or an omission genuinely calling for an implied term. (789.)

The specific new-product right did not make existing-product rights ambiguous. It showed that the parties knew how to distinguish the two categories and had not created comparable exclusivity for existing products. Favorable pricing, sales literature, instruction, samples, field technicians, and monthly reports likewise did not require an exclusive relationship. The contract worked as written: Baxter would buy the stated minimum, and O.R. would supply it. Sophisticated parties with counsel could have written broader exclusivity; implying it would substantially enlarge Baxter’s bargain rather than make performance possible. Note 4 distinguishes Wood v. Duff-Gordon, where structural features of an exclusive license supported an implied best-efforts obligation. (790 & n. 4.)

Baxter also alleged negotiations about an eventual exclusive relationship. The agreement declared itself the entire agreement and excluded unincorporated prior oral or written representations. The court declined to add the alleged negotiation term and rejected both ambiguity theories. This was not a universal rejection of all extrinsic evidence; it was the application of the agreement’s structure and integration provision to the proposed additional obligation. (790–791.)

## UCC delegation and good faith

UCC § 2-210 restricted delegation where the obligee substantially depended on the original promisor’s performance or control, and restricted assignments that materially altered duties, burdens, risks, or expected return performance. Baxter argued that acquisition by a competitor increased its risks. But the predicate assignment or delegation was absent: the same corporation remained its contracting supplier on the agreed terms. Sally Beauty’s rule against delegating an exclusive distributorship to a competitor without consent did not extend to this nonexclusive agreement and stock transaction. (791.)

The covenant of good faith guided interpretation of agreed terms under Illinois law rather than independently creating unbargained duties. Other distributors’ continued participation was known when the parties contracted, not an unforeseen gap requiring a noncompetition term. Where the parties wanted exclusive treatment for new products, they supplied it. Baxter’s request would therefore add obligations rather than construe existing ones. The court affirmed dismissal of that claim as well. (792.)

## Significance and reading scope

The assigned contrast with Sally Beauty separates three questions: whether corporate events actually transfer the contract or performance duties, whether the relationship is exclusive, and whether interpretation supplies a missing obligation. It does not support treating every acquisition as either automatically permissible or automatically prohibited. The actual holding concerns this stock sale, continuing corporate identity, and the particular nonexclusive agreement. All three pleaded theories failed, and dismissal was affirmed with costs against Baxter. (788–792.)
