# Hill’s, Inc. v. William B. Kessler, Inc.
41 Wash. 2d 42, 246 P.2d 1099 (1952), August 7, 1952 | Contracts

## Decision and panel
The Washington Supreme Court affirmed judgment for a retailer whose manufacturer cancelled an order for thirty-four men’s suits. The majority concluded that correspondence evidenced acceptance and recognized an existing contract, that the manufacturer could not defeat liability by disputing its office clerk’s authority on this record, and that the retailer’s lost resale profits were recoverable. Justice Mallery wrote, joined by Chief Justice Schwellenbach and Justices Grady and Weaver. Justice Donworth concurred in the result solely because the later cancellation letter recognized a contract; he did not join the majority’s broader reasons. Thus five judges agreed on affirmance, with four joining the opinion and one giving a narrower rationale. (42–45.)

## Order, response, and cancellation
On May 16, 1950, Hill’s ordered thirty-four suits through Kessler’s salesman, using Kessler’s printed form. The form stated that the order would not bind Kessler until an authorized officer accepted it at the corporation’s office in Hammonton, New Jersey. On May 23, Kessler sent a form letter assuring Hill’s of its best attention to the order. The issue was whether that assurance merely acknowledged receipt and future consideration, or communicated acceptance. (43.)

The trial court found that, on or about July 18, Kessler intentionally cancelled at the instigation of a large downtown Seattle retailer selling its clothing. By then the period for placing fall orders had passed, leaving Hill’s unable to obtain comparable suits elsewhere for its fall trade. Kessler’s July 18 letter identified the May 16 order placed with salesman Jacobus, said shipment was impossible because of previous commitments to other stores in town, characterized its action as cancellation, and apologized for inconvenience. Those were findings and documentary evidence in an actual damages action, rather than facts simply assumed on a dismissal motion. Hill’s sought lost profits calculated from a 66⅔-percent markup, totaling $815.83. It prevailed, and Kessler appealed. The fraction is visible in the original report; the damaged native extraction should not be read as a plain 66-percent figure. (43–44.)

## Acceptance evidenced by correspondence
Kessler argued that the May 23 assurance was not an acceptance. The majority found Bauman v. McManus exactly in point. Bauman reasoned that a promise of prompt, careful attention naturally concerns filling the order, rather than the supplier’s private investigation of whether to accept it; otherwise a supplier could deliberately use equivocal reassuring language and later exploit it. The Washington court accepted that reasoning but did not rely exclusively on the initial letter. (43–44.)

The subsequent letter was independent evidence of an intention to accept. Its statement that Kessler needed to cancel an identified order because of other commitments recognized the existence of the contract it purported to terminate. The court therefore considered the whole correspondence, rather than treating “best attention” as an invariably dispositive formula in every business setting. It did not describe the July letter as a fresh offer needing acceptance by Hill’s. (44.)

The assigned note asks whether these words amounted to acceptance when compared with Wucherpfennig’s failure to communicate unconditional assent. Hill’s resolves its own record using the later recognition as well as the initial assurance. The note’s comparison does not make the other decision’s rule an additional holding of Hill’s. (Assigned PDF 261, printed 216; actual 43–44.)

## Authority of the office signer
Kessler next asserted that its correspondence had not been signed by an authorized officer. Its treasurer testified that Glenda Fitting was only a clerk and that the president, secretary, and treasurer alone could accept orders. The trial court had examined substantial correspondence signed by Fitting, and the Supreme Court considered it justified in disbelieving that restrictive testimony. The evidence did not establish that she had inserted herself into the corporation’s affairs without its knowledge or over its objection. (44.)

The majority then applied apparent-authority principles: when a principal voluntarily puts an agent in a position that reasonably leads a person familiar with the business to assume authority for a particular act, and that person deals on the assumption, the principal cannot defeat the transaction by proving a narrower undisclosed actual authority. The printed officer-acceptance condition did not prevail over the corporation’s correspondence and the supported authority determination. The holding is grounded in the principal’s conduct and this evidentiary record, not a rule that every employee can accept every corporate order. (44–45.)

## Damages, mitigation, and narrower concurrence
Kessler challenged lost profits and argued that Hill’s failed to obtain replacement suits. Hill’s showed that travelling east to procure an equivalent order would cost more than the lost profits. The court considered that expenditure unjustified as mitigation. Kessler understood that the suits were ordered for fall resale at a 66⅔-percent markup; their quality was unavailable in the West, and the cancellation occurred after the ordering season. Under those circumstances lost profits were an appropriate measure. The court did not demand commercially unreasonable replacement efforts or deny damages merely because the retailer sought the margin of the cancelled resale business. (45.)

Donworth concurred only because the July 18 letter acknowledged a contract whose terms appeared in the May 16 order. His separate paragraph supplies a sufficient narrower ground for affirmance without endorsing all of the majority’s acceptance and authority analysis. The formal judgment was affirmed, not remanded for a new calculation. (45.)
