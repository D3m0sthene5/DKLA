# Ziglar v. Abbasi

**582 U.S. 120 (2017); Supreme Court of the United States; June 19, 2017.** Assigned: CivPro PDF 338, printed 641–642, Note 4. The assignment develops the information problem in plausibility pleading through *Turkmen* and the later Supreme Court decision. This brief addresses the Supreme Court stage separately. Pinpoints below refer to the preserved official slip opinion, with each separate writing identified, rather than converting its page numbers into reporter pages.

## Facts and procedural path

After the September 11 attacks, the federal government arrested hundreds of people present in violation of immigration laws and detained some in restrictive conditions until investigators cleared them of terrorism connections. The six respondents were men of Arab or South Asian descent; five were Muslim. They were detained in the Administrative Maximum Special Housing Unit of the Metropolitan Detention Center in Brooklyn and removed from the United States after release. The judicial opinion describes detention lasting three to eight months; its syllabus instead says three to six months. This account uses the judicial passage. These were allegations considered on motions to dismiss, rather than facts established after trial. (Kennedy 1–5.)

The alleged restrictions included confinement in tiny cells for more than twenty-three hours daily, continuously illuminated cells, denial of basic hygiene products, shackling and four-guard escorts when detainees left their cells, restricted outside communication, and frequent strip searches. Separately from formal detention policy, the complaint described guards slamming detainees against walls, twisting their limbs, breaking bones, threatening violence, and directing sexual and religious insults at them. That distinction between official conditions and unofficial guard abuse became important to the Court's disposition. (Kennedy 3–4.)

The respondents sought compensatory and punitive damages and other litigation relief. They sued former Attorney General John Ashcroft, former FBI Director Robert Mueller, former INS Commissioner James Ziglar, Warden Dennis Hasty, and Associate Warden James Sherman. Their constitutional claims alleged punitive confinement and discrimination under the Fifth Amendment, unjustified strip searches under the Fourth and Fifth Amendments, and the wardens' knowing allowance of guard abuse under the Fifth Amendment. They also alleged a discriminatory civil conspiracy under 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3). The claims concerned the conditions and motivations of confinement, while acknowledging that immigration violations could support some detention. (Kennedy 4–6.)

Following *Iqbal*, the fourth amended complaint incorporated material from Justice Department Inspector General investigations. The district court dismissed the claims against the executive officials but permitted claims against the wardens. The Second Circuit largely sustained the claims against the wardens, except for the abuse claim against Sherman, and reinstated claims against the executive officials. Judge Raggi dissented except concerning the Hasty abuse claim. The Supreme Court reviewed that appellate judgment. The assigned Note 4 emphasizes that government investigations provided otherwise difficult-to-obtain facts supporting the detainees' allegations. (Kennedy 6; assigned 641–642.)

## Questions and competing positions

The central question was whether courts should recognize a damages action directly under the Constitution for these claims. A complaint can plausibly allege unconstitutional conduct yet fail because no judicially implied damages remedy is available. The Court therefore separated pleading sufficiency, the existence of a *Bivens* remedy, and immunity from liability under the separate conspiracy statute.

The detainees treated their claims as applications of established constitutional remedies: unreasonable searches, discrimination, and deliberate indifference to abuse in federal custody. The Second Circuit's approach asked whether both the constitutional right and the mechanism of injury appeared in prior *Bivens* litigation. The officials contended that these claims extended the remedy into a materially different setting, implicating high-level policy, national security, and legislative responsibility. Hasty also challenged the plausibility of the abuse allegations. All petitioners claimed qualified immunity from the § 1985(3) conspiracy claim. (Kennedy 14–17, 23–24, 26–29.)

## Rules and the detention-policy holding

Congress expressly supplied a remedy for constitutional violations by state officials through § 1983 but did not create an equivalent general federal-officer remedy. The Court had recognized an implied damages action in *Bivens* for a Fourth Amendment violation, in *Davis v. Passman* for sex discrimination under the Fifth Amendment, and in *Carlson v. Green* for deliberate indifference to a prisoner's medical needs under the Eighth Amendment. Those three Supreme Court decisions provided the comparison points. The opinion preserved *Bivens* in its established setting but described expansion as disfavored. It did not overrule the three decisions. (Kennedy 6–11.)

A claim presents a new context if it differs meaningfully from those Supreme Court precedents. Relevant differences can include officer rank, the constitutional provision, the nature and specificity of the action, available judicial guidance, the officer's legal mandate, potential intrusion into another branch, and previously unexamined special factors. A shared constitutional right and similar injury mechanism do not by themselves establish an existing context. Even a modest extension can be new, although a merely trivial difference will not always suffice. (Kennedy 15–17, 24–26.)

For a new context, courts must consider whether special factors counsel hesitation before creating a damages action without congressional authorization. The inquiry concerns who should decide to authorize this category of litigation and its systemwide consequences. An existing alternative remedial structure can independently constrain judicial implication. The alternatives need not provide a damages recovery equivalent to the requested remedy. If sound reasons suggest Congress might doubt the remedy's efficacy or necessity, the court should refrain from creating it. (Kennedy 11–14.)

The detention-policy claims arose in a new context. They attacked high-level executive decisions following a major terrorist attack, unlike the three recognized settings. These claims included restrictions on confinement and the wardens' strip-search practices; they excluded Hasty's separate allowance of guard abuse. Permitting litigation over officials' own policy decisions could entail examination of deliberations, sensitive communications, and institutional policy formation. The prohibition on vicarious liability did not eliminate those concerns merely because plaintiffs attributed personal participation to the officials. (Kennedy 14–19.)

National security also supplied a reason for judicial hesitation. The challenged policies concerned a major response to September 11, an area primarily entrusted to the political branches. The Court cautioned that an invocation of national security cannot excuse every claimed official wrong. Nevertheless, deciding whether damages litigation should expose policy formulation to judicial examination required a balance the Court assigned to Congress. Congress had received information about the detention practices, including the extensive Inspector General report, and nearly sixteen years had passed without creation of the requested damages remedy. The Court considered that silence potentially deliberate rather than necessarily accidental. (Kennedy 19–21.)

The Court also identified possible injunctive or habeas relief as relevant alternatives. It expressly left unresolved whether habeas could address these conditions-of-confinement claims, because the issue was not briefed. The opinion therefore did not establish that these detainees actually possessed a successful habeas remedy. The broader reasoning was that a potentially available means of challenging detention conditions, together with policy and separation-of-powers concerns, counseled against implying the requested damages action. The detention-policy *Bivens* claims could not proceed. (Kennedy 21–23.)

## Hasty: plausible allegations, but no final authorization of a remedy

Hasty's abuse claim required separate analysis. The parties appeared to agree on deliberate indifference as the substantive standard, which the Court assumed correct for this case. The complaint alleged routine guard abuse; Hasty's encouragement through terrorist labels; obstruction of grievance procedures; deliberate avoidance of the unit; notice through complaints, hunger strikes, suicide attempts, and official records; and failure to intervene. Taken as true, those allegations plausibly supported deliberate indifference and a constitutional violation **if a Bivens remedy were available**. The Court did not find that the abuse actually occurred or award damages. (Kennedy 23–24.)

Even this claim presented a new context. Its Fifth Amendment basis differed from *Carlson*'s Eighth Amendment medical-care claim; supervisory guidance was less developed; possible alternative relief existed; and the Prison Litigation Reform Act had not supplied a standalone damages remedy against federal jailers. These differences required a special-factors inquiry notwithstanding substantial parallels with *Carlson*. (Kennedy 24–26.)

Part IV-B, supported by Kennedy, Roberts, and Alito, declined to perform that inquiry initially because the parties had concentrated their briefing elsewhere and the lower courts had not conducted it. Thomas agreed to vacate and remand on a different basis. Accordingly, four Justices supported that disposition, but only three joined Part IV-B's explanation. The remand allowed the lower courts to undertake the required analysis; it did not finally hold that Hasty could be sued for damages. (Kennedy 26, 33; Thomas 1–2.)

## Conspiracy and qualified immunity

The § 1985(3) claim alleged that the officials agreed to impose discriminatory conditions because of race, religion, and national origin. Assuming the allegations true and adequately pleaded, the Court held that petitioners were entitled to qualified immunity. Immunity turns on objectively reasonable understanding of the law when officials acted. An identical prior case is not indispensable, but an abstract constitutional prohibition alone does not establish that the particular conduct violates the asserted statute. (Kennedy 26–29.)

Courts were divided about applying the intracorporate-conspiracy doctrine to § 1985(3): whether officers acting for a single entity constitute separate conspirators when they consult in their official roles. The Court did not adopt or reject that doctrine for civil-rights conspiracies. The division itself meant these officials lacked sufficiently clear notice that their consultations and resulting policies constituted the alleged statutory conspiracy. Related concerns about chilling necessary intra-department discussion reinforced the conclusion. This was an immunity decision based on unsettled law, not a definitive statutory holding that federal officials can never conspire with colleagues. (Kennedy 29–32.)

## Separate writings and exact judgment

Thomas joined the opinion except Part IV-B and concurred in the judgment. He would confine *Bivens* to its precise recognized circumstances and ordinarily reverse the Hasty judgment too, but accepted vacatur and remand to produce a controlling disposition closest to his preferred approach. He joined the conspiracy immunity ruling under existing precedent while criticizing qualified-immunity doctrine for abandoning the historical immunities Congress would have understood in 1871. His sole starred note identifies the importation of the modern clearly-established standard from *Bivens* cases into the 1871 Act cases. He urged reconsideration in an appropriate case rather than overruling immunity here. (Thomas 1–6 & 5 n.*.)

Breyer, joined by Ginsburg, would affirm. He viewed the claims as familiar applications of *Bivens*, *Davis*, and *Carlson*, rather than an expansion. Officer rank and emergency conditions could bear on the underlying violation or immunity without eliminating the cause of action. He argued that Congress had accepted *Bivens*, including through the Westfall Act's constitutional-claim exception, and stressed that the PLRA's prisoner definition excluded immigration detainees. Prospective relief could not compensate completed injuries, and alleged communication blackouts undermined its practical availability. Plausible pleading, individual-responsibility requirements, immunity, and controlled discovery could protect officials without extinguishing the damages action. Breyer also rejected immunity on the statutory conspiracy claim and stressed the value of later damages review after emergency passions subsided. (Breyer 1–24, particularly 12–17, 20–24.)

The Court **reversed the appellate judgment on all claims except the prisoner-abuse claim against Hasty; it vacated that part and remanded for further proceedings**. Kennedy, Roberts, Thomas, and Alito supported the judgment; Breyer and Ginsburg dissented. Sotomayor, Kagan, and Gorsuch did not participate. (Kennedy 33; syllabus 5.)

The assignment's plausibility quotation retains its express condition concerning an implied remedy. Its surrounding information-asymmetry question concerns access to facts needed to plead, while the complete decision shows that sufficient facts and a plausible constitutional violation do not themselves resolve remedy availability.
