# Validsa, Inc. v. PDVSA Services, Inc.
424 F. App’x 862 (11th Cir. 2011) | Contracts

## Purchases, deliveries, and advances

Validsa, a Florida international food trader, supplied food for Venezuela’s shortage. Bariven, a subsidiary of the national oil company PDVSA, used its own subsidiary PSI as purchasing agent. Purchase orders identified Bariven as purchaser, care of PSI, and Validsa invoiced on that basis. Of eight November 2007 purchases worth roughly $66 million, the litigation concerned contracts 326 and 405 for beef and 368 for chicken. Two March 2008 purchases added contract 632 for 100,000 metric tons of sugar at about $44.7 million and contract 757 for 24,000 metric tons of beef at about $103.9 million. Bariven paid thirty-percent advances totaling approximately $44.58 million on those two contracts. Validsa was required to secure the advances with performance bonds and contracted with upstream sugar and beef suppliers. (Official slip opinion 3–6 & n.1.)

The March purchases contemplated installments. Bariven would pay seventy percent after each delivery, with the corresponding thirty percent drawn from the advance; the advances would not be exhausted until final performance. Delivery schedules could change because of vessels, containers, ports, production, or inspections. This was not an unconditional obligation to deliver everything before further payment or to apply the entire advance to the first deliveries. The parties’ disagreement over that proposed change mattered to the assurance inquiry. (Slip 5, 8.)

## Insecurity and correspondence

Validsa asserted that a businessman demanded a $2 million kickback in March, purportedly requested by Bariven and PSI president Kabboul to preserve the contracts. The defendants disputed the allegation, the connection to Kabboul, and its significance. The opinion presents conflicting accounts rather than finding that Kabboul actually demanded a bribe. On April 15 Validsa inadvertently received an internal email string instructing cancellation of contract 757 and suspension of payments. The messages originally were Spanish; translation differences were immaterial to the court. No March-contract goods had been delivered at that point. Between April 8, when the internal instructions were sent, and April 15, the defendants had not directly announced cancellation or requested the advances’ return. They said the messages followed supplier meetings raising concerns about earlier chicken deliveries. (Slip 6–8 & nn.2–3.)

On April 17 Validsa demanded an immediate written explanation. At an April 29 meeting Kabboul proposed using the advances for the first $44 million of March deliveries and letters of credit afterward; the parties did not agree. A planned follow-up did not occur for reasons unclear in the record. Validsa’s counsel demanded adequate assurances within five days on May 9. The May 16 response said the internal messages had not been intended for Validsa and should not have been taken into consideration. Validsa’s May 22 letter again complained of an inadequate concrete response and late payments. (Slip 8–9.)

On June 3 PSI sent a proposed agreement labeled a draft without legal or commercial value and invited comments. It contained a new delivery schedule, a forum clause, reciprocal-performance language, and a proposed substitution of PSI as the party responsible for payment. Validsa did not respond or contact either defendant again, instead filing suit on June 12. By then the November deliveries were complete, no sugar had been delivered under 632, and one beef installment had been delivered under 757. Payments for delivered goods remained due while Validsa still held nearly all the substantial advances. (Slip 9–12 & nn.4–5.)

## Proceedings and appellate issues

Validsa sought payment for delivered goods and damages for anticipatory repudiation of contracts 632 and 757. Bariven counterclaimed for breach, unjust enrichment, implied good faith and commercial reasonableness, and an equitable accounting. The district court adopted Validsa’s proposed summary-judgment order, found liability on all five claims, and dismissed all counterclaims. It considered insecurity and written demands established, found the responses inadequate, and suggested proposed modifications themselves constituted repudiation. It also held PSI individually liable despite its disclosed agency. A subsequent bench damages trial produced awards for unpaid deliveries, incidental losses, and lost profits of about $40.76 million before offset of the approximately $44.58 million advances. The court excluded certain expert testimony and the seller’s consequential losses, and denied prejudgment interest. (Slip 12–18 & n.6.)

PSI and Bariven challenged anticipatory liability, counterclaim dismissals, and exclusion of their economist’s testimony; PSI separately contested individual liability. Validsa cross-appealed the consequential-damages exclusion, advance offset or recoupment, and prejudgment-interest denial on the earlier contracts. Bariven did not challenge liability for the delivered November goods or the first delivered installment under 757. The Eleventh Circuit’s unpublished April 21, 2011 per curiam decision was issued by Barkett, Hull, and District Judge Schlesinger sitting by designation, with no separate writings. Summary judgment received de novo review with favorable inferences for nonmovants; expert admissibility received abuse-of-discretion review. (Slip 1–3 & designation note, 19 & nn.7–8, 28 & n.16.)

## Adequacy of assurances remained for trial

Florida’s UCC assurance provision, §672.609, permits a written demand when reasonable grounds for insecurity arise. Between merchants commercial standards govern the grounds and the response’s adequacy. Pending assurances, commercially reasonable suspension is permitted for performance for which the agreed return has not already been received. Failure to provide adequate assurances within a reasonable time, capped at thirty days, is repudiation. Insecurity and adequacy ordinarily are factual matters but can be decided as law when a rational factfinder could reach only one conclusion. The appellate court agreed that Validsa had reasonable grounds and made a written demand; the April 17 demand began the assurance inquiry. (Slip 14, 19–22 & n.10.)

But a reasonable factfinder could conclude that adequate assurances had been given. Relevant evidence included the April meeting, May 16 explanation that the email was erroneous and should be disregarded, June draft, and continuing possession of unrecalled advances. The June proposal could be read as an attempt to escape Bariven’s obligations, yet another permissible reading was that substituting a United States corporation added security and changing dates accommodated delays. Footnote 9 explains why those competing inferences defeated summary judgment. The draft was not automatically an agreed modification or necessarily a repudiation. (Slip 21–23 & nn.9, 11.)

Footnote 10 also rejects the district court’s categorical April 29 repudiation date: the statutory window following April 17 had not closed, and the proposals could be read either as refusal or as something less. The court vacated Validsa’s summary judgment against Bariven on Counts IV and V and remanded for trial on adequacy. It did not determine that Bariven had furnished sufficient assurances or that Validsa’s cessation was conclusively justified. (Slip 22–23 & n.10.)

Validsa’s earlier delivery delays did not themselves bar a demand. They reflected the timing of advances, the contracts lacked a time-is-of-the-essence term, and the defendants neither objected nor declared those delays breaches. The court found no reversible error in that limited ruling, but distinguished it from later wrongful termination. If Bariven gave adequate assurances, Validsa’s termination would itself breach; if it failed to do so, Bariven anticipatorily breached. This was the remaining factual liability issue identified for the March contracts. (Slip 23–24 & n.12.)

## Counterclaims and disclosed agency

Because the March-contract breach finding no longer stood, the court reinstated Bariven’s Counts I and II for Validsa’s breach and Counts V and VI concerning good faith and commercial reasonableness. It expressed no view on other possible grounds against the latter claims. It affirmed unjust-enrichment dismissals, Counts III and IV, because express contracts governed, and accounting Count VII because no fiduciary relationship or complex transaction was established and an adequate legal remedy existed. Those independent grounds did not depend on the reversed anticipatory finding. (Slip 23–25 & n.13.)

Under the Florida law applied, an agent of a disclosed principal is not personally liable for its contract debts absent an express contrary agreement. Bariven was disclosed and PSI had not expressly assumed its liabilities. Employee beliefs about liability, negotiations by PSI, a relationship letter, and the unaccepted June draft did not establish an intent to become bound contrary to the identified agency. Offering a future substitution did not create an express assumption of existing obligations. The court reversed PSI’s individual liability and directed judgment for PSI on Validsa’s amended-complaint claims, rather than simply reopening PSI’s liability for trial. (Slip 25–28 & nn.14–15.)

## Damages evidence and its limits

The court found no abuse of discretion in excluding portions of economist Herce’s testimony. Proposed increased supply costs assumed delays beyond October 31, 2008, renegotiation, an increased agreed price, and correspondence with his market indexes. The record did not establish that sequence. His proposed commission deduction used an 8.4-percent rate from earlier transactions, but evidence established neither an obligation nor that rate for contract 757. Unlike an expert study of actual comparable land transactions in the precedent he invoked, the calculations rested on hypothetical future dealings. Testimony with the requisite factual basis could nevertheless be offered at the new trial; the affirmance concerned the first trial’s foundation. (Slip 28–32 & n.16.)

Validsa sought losses associated with an additional 20,369 tons of beef beyond the 24,000-ton purchase it pleaded. An attached reference to 44,369 tons did not substitute for alleging or explaining that larger claim. The court treated extra-quantity losses as consequential rather than direct losses under the pleaded purchase, and followed Florida intermediate authority limiting an aggrieved seller to lost profits and incidental damages. Validsa offered insufficient persuasive evidence that the Florida Supreme Court would reject that rule. The exclusion was affirmed; this is not a ruling about every seller’s consequential recovery under all jurisdictions or contractual arrangements. (Slip 32–34 & nn.17–18.)

## Interest, offset, and precise disposition

Florida’s loss theory generally awards prejudgment interest once liability and pecuniary loss are determined, but recognizes equitable exceptions. Here Validsa held advances exceeding the damages award and, on the accepted findings, possessed those funds before payments on the November contracts became due. The court agreed that interest would create an inequitable windfall rather than compensate deprivation of funds, even though the advances arose under other purchases. It affirmed interest denial and found no error in offset. This was a fact-dependent application, not a universal rule that any unrelated advance defeats interest. (Slip 35–38 & nn.19–20.)

The judgment was affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded. PSI was entitled to judgment. The unchallenged delivered-goods claims against Bariven retained the affirmed interest and offset treatment. The March anticipatory claims and four linked counterclaims returned for trial; three independently defective counterclaims remained dismissed. Evidentiary rulings were affirmed subject to the expert-foundation qualification. The concluding instructions stated that adequate assurances would defeat Validsa’s anticipatory recovery and require consideration of restored counterclaims; inadequate assurances would establish Bariven’s breach and entitle Validsa to the damages already calculated. That conditional instruction is not an unconditional affirmance of a net money award. (Slip 38–40.)

## Assignment and reading scope

The textbook’s “Spam or Beef?” problem and two reprises compress PSI and Bariven into PSI and ask about repudiation, assurances, and modified security or payment terms. The actual appeal separates the principal’s possible liability from the agent’s nonliability and leaves adequacy unresolved. All three segments concern the same appellate judgment. Complete native text and originals were read at physical pages 259–260 (printed 915–916), 269 (925), and 271 (927). The complete forty-page official opinion, all twenty numbered footnotes and designation note, was read through judgment; selected original pages corroborate caption, panel, detailed assurance notes, and remand instructions. Internal contract-number and monetary-total inconsistencies are recorded rather than repeated as new facts. Separate source review is tracked in the accompanying register.
