# Town Planning and Engineering Associates, Inc. v. Amesbury Specialty Co., Inc.
369 Mass. 737, 342 N.E.2d 706 (1976) | Contracts

## Court, decision, and joined proceedings
The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court decided this case on February 13, 1976, after argument on December 5, 1975. Justice Kaplan wrote for the four-member panel consisting of Chief Justice Hennessey and Justices Quirico, Kaplan, and Wilkins. There is no reported dissent or concurrence. The court affirmed judgments for Town Planning on its compensation claim and for Town Planning and its officer Alfred Amato in Amesbury’s companion action seeking repayment. Note 1 identifies that companion caption; it is not a separate appellate decision. The original scan prints 1976, whereas the CAP date metadata and native OCR incorrectly read 1978. (369 Mass. at 737–739 & n. 1, 747.)

## Transaction and chronology
Amesbury sought plans and engineering-related services for an industrial building. Amato, Town Planning’s president, treasurer, employee, and supervisor of this project, had substantial practical design experience but was not a registered professional engineer. In late 1968 Amesbury’s president, Arthur Willette, asked him about a possible site. Town Planning retained registered engineer Frank Gallagher, who advised against that location. In January 1969 attention shifted to Newburyport. The project included test borings, a site plan by a registered surveyor, and work by additional registered engineers. (Id. at 739–740.)

Willette furnished plans for a pre-engineered building prepared by Channel Building Company. Town Planning used those plans together with work it commissioned from registered independent consultants: Rocco Blasi for concrete work, Gallagher for mechanical systems, and Frank Kent for electrical work. Amato and approximately six draftsmen collated and correlated the consultants’ work and the pre-engineered materials, prepared interiors and other components, and composed specifications. Their papers exceeded the engineers’ work in bulk, but bulk alone did not establish which tasks legally constituted engineering practice. (Id. at 740, 744.)

By late March the preliminary plans were ready for informal bidding. Town Planning sent estimated costs of $499,972 on April 7 and billed $9,837 for work already completed; Amesbury paid that bill. The parties used the plans and estimates to seek Small Business Administration financing. At the SBA’s request, they executed a written agreement on April 16, memorializing their earlier understanding, specifying engineering services, and establishing compensation. The services included detailed plans and specifications, estimates, bidding and contracting assistance, review of working drawings, consultation during construction, payment review, and final inspection and reporting. The SBA approved a $500,000 building loan and another $100,000 for equipment in early May. (Id. at 740–741 & n. 2.)

Final bids opened on June 25 were substantially higher than the preliminary estimate: Channel bid $577,790 and LaMont $602,207, each excluding electrical work. Amesbury rejected both. Willette did not know that the SBA could be approached for an increase in the approved loan. On June 26 he instructed Amato to renegotiate or obtain lower bids, and Amato acted on that instruction. Within the following few days, however, Amesbury terminated the contract through counsel, principally on an asserted illegality theory. It then negotiated directly with LaMont, which offered a less elaborate pre-engineered building for $484,000. The auditor found that the resulting design resembled Town Planning’s and that LaMont used its plans, although LaMont disputed those propositions or their implications. (Id. at 741.)

## Claims, findings, and proceedings below
Town Planning sued on the express contract and alternatively on an account annexed, demanding $30,388 calculated under the contract. Amesbury asserted misrepresentation, unsatisfactory work, and illegal performance or failure of consideration. Its companion action against Town Planning and Amato sought return of the $9,837 already paid. A Superior Court auditor heard evidence and recommended recovery for Town Planning and rejection of the repayment claim. The joined cases then went to a jury on that report and additional evidence, producing corresponding verdicts and judgments. The Supreme Judicial Court ordered direct appellate review after review had been sought in the Appeals Court. (Id. at 737–739.)

The misrepresentation defense failed for lack of evidence: Amato had not represented himself as a registered engineer, and the auditor found no fraud by either him or the corporation. The proposed understanding that the building had to cost approximately $500,000 also lacked evidentiary support. That figure was an estimate and financing amount, not a proven contractual price ceiling. As to workmanship, the auditor found proper performance, and the jury’s verdicts resolved that issue for Town Planning. The trial judge permitted the jury to consider failure to register as rebuttable evidence of inadequate qualifications or lack of good-faith engineering performance; the jury was not instructed to treat the missing registration as conclusive proof of defective work. (Id. at 741–742 & n. 3.)

## Illegality issue and statutory setting
The remaining question was whether Amato’s lack of professional registration prevented Town Planning from collecting its contract compensation and entitled Amesbury to recover its prior payment. The contract’s terms were not intrinsically illegal. Amesbury argued instead that the manner of performance violated the engineering-registration scheme. General Laws chapter 112, § 81T generally required registration for engineering practice, while § 81R(f) allowed corporate practice provided that the person in charge was a registered professional engineer or surveyor. Amesbury argued that Amato was the person in charge and lacked the required certificate. (Id. at 742–744 & nn. 4–5.)

Town Planning responded that the actual engineering was performed by registered independent consultants and that the remaining work was nonengineering or marginal to engineering. Alternatively, it argued that each consultant was in charge of the engineering segment he performed. Amesbury bore the burden of proving illegality and had made no serious expert effort to characterize the separate tasks. Nevertheless, the court assumed, without finally resolving those classification questions, that the statute required a registered engineer acting for the corporation to coordinate the independent engineers’ contributions. It then considered the consequences of that assumed violation. (Id. at 744–745 & n. 6.)

## Governing approach and reasoning
The statute served at least in part to protect public safety, and a violation could be punished as a misdemeanor. But it did not expressly prescribe forfeiture of compensation for unlawful engineering practice, unlike the separate statute governing unlicensed real estate brokers. The court therefore asked whether the civil consequence urged by Amesbury should be added to the statutory sanction in the circumstances presented. It did not conclude that the registration requirement was optional or purely a revenue measure. (Id. at 745 & n. 7.)

Kaplan prescribed an evaluation of the entire transaction: the nature of the contract, the extent and centrality of the unlawful conduct, the relationship between the valuable services and the statutory violation, the strength of the protective policy, the extent to which denying an additional sanction would undermine that policy, and the seriousness of the plaintiff’s forfeiture compared with the defendant’s undeserved gain. The court’s conclusion was that those considerations favored Town Planning. Registered professionals had performed important substantive work; fraud and defective performance had been rejected on the record; the questionable conduct concerned the corporation’s coordination through an unregistered supervisor; and complete nonpayment would give Amesbury a substantial benefit at Town Planning’s expense. (Id. at 744–746 & nn. 6–13.)

The protective scheme also had a backstop: chapter 143, § 54A generally prevented public agencies administering building requirements from accepting plans or specifications without the seal of a registered architect or engineer, subject to statutory exceptions. That independent mechanism informed the question whether compensation forfeiture was necessary to effectuate the registration policy. The court did not say that every plan in this record had been proved to bear a particular seal; it referred to the statutory protection. (Id. at 745 n. 11.)

The court rejected automatic accumulation of sanctions whenever illegality appears. It relied on Massachusetts precedents refusing to impose a gift of valuable services on a defendant unless the statutory policy required that consequence. Its discussion and footnote 4 also reject a categorical claim that a contract lawful in its terms can never be affected by illegal performance: some such performance does bar recovery, and the inquiry concerns when that consequence is appropriate. Footnote 14 likewise distinguishes a summary disposition on inadequate information about professional services from the developed record here. (Id. at 742–743 n. 4, 746 & n. 14.)

Because the material circumstances bearing on illegality were substantially undisputed, the trial judge could determine its civil consequences as a matter of law. That differed from cases in which the underlying facts themselves required jury resolution. Town Planning recovered under the express agreement. Kaplan recognized that a lesser quantum-meruit recovery might be suitable where illegality was serious enough to warrant a reduction yet not serious enough to bar the action. Amesbury had not urged that intermediate measure, and the court found no reason to impose it on these facts. The discussion is an available distinction, not the disposition actually entered here. (Id. at 747 & nn. 15–16.)

## Exact disposition and assigned significance
The judgments were affirmed: Town Planning retained its $30,388 contract recovery, and Amesbury failed in the companion action to recover the $9,837 payment from Town Planning and Amato. The decision did not award both the contract amount and an additional quantum-meruit amount. (Id. at 738–739, 747.)

The assigned note on printed page 763 accurately focuses on the court’s range of possible civil responses to licensing violations. The detailed opinion explains why contract compensation, rather than no recovery or a reduced restitutionary measure, was permissible here. A public-protection licensing statute is a serious consideration; it is not necessarily an automatic rule allowing a customer to retain competent services free of charge when the statute has not commanded that remedy.
