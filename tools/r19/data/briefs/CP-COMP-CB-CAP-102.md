# Jones v. Governor of Florida

**975 F.3d 1016 (11th Cir. 2020) (en banc). September 11, 2020. Civil Procedure.** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, physical PDF page 167, printed pages 285–287, Note 8. The assigned material is an editorial account and selected judicial quotations. Judicial pinpoints below use the court-issued opinion’s consecutively numbered pages, 1–200; they are not an invented Federal Reporter pagination map.

## What happened and how the case reached the court

Florida traditionally disenfranchised people convicted of felonies, subject to discretionary restoration of civil rights. In November 2018, about 65% of Florida voters approved Amendment 4. It restored voting rights upon completion of all terms of sentence, including parole or probation, except for convictions for murder or a felony sexual offense. The legislature then enacted Senate Bill 7066, defining sentence completion to include financial obligations imposed in the sentencing document: restitution, fines, fees, and costs. The Florida Supreme Court agreed that the amendment included financial terms. The dispute was whether federal constitutional protections required exceptions or additional procedures for people who could not pay or could not reliably determine what they owed. (Opinion 3–5.)

The financial-record problems were concrete. The district court found that many people lacked old sentencing documents, could not separate felony obligations from misdemeanor obligations, or could not reliably reconstruct their payments. Collection agencies could retain fees that were absent from county payment records; restitution paid directly to victims might not appear in government records at all. An expert’s diligent investigation of 153 randomly selected people found inconsistencies for all but three. Another plaintiff’s inquiry took county financial personnel 12–15 hours without resolving all discrepancies. Jordan and Martin’s dissents detail these findings and emphasize that Florida did not challenge the relevant factual findings on appeal. These are reported trial findings, rather than factual conclusions independently established by this brief. (Martin 88–93; Jordan 99–110.)

Before trial, the State adopted the “every-dollar” method. For voting eligibility, payments connected with a sentence—including payment-plan or collection charges—would count toward the original sentence total. A person could therefore satisfy the voting requirement even though some underlying debt remained unpaid. The majority treated this as a narrowing, administrable definition of sentence completion; the dissents thought it undermined both the asserted full-payment justification and the reliability of the records, because county systems might show only net payments. (7, 27–28, 54–55; Jordan 104–107, 148–149.)

Several affected individuals sued the Governor and Secretary of State. The district court initially enjoined exclusion based solely on the plaintiffs’ inability to pay. A panel affirmed that preliminary injunction in **950 F.3d 795**, applying heightened scrutiny to wealth discrimination. That February 2020 interlocutory decision is a separate procedural stage from this September en banc appeal. The district court then certified a Rule 23(b)(2) class of otherwise eligible people with unpaid obligations and a subclass asserting genuine inability to pay. After trial, it held the payment restriction unconstitutional as applied to indigent people and treated fees and costs as prohibited taxes on voting. (5–8.)

The permanent relief also protected people whose obligations were unknown and could not be determined diligently. It required an advisory-opinion request form and a response mechanism: someone checking the inability-to-pay box could register and vote if the Division of Elections failed to answer within 21 days. The injunction retained the requirement to pay determinable fines and restitution when the person could afford them. The appellate majority said the district court had not separately decided the due-process theories, although its relief reached uncertainty about obligations. Jordan disagreed, reading the district court’s ruling against unknowable payment requirements as an actual due-process holding. Both accounts of that procedural disagreement must be preserved. The State obtained an initial en banc hearing and a stay of most challenged relief. (8–9, 52; Jordan 151–152.)

## Questions, arguments, and holdings

The plaintiffs advanced three overlapping constitutional objections: unequal treatment of people genuinely unable to pay; the Twenty-Fourth Amendment’s prohibition on denying or abridging voting because of unpaid poll or other taxes; and due-process objections to uncertain standards and inadequate eligibility procedures. The State defended completion of the entire sentence as a permissible voting qualification and contended that registration procedures, advisory opinions, and safeguards before removal supplied adequate protection.

The en banc court rejected the constitutional challenges, **reversed the district court’s judgment, and vacated the challenged portions of its injunction**. It also lifted the stay in the distinct cross-appeal, No. 20-12304, directed severance and briefing, and directed assignment to a panel. Thus this judgment did not decide every issue in that cross-appeal, and its mandate should not be compressed into an unqualified statement that every portion of every injunction was vacated. The court reviewed entry of permanent relief for abuse of discretion, legal conclusions de novo, and factual findings for clear error. (9–10, 60.)

## Why the majority reached that result

**Equal protection.** The majority began with Richardson v. Ramirez: the Fourteenth Amendment permits felony disenfranchisement, including after sentence completion. It reasoned that felons seeking restoration could not invoke the same fundamental-right analysis as citizens never disenfranchised. Although suspect classifications remained forbidden, wealth itself was not a suspect classification. Florida’s distinction was between people who had and had not completed their sentences, rather than an expressly wealth-based classification. Rational-basis review therefore applied. (10–15.)

The majority distinguished Harper’s poll tax, which imposed an irrelevant financial condition on the electorate generally. A criminal sentence, including its monetary terms, was instead directly connected to the offense underlying disenfranchisement. It also confined the Griffin access-to-courts and Bearden poverty-based-imprisonment precedents to their contexts. Florida was not imposing additional imprisonment for nonpayment or withholding judicial review. In its view, the amendment lifted an existing disqualification conditionally rather than newly punishing inability to pay. It expressly overruled the earlier panel’s heightened-scrutiny holding. (15–22.)

Under deferential review, Florida could rationally distinguish people who had fully satisfied their sentences as more suitable candidates for restoration, pursue rehabilitation and satisfaction of justice, and expand voting access incrementally. The Constitution did not require a perfect line or invalidate the classification merely because many people could not satisfy it. The majority rejected an analysis confined to the unique circumstances of the named plaintiffs and defended the every-dollar policy as at least debatable. It also pointed to 85,000 registrations awaiting screening: on its reading, those people remained registered and entitled to vote unless the State developed credible, reliable information supporting removal. The dissents strongly disputed the practical significance of that observation. (22–30.)

**Twenty-Fourth Amendment.** Six judges agreed that court fees and costs imposed within criminal sentences were penalties, not taxes. The majority emphasized their connection with criminal wrongdoing, their inclusion in a sentence, and their relationship to the sentencing court’s punitive and rehabilitative powers. Revenue generation did not by itself transform a penalty into a tax. Financial sanctions did not have to vary with the seriousness of every offense to remain punitive. This conclusion supplied the majority basis for rejecting the tax claim. (31–36.)

Part III-B-2 offered an additional rationale **joined by only three judges**. William Pryor, Newsom, and Lagoa rejected the State’s broad claim that the voting-rights amendments never apply to reenfranchisement: selective restoration could not evade prohibitions on race, sex, age, or taxes. But they interpreted the Twenty-Fourth Amendment’s “by reason of” language to require more than but-for causation, drawing a distinction from other amendments’ “on account of” wording. They would allow a financial condition supported by a constitutionally legitimate voter qualification even if unpaid taxes were part of the causal chain. That alternative was not a six-judge holding. (36–51.)

**Vagueness and procedural due process.** The majority distinguished uncertainty about a law’s standard from difficulty discovering facts needed to apply it. Florida prohibited a person from registering or voting while knowingly ineligible; the scienter requirements reduced notice and enforcement concerns. Missing judgments or payment records could make it difficult to ascertain sentence completion, but did not make the operative requirement unclear. It viewed every-dollar accounting as narrowing criminal exposure rather than introducing vagueness. (52–55.)

For procedural due process, the court assumed a protected liberty interest without conclusively deciding that question. It then distinguished legislative from adjudicative action under Bi-Metallic. Generally applicable rules fixing qualifications receive the process inherent in lawmaking. Individual decisions directed at particular people can require additional safeguards under Mathews v. Eldridge. In this case, the majority characterized disenfranchisement and the restoration conditions as legislative acts; plaintiffs were not challenging particular eligibility adjudications. It therefore rejected a constitutional requirement that Florida supply individual assistance reconstructing facts needed to comply with a generally applicable law. Separately, it held that existing notice, hearing, and de novo judicial review before removal adequately guarded individual ineligibility decisions. The 21-day advisory process, in its view, created new factual assistance rather than curing a proven adjudicative deprivation. (55–59.)

## Concurrences, dissents, and exact vote

The judgment was **6–4**. William Pryor wrote the main opinion, joined by Newsom, Branch, Grant, Luck, and Lagoa except for Part III-B-2, which only Newsom and Lagoa joined. Wilson, Martin, Jordan, and Jill Pryor dissented. Rosenbaum and Brasher were recused. The complete source contains six writings and no appendix. (1–2, 61–200.)

William Pryor, joined by Lagoa, added a one-page concurrence emphasizing fidelity to law and judicial limits rather than predicted historical approval. Lagoa wrote separately to defend rational-basis review and to stress alternatives to full payment: payee-approved termination, community-service conversion, modification of sentencing obligations, and executive clemency. Her nine notes discuss constitutional authorization, relevant precedents, differences for out-of-state and federal convictions, and court-fee practices. She acknowledged that alternatives could be discretionary but rejected the premise that this rendered them constitutionally insufficient. These were her additional grounds, rather than a unanimous finding that every indigent person would successfully obtain restoration. (61–80, particularly 73–75 and notes 6–7.)

Martin, joined by the other three dissenters, accepted a due-process interest in the objectively defined promise of restoration. She would apply Mathews to the actual individualized implementation, not merely to enactment of the rules. She found a strong voting interest, a high risk of erroneous or deterred voting caused by missing financial information and prolonged screening, and insufficient countervailing administrative justification. Her three notes address the adjudicative character of eligibility, unchallenged findings, and the majority’s contested description of the 85,000 registrants. (81–96.)

Jordan, also joined by the other three dissenters, would uphold relief under heightened scrutiny and independently under rational-basis review. In his view, Griffin, Bearden, and Harper prevented the State from continuing a severe disability solely because someone could not afford payment, even when the original disability was lawful. Richardson did not resolve the conditions of later restoration. An inability-to-pay subclass was not a collection of merely unusual individual examples. Nor could an incentive rationally induce payment by people genuinely unable to pay. Ordinary debt collection remained available without denying the vote. (97–151, including notes 3–9.)

Jordan also treated the State’s actual eligibility screening as adjudicative and considered removal-only procedures inadequate for someone who could not safely register in the first place. His driver’s-license hypothetical, reproduced in the assignment, illustrates that a government cannot promise restoration conditioned on paying an amount while refusing meaningful access to the information needed to determine it. He objected to arbitrary implementation across counties and incompatible accounting practices, not simply to factual difficulty in an otherwise settled standard. For the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, he emphasized fees’ revenue-raising substance and considered “by reason of” and “on account of” ordinary synonyms. His dissent includes sixteen notes, all within the source comparison. (151–189, especially 160–171 and 179–188.)

Jill Pryor joined Martin and Jordan in full and added contextual criticism of the legislature’s implementation. She emphasized substantial obligations borne largely by indigent people, known administrative difficulties, the lack of additional personnel, and deterrence caused by criminal-prosecution warnings. Her eleven notes include sources and qualifications about legislative intent, the amendment’s financial terms, and staffing assistance. Her account is dissenting reasoning, not the court’s disposition. (190–200.)

## What the assignment highlights

The assigned Note 8 places the case alongside Mathews and quotes the legislative/adjudicative distinction, followed by Jordan’s hypothetical. The majority does not abolish Mathews review of individual voting decisions; its premise is that the challenged deprivation arose through general legislation and that removal safeguards were already sufficient. The dissents challenge that premise by treating factual eligibility implementation as the relevant state action.

The editorial account compresses the financial requirement into fees and costs “incurred in connection with” convictions. The full opinion defines the voting requirement by financial terms imposed in the sentencing document and distinguishes later charges through the every-dollar policy. The assignment also describes any unanswered 21-day request as permitting registration; the majority’s procedural description specifically links that route to the requester’s inability-to-pay checkbox. Those qualifications matter when using the note as an account of the actual judgment. This brief concerns the 2020 en banc decision and does not assert a current-law or citator update.
