# Oppenheimer Fund, Inc. v. Sanders

**437 U.S. 340 (1978) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 19, 1978.** Justice Powell wrote for a unanimous Court. Civil Procedure: identification of class members, Rule 23 notice, discovery relevance, and allocation of notice-related costs. Assigned account: CivPro, physical PDF 436, printed 843; the rest of the supplied page gives comparative notice discussion.

## The underlying suit and the practical notice problem

The representative plaintiffs bought shares in Oppenheimer Fund during 1968 and 1969. The Fund was an open-end investment fund whose shares were sold at net asset value plus a sales charge. Oppenheimer Management managed its portfolio for a fee calculated partly from that value; the brokerage firm Oppenheimer & Co. controlled the management corporation. Three complaints filed in 1969, later consolidated, alleged that prospectuses and annual reports failed to disclose restricted-security investments, their risks, and how they were valued. Plaintiffs alleged overvaluation that inflated share prices. Their proposed purchaser class sought the difference between the prices paid and the shares' actual value; a separate derivative count sought excessive management fees for the Fund. Defendants denied liability and raised setoff and contingent-liability positions. These allegations were not adjudicated as established fraud in this decision. **342–344 & nn.1–4.**

In April 1973 plaintiffs sought Rule 23(b)(3) certification for buyers from March 28, 1968, through April 24, 1970. That class contained about 121,000 people, approximately 103,000 of whom still held Fund shares. About 18,000 had ceased to be shareholders. Meanwhile, approximately 68,000 current shareholders were outside the purchaser class. A list of current shareholders therefore would both omit former shareholders entitled to class notice and include many people outside the class. **344–345.**

The Fund's transfer agent possessed records from which the correct names and addresses could be derived. Producing the list required substantial manual sorting, keypunching 150,000–300,000 cards, and writing eight computer programs using existing or newly created tapes. In 1973 the estimated expense exceeded $16,000. Plaintiffs proposed limiting the class to buyers who still held shares and placing notice in an ordinary Fund mailing; they offered about $5,000 for printing and insertion. Defendants objected that dropping former shareholders arbitrarily excluded their interests and that notifying current nonclass shareholders could prompt harmful selling. **345–346 & n.5.**

## The order and the appellate disagreement

In May 1975 the district court approved class treatment but rejected the narrower class definition. It also initially refused to put notice in a general shareholder mailing. Nevertheless, it required defendants to produce and pay for the class-member list, while plaintiffs would prepare and mail the notices. Its reasons were that defendants had opposed the cheaper proposals and that the identification expense was relatively modest. A later modification allowed notices inside the Fund's periodic mailing only when addressed to class members and with plaintiffs paying the extra mailing and segregation expenses; the Fund would initially bear identification costs, without prejudice to a later reimbursement claim. **346–347 & nn.6–7.**

A Second Circuit panel reversed the identification-cost allocation but upheld certification. The en banc court, seven to three, restored the cost order, treating identification as discovery ordinarily performed at the responding party's expense, subject to protection against undue burden. The Supreme Court accepted collateral-order appealability but did not review whether certification itself was proper. Its task was to identify the source of authority for obtaining the list and the proper allocation of the expense. **347–349 & nn.8–10.**

## Holding one: notice identification was authorized by Rule 23(d)

The relevant notice rule required the best practicable notice, including individual notice to reasonably identifiable members of a Rule 23(b)(3) class. *Eisen* had required the representative plaintiff to pay to prepare and mail notice, but it had not resolved the different situation in which the court ordered defendants to perform identification tasks. An order requiring cooperation and an order placing its expense on defendants therefore needed separate analysis. **349–350.**

The discovery rules were the wrong authority for this particular request. Under the then-existing Rule 26(b)(1), relevance extended beyond evidence already admissible at trial and beyond merits issues narrowly stated in pleadings. Discovery could help clarify claims or determine jurisdiction, venue, or class-certification questions. But it still had to bear on an issue in the litigation. Here plaintiffs wanted the complete list solely to send notice, not to obtain facts supporting their claims or to establish certification prerequisites. Their willingness to abandon the request if a cheaper notice arrangement were accepted confirmed that purpose. **350–353 & nn.12–18.**

The en banc court's suggestion that the list might resolve a future issue about whether notice was properly sent did not change the analysis. That issue could arise only after the information had already been obtained and used. Yet the Court carefully reserved other uses of class-member identities: names and addresses could be discoverable when genuinely relevant to certification or another issue, or when communication with members might yield relevant evidence. The decision did not make class identities categorically immune from discovery. **354 & nn.19–20.**

Rule 23(d), governing the conduct of class proceedings and related notice orders, supplied the needed authority. Courts could order a defendant to cooperate in identifying class members, just as they could require use of a defendant's regular mailings to facilitate notice. An appropriate procedural task did not become impermissible merely because plaintiffs ultimately should finance it. **354–356 & nn.21–23.**

## Holding two: who performs the work differs from who pays

Ordinarily the representative plaintiff should carry out notice tasks because that party seeks class treatment. But a defendant may be able to perform a task with less difficulty or expense. Rule 23(d) permits the court to assign such work to the defendant. The Court drew a rough analogy to the historical interrogatory rule permitting access to business records when either party could derive the answer with substantially equal effort; greater efficiency on the responding party's side could justify requiring that party to derive it instead. This was an analogy guiding discretion, not a conclusion that the list request itself was discovery. **356–357 & nn.24–26.**

Even when defendants are properly ordered to do the work, plaintiffs ordinarily should bear substantial notice-related expenses. Genuine discovery often advances the responding party's litigation as well, whereas performing the opposing class's notice tasks rarely does. Courts therefore should be more willing to shift such task expenses to the representative plaintiffs than to shift costs of genuine discovery. The usual notice-cost inquiry is whether the expense is substantial, rather than whether it satisfies discovery's undue-burden threshold. **358–359 & n.27.**

Some discretion remains. Calculating and shifting a trivial expense may itself be inefficient. A task defendants already must perform in ordinary business may also justify leaving the expense where it falls. Those examples did not authorize a broad departure from the principle that plaintiffs finance their effort to maintain a class action. **359 & n.28.**

## Why the specific cost order was an abuse of discretion

Neither side could itself produce this list; both needed the transfer agent's paid services. Plaintiffs could buy the same work for the same price as defendants. The court could require defendants, who apparently controlled access to the records, to make them available. But equal third-party expense removed an efficiency reason for putting payment on defendants. The plaintiffs' notice obligation supplied the reason for performing the work, so plaintiffs should pay. **359–360 & n.29.**

Defendants were not required to purchase the right to make successful objections. If excluding former shareholders was improper, the court should reject that definition regardless of defendants' willingness to fund the resulting list. Likewise, protecting the Fund from notice to outsiders should not depend on whether it paid for that protection. The Court also rejected compelling an unwilling defendant to buy supposed res judicata advantages from a larger class. **360–361 & nn.30–31.**

The Fund's assets exceeded $500 million, but a $16,000 expense benefiting plaintiffs was still substantial. Wealth did not make it negligible. Nor did electronic recordkeeping justify a penalty: there was no indication or contention of bad-faith concealment, no basis to assume paper records would be cheaper, and no duty to anticipate future plaintiffs' preferred record format. Finally, an allegation of fiduciary breach did not establish liability or require the fiduciary—and indirectly other beneficiaries—to finance every claimant's suit. **361–363 & n.32.**

## Judgment, scope, and the assigned account

The unanimous Court **reversed the Second Circuit and remanded for further proceedings**, holding that the district court abused its discretion by failing to require plaintiffs to pay the transfer agent to identify their class. It did not reverse certification or determine the securities claims. **363–364.**

The assigned account accurately emphasizes both Rule 23 authority and the limited cost-allocation discretion. Its ensuing *Larson* and *Hunt* discussions concern other proceedings and contexts; they do not turn Oppenheimer into a merits-liability or settlement-cost ruling. The discovery language used here is the rule as it existed in 1978, rather than a representation of current Rule 26 wording.
