# Ogle v. Barnes

**8 T.R. 188 (K.B. 1799); assigned parallel citation 101 Eng. Rep. 1338; Court of King's Bench; April 15, 1799.** Assigned: CivPro PDF 303, printed 570, the ship-collision account under “Case Captures Negligence.” The complete report is preserved in Durnford and East, *Term Reports*, volume VIII, the corrected 1802 edition. Pinpoints refer to its printed pages 188–192; the English Reports parallel has not been independently collated.

## Facts and procedural posture

The plaintiffs' declaration alleged that they possessed the ship *Anne* and that the defendants possessed another ship, *Acteon*. While both were sailing, the defendants allegedly managed and steered their vessel incautiously, carelessly, negligently, and inexpertly. Through that negligent management, their ship struck the plaintiffs' vessel with great force and damaged it. A second count varied the allegation by placing the defendants' ship under the care and direction of Barnes and certain servants of the defendants. The report does not provide a damages amount, a location, or a detailed reconstruction of the collision. (188–189.)

The plaintiffs brought an action on the case and obtained a jury verdict. The defendant then moved in arrest of judgment, contending that the plaintiffs had selected the wrong form of action: the immediate collision required trespass rather than case. The issue therefore arose after a verdict, on the sufficiency of the action as pleaded and supported by that verdict. The court was not beginning a new factual inquiry into whose navigation caused the accident. (189, 191–192.)

## Arguments and governing distinction

Erskine, Gibbs, and Alderson argued for the plaintiffs that immediate injury ordinarily called for trespass and consequential injury for case, but that trespass required an act attributable to the defendant. They distinguished *Day v. Edwards* and *Savignac v. Roome* by treating the furious or willful driving described there as an affirmative act. Here, the declaration alleged negligent management rather than such an act. Counsel relied on *Morley v. Gaisford*, involving negligent driving by a servant, to support case, and argued that the distinction could depend on evidence at trial: proof of willful action would require trespass, while negligent conduct supported the form actually chosen. These were counsel's proposed distinctions, not a single undifferentiated holding by all judges. (189–190.)

Law and Bayley, for the defendant, argued that the damage arose from an immediate act and therefore required trespass. They invoked accidental injuries, including the musket injury in *Weaver v. Ward*, and the squib injury in *Scott v. Shepherd*. Their point was that trespass did not require an intention to inflict the resulting harm. Lawrence interjected that the historical thorn case involved entry onto another person's land to retrieve the thorns, illustrating how characterization of the underlying act mattered. (190.)

## The judges' reasoning

Lord Kenyon retained the immediate-versus-consequential distinction. He expressly accepted that a defendant's mind need not concur in the act producing injury for trespass to be appropriate. But he did not regard the present record as establishing that the defendants themselves committed the immediately injurious act. The charge was that negligent steering and management caused their ship to collide with the plaintiffs' vessel. On that account, liability could be pursued in case. Thus, his reasoning did not replace the direct-injury rule with a general rule that every unintentional collision belonged in case. It distinguished the personal acts alleged in *Day* and *Savignac* from the negligent management charged here. (190–191.)

Justice Grose stressed the procedural timing. On a motion in arrest of judgment, presumptions should favor the verdict, and nothing should be presumed against it. The declaration charged negligence, not willful action. He compared *Morley*, where case was accepted, with the reported boat-and-net incident in *Tripe v. Potter*, where willful action had made the plaintiffs' case action inappropriate. If the evidence here had shown willful conduct, the plaintiffs should have been nonsuited at trial. Because the jury instead found for them, the court should understand that the negligence pleaded had been proved and that case was proper. (191.)

Justice Lawrence likewise treated willful action and negligence differently for purposes of this record. Negligent and improvident management did not necessarily imply that the defendants performed the immediate collision-producing act. They might, after their earlier negligence, have done everything possible to avoid harm, with wind and tide ultimately driving their ship into the other. That was an illustration of what the record allowed, not a finding that wind or tide actually caused this collision. If willful conduct had been proved, the objection should have been raised at trial. After the plaintiffs' verdict on a negligence declaration, the court should not presume such contrary proof. (191–192.)

## Disposition and scope

The court **discharged the rule**, rejecting the effort to arrest judgment and leaving the plaintiffs' verdict undisturbed. The report contains reasons from Kenyon, Grose, and Lawrence followed by a per curiam disposition; it reports no dissent. It does not state a numerical tally for the entire court, so none is supplied here. (192.)

The case illustrates how procedural form depended on the acts charged, the role of servants, and the stage of litigation, as well as on whether harm was immediate. The assignment explains that two of the three reported judges accepted a willful-versus-negligent distinction while Kenyon continued to stress direct and consequential injury. The full report qualifies that contrast: all three explanations concern what could be inferred from this declaration and verdict, and Kenyon expressly refused to make intent a necessary condition of trespass. The later *Leame* discussion on the same assigned page supplies editorial context, rather than an additional holding in *Ogle*.
