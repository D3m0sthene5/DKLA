# ATS Tree Services, LLC v. Federal Trade Commission
2024 WL 3511630 (E.D. Pa. July 23, 2024), No. 2:24-cv-01743-KBH, memorandum, ECF No. 80 | Contracts

## Facts and the motion before the court

ATS operated a tree-care business in Bucks County, Pennsylvania, with twelve employees. It required employee noncompete agreements and asserted that those restrictions protected its investment in specialized training. Its account described a one-year restriction on performing the same work for nearby direct competitors. That description was qualified: the agreements had been narrowed over time, and ATS said they either contained or would be enforced only to contain those limitations. ATS did not submit the actual employment contract or noncompete language. Judge Kelley B. Hodge therefore expressly declined to determine whether the restrictions were independently enforceable under Pennsylvania law. (Memorandum 2 & nn. 1–3.)

On April 23, 2024, the FTC adopted a rule prohibiting most employment noncompetes, scheduled to take effect September 4. ATS sued two days later. Its complaint asserted lack of substantive rulemaking authority, an excessive exercise of any such authority, arbitrary and capricious treatment of existing agreements, and unconstitutional legislative delegation. Its May 14 motion sought a stay of the effective date and a preliminary injunction, but deliberately excluded the arbitrary-and-capricious count. The July 10 hearing was oral argument, not a witness hearing: the parties stipulated that the facts in the written record would be the same if presented there. The court subsequently denied ATS’s motion to supplement that record. The July 23 memorandum decided the request for interim protection on that record; it did not enter final judgment resolving the entire complaint. (3–4 & n. 4.)

The rule would have barred new noncompetes, made existing agreements unenforceable except for qualifying senior executives, and required covered employers to notify affected current and former workers. The court described the FTC’s extensive labor-market research and asserted competitive harms as the agency’s rulemaking basis. Those agency findings were distinct from proof that any particular ATS employee planned to leave or that ATS’s own contracts were enforceable. (12–13 & n. 9.)

## Preliminary-injunction standard and irreparable injury

The plaintiff had to establish both a likelihood of success and a likely immediate, irreparable injury before the court balanced equities and public interests. Neither speculative risk nor an ordinary loss adequately remedied by damages sufficed. A constitutional label did not automatically establish irreparable harm. The court found ATS failed this threshold, which alone was enough to deny relief, but also considered likelihood of success because the parties had extensively litigated that question. (13–16 & n. 10.)

ATS first invoked unrecoverable compliance costs: notifying its twelve employees, changing contracts and business plans, and revising training practices. It conceded that the estimated one-time notice expense of $27.78 was de minimis; the cited contract-revision estimate was up to $1,211.58. Applying the Third Circuit’s A.O. Smith decision rather than an out-of-circuit approach, the court concluded that ordinary mandated business expenses did not become irreparable simply because they could not later be recovered from the government. ATS offered no evidence of the kind of exceptional disruption that distinguished ordinary expenditure from an immediate equitable injury. The court also distinguished Ohio v. EPA: that decision concentrated on likelihood of success and involved claimed costs vastly greater than ATS’s estimate of less than $1,250. (16–18 & nn. 11–13.)

The projected loss of training investment rested on further contingencies. ATS feared that employees freed from the covenants would immediately join competitors, prompting it to scale back training. But it identified no employee who had left, planned to leave, or was even likely to leave because the rule took effect. An unknown reduction in training chosen in response to that fear was too attenuated. Similarly, the loss of contractual rights did not independently satisfy a case-specific requirement of immediate injury. Cases involving an employee already working for a competitor, or an employer required to discharge identified unvaccinated personnel, did not establish that ATS’s hypothetical departures were imminent. The court also observed that appropriately tailored nondisclosure agreements offered another means to protect confidential information; ATS had not explained why such alternatives would be inadequate. (18–22 & n. 14.)

## Statutory authority and Congress’s amendments

ATS also failed to show the requisite probability of prevailing on the claims included in its motion. The court independently interpreted the FTC Act, expressly acknowledging Loper Bright rather than deferring to an agency interpretation because of statutory ambiguity. Section 5 directed the FTC to prevent unfair methods of competition; section 6(g) authorized rules and regulations carrying out the statutory subchapter. The court read them together as permitting substantive as well as procedural competition rules. Neither provision restricted rulemaking to procedure, and an express adjudicatory mechanism did not make adjudication the exclusive means of enforcement. ATS’s reliance on Schechter Poultry confused that decision’s comparison of statutory delegations with a holding about the exclusivity of FTC adjudication. (22–27 & nn. 15–17.)

The preventive directive mattered to this reading. A solely reactive scheme would address harm after it occurred, whereas Congress directed the agency to forestall incipient restraints on competition. The court regarded substantive rulemaking as one available means of fulfilling that preventive task. It also relied on National Petroleum Refiners and the Seventh Circuit’s adoption of its rulemaking analysis. These were precedents supporting the district court’s interpretation, not an assertion that those decisions had already adjudicated the 2024 noncompete rule. (27–29 & n. 18.)

Congress’s 1975 amendments strengthened the contextual argument. New section 18 supplied special procedures for unfair-or-deceptive-act rules, expressly preserved any authority for unfair-methods-of-competition rules, and was cross-referenced in section 6(g). Interpreting section 6(g) as purely procedural would deprive that distinction of meaningful work. Congress left the relevant competition-rulemaking language materially unchanged after National Petroleum, and legislative discussion showed awareness of that decision. The court rejected ATS’s argument that the amendments prevented ratification simply because some statutory text changed: the relevant inquiry was whether the pertinent authority materially changed. The 1980 definition of “rule,” which excluded procedural and interpretive measures while referring to rules under section 6, reinforced the statutory context. The agency’s earlier delay in exercising an available power did not extinguish it. (10–11; 29–32 & nn. 19–20.)

## Challenges to this rule and constitutional delegation

ATS alternatively argued that particular reasonable noncompetes should be evaluated under a rule of reason, that the FTC was displacing an area belonging to the states, that the major questions doctrine required further authorization, and that substantive rulemaking would make the delegation unconstitutional. The court rejected these arguments for purposes of the requested interim relief. Section 5’s preventive reach extended beyond completed antitrust violations. The cited du Pont decision did not impose a rule-of-reason test for noncompetes; the court accepted the FTC’s research-based characterization of such restrictions as unfair methods of competition rather than requiring contract-by-contract adjudication. This reasoning concerned the rulemaking authority and the preliminary merits showing, while the separately pleaded arbitrary-and-capricious challenge remained outside the motion. (23 n. 15; 32–34.)

State regulation did not create an exclusive state domain. The court considered federal and state competition jurisdiction overlapping; the rule left room for parallel state restrictions while displacing conflicting provisions. It found no major question requiring a different outcome because competition regulation was within the FTC’s core mission, the agency had previously used section 6(g) for substantive rules, and courts had sustained that authority. It distinguished the novelty and mismatch of authority discussed in West Virginia v. EPA. Finally, the mandate to prevent unfair competition supplied an intelligible principle. Schechter’s invalidation of the National Industrial Recovery Act contrasted that much broader delegation with the FTC Act; it did not establish that a standard adequate for adjudication became unconstitutional when used in rulemaking. (35–38.)

## Exact disposition, later context, and source scope

The court denied ATS’s motion for a stay of the effective date and preliminary injunction. Because both threshold factors failed, it declined to analyze the balance of equities or public interest. The memorandum stated that an appropriate order would follow. This is a single district judge’s interim ruling, with no separate writing or recorded panel vote; it neither ordered enforcement of ATS’s contracts nor finally adjudicated the omitted arbitrary-and-capricious claim. (38–39.)

The assigned Federal Register account on physical PDF 105 and its linked footnote 19 on PDF 107 identify this denial alongside the different Properties of the Villages and Ryan decisions. The same assigned account reports that the FTC voted in September 2025 to dismiss the Ryan and Properties appeals and accede to the rule’s vacatur, followed by removal of the regulatory provisions in the February 2026 notice. Those later developments prevent reading this July 2024 refusal of preliminary relief as a certification that the nationwide noncompete rule remains operative. The three judicial decisions retain their distinct stages and reasoning.
