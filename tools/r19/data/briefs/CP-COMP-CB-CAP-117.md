# John R. Sand & Gravel Co. v. United States

**552 U.S. 130 (2008) — Supreme Court of the United States, January 8, 2008.** Justice Breyer wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Alito. Justice Stevens dissented, joined by Justice Ginsburg; Ginsburg also wrote separately. The judgment was **7–2**. Assigned account: *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 176, printed page 304, in the discussion distinguishing jurisdictional conditions from merits requirements.

## The claim and the unexpected appellate obstacle

John R. Sand & Gravel held a fifty-year mining lease. In May 2002, it sued the United States in the Court of Federal Claims, alleging that Environmental Protection Agency activities on the leased land, including building and moving fences, took its leasehold rights without constitutionally required compensation. Under 28 U.S.C. § 2501, a claim within that court’s jurisdiction had to be filed within six years after it first accrued. The Government initially argued that all the claims were late, but its pretrial brief effectively conceded that certain claims were timely. It then won on the merits. **132.**

The company appealed. The Government’s appellate brief did not invoke the limitation period, but an amicus raised it. The Federal Circuit considered itself obliged to address timeliness, concluded that the action was late, and rejected it on that ground. The Supreme Court reviewed whether that court properly reached the issue despite the Government’s waiver. The question was therefore about a court’s duty to enforce a particular filing limitation on its own, not a fresh assessment of whether the fences constituted a taking. **133.**

## Holding and statutory framework

The Court held that § 2501’s special limitation period requires courts to consider timeliness independently even when the Government waives the issue. It **affirmed the Federal Circuit**. **132, 139.**

Most limitation periods protect a defendant from stale claims. Courts ordinarily treat them as affirmative defenses that can be forfeited or waived, and they often permit equitable tolling. Other deadlines serve broader systemic interests, such as administering claims, defining the scope of the Government’s consent to suit, or promoting judicial efficiency. The Court sometimes calls these more absolute limitations “jurisdictional”: the label signals, for example, that waiver cannot excuse untimeliness or that courts cannot add equitable exceptions. The opinion did not classify every deadline against the Government in that second category. It emphasized § 2501’s particular interpretive history. **133–134.**

## Why the older cases controlled

In *Kendall v. United States*, the Court had refused to add an equitable exception to a predecessor six-year statute and expressly required courts to raise timeliness whether or not a party pleaded it. In *Finn v. United States*, it applied the deadline despite a Government agency’s voluntary referral of the claim to the Court of Claims. Those decisions, and later cases including *Soriano*, repeatedly treated this limitation as unusually absolute. The precedents therefore answered the very waiver question before the Court. **134–135.**

The company could not avoid that line merely because Congress revised the statute in 1948. The older version barred claims “cognizable by” the Court of Claims; the newer version addressed claims of which that court “has jurisdiction.” The Court found those formulations substantially equivalent. The recodification generally reorganized existing law, and neither the text’s changes nor the reviser’s materials clearly expressed an intention to change the limitation’s substantive effect. *Soriano*, decided after recodification, also retained the jurisdictional interpretation. **135–136.**

## Irwin, Franconia, and the refusal to overrule

The company argued that *Irwin v. Department of Veterans Affairs* had already displaced the older approach. *Irwin* adopted a rebuttable presumption that equitable tolling principles governing private defendants also apply to suits against the United States. Its employment-discrimination deadline resembled § 2501’s language. **136–137.**

The majority nevertheless distinguished a general interpretive presumption from the definitive interpretation of this particular statute. *Irwin* involved a different limitation that had not already received the same settled construction. It mentioned *Soriano* without announcing that the Court was overruling the older line. Moreover, because the tolling presumption was rebuttable, a definitive earlier construction establishing contrary congressional intent could rebut it, just as specific statutory language could. The majority therefore understood *Irwin* as a prospective background rule rather than an invitation silently to reopen settled statutes. **137–138.**

*Franconia Associates v. United States* did address § 2501 and described ordinary limitations principles as applicable to the Government. But that decision concerned when a claim accrued. The majority separated an ordinary accrual rule from the later question whether an accrued claim’s filing deadline could be waived or tolled. *Franconia* did not erase that distinction. **138.**

The company alternatively asked the Court to overrule the older cases. The majority acknowledged that they might appear anomalous beside *Irwin*, and that the Government might not demonstrate detrimental reliance. It nonetheless found the anomaly noncritical: differently construed statutes were not unworkable law. Statutory stare decisis carried special force because Congress could amend the result and had long acquiesced in the interpretation. Even without identified reliance, reopening a settled interpretation simply because it appeared wrong could produce disruption and uncertainty. **138–139.**

## The dissents

**Stevens, joined by Ginsburg,** thought *Irwin* had already rejected the older framework. Decisions such as *Honda* and *Bowen* had moved away from inflexible sovereign-immunity reasoning before *Irwin* explicitly adopted a general tolling presumption. *Irwin* compared its language to *Soriano* and declined to distinguish them; Justice White’s separate opinion even characterized the decision as directly overruling *Soriano*. Stevens saw no implied exception for statutes previously construed under the discarded approach. His notes also explained that *Franconia*’s reliance on *Irwin*, although made in an accrual case, undermined the claim that § 2501 remained untouched. **140–143 & nn.1–7.**

Stevens accepted the importance of statutory precedent but rejected its use to preserve a rule already displaced in substance. A coherent general rule would help Congress legislate and courts apply common timing provisions. He would apply *Irwin*’s background rule here rather than revive the old uncertainty. **142–143.**

**Ginsburg’s separate dissent** supplied the alternative argument for express overruling. She agreed that *Irwin* had displaced the older cases, but even if not, intervening doctrine had removed their foundation. Preserving an outworn rule could undermine rather than protect coherence. She pointed to conflicting circuit interpretations of the materially similar § 2401(a), the absence of claimed public or private reliance on § 2501’s jurisdictional status, and the Court’s willingness elsewhere to overrule statutory decisions. In her view, predictability did not justify this inconsistent exception. She would reverse and hold that the Federal Circuit lacked warrant to declare the action time-barred. **144–147.**

## Result and assigned-source distinction

Affirmance left the company’s action defeated on timeliness; the Supreme Court did not determine the taking claim’s substantive merit. The case teaches that waiver consequences can depend on a statute’s settled judicial construction, not merely on the general category “statute of limitations.”

The assigned account accurately identifies the six-year rule and the reliance on precedent, but its concluding emphasis on statutory text needs that context. The majority did not treat the words “has jurisdiction” as a newly decisive clear statement: it found the revised language equivalent to the older wording and gave substantial weight to precedent and stare decisis.
