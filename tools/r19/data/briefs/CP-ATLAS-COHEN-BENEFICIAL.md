# Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.

**Course:** Civil Procedure

## Citation

Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 541–561 (1949), together with Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp. v. Smith, No. 512. Supreme Court of the United States. Argued April 18, 1949; decided June 20, 1949. Justice Jackson delivered the Court's opinion. Justice Douglas dissented in part, joined by Justice Frankfurter. Justice Rutledge filed a dissent addressing this case, Woods, and Ragan. Assigned accounts: Civil Procedure casebook pp. 461–462, repository PDF p. 251, note 4; and pp. 1223–1224, repository PDF p. 618, the collateral-order account before Will v. Hallock.

## Parties and material facts

A shareholder, now represented by the petitioners following his death, brought a derivative action in Beneficial's right against the Delaware corporation and certain managers and directors. He alleged eighteen years of mismanagement and fraud, including diversion or waste exceeding $100 million, and alleged that those controlling the corporation prevented it from acting despite his demand. Those were pleaded allegations, not established findings of misconduct. 337 U.S. at 543.

The shareholder held 100 shares, and an intervenor held another 150, out of more than two million outstanding shares. Their combined position was approximately 0.0125%, with market value never exceeding $9,000. The suit began in federal court in New Jersey in 1943 under diversity jurisdiction. In 1945 New Jersey enacted an expense-security statute applicable to pending actions without final judgment. Id. at 544–545 & n.1.

The quoted statute entitled a corporation to demand security for reasonable expenses, including counsel fees and amounts it might owe other defendants, where suing holders possessed less than 5% of the outstanding capital measure, unless their shares' market value exceeded $50,000. The court could adjust security as circumstances changed. Beneficial invoked its indemnification bylaws and requested a $125,000 bond. That was its requested amount; the Supreme Court did not itself fix that bond. Id.

## Procedural history

The District Court held the state statute inapplicable in federal court and declined to require security. The Third Circuit reversed. The shareholder side sought certiorari, while the corporation also pursued a companion request for mandamus to compel security. The Supreme Court first addressed whether the District Court's order was appealable under § 1291, then considered constitutional challenges and the statute's application in diversity. Id. at 545–547.

## Issues

Was the completed refusal to recognize the statutory right to security immediately appealable even though the derivative merits remained pending? Was the statute constitutional under the federal objections presented, and did it apply in federal diversity court despite the Federal Rule governing derivative actions? Id. at 545–557.

## Rules and holdings

The order qualified for immediate appeal because it conclusively resolved a claimed right separate from the merits that would probably be irreparably lost if review waited until final judgment. The Court identified a small class of decisions involving collateral rights. It expressly distinguished denial of the right to security from discretionary adjustment of an admitted security amount; it did not make every security order appealable. Id. at 545–547.

The federal constitutional objections did not invalidate the statute as the Court construed it. Its application to this diversity action was required because it created liability for reasonable defense expenses and secured that liability before the expenses were incurred. The historical Rule 23 did not conflict with the state requirements: its disclosure, shareholder-status, demand, and settlement protections could operate alongside them. Id. at 547–557.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court affirmed the Third Circuit's judgment requiring application of the New Jersey statute. The District Court had erred in refusing to determine reasonable security as a condition of continued prosecution. Because appeal provided review, the companion mandamus petition in No. 512 was dismissed. No judgment established the alleged corporate fraud or awarded the requested $100 million recovery. Id. at 547, 557.

## Reasoning on appealability

Section 1291 ordinarily consolidates review of litigation steps into an appeal from the final judgment. A tentative ruling is not appealable, and a completed intermediate ruling ordinarily waits if it will merge into a reviewable merits judgment. Here the refusal to apply the statute was complete, independent of the derivative claim, and would not be effectively repaired after the corporation incurred the unprotected expenses. Id. at 545–546.

The right to security presented a serious, unsettled question separate from liability for the asserted wrongs. That justified immediate review. If the existence of the right were undisputed and the order concerned only an adjustable amount, the appealability inquiry would differ. The qualification limits the holding and prevents turning the case into an unrestricted license for interlocutory appeal. Id. at 546–547.

## Reasoning on constitutionality

The Court described derivative litigation as an equitable response to shareholders' inability to make faithless managers vindicate the corporation's rights. It also recognized the opportunity for nuisance-value settlements and abuse. The legislature could regulate this representative litigation and impose accountability on the shareholder who chose to act for others. The Court assessed constitutional power, not whether the policy was wise or every suit involved such abuse. Id. at 547–551 & nn.2–6.

The Contract Clause challenge failed because the shareholder had no contractual right impaired by the legislation. Reasonable expense liability and adjustable security did not violate due process merely because costs could deter litigation. The legislature could use the size of a shareholder's financial interest as a measure of responsibility and good faith without denying equal protection. The Court acknowledged that financial cutoffs inevitably create imperfect boundaries. Id. at 551–553.

The Court's treatment of pending suits was expressly qualified. It did not construe the statute, without a controlling state interpretation, to create liability for expenses incurred before enactment. It contemplated liability starting at enactment or possibly the grant of security and reserved the constitutional effect of a different application. Its holding does not approve every retroactive expense assessment. Id. at 553–554.

Beneficial's Delaware incorporation did not itself free a plaintiff using the New Jersey forum from that forum's conditions. Particular conflicts and full-faith-and-credit questions were not before the Court. New Jersey constitutional objections ultimately belonged to state courts; absent a contrary state determination, the Court presumed compliance in these circumstances. Id. at 547, 554–555.

## Reasoning on Erie and the Federal Rule

The procedural/substantive label did not settle the statute's application. Liability for defense expenses exceeded ordinary litigation costs and created a new obligation. Security supplied a way to make that obligation effective before the corporation incurred the outlay. Treating the statute as merely procedural would disregard its practical and substantive operation. Id. at 555–556.

The historical Rule 23 regulated shareholder standing, verification, demand, collusion, and protection against improper dismissal or settlement. Those provisions neither imposed nor exempted the plaintiff from expense liability. A federal court could comply with both the Rule and the statute. The Court therefore applied York rather than treating silence about security as a contradiction of the state law. Id. at 556–557.

## Separate opinions

Douglas and Frankfurter viewed the security statute as regulating only the method of enforcing the corporation's existing cause of action. It did not alter the claim against the alleged wrongdoers. Douglas would let the federal procedural Rule govern instead of importing the state condition. This was a partial dissent directed to the diversity/procedure question, not a developed rejection of the Court's collateral-order analysis. Id. at 557.

Rutledge agreed with Douglas here and with Jackson in Woods, and also dissented in Ragan. He accepted Erie as an administrative rule for substantive state law but doubted its constitutional basis and objected to later decisions ceding control over federal litigation to states. He urged examination of the policies behind restrictions rather than a mechanical rule closing federal courts whenever state courts closed. His footnote contrasted Erie's original remand and later conflicts doctrine. Id. at 557–560 & dissent n.1.

For Rutledge, overlap between procedure and substance did not eliminate Congress' power over litigation practices. He regarded both Rule 23's shareholder-status requirement and New Jersey's bond requirement as having substantive aspects while remaining closely connected to conducting litigation. He would reverse the judgments in Cohen and Ragan and affirm in Woods. Id. at 560–561.

## Notes

The two assigned accounts concern different holdings in the same decision and belong in one comprehensive brief. The Erie account emphasizes expenses and compatibility with the federal Rule; the later account emphasizes immediate review of the collateral right. The latter quotes language from official pp. 546–547 but gives a U.S. Reports pinpoint of “545.” Use pp. 545–547 for the whole appealability discussion, with pp. 546–547 for the reproduced passage. Casebook pp. 461–462, 1223–1224.

The opinion discussed the then-existing Rule 23. The textbook's reference to what is now Rule 23.1 identifies the later organizational counterpart; it does not mean the 1949 opinion named Rule 23.1. All six Court footnotes and Rutledge's footnote are part of the complete source. The shared dissent also belongs in the Ragan and Woods readings.
