# Greater Boston Television Corp. v. Federal Communications Commission
444 F.2d 841 (D.C. Cir. 1970) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and the licensing dispute
The D.C. Circuit affirmed the FCC's selection of Boston Broadcasters, Inc. (BBI), over WHDH, Charles River Civic Television, and Greater Boston Television Corp. for Boston's Channel 5. Leventhal wrote for a unanimous panel with Tamm and MacKinnon; there are no separately written judicial opinions. The reported decision was issued November 13, 1970, amended December 31, and modified when rehearing was denied February 16, 1971. The opinion's disposition ends at report page 863. Its influential discussion of judicial review arose from a sixteen-year licensing contest involving compromised procedures, temporary operating authority, an examiner's contrary recommendation, and evolving licensing policies. (843–845, 850–853, 863.)

The FCC initially awarded WHDH, owned by the Boston Herald-Traveler's corporate publisher, the license in 1957. During judicial review, possible improper contacts between WHDH's Robert Choate and FCC Chairman McConnaughey prompted an evidentiary remand. Choate had arranged luncheons and tried to present a proposed legislative amendment affecting the Commission's media-diversification policy while the licensing adjudication was pending. The special examiner considered the contacts insufficient to taint the grant. The FCC disagreed: it found an attempted pattern of influence, treated the grant as voidable rather than void from inception, and proposed to set it aside, permit temporary broadcasting, and reopen comparative consideration. The court approved that course. (845–846.)

The FCC again selected WHDH in 1962 but limited its operating license to four months because of its incursions on fair adjudication. When WHDH sought renewal, the FCC allowed new competing applications within a designated two-month period. Choate died while another appeal was pending; the court remanded to determine the effect of his death and permitted consolidation with the upcoming comparative proceedings. The ensuing hearing involved WHDH, BBI, Charles River, and Greater Boston. The examiner recommended WHDH, treating its generally favorable actual operating record as more reliable than newcomers' promises and treating Choate's death as removing the need for a continuing misconduct disability. (846–847.)

## What the agency decided and the parties contested
In January 1969 the FCC instead awarded the license to BBI. Using its comparative-policy criteria, it found WHDH's performance favorable but only average, not exceptional enough to earn an affirmative preference. It favored the newcomers' diversification of media control and BBI's greater proposed participation of owners in management. Both BBI and Charles River received slight, offsetting programming demerits: BBI inadequately substantiated an unusually ambitious local-live-program proposal; Charles River's ownership by a charitable foundation raised possible restrictions on editorializing about legislative questions. Greater Boston separately failed preliminary requirements concerning community-needs evaluation and its antenna site. WHDH also received a demerit for unapproved transfers of de facto control upon changes of president. (847–849 & nn. 1–3.)

The FCC's voting division mattered to a later quorum challenge. Four of seven commissioners participated; three voted for BBI and one for WHDH. Commissioner Johnson expressed little passion between the newcomers, but concurred to constitute a working majority. On reconsideration the FCC clarified that WHDH's unusual history distinguished it from an ordinary renewal applicant. A 1970 policy statement retained ordinary renewal protection for solid, substantial public service but excluded unusual cases, commonly involving judicial remands, in which the incumbent should be compared as a new applicant. (849–850, 854–855.)

WHDH argued that its actual license entitled it to ordinary renewal treatment, that the new-applicant criteria imposed an unlawful forfeiture, and that ignoring broadcast stability violated due process and legitimate expectations. It also challenged diversification reasoning and the control-transfer demerit. Charles River challenged the vote and BBI's largely prospective integration claim, which it called unreliable. Greater Boston sought to confine consideration to the earlier record and opposed a fresh comparative hearing. (853–855, 859–863 & n. 44.)

## Judicial supervision, restraint, and reasoned decisions
The court described review as enforcement of reasonable procedure, substantial evidence, and rational support for ultimate inferences, with allowance for witness observation and agency expertise. Expertise does not excuse an unreasoned decision or turn deference into judicial inertia. Agencies have policy latitude within their statutory mandate, but must consider material facts and issues, articulate their reasons, and identify the crucial facts. Comparative findings must address substantial differences and support a composite assessment of the applicants. (850–851.)

A combination of warning signs may justify intervention when the agency has failed to take a hard look or engage in reasoned decisionmaking. Once that task has been performed, however, a court should restrain itself even if it would have reached different findings or adopted different standards. It may uphold a sufficiently discernible agency path despite imperfect clarity and apply harmless error to immaterial defects, but cannot guess at missing findings or reasons. Note 16 cautions that harmless-error doctrine must be used particularly carefully when basic procedural rights are implicated. (851–852 & n. 16.)

Leventhal described agencies and courts as partners in the administrative process. That relationship supports, rather than dilutes, the demand for articulated standards and reflective findings that constrain whim, improper influence, and irrelevant preferences. Reasoned explanation is required even in difficult selections among closely qualified applicants. When policy changes, the agency must show that it deliberately changed its prior standards instead of silently ignoring them. Here the prior contacts, evolving policies, and departure from the examiner's recommendation called for vigilance; the agency nevertheless had examined the problem and explained its permissible course. The examiner's exhaustive analysis strengthened the record even though the FCC rejected his ultimate recommendation. Agency departures require attentive consideration, but where each permissible outcome has substantial support, the agency makes the final choice. (852–853.)

## Application to WHDH's unusual renewal
The court did not resolve whether the FCC could generally strip ordinary renewal applicants of legitimate renewal expectations, nor decide the full validity of the 1970 renewal policy. The reconsideration decision limited this case to WHDH's unusual history. The FCC reasonably treated that history as warranting fresh comparative consideration rather than the usual preference arising from satisfactory prior service. It retained latitude to protect its processes even though the attempted influence was subtle and the chairman rejected the proposed brief. (853–857.)

WHDH had not been expressly told that its renewal would be judged precisely like a new application. Nonetheless, the absence of that announcement did not restrict the proof it could offer or contest: each applicant had a full opportunity to present programming, integration, diversification, past performance, and other fitness evidence. The original misconduct supported a renewed contest rather than either absolute disqualification or complete disregard of the misconduct. Choate's death did not eliminate a deterrence rationale concerning the enterprise's associates and investors, but the final comparative hearing imposed no continuing misconduct demerit. WHDH's actual operation remained relevant; the FCC simply declined to let temporary operation create a built-in lead over stronger newcomers. Its four-month license did not transform it into a conventional renewal applicant. The unusual intermediate treatment was within reasonable discretion. (857–859 & n. 38.)

Diversification remained a permissible public-interest criterion. Fairness regulation did not eliminate the distinct value of independently selected editorial topics and reportorial attention. The court nevertheless questioned the FCC's reliance on the Herald-Traveler's delaying broadcast of a newspaper scoop; any error was minimal in the overall comparison. The control-transfer demerit presented difficult notice and remedy questions, but supported doctrine and the demerit's cumulative importance made further refinement unnecessary here. The opinion did not establish that every such demerit, regardless of notice or effect, would be proper. (859–861.)

## Charles River, remaining applicants, and judgment
Four participating commissioners supplied a quorum and three supplied a majority; Johnson could legitimately vote for BBI to prevent an impasse. His lack of enthusiasm did not nullify his vote, and he later voted without reservation to reject reconsideration. Between Charles River and BBI, the decisive distinction was owner participation in station operation. The FCC identified six BBI stockholders and their proposed functions; neither insincerity nor impracticability had been found. Although the examiner expressed doubts and the programming proposal received a demerit, the FCC reasonably treated that defect as insufficient to vitiate the proposal or the separate integration advantage. Substantial evidence supported its selection. (861–863.)

The lengthy note 43 expressly withholds approval of the FCC's reasoning about Charles River's tax-related editorial restrictions. The FCC could have sought authoritative IRS guidance and interagency coordination; any correction would have been minor compared with BBI's strong integration preference. Note 44 rejects Greater Boston's demand to limit proceedings to the original record. The court affirmed the Commission's order, preserving these qualifications rather than approving every detail of its reasoning. (862–863 & nn. 43–44.)
