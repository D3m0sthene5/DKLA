# Tunick v. Safir — certification, refusal, and renewed injunction

**209 F.3d 67 (2d Cir. 2000), March 24, 2000, dissent filed April 13; 94 N.Y.2d 709 (2000), May 12; 228 F.3d 135 (2d Cir. 2000), May 19, further concurrence and dissent July 11.** These are three distinct decisions in one controversy, not parallel citations for one judgment. The assigned account is CivPro physical pages 280–281, printed page 525, Note 5. It cites the last federal decision while describing the earlier certification and state refusal.

Spencer Tunick, an internationally recognized photographer, proposed arranging seventy-five to one hundred nude models in an abstract formation on Madison Street in lower Manhattan, early on Sunday, July 18, 1999. His two permit applications were identical except that one used nude models and the other clothed models. New York City approved the clothed shoot and refused the nude one. Earlier nude sessions had produced five arrests, with charges ultimately dismissed. For the proposed event, the actual photography would last no more than five minutes within a 5:30–6:30 a.m. period. (209 F.3d at 68–69.)

Tunick sued on July 13 for preliminary and permanent relief. Judge Harold Baer granted a preliminary injunction on July 16 preventing interference with the shoot, finding both protected artistic expression and a statutory exemption. The Second Circuit stayed that injunction the next day, expedited the appeal, and heard argument in September. New York Penal Law §§ 245.01 and 245.02 prohibited public exposure and its promotion but excepted persons entertaining or performing in a play, exhibition, show, or entertainment. The City argued that the exception required an indoor performance before an audience. On appeal it abandoned reliance on its licensing system and time, place, or manner restrictions; the preliminary injunction had not compelled issuance of a permit. (209 F.3d at 69–71.)

The appeal therefore turned on a disputed state statute preceding a possible federal constitutional question. The City favored certification; Tunick argued that the exception clearly covered his artistic arrangement. Two municipal-court decisions pointed in different directions, and no controlling New York appellate decision resolved the question. New York City had not adopted the additional local anti-nudity legislation that the state statutes permitted. (209 F.3d at 70–72 & nn.4–6.)

## March 24: federal certification

Judges Calabresi and Sack agreed to certify, but for markedly different reasons; Judge Van Graafeiland dissented. The court asked whether this arrangement qualified for the statutory exception; whether an applicable exception was limited to indoor activity; and, if the statute prohibited the shoot, whether it was valid under New York's Constitution. It retained jurisdiction and left open reconsideration of the stay if delay or changed circumstances demanded immediate action. It did **not** decide the statutory merits or finally resolve the First Amendment claim. (209 F.3d at 89–90.)

Calabresi drew from *Pullman* abstention, *Arizonans for Official English*, and constitutional avoidance. His six considerations were absence of authoritative state interpretation; importance and recurrence of the issue; serious constitutional problems a construction might avoid; certification's ability to resolve or sharpen the litigation; implications for core state sovereignty; and the effect of delay on the asserted right. Those were **Calabresi's reasoning**, not a unanimously adopted panel test: footnote 7 expressly warns that the relevant analysis did not necessarily represent his colleagues' views. He also distinguished constitutional litigation from certification in diversity cases. (209 F.3d at 72 n.7; 74 n.9; 81–82.)

He found that plausible statutory constructions could avoid serious concerns about expressive nudity and seemingly arbitrary distinctions between photography and other exhibitions. Certification would either end the litigation or make the federal question definite. In context, repeated First Amendment licensing litigation also risked making federal courts de facto local permitting agencies. He recognized that delay can irreparably damage speech, and that lifting the stay might moot the certified dispute. Still, Tunick's continuing interest in a future shoot and the prospect of authoritative guidance persuaded him that certification was the least harmful course. Subsequent arrests might not yield a definitive state interpretation because previous charges had been dropped. (209 F.3d at 82–89.)

Sack rejected that balance. In his view, physically preventing expression under an unclear statute presented the same censorship danger as an unguided licensing scheme. A city could regulate public nudity through sufficiently clear laws and valid procedures, but had not defended such a mechanism here. An arrest **after** the photographs were taken would present different concerns and could permit state litigation; Tunick had conceded that his suit sought protection from prior restraint, rather than immunity from later prosecution. Sack thought every unnecessary moment of prior restraint constitutionally intolerable. Nevertheless, because neither colleague supported his desired immediate relief, he joined certification as the fastest practicable route to a decision, without endorsing Calabresi's framework. (209 F.3d at 90–96 & nn.1–10.)

Van Graafeiland would dismiss as moot, without prejudice to a new properly scheduled shoot. He read the complaint and injunction as confined to July 18, a date already past, and criticized certification's delays, burdens, and introduction of an unraised state constitutional question. Calabresi rejected that mootness position because the intended photograph and threatened arrests remained live and the appellate stay had prevented the event. The opinions also disagreed about the court's internal decisional chronology; those competing accounts are not an independently established finding of misconduct. (209 F.3d at 69–70 n.3; 96–100; 228 F.3d at 138 n.1.)

## May 12: New York declines

The New York Court of Appeals unanimously declined acceptance under its discretionary certification rule. Chief Judge Kaye and Judges Bellacosa, Smith, Levine, Ciparick, Wesley, and Rosenblatt joined the per curiam opinion. It acknowledged certification's general value but explained why this request was exceptional. Additional briefing, argument, and deliberation would add months to an already delayed prior-restraint controversy. The federal court could lift its stay during that process, allowing the shoot and potentially mooting the questions. The parties also had never sought, briefed, or argued relief under New York's Constitution; the court would not responsibly decide that newly introduced question. These concerns together outweighed certification's ordinary benefits. The court **declined the questions without answering them**; it did not approve or condemn the photography, the statute, or the federal injunction. (94 N.Y.2d at 709–712.)

## May 19: federal relief resumes

The Second Circuit's per curiam decision then held that the preliminary injunction was not an abuse of discretion because Tunick had shown irreparable injury and a clear likelihood of success. It dissolved the stay and remanded for consultation over a new date and reentry of a limited early-morning, nonbusiness-day injunction preventing arrests or interference **until the photography was completed**. The district court could impose conditions minimizing neighborhood intrusion. Relief was qualified if intervening law had made the intended activity clearly illegal. It did not bar subsequent arrests or establish a final right to engage in all public nudity. Calabresi and Sack concurred; Van Graafeiland dissented. (228 F.3d at 136–137.)

Calabresi's July concurrence supplied a state-law rationale. With certification declined, a federal court should make its best prediction rather than automatically decide the federal constitutional issue. Applying New York's plain-meaning and avoidance principles, he found a clear likelihood that the arrangement was an exhibition and that the City could not add indoor or audience requirements absent from the text. He did not decide whether all public-nudity bans were unconstitutional or eliminate reasonable time, place, and manner regulation. The preliminary prediction did not bind New York courts or preclude later prosecution through which they could give an authoritative answer. Sack concurred for the prior-restraint reasons in his earlier opinion. (228 F.3d at 137–142 & nn.1–6.)

Van Graafeiland's July dissent added that the shoot had occurred June 4 at a different location, making the Madison Street dispute moot in his view. He would dismiss the appeal and remand to dismiss the complaint, and criticized treating divergent reasoning as precedential per curiam authority. That was the dissent's position, not the panel's mandate. (228 F.3d at 142–143.)

The assignment's refusal-of-permit description accurately reflects the initial facts, but the litigated appellate theory was statutory exposure and prior restraint after the City abandoned licensing arguments. The complete sequence shows certification's discretion, constitutional timing concerns, and the limits of state-law prediction. It does not establish that every certified question must be answered or that Calabresi's six considerations commanded a panel majority. This brief describes these historical decisions without a later-law certification. Independent-review status appears in the accompanying register.
