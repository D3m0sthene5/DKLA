# Lively v. Wild Oats Markets, Inc.
456 F.3d 933 (9th Cir. 2006) | Civil Procedure

## Court, question, and precise judgment

On July 27, 2006, the Ninth Circuit decided No. 04-56682. Judge Paez wrote for a unanimous panel with Judge Hawkins and District Judge Wake, sitting by designation. The court held that removing a diversity action in violation of the forum-defendant rule created a waivable, nonjurisdictional defect. A plaintiff had to seek remand on that ground within thirty days; the violation did not authorize a district court to order remand on its own. Because the district court's remand exceeded its statutory authority, the appellate-review bar did not prevent review. The court vacated the remand order and returned the case for further proceedings. It did not decide the underlying slip-and-fall claim. There were no separate opinions. (Court PDF physical pp. 1–4, 15.)

The assigned account says the Ninth Circuit reversed. The court's express closing mandate is more precise: the order was vacated and the case remanded. References here use the physical pages of the complete court-issued PDF; its printed court pagination is not the bound Federal Reporter pagination.

## How a local defendant reached federal court

Emma C. Lively filed a personal-injury action in state court on September 26, 2003, alleging a slip-and-fall accident at a California Wild Oats store. Wild Oats removed on January 8, 2004. Its notice asserted that it was incorporated in Delaware, had its principal place of business in Colorado, and faced a New York citizen's claim exceeding $75,000. Lively did not object. (PDF pp. 4–5.)

Approximately eight months later, after discovery and a defense summary-judgment motion, the district court asked why it should not remand: Wild Oats' principal place of business appeared to be California rather than Colorado. Wild Oats argued that even California citizenship would leave the parties diverse because Lively was a New York citizen. Being a local defendant would violate the additional removal restriction, but would not eliminate original diversity jurisdiction. With the thirty-day remand-motion period expired, Wild Oats maintained that the court lacked authority to send the case back. (PDF pp. 3, 5.)

The district court nevertheless treated the forum-defendant restriction as jurisdictional and remanded under § 1447(c). On appeal Wild Oats did not contest its California citizenship. The question therefore was not where its principal place of business actually lay, nor whether Lively and Wild Oats shared citizenship. It was the legal consequence of a local defendant's removal in an otherwise diverse case. The court reviewed the statutory interpretation and remand-jurisdiction questions de novo. (PDF pp. 3, 5–6, 9.)

## Original jurisdiction and the additional removal restriction

The then-current § 1441(b) barred diversity removal when a properly joined and served defendant was a citizen of the state where the action was brought. Note 2 reproduces that statutory formulation. The panel distinguished this extra limitation on removal from § 1332's requirements for original diversity jurisdiction. A California defendant sued by a New York plaintiff could be diverse even though California was the forum and removal violated § 1441(b). (PDF pp. 3 & n. 2, 9.)

The terminology required care. Note 4 acknowledged that the rule had a substantive aspect concerning a party's citizenship and did not fit ordinary filing-and-service mechanics neatly. The opinion nevertheless used procedural and nonjurisdictional interchangeably because removal doctrine commonly contrasted that category with defects in subject-matter jurisdiction. The holding did not dismiss the restriction as inconsequential: a timely plaintiff's objection would preserve the statutory protection. It classified the defect for purposes of waiver and remand authority. (PDF p. 4 & n. 4.)

The Ninth Circuit had not previously resolved this precise classification. Note 3 distinguished Spencer, where a local but diverse defendant was joined after removal. That case involved no violation of the rule at the time of removal, so its outcome did not settle Lively's question. (PDF p. 4 & n. 3.)

## Why the remand order could be reviewed

Lively argued that § 1447(d) foreclosed appellate examination because the district court called its order a jurisdictional remand under § 1447(c). The panel refused to treat that label as conclusive. Under Thermtron and subsequent authority, §§ 1447(c) and (d) had to be read together: the review bar protected remand orders within the authority supplied by subsection (c), rather than every order citing it. The opinion preserved the distinction between examining the existence of remand power and reviewing an erroneous exercise of genuine statutory power. (PDF pp. 6–8 & nn. 5–6.)

Section 1447(c) required a motion based on a defect other than lack of subject-matter jurisdiction within thirty days after the removal notice. If subject-matter jurisdiction was lacking, remand was required whenever that deficiency appeared before final judgment. Whether the forum-defendant restriction belonged to the first or second category therefore determined both the district court's power and the appellate court's review authority. The Ninth Circuit had jurisdiction to decide its own jurisdiction and could resolve the intertwined statutory question. (PDF pp. 6–8.)

Note 7 identified § 1291 as the affirmative basis for appellate jurisdiction: although no judgment on Lively's injury claim existed, the remand order effectively ended the federal proceeding. The decision did not establish a general entitlement to appeal all remand orders whenever a losing party disagreed with a court's jurisdictional analysis. (PDF p. 8 & nn. 6–7.)

## Statutory history placed this defect within the waiver rule

The panel traced § 1447(c)'s wording from the original requirement to remand a case removed improvidently and without jurisdiction, through the 1988 thirty-day deadline for defects in removal procedure, to the 1996 deadline covering any defect other than lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The middle formulation left uncertainty about restrictions that were substantive but did not destroy original jurisdiction. The 1996 change addressed that uncertainty by making such nonjurisdictional defects subject to the thirty-day limit. (PDF pp. 9–10 & n. 8.)

Consequently, the rule's concern with citizenship rather than filing mechanics did not remove it from the waiver category. Congress had selected the broader distinction between original subject-matter power and other removal defects. A litigant could not avoid the deadline merely by observing that a restriction had substantive significance. (PDF pp. 10–11.)

The policy reinforced that interpretation. Diversity removal protects out-of-state defendants against potential local prejudice. A defendant already a citizen of the forum ordinarily lacks that concern. The forum-defendant restriction consequently gives the plaintiff a choice: move promptly to return to state court or accept the federal forum by doing nothing. Treating the restriction as jurisdictional would permit a judge to remand over both parties' preference to remain, undermining rather than preserving the plaintiff's control. (PDF p. 11.)

## Other circuits and the limited significance of Grubbs

At the time of this decision, eight of the nine other circuits addressing the issue treated the rule as nonjurisdictional; the Eighth Circuit's Hurt took the contrary approach. The panel rejected the proposition that every statutory limit on removal necessarily removed original subject-matter jurisdiction. Note 9 compared the circuit's treatment of another statutory nonremovability restriction; note 10 identified further criticism of Hurt. These are the authorities and circuit alignment the 2006 opinion reported, not a certification of today's alignment. (PDF pp. 11–13 & nn. 9–10.)

The district court had also relied on a district decision reading Grubbs as curing a forum-defendant violation only after a merits judgment. The panel disagreed. Grubbs asked whether a federal court would have had original jurisdiction when a removed case reached judgment without an objection, rather than whether removal itself had been proper. Its distinction between the route into federal court and original jurisdiction supported the nonjurisdictional characterization. (PDF pp. 13–15.)

That precedent did not mean an otherwise waivable restriction remained jurisdictional until trial. Note 11 explained that the circuit also applied Grubbs to merits summary judgment. Note 12 reasoned that distinguishing complete silence from a late objection could not save Hurt's jurisdictional premise: either way, an actual lack of subject-matter jurisdiction would require judicial attention. The panel rejected the district court's proposed pretrial exception. Note 13 identified another district decision accepting the nonjurisdictional view. (PDF pp. 13–15 & nn. 11–13.)

## Result and assigned limits

Lively had not timely objected to the forum-defendant violation. The district court therefore exceeded § 1447(c)'s authority by ordering remand on its own. The appellate court could review that unauthorized order, vacated it, and remanded for further proceedings. The parties' diversity remained distinct from the defect in the manner of removal; no negligence or damages issue was adjudicated. (PDF p. 15.)
