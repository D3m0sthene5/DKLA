# State of California Public Employees’ Retirement System v. Shearman & Sterling
95 N.Y.2d 427, 741 N.E.2d 101, 718 N.Y.S.2d 256 (2000) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

The New York Court of Appeals decided the case on November 16, 2000. Judge Wesley wrote; Chief Judge Kaye and Judges Smith, Levine, and Ciparick concurred, and Judge Rosenblatt took no part. The court affirmed, with costs, an Appellate Division order dismissing the complaint in its entirety. This pleading-stage disposition rejected both CalPERS’s direct claims against a law firm retained by its commercial counterparty and its claims as that counterparty’s purported assignee. It did not conduct a malpractice damages trial or award the alleged acceleration-fee shortfall. (427, 433–436.)

## Transaction and alleged drafting loss

In 1988, CalPERS and Equitable Real Estate Investment Management entered a correspondent agreement under which Equitable originated and closed commercial property loans for sale and assignment to CalPERS. Equitable retained lawyers for the transactions. In August 1993 CalPERS approved purchase of a $23.3 million long-term loan to Nathan Serota, whose commitment was subsequently assigned to Sersons Corporation. Equitable retained Shearman & Sterling to negotiate and close the loan. CalPERS and Equitable had developed standard forms, including a note with prepayment and acceleration provisions. CalPERS alleged that Equitable requested use of that standard note. The firm sent the draft to CalPERS and its own counsel, accompanied by a letter identifying black-lined changes required by New York law or negotiated with the borrower. The acceleration clause was among the changed provisions. CalPERS did not object. (431–432.)

Sersons executed the note in November 1994. The following month Equitable assigned its rights under the loan documents to CalPERS, without recourse or warranties except those in the correspondent agreement. After Sersons defaulted, CalPERS accelerated the loan and allegedly discovered that the note produced an acceleration fee of approximately $1.1 million instead of approximately $9.1 million under the standard form. Sersons paid $1.1 million in March 1997. The alleged drafting defect and shortfall are the premise of the suit, not a judicial finding that malpractice caused an awarded $8 million loss. (432.)

Before suing the firm, CalPERS settled with Equitable, which paid $400,000. The settlement recited that the earlier assignment was intended to include malpractice and contract claims against Shearman & Sterling and expressly assigned any such rights not previously transferred. The opinion’s sole note records CalPERS’s concession that the payment was unrelated to this litigation and was made to preserve and advance the parties’ business relationship; it was not treated as proof that Equitable suffered the particular malpractice injury. (432–433; 432 sole note.)

## Procedural history and competing theories

CalPERS asserted professional-negligence and contract claims as assignee and direct claims based on a relationship approaching privity and intended-third-party-beneficiary status. Supreme Court dismissed the direct claims but allowed the assigned claims: the original omnibus assignment was insufficient to transfer malpractice claims, yet the later settlement assignment expressly did so. The Appellate Division dismissed the entire complaint. It agreed that the first assignment was too narrow and concluded that the later assignment could not convey an actionable malpractice claim because Equitable had received the full benefit of its bargain and suffered no injury from the reduced acceleration payment. The Court of Appeals affirmed that result. (433.)

## Near-privity and reliance

The court required actual privity or a relationship sufficiently close to privity for a tort claim based on negligent misrepresentation causing pecuniary loss. Near-privity required awareness that a statement would be used for a particular purpose, reliance by a known party for that purpose, and conduct linking the maker to the relying party and showing an understanding of that reliance. The firm’s only direct preclosing contact was its request that CalPERS and its counsel review and approve the marked-up documents. (434.)

CalPERS had retained final approval for itself and its own lawyers and received a version showing the changed terms. Its failure to object did not establish that the firm understood CalPERS would rely on the note without reviewing it. The complaint and documents therefore did not satisfy the required linking conduct and understood reliance. The decision does not make all communications from counterparty counsel irrelevant; it evaluates the particular request for review and the plaintiff’s retained approval role. (434.)

## Intended beneficiary and potentially opposed interests

Third-party enforcement required a valid contract between others, intent to benefit the claimant, and a benefit sufficiently immediate to show that the contracting parties assumed a duty to compensate the claimant if it was lost. Equitable’s retainer was a valid contract, but it was not intended to impose a legal-service duty to CalPERS. The correspondent agreement explicitly treated Equitable’s agents as independent, not CalPERS’s agents, and required CalPERS’s own counsel to approve closing documents. (434–435.)

Although the program enabled CalPERS’s investments, the parties did not invariably share interests. The firm represented Equitable in satisfying the correspondent agreement while protecting Equitable’s interests under New York law. CalPERS stood on the other side of that commercial agreement. The indirect economic benefit of accurate transaction documents did not establish that the retainer assumed an immediate duty to this nonclient. This is the principle emphasized in the assigned account: a common transaction does not erase potential conflicts between a lawyer’s client and the claimed beneficiary. (435.)

## Assignment scope and absence of assignor injury

The word all in the omnibus assignment did not transfer every claim related to the broader loan transaction. Its object was Equitable’s rights in and under the loan documents between Equitable and Sersons. It carried rights against the borrower arising from those documents, but not the alleged malpractice claim concerning the firm’s failure to prepare documents in accordance with the separate correspondent agreement. Thus the court construed the assignment’s specified subject matter, rather than announcing that legal malpractice claims can never be assigned. (435–436.)

The settlement’s express residual assignment was broader, but still could not transfer a claim Equitable did not possess. CalPERS had paid Equitable in full, with no discount attributable to the alleged acceleration-clause defect. Equitable had already transferred the note and had no remaining right to the default or acceleration payment when Sersons defaulted. The reduced fee therefore caused Equitable no injury, leaving it without the asserted malpractice claim to assign. Express language identifying a cause of action cannot create its missing substantive element. (436.)

## Disposition and reading scope

The order dismissing all claims was affirmed with costs. CalPERS failed to establish either its own enforceable relationship with Shearman & Sterling or an assigned viable claim belonging to Equitable. The court’s result leaves distinct the questions of nonclient duty, the language defining an assignment, and whether the assignor actually possessed the claim purportedly transferred. (433–436.)
