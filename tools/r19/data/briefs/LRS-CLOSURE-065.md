# In re Hennen (Ex parte Hennen)
38 U.S. (13 Pet.) 230 (1839) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Removal of a court clerk and interruption of judicial business

Duncan N. Hennen became clerk of the federal district court in eastern Louisiana on February 21, 1834, through an appointment by Judge Samuel H. Harper. He accepted a commission, provided the required bond, and performed the office’s duties. When the circuit system was extended to Louisiana, federal law made the district clerk the circuit clerk as well. This added duties to the existing office; it did not require a separate appointment by the circuit court. (256–258.)

On May 18, 1838, the succeeding district judge, Philip K. Lawrence, informed Hennen that he had been removed and that John Winthrop had been appointed in his place. Lawrence acknowledged that Hennen had conducted the office promptly, skillfully, and uprightly. His letter explained that Winthrop was a close friend whose qualifications were unquestioned, and invoked feelings of kindness and a sense of duty. The Court recounted that explanation without deciding whether those motives represented a proper exercise of power. (256–257, 261–262.)

When the circuit court met on May 21, Justice John McKinley and Judge Lawrence disagreed about which claimant was the lawful clerk. Neither Hennen nor Winthrop was allowed to act; the court could transact no business and adjourned. Hennen maintained that he had neither resigned nor been legally removed. His renewed petition also described his own pending property litigation, involving more than $6,000, and argued that the institutional impasse blocked both ordinary proceedings and the Supreme Court’s appellate jurisdiction. The report identifies this as a renewed application after an earlier rule had been discharged, rather than an appeal from a merits judgment in the property suit. (230–231, 257.)

## Application and governing appointment law

Hennen sought a rule requiring Lawrence to show cause why mandamus should not compel restoration to the district clerkship. Lawrence appeared through counsel to oppose the motion, without denying the petition’s factual account. The immediate question was whether those facts entitled Hennen to the preliminary rule. If his removal was lawful, the requested proceeding had no foundation. Justice Thompson delivered the Court’s opinion. The report contains no separate judicial writing and does not supply a numerical vote. (256–257.)

The Appointments Clause permitted Congress to vest appointment of inferior officers in the President alone, courts of law, or department heads. The Court treated a clerk as an inferior officer whose appointment appropriately belonged to the courts. Section 7 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 authorized federal courts to appoint their respective clerks and made each district clerk the clerk of the corresponding circuit court. Louisiana’s 1804 and 1812 enactments vested appointment in its district judge, while the 1837 circuit legislation placed the Louisiana clerk on the same footing as other district clerks. Hennen’s circuit duties therefore did not insulate him from the district court’s appointment authority. (257–258.)

## Removal as an incident of appointment

The Constitution expressly gave federal judges good-behavior tenure, but provided no corresponding tenure for their clerks. Congress had fixed terms and expressly preserved removal at pleasure for certain other officers; that statute did not cover court clerks. Neither the Constitution nor an applicable statute limited this clerk’s tenure. The Court reasoned that an office without a fixed constitutional or statutory tenure must be held either for life through good-behavior tenure or subject to removal at some governmental department’s discretion. It rejected inferred lifetime tenure for inferior offices. In the absence of constitutional provision or statutory regulation, removal followed appointment authority. (258–259.)

Thompson supported that default with early governmental practice. The controversy surrounding the Decision of 1789 concerned whether the President alone, or President and Senate together, could remove officers appointed with Senate concurrence. The Court described the resulting practice and early department statutes as recognizing presidential removal of department heads. It also distinguished subordinate clerks appointed by department heads: those clerks served at the head’s discretion, and the Court stated that the President had no removal power over them. Its reasoning placed control with the legally designated appointing authority; it did not make this a case deciding a President’s power to remove every federal officer. (259–260.)

The opinion generalized the default to inferior officers whom Congress authorized the President alone, courts, or department heads to appoint. But its premise remained the absence of a governing constitutional or statutory tenure provision. Reading the assigned quotation apart from that premise would obscure the very qualification developed immediately before it. (259–260.)

## Historical offices and the effect of appointing a successor

Ancient English common-law offices could have tenure determined by usage. Newly created American offices had no comparable ancient tenure and depended on the governing constitutions and statutes. Even modern English statutory offices turned on legislative meaning. The Court used *Smyth v. Latham* as an example of statutory tenure at pleasure, and state decisions as examples of construing each jurisdiction’s own governing law. It distinguished *Hoke v. Henderson*: North Carolina’s express good-behavior tenure made that office materially different. These comparisons supported a default construction, rather than a rule overriding an express tenure protection. (260–261.)

Federal law prescribed no special form for appointing the clerk. Lawrence’s commission to Winthrop, recognition of him as clerk, and notice to Hennen sufficed. Appointment authority was continuing, and appointing a successor itself removed the incumbent as far as the incumbent’s rights were concerned: two incompatible holders could not occupy the same office. The Court expressly left any effect on third persons’ rights undecided. (261.)

## Judgment and limits

Because appointment belonged exclusively to the district court and this clerkship was held at its discretion, the Supreme Court could not control the appointment or removal or investigate its grounds through this proceeding. If Lawrence had abused his authority, this was not the tribunal to which he was answerable. The Court expressly abstained from passing on that aspect of the controversy. It denied the motion; the formal judgment overruled the motion and denied the requested mandamus or rule. It did not issue a restoration order or adjudicate Hennen’s separate property action. (261–262.)
