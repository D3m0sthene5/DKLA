# Houseman v. Dare
405 N.J. Super. 538, 966 A.2d 24 (App. Div. 2009) | Contracts

## Decision and significance

The New Jersey Appellate Division held that a pet's classification as personal property does not categorically foreclose specific performance of an agreement governing its ownership and possession. Money may fail to compensate a party for sincere affection and attachment to a particular animal. The court reversed the dog-related part of the judgment and remanded for findings about the alleged oral agreement and the propriety of specific performance. It did not itself give Houseman the dog or finally determine that specific performance must issue. Judge Grall wrote for the panel of Judges Skillman, Graves, and Grall; the published report contains no separate opinion. (405 N.J. Super. at 539–540, 542–547.)

## Facts and litigation

Doreen Houseman and Eric Dare had a thirteen-year relationship. They bought a home as joint tenants in 1999, became engaged in 2000, and jointly purchased a pedigree dog for $1,500 in 2003. Both were listed as owners in the American Kennel Club registration. Dare ended the relationship in May 2006 and wanted to purchase Houseman's interest in their house. Houseman signed a deed in June, moved out on July 4, and took the dog and its belongings, leaving photographs and a jersey for Dare. (Id. at 540–541.)

Houseman testified that Dare had agreed she could keep the dog when they separated. Her main concern in their negotiations was possession of the pet; she also accepted his representation that $45,000 represented her half of the house equity. Dare acknowledged that she asked who would get the dog. He did not expressly deny agreeing she could have it, but maintained that the $45,000 house transaction did not depend on her receiving anything besides the money. No written dog agreement existed. According to Houseman, when she requested a writing Dare said she could trust him and he would not keep the dog from her. He admitted making that assurance in his answer but did not testify about it at trial. (Id. at 541.)

Houseman initially permitted visits during which Dare returned the dog afterward. She left the dog with him while vacationing in late February 2007, but he did not return it when she requested it on March 4. She sued on March 16. Dare retained possession when the case went to trial in December. Meanwhile, he had sold the house in December 2006; the parties stipulated that his equity proceeds exceeded $90,000. (Id. at 541–542.)

Before trial the Family Part ruled that pets were personal property lacking the uniqueness required for specific performance, and it limited the dog-related evidence accordingly. The parties stipulated to a $1,500 intrinsic monetary value. The judge found Houseman particularly credible and determined that Dare had taken unfair advantage of her in the house-equity transaction. As to the dog, the judge referred uncertainly to an understanding under which Houseman thought she would receive it, but awarded possession to Dare because he presently had it and awarded Houseman $1,500. The appellate court observed that those findings suggested an oral agreement, but lacked unmistakable clarity. Thus an agreement's existence remained a matter for further findings rather than an appellate fact conclusively established by the appeal. (Id. at 541–542.)

## Claims and issues

Houseman sought enforcement of the alleged oral agreement and a declaration of ownership. She challenged the pretrial rule that a dog could not support specific performance. Footnote 1 records a conversion theory, which the court did not separately analyze because the asserted rights depended entirely on the oral agreement and its specific enforcement would fully address a viable claim. Other property-compensation provisions were not challenged, and her intentional-infliction-of-emotional-distress claim was abandoned because she did not pursue it at trial or on appeal. (Id. at 539–540 & n.1.)

Two animal-law amici urged a best-interests-of-the-dog approach. The court instead addressed the parties' agreement, subjective interests, and ordinary equitable principles. Its holding neither adopted child-custody standards for pets nor announced a universal ownership rule based on who currently cared for the animal. (Id. at 540 n.2, 544–546.)

## Rule and reasoning

Specific performance is available for an enforceable agreement when damages inadequately protect the injured party's expectation and enforcement will not create inequity, reward unfair dealing, or conflict with public policy. A chattel's market price does not necessarily measure the value of possessing it. Equity already recognizes the special subjective benefits of heirlooms, family treasures, and art. Courts similarly consider those benefits when resolving wrongful detention or disputes about jointly owned property that cannot adequately be partitioned or sold. (Id. at 542–544.)

The court reasoned that sincere affection for a particular pet deserved no greater suspicion than sincere sentiment for an inanimate object associated with a donor or family member. The relevant sentiment must be grounded in facts and circumstances; greed, ill will, or an attachment assumed for litigation does not deserve equitable protection. Courts can assess the competing parties' interests and relevant public policies. The court expressed less confidence that manageable standards existed for deciding possession from the animal's own perspective, apart from abuse or neglect implicating animal-protection policies. That observation explained its treatment of the amici's proposal; it did not eliminate ordinary equitable relief concerning pets. (Id. at 544–546.)

Houseman's evidence required consideration of both the agreement and specific performance. Her testimony about the dog and prompt effort to regain possession supported an inference of special subjective value. The $1,500 stipulation fixed intrinsic monetary value, not the adequacy of damages for her attachment. Dare had not demonstrated that enforcement would oppress him, reward unfairness by Houseman, or violate public policy. Assuming the alleged agreement and breach, awarding him the dog simply because he retained it could reward the breach itself. Nevertheless, because the pretrial ruling had removed his incentive to present relevant equitable evidence, the appellate court left the ultimate decision to the trial court after further proceedings. (Id. at 546–547.)

## Exact result and assigned-account correction

The court affirmed the findings that the dog and house agreements were independent and the supported compensation determinations concerning the residence and joint savings account. It reversed and remanded the portion awarding Dare the dog and Houseman $1,500 for the pet. Further proceedings had to determine the existence of an oral agreement about ownership and possession and whether specific performance was appropriate. (Id. at 546–547.)
