Parties and requested relief
Fairmount Glass Works was the manufacturer and proposed seller of Mason fruit jars.
Crunden-Martin Woodenware Company was the commercial buyer seeking ten carloads of
jars. Crunden-Martin sued for damages after Fairmount refused to fill the order. The buyer
prevailed below, and the manufacturer appealed, disputing both formation and the
definiteness or conformity of the supposed acceptance. The opinion does not state the
amount of the trial judgment, so the brief does not supply one. 106 Ky. at 662–663.
Material facts
On April 20, 1895, Crunden-Martin asked Fairmount for its lowest price on an order of ten
carloads of Mason green jars, complete with caps and packed a dozen to a case. It requested
terms and a cash discount and allowed a price either delivered or free on board at the
manufacturer's location. The letter identified a proposed order and quantity; it was not
merely a request for a general catalogue. Id. at 662.
Fairmount replied April 23 with prices per gross: $4.50 for pints, $5 for quarts, and $6.50 for
half gallons. It specified delivery in East St. Louis, shipment no later than May 15, a
credit-payment alternative, and a two-percent cash discount if paid within ten days.
Critically, the quotation was stated to be for immediate acceptance. Fairmount also included
a reservation concerning agency or transportation contingencies, delays, and accidents
beyond its control. Id. at 663.
On April 24 the buyer telegraphed that the seller should enter an order for ten carloads
according to the quotation and that specifications had been mailed. Fairmount replied that it
could not book the order because its output was already sold. The buyer's mailed
confirmation repeated the prices, payment terms, shipment deadline, and delivery location. It
requested jars complete with caps and rubbers, described jars and caps as strictly first
quality, and specified the first carload's assortment: five gross pints, fifty-five gross quarts,
and forty gross half gallons. Specifications for the remaining nine carloads would follow. Id.
at 663, 665.
The record established a trade meaning for a carload: one hundred gross. Thus ten carloads
meant one thousand gross. The seller's refusal preceded its receipt of the mailed

Procedural history
Crunden-Martin obtained judgment for breach of contract in the trial court. Fairmount
appealed, relying on decisions that treat price quotations as invitations for orders rather than
offers. It also asserted that the buyer had added a quality requirement, failed to fix quantities
of the individual jar sizes, used an indefinite total quantity, and improperly contemplated
deliveries on different dates. The appellate court addressed these points and affirmed. Id. at
663–666.
Issues
Did the April 23 response create an offer, despite using the word “quote,” when read
together with the buyer's inquiry and the invitation to immediate acceptance? Did the April
24 telegram accept that offer? Did the mailed specifications materially change the proposed
bargain or reveal indefiniteness that prevented enforcement?
Rule and holdings
A price communication's legal effect depends on the whole exchange and its language, not its
label. Here the targeted response to a stated ten-carload inquiry, coupled with the
immediate-acceptance language, was a definite offer to sell on the specified terms. The
buyer's immediate response closed the bargain, and the seller could not then withdraw it. Id.
at 664–665.
The asserted differences and omissions did not defeat the contract on this record. Trade
evidence supported treating the buyer's quality wording as equivalent to the seller's
description. Trade usage made the total quantity definite, and the offer permitted the buyer
to select the mix of sizes and delivery dates within the stated shipment period. Id. at 666.
Actual judgment
The judgment for Crunden-Martin was affirmed. The court did not merely remand to
determine whether the quotation might have been an offer. Its determination sustained the
buyer's existing recovery. Any pleading defect was treated as cured by the judgment and the
supporting evidence. Id.
Reasoning
Reading the response in the setting created by the inquiry
The seller cited a familiar rule: a quotation often invites the recipient to submit an order,
which the seller remains free to accept or reject. Hobson accepted that such cases exist but
insisted that each turns substantially on the language used. Crunden-Martin asked what
Fairmount would charge on a specific order for ten carloads. Fairmount's reply was intended
to answer that inquiry. Reading its prices as unrelated general information would detach the
response from the question it answered. Id. at 664.

The immediate-acceptance wording then supplied a further indication of commitment. It
invited a responsive act capable of making a bargain now. If the seller still intended an
unrestricted right to reconsider after the order arrived, that invitation would be difficult to
explain. The combination of the initial inquiry and the seller's response therefore supported
formation in a way that a generic price circular might not. Id. at 664–665.
This reasoning does not make every use of “immediate acceptance” conclusive. It identifies
that phrase's force in this exchange. Nor does it declare the word “quote” meaningless. It
gives the word its contextually appropriate role instead of treating it as a talisman that
invariably prevents formation.
The telegram completed the bargain before the seller's refusal
The telegram accepted the specified order on the quoted terms. The seller's message that its
output was sold came in response. Once the buyer accepted the outstanding offer, the seller's
later unwillingness or inability to book the order was not a revocation of an unaccepted
proposal. It was a refusal to perform an existing obligation. Id. at 665.
The opinion does not base the result on a detailed mailbox-rule analysis or announce when
every telegram becomes effective. Its focus is that this immediate acceptance closed the
contract before the seller could withdraw. A brief should not invent a transmission-time
dispute that the reported reasoning does not decide.
The quality phrase was addressed through meaning and the actual record
The buyer's specifications included a first-quality requirement not stated in those exact
words in the seller's response. Fairmount characterized that as a variance, which under the
applicable common-law approach could prevent acceptance as made. The court did not
simply excuse a material new condition because the parties were merchants. The decision
predates the Uniform Commercial Code. Id. at 665–666.
Instead, the court relied on evidence that the expressions meant the same thing in the
parties' trade. The timing and content of Fairmount's objections reinforced that evidence: it
had already refused before receiving the specifications and had not complained about the
phrase during the ensuing correspondence. Those circumstances supported interpreting the
letter as elaborating the agreed description, rather than imposing a new bargain. Id. at 666.
The safe lesson is that different words need not create different terms. The case does not
establish a general rule that a party loses every unmentioned formation defense by initially
giving a different reason for nonperformance. Its conclusion depends on the trade evidence
and conduct actually before it.
The contract could allocate later selections without leaving agreement incomplete
Trade usage supplied a definite aggregate quantity of one thousand gross. The quoted
alternatives for different jar sizes gave the buyer a choice within that quantity. The offer's
shipment deadline likewise permitted the buyer to name dates before May 15. These were
later exercises of choices authorized by the bargain, not unresolved negotiations requiring
fresh assent by both sides. Id.

That distinction is useful beyond this case. A contract can leave a performance detail for one

Study synthesis and limits
Fairmount and Owen should be compared at the commitment stage. In Owen, a minimum
acceptable price did not itself commit the owner to sell. Here the specific inquiry and the
invitation to immediate acceptance supported a present offer. That comparison does not
require different rules for land and goods; it turns principally on what the communications
conveyed.
The case also shows why formation and interpretation often overlap. Trade meaning helped
decide whether the buyer accepted the same bargain and whether its quantity was definite.
Modern UCC analysis may address similar exchanges through additional provisions, but
importing § 2-207 into Hobson's actual holding would be anachronistic. The brief preserves
the court's own route to affirmance while using the comparison as separately identified study
analysis.
