# Lawrence v. Fox
20 N.Y. 268 (N.Y. 1859) | Contracts

## Facts and procedural history
In November 1857, Holly loaned $300 to Fox at Fox's request. Holly stated that he owed that sum to Lawrence for money he had borrowed and had promised to repay it the next day. In consideration of receiving Holly's money, Fox promised Holly that he would pay Lawrence the next day. A bystander testified to Holly's statement and Fox's promise. Lawrence had not himself participated in making this agreement. The transaction was a loan: Fox received the money as his own, rather than as a segregated trust fund belonging to Lawrence. (268–269, 271, 279.)

When Lawrence sued Fox, Fox sought a nonsuit on three grounds: inadequate proof of Holly's debt to Lawrence, lack of consideration, and lack of contractual privity between Fox and Lawrence. The trial judge rejected the motion. The jury awarded Lawrence the loan plus interest, $344.66, and the Superior Court at General Term affirmed. Fox appealed to the Court of Appeals. (268–269.)

## Questions and result
The court affirmed by six votes to two. H. Gray's opinion rejected all three objections and recognized Lawrence's ability to enforce Fox's promise, made to Holly on valid consideration for Lawrence's benefit. The six judges did not all accept the same conceptual basis: Johnson C.J. and Denio treated the promise as made to Lawrence through an agent whose unauthorized act Lawrence could later ratify. Gray, Selden, Allen, and Strong supported the broader third-party enforcement reasoning. Comstock dissented, and Grover also dissented without a separate reported opinion. The precise division matters: the six-vote judgment should not be presented as six judges adopting every proposition in Gray's rationale. (270–275, 281.)

## Evidence and consideration
Gray first treated the bystander's account of Holly's declaration as competent evidence of the debtor-creditor relationship. Holly's acknowledgment would be competent between Holly and Lawrence; it would likewise support Fox if, after paying Lawrence, he had to defend against Holly's demand for the same loan. Fox was entitled to competent evidence establishing the relationship, which the testimony supplied. This was the court's resolution of the evidence objection in the reported transaction, not a general modern hearsay formulation. (270.)

Consideration moved from Holly to Fox when Holly advanced the money. The promise was therefore supported although Lawrence furnished no new consideration directly to Fox. Gray relied particularly on Farley v. Cleaveland, involving property transferred in exchange for a promise to pay the transferor's creditor. The fact that Holly still owed Lawrence did not invalidate Fox's undertaking. The remaining distinction was that Fox's promise was addressed to Holly, whereas some precedents included a direct promise to the plaintiff; that raised privity, rather than absence of consideration. (270–271.)

## Gray's third-party enforcement reasoning
Gray read New York and Massachusetts authorities as recognizing an action by the person for whose benefit a promise was made on valuable consideration. The principle did not depend exclusively on a preexisting trust. A trustee's duty could imply a promise to the beneficiary, but that demonstrated an application of the broader principle rather than a restriction to trust assets. Here Fox's receipt of consideration and promise to pay Holly's creditor imposed a duty to pay Lawrence, although the advanced money became Fox's property. Gray reasoned that this duty could support an implied promise enforceable by Lawrence just as a trust obligation could. (271–274.)

The distinction between a debt and a specific trust res was thus acknowledged rather than ignored. Lawrence did not have to prove equitable ownership of the actual dollars in Fox's hands. Gray also distinguished adverse authority, including Mellen v. Whipple, as involving materially different arrangements. His approach emphasized the established state precedents and the justice of requiring Fox to perform the very payment promised for consideration received. (272–275.)

Fox argued that Holly could release or redirect the undertaking and that Lawrence therefore could not enforce a demand outside his control. Gray treated an actual release as absent and the possible power of release as unnecessary to the disposition, while questioning how Holly could defeat a promise legally presumed accepted for Lawrence's benefit. The opinion's fuller discussion considers a hypothetical note made payable to Lawrence and accepted while Holly remains liable. It does not supply a comprehensive rule fixing when every beneficiary right vests or when contracting parties may modify their bargain. (274–275.)

## Agency concurrence and dissent
Johnson and Denio regarded Holly as the medium of an agency transaction that Lawrence could ratify upon learning of it, even though he was initially unaware. This narrower rationale allowed recovery without endorsing Gray's entire account of a promise addressed only to another contracting party. Their reasoning is recorded in the concurrence statement, not a separately developed opinion. (275.)

Comstock insisted that an action ordinarily required privity: the plaintiff must be the promisee or possess a legal interest in the undertaking. Holly could demand repayment himself, redirect the payment, release the debt, or assign it; an expected benefit to Lawrence did not itself give him control of the contract. Since Fox borrowed the money as his own, Lawrence had no legal or equitable interest in a trust fund. Comstock rejected both a trust and an agency characterization here. (275–279.)

The dissent reviewed the cited precedents in detail. Some were dicta, some involved promises directly to the plaintiff supported by another person's consideration, and some involved genuine trusts. Dutton v. Poole depended on a close family relationship rather than a general right of every beneficiary; later English decisions denied enforcement by strangers to the transaction. Comstock read the later Massachusetts decision Mellen as repudiating the broad rule and would have reversed for a new trial. The disagreement therefore concerned the scope and force of precedent as well as the conceptual demand for privity. (276–281.)

## Exact judgment and scope
The Court of Appeals affirmed Lawrence's judgment, rather than imposing a trust over the money or ordering a new trial.
