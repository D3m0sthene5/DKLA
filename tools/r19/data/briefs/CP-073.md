# Beeck v. Aquaslide 'N' Dive Corp.

**Civil Procedure · CP-073 · 562 F.2d 537 (8th Cir. 1977).** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, PDF pp. 361–363, printed pp. 688–692. District Judge Paul Benson, sitting by designation, wrote for a panel with Circuit Judges Bright and Henley. No separate opinion is reported.

## The accident and mistaken product identification

Jerry Beeck suffered severe injuries on July 15, 1972 while using a water slide at a social gathering sponsored by his employer, Harker Wholesale Meats, at Kimberly Village in Davenport, Iowa. He and his wife Judy sued Aquaslide, alleging that it made the slide and seeking damages under negligence, strict-liability, and implied-warranty theories. Their combined demand was $2,225,000. Aquaslide was the sole named defendant. (¶¶ 6, 12, 33; n.3.)

Kimberly Village had ordered an Aquaslide product in 1971 through local distributor George Boldt. The order traveled through Sentry Pool and Chemical Supply to Purity Swimming Pool Supply, whose warehouse delivered a slide installed by Kimberly's employees. The delivered slide resembled an Aquaslide and lacked identifying marks; Kimberly believed it had received the product it ordered. Investigators for the employer's insurer, Kimberly's insurer, and Aquaslide's insurer all concluded that Aquaslide manufactured the slide. Aquaslide had received notice of the accident in October 1972 and its insurer inspected the site in May 1973. Relying on these investigations and lacking contrary information, the company admitted manufacture in its December 12, 1973 answer. It repeated the admission in June 3, 1974 interrogatory answers. (¶¶ 12, 22, 32; nn.4, 6.)

The two-year Iowa limitations period for the personal-injury claim expired July 15, 1974. About six and a half months later, Aquaslide's president and owner, Carl Meyer, inspected the slide before his deposition and concluded that it was not Aquaslide's product. Plaintiffs apparently requested that inspection to investigate installation or assembly defects. Aquaslide then sought leave to amend its answer to deny manufacture. The discovery created a disputed product-identity question; plaintiffs continued to assert that the slide was Aquaslide's. (¶¶ 13, 21, 32; n.5.)

## Procedure and appellate questions

District Judge Hanson permitted the amendment over plaintiffs' objection. He then granted Aquaslide's request for a separate jury trial on whether the company designed, manufactured, or sold the slide. The jury found for Aquaslide, and the court entered summary judgment dismissing the case. Plaintiffs appealed both discretionary decisions: could the company withdraw its admissions after limitations expired, and could the court try product identity separately? The jury's nonmanufacture finding itself was not challenged on appeal. (¶¶ 5, 7–9, 33; n.1.)

The appellate standard was abuse of discretion. Review of the amendment had to consider the record as it stood when the district court allowed it, rather than reasoning backward from the later verdict. At the amendment stage, neither product identity nor the availability of other litigation had been finally determined. (¶¶ 20–23.)

## Why amendment was permissible

Rule 15(a) directed courts to give leave freely when justice required. Foman v. Davis recognized grounds such as undue delay, bad faith, dilatory motive, repeated failed amendments, undue prejudice, and futility that might justify refusal. Plaintiffs opposing amendment bore the burden of showing prejudice, assessed in the particular circumstances rather than assumed whenever an admission was withdrawn. (¶¶ 15–20.)

The district court examined good faith, diligence, and prejudice. Aquaslide had relied on three independent insurers' investigations. Nobody contended that it had manipulated their conclusion. The court found no bad faith sufficient to prevent a trial on identity and regarded responsibility for the initial identification as shared. It also found that the company and its carrier were not so lacking in investigative diligence as to lose the right to contest manufacture. These findings did not establish perfect investigation or excuse all delayed amendments in other cases; they supported the discretionary decision on this record. (¶¶ 22, 24.)

Plaintiffs argued that expiration of the limitations period made the amendment fatal to their suit. The district court would have had to assume both that Aquaslide would win the identity issue and that no claim against anyone else remained possible. The record did not justify both assumptions at that stage. Amendment let Aquaslide litigate a material factual issue; prohibiting it could impose liability on a company without permitting it to prove it did not make the product. The court considered the speculative prejudice insufficient to outweigh that concern. (¶ 23.)

Footnote 7 explains why possible alternative litigation was not treated as definitively barred. If another slide had been substituted somewhere in the distributor chain, fraud or contract claims might have different unexpired limitations periods; equitable estoppel might also affect a limitations defense by another defendant. The record on these possibilities was unclear. The opinion did not hold that a counterfeit substitution occurred, identify a liable distributor, or guarantee plaintiffs a successful alternative suit. The footnote supports the refusal to assume total foreclosure, not a final ruling on those hypothetical claims.

The Eighth Circuit consequently held that granting leave to amend was not an abuse of discretion. The availability of amendment made the initial admission revisable with permission; it did not erase admissions automatically whenever their maker changed its position. (¶ 25.)

## Why the separate trial was also permissible

The full opinion addresses a second issue largely omitted from the assigned excerpt. Rule 42(b) authorized separate trials for convenience, economy, expedition, or avoidance of prejudice while preserving the jury-trial right. Product identity was potentially dispositive: if Aquaslide did not make the slide, extensive proof of injuries and damages would not establish its liability. (¶¶ 27–32; n.8.)

Proof of Beeck's severe injuries could require several days and could prejudice a jury's consideration of the company's nonmanufacture defense. A separate identity trial avoided that unnecessary evidence if the defense prevailed. Plaintiffs nevertheless retained a jury determination of the disputed identity question. Because the jury found the slide was not made by Aquaslide, and that finding was not attacked on appeal, separation accomplished the identified economy. The appellate court found no abuse of discretion in this ruling either. (¶ 33.)

## Judgment and source scope

**The district court's judgment was affirmed.** The amendment and separate trial stood, as did dismissal following the unchallenged jury finding that Aquaslide did not manufacture the slide. The court did not hold that the slide was safe, that nobody was responsible for the injuries, or that plaintiffs were barred from every conceivable claim against other parties. (¶ 34.)
