# NLRB v. New Vista Nursing and Rehabilitation
719 F.3d 203 (3d Cir. 2013) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Panel, court stage, and result

On May 16, 2013, a divided Third Circuit panel held that the Recess Appointments Clause permitted appointments only during breaks between Senate sessions. Judge Smith wrote for himself and Judge Van Antwerpen; Judge Greenaway dissented. Because Craig Becker had been appointed during a March 2010 intrasession break, the majority concluded that the three-member NLRB group deciding New Vista’s unfair-labor-practice case lacked the membership required to act. It vacated the Board’s original and reconsideration orders. It did not decide the nurses’ supervisory status, the meaning of vacancies that “happen,” or the validity of the later groups’ appointments. (Majority slip 5–6, 101–102 & n.34.)

Pinpoints below use the archived official opinion’s pagination. The majority runs through page 102; the dissent separately begins at page 1 and runs through 55. These are parts of the same judicial decision, not a subsequent appeal.

## Labor dispute and administrative proceedings

New Vista operated a nursing and rehabilitation center in Newark. A healthcare workers’ union petitioned in January 2011 to represent its licensed practical nurses. New Vista maintained that they were supervisors excluded from the NLRA’s protected employee category. The regional director rejected that position, the Board upheld the representation determination, and the union won the ensuing election. New Vista refused to bargain, prompting an unfair-labor-practice charge and a motion for summary judgment. This refusal furnished the ordinary route to obtain judicial review of a representation determination otherwise not directly reviewable. (Majority 6–7 & n.1.)

The Board’s unanimous decision and order was dated August 26, 2011. Its deciding group comprised Chairman Wilma Liebman and Members Becker and Hayes. Liebman left the Board the next day. New Vista argued that the later mailing meant the order actually issued after her departure. It sought reconsideration in September; the Board meanwhile applied for appellate enforcement. The court held record filing in abeyance while reconsideration proceeded. (7–9, 20–21.)

Following a December 30 denial, New Vista renewed objections concerning a recusal and expiration of a recess appointee’s tenure. It sought review in January 2012. After another denial on March 15, it argued that the relevant later members’ January appointments were invalid because the Senate’s pro forma meetings interrupted any recess. The Board denied the next motion on March 27 and filed the record, giving the appellate court exclusive jurisdiction under § 160(e). An April petition joined the earlier review and enforcement proceedings. The majority resolved the original group’s membership instead of adjudicating each objection to the subsequent groups. (9–12, 19–20, 102.)

## Group membership versus quorum

The NLRA allowed the five-member Board to delegate powers to a group of three or more members. New Process Steel required that group to maintain three members. The majority distinguished this composition requirement from a quorum: a properly constituted three-member group could act with two present, but two members could not themselves constitute the group. The original group’s lawful membership was the threshold question. (7–8; 17 n.2.)

The court examined that question sua sponte. Treating composition as a limit on the agency’s adjudicatory authority, it read New Process Steel, its own precedent, and the Supreme Court’s more disciplined jurisdiction decisions as establishing a jurisdictional condition. It acknowledged that an Appointments Clause objection considered independently could be nonjurisdictional. Its theory was narrower: an invalid appointment could mean the group failed the jurisdictional statutory membership requirement. The resulting question therefore could be raised at any stage. (12–20 & nn.2–3.)

That distinction made Becker’s appointment central even though the parties’ initial recess arguments concerned later appointees. Becker was appointed March 27, 2010 during a seventeen-day Senate break beginning March 26 and ending April 12. Unlike the January 2012 appointments, this was an actual intrasession adjournment during which the Senate indisputably was not conducting business. (19–20, 31–32.)

## Rejected nonconstitutional ground and political-question objection

To avoid unnecessary constitutional decision, the majority first addressed Liebman’s departure. The order’s face and docket showed August 26; the later mailing did not rebut the presumption that the named members had decided it by that date. Service was ministerial and did not nullify a completed decision because a member became unavailable before mailing. New Vista offered no additional evidence overcoming regularity and no adequate reason for further discovery into votes or nonexistent minutes. (20–23.)

An amicus urged that defining recess belonged to the President and presented an unmanageable political dispute. The majority rejected that argument. The Clause distributed and limited powers rather than committing its interpretation exclusively to one branch. The court could examine procedural Senate records and constitutional standards without resolving partisan confirmation obstruction. Political consequences did not remove its duty to interpret the constitutional limit. Other political-question considerations were rejected in note 5. (23–31 & nn.5–6.)

## Three definitions and acknowledged textual ambiguity

The majority separated three proposed definitions: intersession breaks; intersession breaks plus sufficiently long intrasession breaks; and any period when the Senate was unavailable to conduct business and provide advice and consent. It treated duration-based and purely functional theories separately because their histories and limiting principles differed. The January 2012 pro forma practice, including legislation passed by unanimous consent during such a meeting, illustrated the functional theory’s difficulty. (32–40 & nn.8–10.)

Dictionary usage, parliamentary practice, state constitutions, and early executive actions did not unambiguously establish an intersession-only meaning. Massachusetts and New Hampshire provisions supported that reading, while some governors’ conduct supported intrasession authority. The majority expressly acknowledged the ambiguity and declined to make the definite article “the” decisive. Historical examples undermined a duration-free functional test but did not alone resolve the remaining choice. This reasoning differed from treating every lexical or historical item as unequivocal proof of the narrow rule. (40–58 & nn.11–19.)

## Constitutional structure and the majority’s decisive reasoning

The Appointments Clause’s shared nomination-and-consent system was the ordinary method; recess appointment was auxiliary. The majority considered a bare unavailable-for-business test broad enough to permit appointment during lunch, evenings, or weekends. It also reasoned that a pro forma Senate meeting could conduct business, as demonstrated by passage of legislation, so an agreement initially to do none did not conclusively establish inability to consent. The dissent’s more demanding conception of advice and consent did not persuade it. (58–70 & nn.20–23.)

Long intrasession breaks avoided some of that overbreadth, but the majority found no constitutional basis for choosing a ten-day or three-day minimum. It rejected importing the Adjournment Clause or Pocket Veto Clause’s timing into the appointments provision. Its decisive contextual inference came from the commission’s expiration at the end of the Senate’s “next Session.” An intersession appointment lasted through one ensuing session in which the Senate could address it; an intrasession appointment could survive the remainder of the current session plus the next. For the majority, that changed auxiliary appointment into an additional route after an opportunity for the ordinary process had already passed. (70–88 & nn.24–30.)

The majority recognized that the longer tenure could be read consistently with intrasession appointment, but did not consider that the most natural construction. It distinguished temporary state-Senate appointments ending at the legislature’s next meeting and examined rare sessions ending without an earlier return. Its rule followed that structural interpretation rather than an express textual prohibition using the word “intrasession.” (75–88.)

## Historical practice and acknowledged opportunities for manipulation

The majority considered early presidential practice largely consistent with intersession-only appointments, with disputed Andrew Johnson exceptions. It acknowledged that a 1921 Attorney General opinion supported intrasession appointments and that later Presidents increasingly relied on them. It viewed the modern practice as comparatively recent and disputed, and declined to give political-branch acquiescence power to revise constitutional structure. It also questioned deference to executive constitutional judgments in separation-of-powers disputes. (88–96 & n.31.)

Clear intersession boundaries were preferable, in its view, to an uncertain functional test or an arbitrarily selected day count. It acknowledged that the Senate could manipulate timing and that its rule could permit presidential appointments during extremely brief intersession breaks. The majority accepted those possibilities rather than claim its interpretation eliminated strategic behavior. Courts should define allocated powers, not select a rule by predicting which branch would misuse them. (96–101 & nn.32–33.)

## Greenaway’s dissent: availability, function, and balanced powers

Greenaway would include both intersession and intrasession recesses because the Senate could be unavailable to consent in either. The text lacked an intersession modifier; its definite article did not identify that species of break. He disputed reliance on technical procedures and selected historical examples to narrow an ordinary term. Pocket-veto decisions offered a functional analogy, although they did not directly govern appointments. (Dissent 1–20 & nn.1, 4–13.)

The dissent understood the recess power as helping keep offices occupied and allowing senators to leave, while preventing the Senate’s confirmation power from becoming an unchecked barrier to presidential administration. Temporary tenure, senatorial control of recesses, political accountability, and incentives to retain cooperation constrained presidential use. Ordinary lunch, overnight, and weekend breaks were outside that understanding; a functional test did not mean any brief absence automatically permitted appointment. A commission’s fixed end did not require every appointee to receive the same duration or the Senate only one opportunity to consider a nomination. (20–41 & nn.14–22.)

Longstanding intrasession practice, the Senate’s 1905 report, changed congressional schedules, and the Pay Act reinforced the dissent’s reading. Nonuse of a power early in history did not surrender it. Greenaway found the intersession-only rule vulnerable to procedural relabeling: a momentary intersession break could permit appointments while a months-long intrasession absence could not. He considered the pro forma period functionally continuous and would uphold Becker’s and the identified January 2012 appointments. Separately, dissent note 3 rejected the majority’s jurisdictional characterization, observing that New Process Steel had not called the requirement jurisdictional. He did not decide the nurses’ supervisory merits. (Dissent 2–3 & nn.2–3, 41–55 & nn.23–28.)

## Exact disposition and assigned historical scope

The majority vacated the Board’s orders because Becker’s invalid appointment left the original group without three lawful members. The meaning of vacancies that “happen,” the unfair-labor-practice merits, and composition of later reconsideration groups remained unadjudicated; reconsideration of an invalid original order did not supply a reason to preserve those orders. The opinion should not be described as separately deciding every January 2012 appointment. (Majority 101–102 & n.34.)
