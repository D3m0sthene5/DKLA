# Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court (CPR-004)

480 U.S. 102 (1987). U.S. Supreme Court. O'Connor, J. No. 85-693; argued November 5, 1986; decided February 24, 1987. Certiorari to Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court, 39 Cal. 3d 35, 702 P.2d 543 (1985).

## Parties

Petitioner / cross-defendant: Asahi Metal Industry Co., Ltd., a Japanese corporation that manufactured tire-valve assemblies in Japan and sold them to tire-tube manufacturers abroad. It moved to quash service, sought mandate relief, and obtained reversal in the U.S. Supreme Court. Respondent in the writ caption: The Superior Court of California for Solano County, whose jurisdictional order Asahi sought to vacate. A trial judge appears as respondent in an extraordinary-writ case because the writ would command the court to change its ruling. Real party in interest: Cheng Shin Rubber Industrial Co., Ltd., a Taiwanese corporation that manufactured the motorcycle tube and pursued indemnification against Asahi. Original claimant: Gary Zurcher, a California resident injured in the motorcycle accident; his passenger and wife, Ruth Ann Moreno, died. Their resolved product-liability claims were outside the dispute that reached the Supreme Court.

## Procedural History

**Accident and original complaint:** The September 23, 1978 accident occurred on Interstate 80 in Solano County. In September 1979, Zurcher filed a products-liability action in the Solano County Superior Court. He alleged defects in the motorcycle tire, tube, and sealant and named Cheng Shin, the tube's Taiwanese manufacturer, together with a California retailer and other defendants.

**Cross-complaint:** Cheng Shin filed a cross-complaint seeking indemnification from its codefendants and from Asahi, which manufactured the tube's valve assembly. Indemnification reallocates an underlying liability from the party that paid the injured claimant to another party asserted to bear responsibility. Cheng Shin's claim therefore concerned the commercial relationship between the Taiwanese tube maker and the Japanese component maker. Zurcher's claims against Cheng Shin and the other defendants eventually settled and were dismissed. At the stage reviewed by the U.S. Supreme Court, Cheng Shin's indemnity claim against Asahi was the sole remaining controversy.

**Long-arm statute and motion to quash:** California Code of Civil Procedure Section 410.10 authorizes personal jurisdiction on every basis consistent with the California and United States Constitutions. The statutory inquiry therefore reached the constitutional boundary. Asahi moved to quash Cheng Shin's service of summons, asserting that the Fourteenth Amendment denied California adjudicatory authority over it. The motion attacked the court's power over Asahi and left the indemnity merits for later adjudication. Under California law, Cheng Shin carried the burden of establishing personal jurisdiction through competent evidence.

**Jurisdictional evidentiary record:** The parties submitted declarations concerning Asahi's sales to Cheng Shin, Cheng Shin's sales in California, a Solano County store survey, discussions between the companies, and Asahi's understanding of the distribution path. Asahi's president stated that the company never contemplated California litigation from the Taiwanese sales. Cheng Shin's manager stated that worldwide and United States distribution, including California, had been discussed with Asahi. The Supreme Court described the submitted declarations and stated that the record included no contract between Asahi and Cheng Shin. It did not certify that this description reproduced every item in the parties’ jurisdictional showing. 480 U.S. at 106–107.

**Superior Court:** On April 20, 1983, the trial court denied the motion to quash. It relied on the number of Asahi valves incorporated into tubes sold in California, the volume of Asahi's sales to Cheng Shin, Cheng Shin's substantial California distribution, and Asahi's awareness of that distribution. The court reasoned that a business operating internationally could reasonably defend international product claims.

**California Court of Appeal:** Asahi sought immediate extraordinary relief because the denial of a motion to quash left the action pending. The Court of Appeal issued a peremptory writ of mandate directing the Superior Court to quash service. It treated worldwide product foreseeability as an inadequate basis for forcing Asahi to defend in California. The writ would have terminated California's authority over Asahi before adjudication of indemnity.

**California Supreme Court:** The state high court reversed the Court of Appeal's writ disposition and restored the Superior Court's jurisdictional ruling. 39 Cal. 3d 35, 702 P.2d 543 (1985). Chief Justice Bird's five-Justice opinion concluded that Asahi intentionally placed components into a regular distribution stream, knew finished tubes would be sold in California, and indirectly benefited from those sales. It also credited California's consumer-safety and efficient-litigation interests. The court denied mandate relief and discharged the alternative writ. Justice Lucas, joined by Justice Mosk, dissented. The state decision was dated July 25, 1985. Its note 9 said the underlying claims had settled after the petition for hearing but that other cross-claims then remained; the U.S. Supreme Court’s later account identifies Asahi–Cheng Shin indemnity as the sole controversy at its review. These descriptions concern different procedural stages. 39 Cal. 3d at 50 n.9; 480 U.S. at 106. Lucas emphasized Cheng Shin's evidentiary burden, the small share of Asahi revenue traceable to Cheng Shin, the foreign indemnity claim that remained after settlement, and the overseas commercial relationship.

**U.S. Supreme Court review:** The Supreme Court granted certiorari, 475 U.S. 1044 (1986), from the completed state extraordinary-writ proceeding while the indemnity action remained pending below. It reviewed the jurisdictional record and the Fourteenth Amendment question. The Court unanimously agreed on the judgment reversing California, while its reasoning divided into three writings. The formal disposition reversed the California Supreme Court and remanded for proceedings consistent with the opinions, requiring California to release Asahi from the indemnity action.

**Vote structure and precedential force:** Justice O'Connor's Part I recited the case and commanded unanimous agreement. Eight Justices joined Part II-B's holding that California jurisdiction was unreasonable and unfair; Justice Scalia withheld his vote from that part. Four Justices - O'Connor, Chief Justice Rehnquist, Powell, and Scalia - joined Parts II-A and III, which required stream-of-commerce-plus conduct and found deficient minimum contacts. Four Justices - Brennan, White, Marshall, and Blackmun - found minimum contacts under an awareness-based stream theory and concurred because Part II-B made jurisdiction unreasonable. Three Justices - Stevens, White, and Blackmun - also reasoned that fairness resolved the case and treated volume, value, and hazardous character as relevant to any contacts analysis. The eight-Justice fairness conclusion constitutes the Court's controlling holding. The competing stream formulations each lacked a majority.

**Scope of decision:** The Court adjudicated California state-court authority under the Fourteenth Amendment. It expressly reserved whether Congress could authorize a federal court, consistently with the Fifth Amendment, to exercise jurisdiction over an alien defendant based on that defendant's aggregate contacts with the United States. It also left the indemnity merits, governing substantive law, and allocation of product responsibility undecided.

## Material Facts

Asahi manufactured valve assemblies in Japan and sold them to Cheng Shin and other tire manufacturers for incorporation into finished tubes. Its sales to Cheng Shin occurred in Taiwan, and Asahi shipped the components from Japan to Taiwan. Cheng Shin sold tubes throughout the world. It represented that approximately twenty percent of its United States sales occurred in California.

Cheng Shin bought 150,000 Asahi valve assemblies in 1978; 500,000 in 1979; 500,000 in 1980; 100,000 in 1981; and 100,000 in 1982, for a five-year total of 1,350,000. These sales represented 1.24 percent of Asahi's income in 1981 and 0.44 percent in 1982. Cheng Shin also used valves from other suppliers, and other tube makers incorporated Asahi valves into products that reached California.

In 1983, Cheng Shin's lawyer informally examined approximately 115 tubes offered in one Solano County motorcycle store. Ninety-seven appeared to have been made in Japan or Taiwan. Twenty-one of those bore Asahi's circled-A valve marking, and twelve of the twenty-one were installed in Cheng Shin tubes. The survey provided evidence of Asahi components in a local retail channel; the annual purchase figures separately showed recurring sales to Cheng Shin. The survey did not identify a direct Asahi sale or California-directed distribution contract. 480 U.S. at 106–107, 121 n.4.

Asahi had no office, agents, employees or property in California, did not advertise or otherwise solicit business there, and made no direct California sales. It did not create, control or employ the distribution system that brought its valves there. The declarations supported the California Supreme Court’s finding that Asahi knew finished tubes would reach California; the U.S. plurality assumed that awareness for purposes of its analysis. The plurality found no demonstrated additional conduct purposefully serving California and no evidence of product design anticipating California sales. Those are findings about absent California connections, rather than affirmative findings locating all design, advice and sales-agency activity overseas. 480 U.S. at 107–108, 112–113.

## Question Presented

Two related questions: (1) Does a foreign component manufacturer's regular placement of components into an international distribution stream, with awareness that finished products incorporating them will be marketed in California, create purposeful minimum contacts with California? (2) After the California residents settle and dismiss their product-liability claims, does fair play and substantial justice permit California to retain jurisdiction over the remaining indemnity dispute between a Taiwanese purchaser and a Japanese seller concerning sales and shipments made in Asia?

## Holding

The Court unanimously reversed California's assertion of personal jurisdiction over Asahi. Eight Justices held that the international burden on Asahi, California's diminished interest after settlement, Cheng Shin's limited California interest, the overseas location of the relevant commercial transaction, and the interests of other nations made jurisdiction unreasonable and unfair. That fairness conclusion is the Court's majority rule. Justice O'Connor's four-Justice plurality required conduct purposefully directing the distribution toward the forum in addition to awareness. Justice Brennan's four-Justice concurrence treated awareness of a regular and anticipated flow into the forum as sufficient. Justice Stevens resolved the case on fairness and identified volume, value, and hazardous character as relevant if minimum contacts were reached.

## Reasoning

**Relationship to World-Wide Volkswagen and Burger King:** World-Wide Volkswagen required a defendant-created affiliation and described products delivered into a distribution stream with an expectation of forum purchases. Lower courts divided over whether that passage required awareness of regular forum sales or additional conduct directed at the forum. Burger King supplied the two-stage structure: purposeful minimum contacts create presumptive authority, and the five fairness interests may still make the exercise unreasonable. Asahi exposed disagreement at the first stage and produced broad agreement at the second.

**O'Connor plurality - stream of commerce plus:** The plurality required both awareness of distribution and an act purposefully directed toward the forum. Forum-specific intent can be shown through conduct such as designing for the forum market, advertising there, establishing regular customer-advice channels there, or using a distributor that agreed to serve as a sales agent there. This approach treats placement into the distribution stream as commercial participation and additional forum targeting as purposeful direction.

**Plurality application:** Asahi made and sold the valve assemblies in Asia. Cheng Shin determined where the finished tubes would be sold. Asahi maintained an ongoing, high-volume component relationship and could be charged with awareness of California sales. The plurality found no demonstrated action purposefully directed at the California market: no California office, agents, employees, property, advertising or solicitation; no distribution system created, controlled or employed by Asahi to bring its valves there; and no evidence of design anticipating California sales. 480 U.S. at 112–113. The O'Connor plurality therefore found the purposeful-direction element unsatisfied.

**Brennan concurrence - regular and anticipated flow:** Justice Brennan, joined by White, Marshall, and Blackmun, treated the stream as the regular and anticipated movement from manufacture through distribution to retail sale. A participant that knows the final product is being marketed in the forum benefits economically from those sales and from the forum's commercial legal system. Awareness of that regular flow gives advance notice of litigation and fulfills purposeful availment.

**Brennan application:** Asahi sold Cheng Shin hundreds of thousands of valves annually over a continuing relationship; the store survey found Asahi valves in a substantial share of the Japanese and Taiwanese tubes offered locally; and the parties had discussed United States and California distribution. Brennan therefore found minimum contacts. He joined the judgment because the fairness balance defeated California jurisdiction despite those contacts.

**Stevens concurrence:** Justice Stevens, joined by White and Blackmun, concluded that the fairness holding supplied a complete ground for reversal and allowed the Court to leave the stream test open. He also questioned the plurality's characterization of Asahi's activity as bare placement. A purposeful-availment analysis could account for the volume, value, and hazardous character of the components. Asahi's five-year sales volume and the safety role of tire valves could support a greater quantum of forum-relevant conduct than the plurality acknowledged.

**Controlling fairness holding - burden on Asahi:** Defending in California required a Japanese company to cross a substantial geographic distance and submit an Asian commercial dispute to a foreign nation's judicial system. The Court specifically emphasized the distance and the unique burden of defending in a foreign legal system. It separately considered other nations’ policies and the Federal Government’s foreign-relations interests; it did not make individual evidentiary findings about translation or witness expenses. 480 U.S. at 114–115.

**Forum and claimant interests:** Settlement transformed the litigation. California residents no longer sought compensation, and the product-liability claims enforcing California safety standards had ended. Cheng Shin, a Taiwanese corporation, sought indemnity from a Japanese corporation. The component sale occurred in Taiwan and the shipment traveled from Japan to Taiwan. Cheng Shin offered no showing that California provided a more convenient forum than Taiwan or Japan for this remaining dispute. California's interest therefore became slight.

**Efficiency, governing law, and international policy:** California's former interest in consolidating defendants and avoiding inconsistent product-liability judgments diminished with dismissal of the main case. The remaining claim could require interpretation of an Asian commercial relationship, and the record gave no clear basis for applying California indemnity law. The several-states factor from World-Wide Volkswagen expands in an international case to include the procedural and substantive policies of foreign nations and the federal government's foreign-relations interest. Comity called for careful restraint when local interests were small and foreign burdens were heavy.

**Why the claim's procedural identity matters:** Specific jurisdiction evaluates the relationship among this defendant, this forum, and this claim at the stage presented. The California accident explained the original products-liability suit. The controversy surviving at Supreme Court review concerned indemnity between foreign companies arising from their Asian transactions. That procedural change reduced the forum's regulatory and remedial stakes and drove the unanimous judgment.

**Precedential rule:** The eight-Justice Part II-B establishes that fair play and substantial justice can defeat jurisdiction even when a purposeful-contact theory is available. The stream-of-commerce-plus and awareness approaches each received four votes and form distinct analytical positions. The international reasonableness holding binds lower courts; this decision supplied no majority resolution of the competing stream-of-commerce minimum-contacts formulations.

**Exam sequence:** Begin with the forum's long-arm statute and the procedural burden for proving jurisdiction. Identify the claim remaining at the time of decision. Attribute forum conduct to the particular defendant. In a distribution case, analyze O'Connor's additional-conduct approach, Brennan's regular-flow awareness approach, and Stevens's volume-value-hazardous-character considerations. Then apply every fairness factor, with special attention to foreign legal systems, the claimant's citizenship and convenience, the governing transaction, comity, and federal foreign-relations interests. State separately the proposition supported by a Court majority and the propositions supported by pluralities or concurrences.
