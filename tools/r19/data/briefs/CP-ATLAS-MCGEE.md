# McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.

**355 U.S. 220 (1957) · Supreme Court of the United States · December 16, 1957**

## A California insurance claim that had to be enforced in Texas

Lowell Franklin lived in California when he bought a life insurance policy from Empire Mutual Insurance Company, an Arizona corporation, in 1944. In 1948, International Life Insurance Company, a Texas corporation, agreed to assume Empire Mutual’s insurance obligations. International then mailed Franklin a reinsurance certificate offering to insure him on the terms of his existing policy. Franklin accepted the offer in California and mailed premiums from his California home to International’s Texas office until his death in 1950. His mother, Lulu B. McGee, was the beneficiary. She submitted proof of his death, but International refused payment on the ground that he had committed suicide. That was the insurer’s asserted defense, rather than a finding the Supreme Court made about Franklin’s death. 355 U.S. at 221–222.

Neither insurance company had an office or agent in California. The record showed no California insurance business by International beyond this one policy. The jurisdictional question therefore did not depend on an extensive local sales operation: it concerned the legal significance of International’s particular insurance relationship with a California resident. Id. at 222.

McGee sued International in a California state court. The court used a California statute permitting suits against foreign insurers on insurance contracts with California residents even when the insurer could not be served within the state. International received process by registered mail at its principal place of business in Texas. McGee obtained a California judgment, but could not collect it there, so she sued on that judgment in Texas. The Texas courts refused enforcement. They considered the California judgment void under the Fourteenth Amendment because service outside California had not given its courts jurisdiction over International. Id. at 221 & n.1.

The Supreme Court reviewed the Texas Court of Civil Appeals’ judgment. The parties did not dispute the next step if California’s jurisdiction was valid: under the full faith and credit requirement implemented by 28 U.S.C. § 1738, Texas had to recognize that judgment. Thus the Court was reviewing a refusal to enforce an existing judgment, rather than deciding the insurance claim in the first instance. Id. at 221.

## The constitutional questions and the insurer’s objections

The principal question was whether California could enter a binding personal judgment against an insurer whose relevant California activity consisted of this single insurance contract conducted by mail. International’s lack of a local office, agent, and other business supported its objection to California’s jurisdiction. McGee relied on the policy’s connection with California and the statute providing a local forum for claims against foreign insurers.

International also argued that applying the California statute impaired its contractual obligation because the statute became law in 1949, after it assumed Franklin’s policy in 1948. This was a distinct objection to the timing and effect of the statute, not merely another way of asserting that International lacked sufficient contacts. Id. at 224.

## Holding and the Court’s reasoning

The Court held that California’s exercise of jurisdiction satisfied due process. The suit concerned a contract with a substantial connection to California: the contract was delivered there, Franklin mailed premiums from there, and he lived there when he died. Those facts linked the defendant’s insurance obligation and the claim on it to the forum even though International had conducted no other California insurance business shown by the record. Id. at 222–223.

The Court began with the constitutional evolution from *Pennoyer v. Neff* to *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*. Instead of treating corporate presence or in-state service as the exclusive explanation for judicial power, *International Shoe* asked whether the defendant had minimum contacts consistent with fair play and substantial justice. The Court understood this development against changes in interstate commerce: contracts increasingly crossed state lines by mail, while transportation and communications made defending an action in a distant state less burdensome. A corporation could participate in a state’s economic life without establishing a conventional local office. Id. at 222–223.

The Court then explained why the forum connection mattered in practical terms. California had a substantial interest in providing an effective remedy when an insurer refused payment to its residents. Requiring an individual claimant to sue an insurance company in a distant state could make a small or moderate claim too costly to pursue, effectively insulating the insurer from collection. California also provided access to important witnesses. Here, evidence relevant to International’s suicide defense would often be found where Franklin had lived. The forum’s interest and the location of proof reinforced the fairness of litigating this insurance obligation in California. Id. at 223.

These considerations did not eliminate attention to the defendant. The Court acknowledged that defending in California could inconvenience International, but held that the inconvenience shown did not amount to a denial of due process. International did not claim that it lacked adequate notice or sufficient time to prepare its defenses and appear. The Court therefore distinguished a permissible forum burden from an unfair proceeding that denied a real opportunity to defend. Id. at 224.

The ruling supports jurisdiction over this claim arising from the particular California-connected contract. It does not say that the single policy made International answerable in California on every unrelated claim. Nor does it reduce the analysis to the beneficiary’s desire for a convenient forum: the defendant had mailed the offer to the insured in California and maintained the policy relationship through premiums sent from that state. Id. at 221–224.

## Why the statute could apply to an existing policy

The Court rejected the impairment objection because the statute changed the means of enforcing the insurance obligation without enlarging or reducing the parties’ substantive contractual rights. It supplied a California forum for whatever insurance rights McGee already had. International still received a reasonable opportunity to appear and contest liability. Under those circumstances, it had no vested right to avoid suit in California merely because the forum statute postdated the policy assumption. The holding concerned this remedial statute and these preserved substantive rights; it did not authorize every retroactive change to a contract. Id. at 224.

## Judgment, participation, and the assigned reading

The Court reversed the Texas judgment and remanded to the Texas Court of Civil Appeals, First Supreme Judicial District, for proceedings consistent with its opinion. The immediate result removed the jurisdictional basis for refusing enforcement of McGee’s California judgment. The Supreme Court did not itself conduct an insurance trial, make a finding about suicide, or calculate policy proceeds. Id. at 224.

Justice Black wrote the opinion, which Justice Douglas announced. Chief Justice Warren did not participate; the other eight Justices decided the case without a separate opinion or recorded dissent. The complete judicial opinion contains two footnotes: the first identifies the California insurance provisions, and the second supplies additional supporting jurisdiction decisions. Id. at 221 n.1, 223 n.2, 224.

The assigned casebook presents an editorial account with quotations from the Court, at printed pages 102–103, followed by two questions about minimum contacts, litigation burdens, and California’s regulatory interest. It omits the Court’s separate explanation of why the 1949 statute could apply to the existing policy, which the full opinion supplies. The account and questions explain the assigned teaching emphasis; they are distinct from the judicial holdings above. This brief addresses the 1957 decision on its historical terms.
