# Snyder v. Phelps — district voir-dire and posttrial ruling

**533 F. Supp. 2d 567 (D. Md. 2008), No. RDB-06-1389, February 4, 2008.** Richard D. Bennett, District Judge. Civil Procedure. Assigned account: *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 561, printed pages 1106–1107. Pinpoints refer to the complete reported memorandum and accompanying order; the original docket order is also identified where it corrects an OCR discrepancy.

This is the district court's posttrial decision, which the assignment uses to illustrate voir dire in a heavily publicized civil case. Bennett rejected challenges to the jury selection and liability verdict, retained $2.9 million in compensatory damages, and reduced punitive damages from $8 million to $2.1 million. The resulting award was $5 million. The assignment identifies later appellate reversal and Supreme Court affirmance of that appellate judgment; those later judgments are distinct stages. This brief reports the reasoning and result of the **2008 district ruling**, rather than treating its First Amendment analysis as the final disposition of the litigation. **569–571, 597–598.**

## Facts, claims, and the jury verdict

Albert Snyder's son, Marine Lance Corporal Matthew Snyder, was killed in Iraq on March 3, 2006. His funeral was held at a Catholic church in Westminster, Maryland, on March 10. Fred Phelps, members of his family, and their Westboro Baptist Church used military funerals to publicize their religious views condemning America's tolerance of homosexuality. They attended this funeral with signs, although they had never met Matthew or his family. The district court distinguished signs expressing general views from messages it considered potentially directed at the bereaved family. **570–572.**

The defendants complied with local ordinances and police directions about their distance from the church. Albert did not read the signs' contents during the funeral procession; he saw them later in television coverage. Shirley Phelps-Roper subsequently published an online account accusing Matthew's parents of raising him to defy God. Albert encountered that publication after a Google search concerning his son. He described distress, vomiting, and difficulty separating his memories of Matthew from the protest. Medical testimony addressed aggravation of depression and diabetes, while acknowledging difficulty isolating these effects from bereavement and preexisting conditions. **572, 580–581, 588–589.**

Snyder initially sued the church and Fred Phelps in June 2006; Shirley Phelps-Roper and Rebekah Phelps-Davis were added in February 2007. Diversity supplied federal jurisdiction. Before trial, the court entered summary judgment for defendants on defamation and publicity-given-to-private-life claims: the online account was religious opinion rather than the requisite defamatory communication, and the relevant personal information was already public. Intentional infliction of emotional distress, intrusion upon seclusion, and civil conspiracy remained for the jury. **572–573.**

On October 31, 2007, the jury found for Snyder on all three surviving claims and awarded $2.9 million in compensation. Following further instructions and argument, it awarded $8 million in punitive damages: $1.5 million against each defendant for intrusion and $500,000 against each for emotional distress. Defendants then sought judgment as a matter of law, reconsideration, a new trial, reduction of damages, relief from judgment, and a stay. **573 & n.4.**

## The assigned voir-dire issue

Defendants argued that the court should have allowed individualized questioning of every prospective juror. Under Rule 47(a), Bennett described broad trial-court discretion, with the controlling objective being an impartial jury. He concluded that the precautions actually taken supplied an adequate opportunity to discover bias. **582–583.**

At the parties' request, the court sent questionnaires to several hundred prospective jurors, covering protests, the military, the First Amendment, and related screening matters. The resulting venire exceeded one hundred people, more than three times the usual civil pool. Parties received the responses before trial. Those responses allowed the judge to remove people for cause where their answers revealed bias. **583.**

Every prospective juror who indicated prior knowledge of the case was questioned individually at the bench; many who had already formed an opinion were excused. Most members of the pool were called to the bench at some point, permitting attorneys to question nearly all individually. These measures addressed exposure to publicity and its actual effect on impartiality. They did not amount to a holding that defendants received an unrestricted right to question every juror in precisely their preferred format. The court found no voir-dire error. **583.**

The defendants also alleged judicial hostility, particularly about religion and interruptions. Bennett said the challenged religious comments occurred outside the jury's presence, arose from evidence introduced during the case, and did not display the deep antagonism necessary under the cited judicial-bias precedent. Keeping an eight-to-ten-day trial moving was ordinary trial administration. He denied reconsideration and the related mistrial contention. **583–584.**

## Liability and the district court's First Amendment reasoning

Bennett rejected an absolute First Amendment defense. He characterized Albert and the funeral as private, declining to treat publication of an obituary as voluntary participation in a public controversy. Defendants could not themselves create publicity and then use it to transform the family's status. He distinguished protected general messages from signs and online statements the jury could understand as directed at the family. The court also rejected the proposition that religious belief automatically exempted conduct from otherwise applicable tort responsibility. These were **the district judge's reasons**, not a statement of the eventual Supreme Court holding. **576–580.**

Applying Maryland law, the court found a sufficient basis for intentional infliction of emotional distress, including extreme and outrageous conduct and severe injury; intrusion upon seclusion during bereavement; and agreement supporting conspiracy. Conspiracy liability depended on a finding of one of the underlying torts. Bennett relied on both the funeral-related conduct and the online publication, notwithstanding different defendants' degrees of participation in publishing it. **580–582; nn.13–14.**

The judge separately considered the new-trial motion, for which he could weigh evidence and credibility rather than merely apply the judgment-as-a-matter-of-law standard. He found neither an outcome contrary to the clear weight of the evidence nor a miscarriage of justice. The defendants did not assert false evidence as a basis. Their religious beliefs had become part of the trial because they themselves relied on them as a defense, rather than because plaintiff's counsel had improperly introduced religion to inflame the jury. **574, 584–585.**

## Why compensation remained but punishment was reduced

For compensation, the court applied Maryland's substantive excessiveness standards under *Gasperini*, while retaining the federal procedural framework for the motion. It declined to apply the state's noneconomic-damages cap because the cited Maryland intermediate appellate decision excluded intentional torts. It also rejected certification and defendants' request to disregard that controlling state decision. It found the $2.9 million award supported by the expert evidence and Albert's testimony and not excessive under the state standard. **585–589; nn.15–18.**

For punitive damages, Bennett addressed constitutional guideposts and additional state considerations. He regarded the conduct as reprehensible, but recognized limits: no evidence showed additional publications directed at this family, and the physical injury was relatively limited. He did not call the punitive-to-compensatory ratio itself constitutionally excessive. He also rejected automatic reduction based on a funeral-picketing criminal statute that took effect after this funeral, and distinguished an instruction punishing prior injuries to nonparties from counsel's call for deterrence of future conduct. **589–593; nn.19–20.**

The court then considered each defendant's finances, relative participation, and the partly overlapping basis of the two tort awards. It reduced punitive damages to $1 million against the church, $600,000 against Shirley Phelps-Roper, $300,000 against Fred Phelps, and $200,000 against Rebekah Phelps-Davis. The compensatory obligation remained joint and several against all four defendants. The judge based reduction on constitutional as well as state-law grounds and therefore did not offer plaintiff a new-trial option, distinguishing a constitutionally required correction from ordinary discretionary remittitur. **575, 592–596, 597–598.**

## Exact order, remaining stay issue, and source limits

The court denied the posttrial challenges to liability and denied Rule 60 relief because no specified ground justified disturbing the judgment. It granted the reduction motions only as to punitive damages. Requests for a stay while those posttrial motions were pending became moot; it withheld a decision on a stay pending appeal until defendants demonstrated a bond or other security for the revised judgment. It did **not** grant an unconditional bond-free stay. **596–598; n.22.**
