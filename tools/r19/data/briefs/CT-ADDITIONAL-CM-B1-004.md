# Lumley v. Wagner
1 De G. M. & G. 604 (1852); 42 Eng. Rep. 687 | Contracts

## Court and exact posture
Lord Chancellor St. Leonards heard the appeal motion on May 22 and 26, 1852. He refused, with costs, the defendants' motion to discharge Vice-Chancellor Sir James Parker's injunction. The restraint on Johanna Wagner's competing performances consequently remained in force. This was review of an injunction, rather than an order compelling her to sing for Lumley or a judgment awarding damages. The recovered complete report runs through original star pages 604–634 in De Gex, Macnaghten and Gordon, volume I, as reproduced in Perkins's 1871 American edition. Pinpoints below refer to those original star pages, not the reprint's pages 466–490. One Chancellor decided the motion; there are no separate opinions. (*604, *607–608, *632–634.)

## Engagement and competing theatre
Benjamin Lumley, lessee of Her Majesty's Theatre, filed his bill on April 22, 1852 against singer Johanna Wagner, her father Albert Wagner, and Frederick Gye, lessee of Covent Garden Theatre. The bill described a French-language agreement made at Berlin on November 9, 1851 through Joseph Bacher. Johanna would sing at Lumley's London theatre for three months beginning April 1, 1852, generally twice weekly. The agreement identified six operatic parts, prescribed the sequence of the first three, and reserved those parts to her during the engagement. It provided £400 per month, paid at £100 weekly, with £50 for each additional performance beyond the agreed frequency. It also required Lumley to advance £300 at Berlin on March 15, deductible at £100 monthly from the engagement. Illness, delayed arrival, and transfer to a successor were separately addressed. (*604–606.)

The initial instrument lacked the exclusivity provision Lumley wanted. The bill alleged that, when Bacher met Lumley in Paris that November, Lumley objected and Bacher added a written article as the Wagners' authorized agent. Johanna would not use her talents at another theatre or at any public or private concert or reunion without Lumley's written authorization. The bill further alleged that Johanna and Albert subsequently arranged for her to sing for Gye at Covent Garden for greater compensation and abandon Lumley's engagement. It alleged that Gye knew about Lumley's prior agreement. The Wagners repudiated the arrangement on the ground that Lumley had failed to perform its financial requirements. (*606–607.)

Lumley sought restraints against Johanna's competing performances during the agreement, Albert's permitting or sanctioning them, and Gye's accepting her professional services without Lumley's written permission. The Wagners' answer disputed Bacher's authority to add the restrictive article and asserted untimely payment. Parker granted an injunction on May 9. The defendants then moved by way of appeal before the Chancellor to discharge it. The report's original starred note explains that the matter came to him on a representation that argument would be confined to the important, disputed point of equity jurisdiction, although the reported decision also addresses the factual objections. (*607–608 & original starred note.)

## Arguments over equitable power
The defendants argued that equity could not enforce the affirmative promise to perform personal services and therefore could not enjoin breach of the associated negative promise. They characterized damages as a complete remedy for a purely personal hiring contract. They sought to confine injunctions to recognized categories, including prevention of irreparable harm, relief ancillary to an otherwise available equitable remedy, completion of an agreement whose only remaining obligation was negative, and bills of peace. On their view, restraining Wagner without being able to require her performances would indirectly enforce an agreement that the court could not execute as a whole. They relied especially on Kemble v. Kean and Kimberley v. Jennings and attempted to distinguish contrary authorities as partnerships or substantially completed contracts. (*608–611, *612–614.)

Lumley answered that his bill requested an injunction against the negative undertaking, rather than specific performance with an ancillary restraint. Prior decisions had enforced negative obligations even though other promises could not be specifically enforced. Counsel disputed the proposed partnership distinction and argued that preparations and expenditure for Wagner's operas also supported intervention. These are reported advocacy positions; the Chancellor's own explanation follows at *614. (*611–613.)

## Affirmative and negative obligations
The Chancellor rejected the asserted categorical limit on jurisdiction. Decisions restraining bell ringing, harmful uses of leased property, and violations of professional apprentices' restrictive promises showed that an injunction need not depend on the ability to command affirmative performance of the entire agreement. The existence of a legal remedy was not itself an objection to equitable jurisdiction. Practical difficulties could justify refusing a particular injunction, as in Collins v. Plumb's restriction on selling water to the plaintiffs' prejudice, where the court could not ascertain injury each time water was supplied. That difficulty concerned beneficial exercise of the power, rather than its existence. (*614–618.)

He treated Wagner's promise to sing for Lumley and her promise not to sing elsewhere as parts of one engagement, operating concurrently. The negative undertaking protected the purpose of the affirmative one. A competing performance during the engagement would undermine that purpose. Although he observed that competition would also violate the spirit of the engagement absent an express restriction, he later expressly stated that, had he faced only the affirmative promise to sing, he would have granted no injunction. The actual decision enforced the existing negative article; it should not be converted into a universal rule supplying exclusivity to every personal-service contract. (*618–619, *622–623.)

St. Leonards acknowledged that the restraint might induce Wagner to fulfill her engagement. He nevertheless distinguished that possible consequence from an order commanding her to sing, which he could not make. He regarded enforcement of her promised abstention as proper and disclaimed doing indirectly what he could not do directly. His explanation invoked maintaining contractual good faith and avoiding reliance solely on uncertain jury damages. The report does not award such damages or decide a later tort action against Gye. (*619–620.)

## Treatment of precedents and limits
The Chancellor considered Kemble v. Kean wrongly decided. Its refusal to restrain an actor from performing elsewhere rested on the inability to command his promised performances and an unduly narrow partnership reading of Morris v. Colman. He found Shadwell's later Rolfe v. Rolfe, granting a restraint despite an unexecuted employment obligation, removed the weight of the earlier contrary decisions. By contrast, Clarke v. Price involved no negative promise and could not support compelling a reporter to attend court and prepare reports. (*620–624, *631–632.)

He preserved a distinction illustrated by Hills v. Croll. There the purchaser's promise to obtain acids only from one supplier was connected to that supplier's continuing obligation to supply them. Restricting alternative purchases when equity could not compel supply risked ruining the purchaser if the supplier defaulted. The complete Lumley report includes, in its original lettered note, the material portions of Lyndhurst's Hills judgment from shorthand notes, including its refusal of the interlocutory motion with costs. That is a reproduced earlier judgment used as supporting material, not a separate Lumley opinion. Perkins's additional later commentary questioning reconciliation of Hills with Lumley is likewise editorial, rather than St. Leonards's 1852 holding. (*626–631 & original note (b); reprint editorial note at page 484.)

## Factual objections and disposition
The report next records the Chancellor's examination of the answers and affidavits. Whether Bacher initially had authority to add the restriction, the evidence showed the Wagners' acquiescence in its remaining. The engagement had initially been postponed for their convenience. Lumley could not seek equitable enforcement without tendering the stipulated advance, but the £300 had in fact been paid to the parties' common agent for transmission to the Wagners. On both the legal issue and the merits presented on this motion, the Chancellor refused the motion with costs. (*632–633.)

A concluding evidentiary ruling supports that payment account. Bacher swore that he had written Johanna acknowledging receipt of the £300 and offering payment according to her instructions. She admitted receiving a letter of that date but denied its stated contents. The original was not produced, its absence was unexplained, and Bacher had kept no copy. The Chancellor accepted the affidavit's account because Bacher, when swearing, could not know the letter would not be produced and would have risked immediate exposure if he misstated it. This ruling appears after the main disposition and forms part of the complete report. (*633–634.)
