# Kinsey v. Preeson

**746 P.2d 542 (Colo. 1987)** — Colorado Supreme Court, en banc, November 30, 1987; modified on denial of rehearing December 21, 1987; No. 85SA429. Justice Vollack wrote; Justice Rovira did not participate. Assigned: CivPro physical PDF pages 611–612, printed 1208–1209, Note 1.

## Posture and outcome

On appeal after a bench trial, the court held Colorado's body-execution statute, section 13-59-103, unconstitutional under equal protection even under rational-basis review. It reversed an order imprisoning the debtor for up to one year or until payment. It **affirmed $7,500 punitive damages** and reversed the **$3,306 attorney-fee award**, remanding for further proceedings on fees. The $7,500 compensatory award was not reversed. The court invalidated the statute rather than merely directing a new indigency hearing under it. (544–545, 550–552.)

## Facts and trial

Preeson's October 19, 1981 letter promised to form Chief Motorhome Rentals, Inc., a Delaware corporation, and give Kinsey five percent of its stock for $7,500. He promised European promotional work, weekly reports, and monthly payments equal to ten percent of business income until repayment of her investment. With no return forthcoming, Kinsey sued in November 1983 for breach and fraud. Preeson obtained counsel after an initial pro se continuance, and his answer asserted legal insufficiency, assumption of business risk, and the plaintiff's negligent or willful conduct. (544.)

The bench judge found Kinsey an unsophisticated investor with limited English, raised and educated in Okinawa. Preeson had failed to form the corporation or perform the contract and had fraudulently obtained her money. The judge disbelieved his testimony, awarded $7,500 compensatory and $7,500 punitive damages, and regarded the negligence defense as frivolous. Counsel submitted a fee affidavit and prepared the judgment. The signed order fixed fees at $3,306 and imprisonment at one year or until payment. (544–545.)

Note 4 is crucial to the imprisonment procedure: there was no preincarceration hearing or economic-condition finding. The proposed order left confinement duration blank; apparently the judge inserted one year and mailed the order. Preeson had no opportunity to present indigency, although trial testimony described him as broke. This was not a finding that he possessed funds and deliberately refused a feasible payment order. (545 n. 4.)

## Appeal and preservation

The case reached the Court of Appeals, then transferred to the Supreme Court because a statute's constitutionality was challenged. Written judgment was entered November 1, 1984; the new-trial motion followed November 16 and was denied December 5. The forty-five-day appeal period ended Saturday January 19, making the January 21 notice timely. Note 5 explains why the signed written order controlled entry of judgment after a directed draft. (544 & nn. 1–3, 545 & n. 5.)

The constitutional issue was raised in the new-trial motion and ruled on below, defeating the preservation objection. The court also noted its authority to consider issues of constitutional proportion despite inadequate new-trial preservation. Note 1 limited review to the statute because analogous Civil Rule 101 had been repealed. Note 3 cautions that pretrial events were not fully recorded. (544–545 & nn. 1, 3.)

## Equal-protection holding

Preeson invoked federal Fourteenth Amendment protection and Colorado Article II, section 6. The court explained that the parallel state equal-protection guarantee came through Article II, section 25's due-process clause; section 6 concerned access to courts. It reviewed body execution's history and Article II, section 12, which allowed certain exceptions to the prohibition of debt imprisonment but was not self-executing. Legislative authorization remained necessary. (545–547 & nn. 6–8.)

Section 13-59-103 authorized body execution after specified tort findings involving malice, fraud, willful deceit, or reckless/willful negligence, excluding persons criminally convicted for the same wrong. Legislative age and presumptive validity did not insulate the enactment. Wealth alone was not generally a suspect classification under the discussed precedents. But the court **reserved whether personal liberty was a fundamental right in this setting or whether a suspect class was implicated**, because the statute failed rational-basis review. It did not apply strict scrutiny. (547–548 & n. 9.)

The statute's coercive purpose was to obtain payment. An indigent debtor could not comply, so confinement produced punishment without achieving collection, left the debt outstanding, and prevented gainful employment. A solvent debtor could buy release; the apparent equal opportunity to pay was illusory for an indigent person. Analogies to Williams and Tate reinforced the discriminatory effect of converting inability to pay into incarceration. The statute was unconstitutional under the Fourteenth Amendment; the court reversed body execution. It did not hold that every contempt sanction for willful refusal to obey a feasible payment order was invalid. (548–550 & nn. 10–14.)

The comparative notes also limit the holding. Palumbo preserved Connecticut's statute while rejecting a particular indigency-based imprisonment; Abbit's broader reasoning was different. Landrigan invalidated the lack of a prior ability-to-pay hearing, but Colorado rejected that narrow saving approach: its statute was penal, required strict construction, and lacked a poor-debtor oath mechanism. Other notes describe statutory history and comparative repeals; they are not new Colorado remedies. (548–549 nn. 9–14.)

## Punitive damages affirmed

The fraud complaint sufficiently alleged the material elements with particularity under Rule 9(b). A promise of future conduct could be actionable where made with a present intention not to perform. The judge's findings supported that theory rather than merely treating an unsuccessful investment as automatic fraud. (550–551.)

Exemplary damages required proof beyond a reasonable doubt under the provisions quoted in note 15. The original written order had handwritten, initialed language stating that standard. Preeson disputed when it was added, but the appellate court found it unnecessary to determine that factual question: the oral ruling and credibility findings sufficiently met the required burden on this record. It affirmed the punitive award. The civil fraud claim's ordinary burden should not be confused with the particular exemplary-damages requirement. (550–551 & n. 15.)

## Fees reversed, with further proceedings allowed

The governing pre-July 1984 version of sections 13-17-101 through -106 applied because suit began in 1983. The requesting party needed a preponderance showing of entitlement, and findings permitting meaningful review. Frivolousness required absence of a rational factual or legal argument. Statutory considerations included investigation efforts, disclosure of nonliability facts, finances, bad faith or procedural abuse, and conflicting evidence on liability. (551–552 & n. 16.)

The negligence defense was inappropriate to fraud, but that alone did not resolve the actual trial defense: denied misrepresentation, assumed risks, and a claimed good-faith unsuccessful performance effort. The judge failed to examine the statutory factors, including finances, or whether that defense was itself frivolous. Preeson also lacked an opportunity to challenge the reasonableness of the posttrial affidavit's fee amount. Fees were reversed and remanded for appropriate further findings; the court did not categorically forbid any later fee award. (552.)
