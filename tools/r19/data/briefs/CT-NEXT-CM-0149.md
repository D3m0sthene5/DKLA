# R. E. Davis Chemical Corp. v. Diasonics, Inc.
826 F.2d 678 (7th Cir. 1987) | Contracts

## Facts and procedural posture
Davis agreed in February 1984 to buy medical diagnostic equipment from Diasonics and paid a $300,000 deposit. It planned to use the equipment at a facility it would establish with physicians Dobbin and Valvassori. After the physicians breached their agreement with Davis, Davis refused delivery and payment of the remaining equipment price. Diasonics resold the equipment to another customer at the same price. The resale therefore eliminated a simple contract-price/resale-price differential, but Diasonics maintained that it had lost the profit on an additional sale. 826 F.2d at 679–680.

Davis sought restitution of its deposit under Illinois UCC § 2-718. Diasonics accepted that the statutory deposit rule allowed restitution less $500, subject to the separate offset for damages it could establish under Article 2. It claimed lost-volume profits under § 2-708(2). It also filed a third-party complaint alleging that the physicians knew their own breach would leave Davis unable to use the equipment and was reasonably certain to cause Davis’s breach. Those assertions about the physicians’ knowledge and conduct were allegations tested on dismissal, rather than adjudicated tort findings. Id. at 680, 685–686.

The district court limited a reselling seller to § 2-706 and entered summary judgment for Davis for $322,656, described in the opinion as the deposit plus prejudgment interest less incidental damages. It dismissed the third-party complaint because it did not allege intentional inducement of Davis’s equipment-contract breach. Diasonics appealed both rulings. Id. at 680–681.

## Issues and Erie posture
The court addressed whether a commercially reasonable resale foreclosed § 2-708(2), what a seller must establish to recover lost-volume profit, and whether resale proceeds must be credited against that profit. Because Illinois courts had not resolved these issues, the Seventh Circuit predicted how the Illinois Supreme Court would decide them. Its ruling was a federal court’s application of state law in that historical posture, rather than an Illinois Supreme Court holding. It also addressed tortious interference and the sufficiency of the notice of appeal. Id. at 681, 685–688.

## Available remedies and lost volume
The court rejected a compulsory hierarchy that sent every commercially reasonable reseller exclusively to § 2-706. Article 2’s seller remedies are generally cumulative, subject to the facts and the prohibition on duplicate compensation. The court allowed Diasonics to seek § 2-708 damages notwithstanding resale. It did not decide the broader question whether a reseller could elect an overcompensatory contract/market differential under subsection (1), because Diasonics sought only subsection (2)’s profit measure. An action for the full price under § 2-709 was unavailable after resale. Id. at 681–683 & nn. 4–9.

Subsection (2) applies only when subsection (1) is inadequate to place the seller as well as performance would have done. A resale may replace the lost transaction for a seller with limited demand or capacity. For a lost-volume seller, however, the second purchaser would have bought anyway, so the breach reduces total sales and the corresponding profit despite resale at the original price. Describing oneself as a manufacturer or proving a resale does not establish that status. Id. at 683–684.

The court required three propositions on remand: capacity to supply both Davis and the resale purchaser; profitability of making both sales; and probability of making the second sale even absent Davis’s breach. Capacity alone omits the possibility that rising marginal costs would make the additional sale unprofitable. Diasonics carried the burden because it claimed the damages and had access to the pertinent production and cost information. The court did not find these elements already proved on the summary-judgment record or award a specified lost-profit sum. Id. at 684–685 & nn. 13–16.

## Resale credit and the deposit
Section 2-708(2) includes reasonable overhead in the recoverable profit, allows appropriate incidental damages and costs, and calls for credit for payments or resale proceeds. Crediting the full replacement-sale price against genuine lost-volume profit would ordinarily erase the recovery and defeat the section’s compensatory function. The court predicted that Illinois would follow the narrower reading associated with unfinished goods sold for scrap, supported by the drafting history identified in footnote 15. It therefore declined to apply the resale-proceeds credit to this asserted lost-volume sale. That conclusion did not eliminate credit for the buyer’s deposit or authorize recovery twice for the same loss. Davis’s restitution remained subject to the damages offset established on remand. Id. at 680–681, 684–685 & n. 15.

## Third-party complaint and appeal
Illinois intentional-interference law required more than alleging that the physicians knew their independent contractual default was substantially certain to interfere with another agreement. Diasonics did not allege that they desired to induce the equipment-contract breach or facts showing the required improper inducement beyond that knowledge. The court considered the Restatement’s distinction between intent and impropriety: knowledge of an incidental consequence may supply intent in some settings but does not invariably make interference wrongful. It affirmed dismissal under the pleading standard then governing the decision. Id. at 685–687 & n. 17.

The physicians also argued that the notice named only the final November 4 order, omitting the earlier September 19 dismissal. The earlier order had not independently become final while the principal claims remained unresolved. Appeal from the final judgment brought prior nonfinal orders before the court. In any event, the intended appeal was clear, the physicians acknowledged no prejudice, and Diasonics had previously filed an appeal from the dismissal and voluntarily withdrawn it because final judgment was lacking. The court rejected the notice objection and declined the requested direction to impose Rule 11 or § 1927 sanctions, finding no basis for it. Id. at 687–688 & n. 18.

## Disposition and sources
Cudahy wrote for Chief Judge Bauer, Cudahy, and Flaum; no separate writing was reported. The court affirmed dismissal of the third-party complaint, reversed summary judgment for Davis, and remanded for proceedings applying the three-part lost-volume inquiry and the appropriate damages calculation if Diasonics proved it. Rehearing and rehearing en banc were denied September 10, 1987. Id. at 679, 685, 688.
