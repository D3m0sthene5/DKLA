# Herzog v. Irace
594 A.2d 1106 (Me. 1991) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
The Maine Supreme Judicial Court unanimously affirmed judgment enforcing a patient's assignment of settlement proceeds against the attorneys who received and distributed them. Justice Brody wrote the August 6, 1991 opinion, with Chief Justice McKusick and Justices Roberts, Wathen, Glassman, Clifford, and Collins participating; all concurred. The Superior Court had acted as an intermediate appellate court, so the Supreme Judicial Court reviewed the District Court's decision directly. (1106–1108, 1110.)

## Facts and procedure
Gary Jones retained Irace and Lowry for a motorcycle personal-injury claim. He later dislocated his shoulder twice in unrelated incidents. Dr. Herzog determined surgery was needed, but Jones could not pay. In exchange for the surgery, Jones signed a June 14, 1988 letter on the doctor's letterhead requesting direct payment to Herzog from the settlement of the pending unrelated claim. It referred to treatment of the shoulder injury, rather than specifying a dollar amount. Herzog notified the firm of this assignment and a firm employee said it was sufficient for payment of the bills at the case's conclusion. The doctor performed surgery and treated Jones for approximately a year. (1107–1108.)

The motorcycle case settled for $20,000 in May 1989. Jones directed the attorneys not to pay Herzog, saying he would handle payment himself. The attorneys told the doctor that their client had revoked his permission and that they would follow the client's instruction. They paid Jones $10,027 and distributed the rest to other creditors. Jones's subsequent check to Herzog was dishonored for insufficient funds; the doctor remained unpaid. (1108.)

Herzog sued the attorneys to enforce the assignment. The case was tried on stipulated facts, and the District Court found a valid transfer enforceable against them. After an unsuccessful Superior Court appeal, the attorneys challenged both validity and consistency with their professional duties. Contrary to their proposed de novo factual review, findings based on stipulated facts and documentary evidence were reviewed for clear error under the Court's more recent precedents. (1108.)

## Assignment and notice rules
An assignment manifests the right-holder's intent to transfer the right. For an effective transfer the assignor must relinquish control and power of revocation over the assigned right. The obligor's acceptance is unnecessary. Once notified, the holder must preserve the assigned fund for the assignee; paying the assignor or another creditor instead exposes the holder to direct enforcement by the assignee. Ordinary future rights are generally transferable subject to limits involving materially changed duties, risks, return performance, or legal restrictions. Maine recognized equitable assignments of future litigation proceeds, including partial assignments. Note 1 expressly distinguishes proceeds from assignment of the personal-injury cause of action itself. (1107 n. 1; 1108–1109.)

The attorneys argued that the word “request” merely authorized payment and failed to show an irrevocable transfer. Context supported the trial finding instead: the letter reserved no control, the surgery furnished consideration, and both the necessary services and reasonable charges were undisputed. Absence of a fixed sum did not make the treatment-related assignment unenforceable in these circumstances. The attorneys had ample notice and sufficient settlement funds to satisfy all creditors, including Herzog, and still pay Jones. The federal bankruptcy preference case on which they relied did not defeat the District Court's supported finding here. A later attempted withdrawal of payment permission could not revoke a completed assignment. (1108–1109.)

## Professional duties and competing client instruction
The then-applicable bar rules required prompt delivery of funds the client was entitled to receive and generally prevented an attorney from placing a third-party lien on a client's file. Neither required disregard of this client's own valid assignment. After transfer, Jones was no longer entitled to the assigned portion. He, rather than the lawyers, placed the encumbrance on the funds. Honoring the assignment therefore conflicted with neither rule. The record also supplied no contractual obligation requiring the lawyers to honor his contrary instruction. Note 2 rejects reliance on Vermont authority to the extent it conflicted with Maine's established recognition of these equitable assignments. (1109–1110 & n. 2.)
