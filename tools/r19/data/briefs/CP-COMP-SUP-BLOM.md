# BLOM Bank SAL v. Honickman

**605 U.S. 204 (2025) • Supreme Court of the United States • June 5, 2025**

**Assigned source:** Civil Procedure Supplement, printed pp. 515–517 (PDF 346–347), with the linked authorship note in the appendix (PDF 352). Justice Thomas wrote for the Court. Eight Justices joined the whole opinion; Justice Jackson joined all except Part III and concurred separately in part and in the judgment. All nine agreed with the result and the governing Rule 60(b)(6) standard; eight joined the majority's complete case-specific application.

## Facts and procedural history

Victims and relatives of victims of Hamas attacks occurring from December 2001 through August 2003 sued BLOM Bank in 2019 under the Anti-Terrorism Act as amended by JASTA. They alleged that financial services to Hamas-affiliated customers aided and abetted the attacks. These were allegations, not findings that the bank knowingly financed terrorism. BLOM repeatedly challenged their sufficiency. In a premotion exchange, a conference, and the dismissal hearing, plaintiffs maintained that their pleaded facts were sufficient and declined to identify additional facts or pursue amendment. 206–207.

The district court dismissed with prejudice under Rule 12(b)(6), finding deficient allegations of the bank's general awareness and denying leave to amend in light of plaintiffs' choices. On appeal, the Second Circuit held that the district court had demanded too much foreseeability, but concluded that the complaint still failed under the corrected standard and affirmed. Plaintiffs had not appealed the with-prejudice aspect of the dismissal. 207–208 & n.*.

After that affirmance, plaintiffs sought Rule 60(b)(6) relief so they could amend to meet the appellate standard. The district court denied reopening: clarification of the law was not an extraordinary circumstance, success still appeared unlikely, and deliberate refusals to amend also weighed against relief. The Second Circuit reversed, requiring a combined assessment of Rule 60(b)'s interest in finality and Rule 15(a)'s liberal amendment policy. It considered the district court wrong to insist on two sequential inquiries. The Supreme Court granted review. 208–210.

## Issue and holding

Does Rule 15(a)'s liberal amendment standard reduce the extraordinary-circumstances showing necessary to reopen a final judgment under Rule 60(b)(6) when amendment is the movant's goal?

**No.** The movant must first satisfy Rule 60(b) on its own terms. Only after obtaining reopening can Rule 15(a)'s amendment standard apply. Rule 15(a) cannot dilute Rule 60(b)(6)'s extraordinary-circumstances requirement. 210, 213–214.

The Court also held that the district court's denial fell within its discretion under the proper standard. 216–217. This second holding should be separated from the unanimous agreement on the governing test and Jackson's different explanation for accepting the denial here.

## Reasoning on the governing standard

Rule 60(b)(6) supplies a catchall ground for relief unavailable under the preceding five paragraphs. Using it for matters covered by those paragraphs could circumvent their restrictions. Grounds under paragraphs (1)–(3) carry a one-year limit; paragraph (6) is outside that cap but remains subject to Rule 60(c)(1)'s reasonable-time requirement. The absence of a one-year cap does not imply unrestricted timing or routine relief. 210–211, 218.

Even when paragraphs (1)–(5) do not apply, extraordinary circumstances must justify reopening. Klapprott illustrated an exceptional inability to defend a denaturalization proceeding while imprisoned, ill, unrepresented, and occupied with grave criminal charges. Ackermann contrasted a free and deliberate decision not to appeal. Later cases maintained the strict standard to protect finality. 211–213.

Rule 15(a) operates at a different stage. Once a final judgment closes a case, no pending pleading exists to amend until the judgment is set aside. Combining its liberality with Rule 60(b)(6)'s stringency improperly weakens the latter. A district court may still acknowledge the desired amendment, prior amendment opportunities, and the standard that would later apply; it simply cannot balance those considerations into a lower reopening threshold. 213–214.

Foman did not compel a hybrid test. It concerned a Rule 59(e) motion, whose timely filing suspends finality, rather than a Rule 60(b)(6) motion potentially threatening a settled judgment years later. The same extraordinary-circumstances requirement does not attach to both procedures. 215–216.

## Majority's case-specific application

Rule 60(b) rulings receive limited, deferential abuse-of-discretion review. The district court applied the correct test and gave substantial justification: the appellate clarification was not extraordinary, plaintiffs were still unlikely to succeed, and their deliberate pleading choices counted against reopening. Intervening legal developments alone rarely establish extraordinary circumstances, and diligence matters. The Second Circuit improperly displaced a decision within the district court's permissible range. 216–217. The Court did not freshly adjudicate BLOM's underlying liability or approve the district court's originally incorrect foreseeability test.

## Jackson concurrence

Jackson accepted the extraordinary-circumstances requirement, the rejection of the hybrid standard, and the ultimate conclusion that denying relief here was within the district court's discretion. She declined Part III because she disputed treating plaintiffs' decision to appeal rather than amend as a blameworthy abandonment under Ackermann. Ackermann involved choosing not to pursue appellate review; these plaintiffs continued litigating through their statutory right to appeal. Mere overconfidence in their complaint did not turn that diligence into abandonment or categorically bar reopening. 217–220.

A plaintiff may reasonably seek clarification of pleading law before adding serious allegations, particularly where Rule 11 requires care. Penalizing that choice could deter legitimate appeals and leave important pleading questions unresolved. If extraordinary circumstances otherwise existed, a reasonable choice to appeal rather than amend should not defeat relief. Jackson nevertheless accepted the district court's principal ground here: the clarification, especially given plaintiffs' continuing weak prospect of success, did not warrant reopening. She concurred on that basis, without endorsing the alternative rationale about their earlier refusal to amend. 221–222.

## Exact disposition and course significance

The Court **reversed the Second Circuit's judgment and remanded**. 217. The reversal concerned the appellate reopening decision, not a reversal of the original merits dismissal in plaintiffs' favor. Jackson's reference to upholding the district court's denial describes the practical result within that disposition.
