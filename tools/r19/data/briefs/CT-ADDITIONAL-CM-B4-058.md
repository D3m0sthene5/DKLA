# Kel Kim Corporation v. Central Markets, Inc.
70 N.Y.2d 900, 519 N.E.2d 295 (1987) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
The New York Court of Appeals decided the case December 21, 1987, after argument November 18. Its unsigned memorandum affirmed the Appellate Division's order with costs. All seven listed judges concurred: Chief Judge Wachtler and Judges Simons, Kaye, Alexander, Titone, Hancock, Jr., and Bellacosa. The Appellate Division had been divided, but there was no separate writing at this Court of Appeals stage. (70 N.Y.2d at 900–903.)

Kel Kim and its individual lease guarantors brought a declaratory action seeking excuse from the tenant's liability-insurance obligation. Special Term granted the landlords summary judgment, nullified the lease, and directed the tenant to vacate; the Appellate Division affirmed. The Court of Appeals rejected both common-law impossibility and contractual force majeure as grounds to preserve the lease without the required coverage. It did not merely award arrears or direct a damages trial. (Id. at 901–903.)

## Rink lease and insurance crisis
In early 1980 Kel Kim leased a vacant supermarket in Clifton Park for ten years with two five-year renewals. Both parties understood it would become a public roller-skating rink, although the lease did not restrict use to that activity. The tenant promised to maintain public liability insurance in responsible solvent companies, covering at least $500,000 for one person and $1 million in the aggregate for a single accident. It obtained coverage and operated for six years without incident. (Id. at 901.)

In November 1985 the insurer notified Kel Kim that its policy would expire January 6, 1986 and would not be renewed because of uncertainty about a reinsurer's financial condition under court-appointed administration. Kel Kim informed the landlords and asserted it made every effort to obtain replacement coverage, but the liability-insurance crisis prevented compliance. It obtained a $500,000 aggregate policy effective March 1, 1986 and contended no insurer would provide greater coverage for any roller rink. It finally obtained the specified coverage in August 1987. These assertions concerning the market were reported as the tenant's position; the court did not need to establish a universal factual finding that every insurer refused such policies. (Id.)

On January 7, 1986, with the original coverage expired and the tenant uninsured, the landlords issued a default notice requiring cure in thirty days or surrender of the premises. The later acquisition of insurance did not change the order the Court of Appeals affirmed. (Id. at 901–903.)

## Narrow common-law impossibility
The court began with the binding character of promises despite unforeseen burdens. Excuse doctrines received narrow application because contracts allocate performance risks. Its impossibility rule required objective impossibility from destruction of the subject matter or means of performance, produced by an unanticipated event that could neither be foreseen nor guarded against contractually. (Id. at 902.)

Kel Kim specifically undertook the obligation to procure and continuously maintain stated insurance limits. Inability to obtain that coverage was a contingency the parties could have foreseen and addressed in the lease. Consequently the insurance-market crisis did not excuse this undertaking under the impossibility doctrine. The reasoning was about allocation of the coverage obligation, not a finding that insurance always remains available regardless of market conditions. (Id.)

## Force majeure and the kind of risk the clause covered
The sole judicial footnote reproduces a clause excusing delay or prevention without the party's fault caused by labor disputes, unavailable materials, failed utility service, restrictive laws or regulations, riots, insurrection, war, adverse weather, acts of God, or other similar causes beyond control. Excuse lasted for the period of delay. Insurance unavailability was not listed. (Id. at 902 n. *.)

The court stated that force majeure ordinarily excuses performance only when the preventing event is specifically included. The residual similar-causes language did not expand to every uncontrollable difficulty: general words were confined to the kind of events enumerated. Those events affected daily commercial operations, such as access to labor, materials, and utilities. Liability insurance instead protected the landlord's separate economic interests when the tenant chose to continue operating a public skating rink. Kel Kim's argument that insurance also affected operation did not erase that contractual distinction. (Id. at 902–903.)

The court therefore affirmed with costs in its memorandum. The holding preserves the particular insurance allocation and bounded similar-causes clause; it does not decide whether an expressly drafted insurance-market contingency would produce a different result. (Id. at 903.)
