# American Mining Congress v. Mine Safety & Health Administration

**995 F.2d 1106 (D.C. Cir. 1993) — June 15, 1993.** Judge Stephen F. Williams wrote the panel’s single opinion, with Judges Sentelle and Randolph; the report contains no separate writing. Three petitions, Nos. 91-1501, 92-1188, and 92-1331, challenged successive Program Policy Letters. Assigned in LRS November-a, physical PDF 75–76, printed 650–651.

## Existing reporting duties and the disputed interpretation

The Mine Safety and Health Act authorized the Secretary of Labor to require mine operators to keep records and report information. Under that authority, the agency adopted Part 50 regulations through notice-and-comment rulemaking. Those regulations required operators to report accidents, occupational injuries, and occupational illnesses within ten days. They also required reports when specified illnesses, including silicosis, asbestosis, and pneumoconioses, were diagnosed. Violations could produce citations and penalties. The regulations therefore already established the reporting obligation, but the meaning of a reportable diagnosis remained uncertain. (1107–1108.)

The Mine Safety and Health Administration issued successive Program Policy Letters addressing chest x-rays. The September 1991 letter treated an x-ray rated at least 1/0 on the International Labor Office classification scale, for a miner with the specified dust-exposure history, as a diagnosis for reporting purposes. The scale measured the concentration of opacities, and 1/0 was its fourth-most-severe classification. The agency also provided for more skilled readers to reassess an initial reading. (1107–1108.)

A May 1992 replacement retained the basic approach and clarified that a report did not automatically establish disability-benefit entitlement or constitute the operator’s admission of liability. An August 1992 replacement modified the rereading procedure. When the first reader lacked the relevant B-reader certification, the agency would stay enforcement while a certified reader evaluated the x-ray. A confirming reading required reporting. If the certified reader disagreed, a third reading by another certified reader could be obtained, and the agency would accept the majority of the three readings. (1108.)

The American Mining Congress and National Industrial Sand Association challenged the letters because none had undergone APA notice and comment. The agency defended them solely as interpretive rules. The petitioners did not challenge the lack of Federal Register publication, and they did not raise the substantive validity of the interpretation itself. Those limits mattered: the court addressed the classification needed to determine procedure, not every possible objection to the reporting system. (1108, 1113.)

## Holding and analytical distinction

The panel held that the August 1992 letter was interpretive and that its reasoning covered the earlier letters as well. It dismissed the petitions. The interpretation explained an existing diagnosis-reporting duty rather than exercising legislative authority to create a new duty, and its greater specificity did not make it an amendment of the underlying regulation. The court expressly preserved substantive challenges to the interpretation. (1112–1113.)

The central distinction was between supplying legal duties through delegated legislative authority and construing duties that already existed. The court examined the Attorney General’s Manual and earlier cases to identify circumstances showing that an agency had exercised legislative power. A statute that prohibited nothing until the agency supplied rules would require legislative action before enforcement. Conversely, interpretation could define an existing duty’s scope, even in precise terms. An interpretation might ultimately prove incorrect on the merits without becoming legislative merely because it was wrong. Procedural classification and substantive correctness were separate inquiries. (1109–1110, 1113.)

## The four indicators in the complete opinion

The opinion’s concluding formulation asked four questions: whether the disputed rule supplied a necessary legislative basis for enforcement or benefit-conferral that would otherwise be absent; whether the agency published it in the Code of Federal Regulations; whether the agency explicitly invoked its general legislative authority; and whether the rule effectively amended a prior legislative rule. An affirmative answer to any identified a legislative rule under the court’s formulation. The assigned quotation develops three illustrations earlier in the opinion; the complete concluding test separately includes explicit invocation of legislative authority. (1109–1110, 1112.)

The first indicator is illustrated by the difference between Part 50 and the letters. The statute’s broad reporting authorization did not itself specify all reports that operators had to make, so a legislative regulation created the obligation. Part 50 had already done that work. The letters subsequently construed the word diagnosis within that existing regulation. The distinction did not turn on whether the interpretation was detailed, inconvenient, or phrased as a command. It turned on where the operative legal duty came from. (1109–1112.)

The court also explained why the test for policy statements could not simply be transplanted. A policy statement ordinarily announces future discretionary choices; evidence that it binds the agency can show that its claimed tentativeness is false. An interpretation instead explains an existing command or permission. Interpreting a command will naturally produce imperative language. Restricting agency discretion or saying that an operator must do something therefore does not by itself identify new legislative action. That is why the force-of-law inquiry here required more than the binding-norm analysis associated with Pacific Gas. (1111.)

## Application to the x-ray letters

There was no legislative gap: Part 50 already required diagnoses of the relevant diseases to be reported. The agency had not placed the letter in the CFR or explicitly invoked its general legislative authority. The remaining question was whether the letter effectively amended the existing regulation. (1112.)

Petitioners cited medical definitions suggesting that a diagnosis of pneumoconiosis or silicosis required more than an x-ray, such as tissue examination or additional history. The agency identified other administrative rules and medical sources using qualifying x-rays as a basis for finding pneumoconiosis. Because a finding of disease could ordinarily be described as a diagnosis, the letter’s construction did not repudiate or contradict Part 50. Supplying a sharper line within a previously general term was not necessarily an amendment; otherwise interpretations could do little more than repeat the original language. (1112–1113.)

This analysis did not certify the agency’s interpretation as correct under every applicable source of law. The court stressed that an interpretation could be genuinely interpretive yet exceed the agency’s statutory authority or otherwise fail substantively. Since petitioners had not presented that separate challenge, the procedural holding left it open. (1113.)

## Procedure, protections, and limits

The court rejected the notion that written interpretive guidance necessarily deprived affected parties of protections that they otherwise possessed. Without guidance, the agency could develop its interpretation through individual enforcement actions. Published explanations could improve notice. Moreover, judicial review gave agency personnel an incentive to consider opposing evidence and arguments when implementing an interpretation, especially where no earlier formal notice-and-comment opportunity had occurred. This did not make interpretations legally unchallengeable; it explained why the APA’s interpretive exception had practical value. (1111–1112.)

The agency argued that challenges to the superseded letters were moot, and no citations had issued for violations while those letters were operative. Whether undiscovered violations might still be enforced was unclear. Judicial note 2 explained that the court need not resolve that question because its ruling on the final letter covered the earlier versions. The final disposition dismissed the petitions rather than vacating the letters or deciding an individual operator’s liability. (1112 n.2, 1113.)

The assignment’s later discussion of CFR publication and subsequent cases is textbook commentary. This brief presents the 1993 opinion’s actual test and reservations; it does not treat that historical formulation as a comprehensive certification of later doctrine.
