# Morgan v. Gay — CAFA appeal timing
466 F.3d 276 (3d Cir. 2006) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and procedural posture

New Jersey purchasers of StriVectin-SD skin cream sued in state superior court, alleging false advertising and other claims. The defendants removed the class action to federal court in March 2006. On August 7, the district court granted the purchasers’ remand motion because it found removal jurisdiction lacking. The defendants applied to the Third Circuit for leave to appeal on August 16—seven days later under the then-applicable counting rule, which excluded weekends and holidays. Their application invoked the Class Action Fairness Act’s special provision for discretionary review of class-action remand orders. (277 & n. 1.)

Section 1453(c)(1), as enacted in 2005, allowed a court of appeals to accept such an appeal if the application was made not less than seven days after the remand order. That wording appeared to establish a waiting period rather than a deadline. The court addressed whether to read it literally or as imposing a deadline of not more than seven days. Judges Maryanne Trump Barry, D. Brooks Smith, and Richard L. Nygaard formed the panel. Smith wrote the unanimous October 16, 2006 opinion; there are no separate writings. Statutory interpretation received plenary review. (276–277.)

## The interpretive problem

The court treated acceptance of a CAFA remand appeal as discretionary. Timing was an important factor in deciding whether to grant the requested review. The opinion’s question therefore arose at the permission-to-appeal stage, not in a decision on whether the class action ultimately belonged in federal court. The defendants’ application was filed on the seventh counted day, so it fit the short deadline the court concluded Congress had intended. (277.)

The panel acknowledged the difficulty of replacing an unambiguous term with its opposite. Legislative history ordinarily demands caution because a statute is voted on by many legislators with potentially different purposes, and enacted language generally controls. The court did not claim a general power to rewrite any provision whose policy it disliked. It identified an exceptional situation in which it considered the literal text demonstrably inconsistent with uncontested legislative intent and the word choice a typographical error. (277–278.)

## Evidence of the intended deadline

The Senate report described discretionary review as a way to develop appellate interpretations of CAFA without unduly delaying class-action litigation. It stated that applications should be filed within seven days. The statute’s adjacent requirement that accepted appeals be disposed of within 60 days reinforced the theme of prompt resolution. The panel considered these sources consistent and unequivocal evidence of a filing deadline, rather than a cooling-off period followed by unlimited time to seek review. (278.)

Literal application also threatened strategic delay. A party losing the remand dispute could postpone its application until after months or years of pretrial activity, assess intermediate rulings, and only then seek to change the forum. Meanwhile, an application submitted on day six would be premature. The panel quoted other circuits’ descriptions of the incongruity: a minimum wait at the front end coupled with no closing deadline. That structure, in its view, defeated the very expedition reflected in the report and neighboring provision. (278–279.)

The Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits had already read the provision as imposing a maximum seven-day interval. The Third Circuit agreed that there was no plausible reason for Congress to create the literal alternative. Its reference to Judge Leventhal’s warning about selecting favorable legislative history was followed by the conclusion that here the available evidence spoke consistently. Footnote 2 identifies the source attributing Leventhal’s observation; it is not additional evidence of CAFA’s enactment. (279 & n. 2.)

## Holding and the precise scope of the disposition

The court held that § 1453(c)(1) should be read to require an application not more than seven days after the remand order. The defendants had filed on the seventh counted day under the then-applicable Rule 26(a), satisfying that construction. The opinion concludes with that interpretive holding. It does not itself set out a separate final order granting permission, reverse the district court’s jurisdiction determination, or adjudicate the purchasers’ advertising claims. Those additional dispositions should not be inferred from a report devoted to the timing issue. (277 & n. 1; 279.)

The assigned Katzmann passage describes this Third Circuit construction in contrast with the Seventh Circuit’s later literal reading in Spivey v. Vertrue. That comparison illustrates how courts can differ over correcting apparent drafting errors even when the underlying drafting problem is widely recognized. Katzmann also reports Congress’s later revision; that is subsequent history, not language that was before this panel in 2006. (Assigned printed 99.)
