# Richardson-Merrell Inc. v. Koller

**472 U.S. 424 (1985) — Supreme Court of the United States — June 17, 1985.** Justice O'Connor wrote for Chief Justice Burger and Justices Brennan, White, Marshall, Blackmun, and Rehnquist. Brennan also concurred separately; Stevens dissented; Powell did not participate. The participating justices divided 7–1. The assigned Civil Procedure account is Note 1(a), printed page 1227, physical PDF page 620.

## Underlying claim and counsel's conduct

Anne Koller was born in 1979 without normal arms or legs. Through her guardians, she sued Richardson-Merrell, alleging that her mother had taken the manufacturer's antinausea drug Bendectin early in pregnancy and that the drug caused her birth defects. Those allegations were not resolved by the Supreme Court. She initially had Miami and Washington counsel; the Los Angeles firm Butler, Jefferson, Dan & Allis later led trial preparation. James Butler and Nicholas Allis appeared pro hac vice, and at least eight lawyers from four firms were counsel of record as trial approached. 472 U.S. at 426.

Two episodes produced the disqualification dispute. In December 1982, Allis's secretary, Krystyna Janowski, called the defense firm and said the suit was fraudulent and the mother had not taken Bendectin during the crucial early pregnancy period. She later regretted those calls and reported efforts by defense investigators to obtain a statement. Allis visited her, accompanied on one occasion by an investigator who secretly recorded the conversation, and obtained a signed statement denying that she had heard the mother or anyone else say the mother had not taken the drug. Janowski subsequently recanted that statement. These competing assertions prompted hearings and discovery; they were not a Supreme Court determination that the drug-exposure allegation was true or false. Id. at 426–427.

Separately, the trial court excluded evidence concerning other children with superficially similar birth defects. Butler then submitted Drug Experience Reports to the FDA, including reports about those children, urged removal of Bendectin from the market, and provided materials to a newspaper reporter. The court also excluded reports submitted more than a year after the children's births, a category encompassing Butler's fourteen reports. After the anticipated jury pool had been called, the newspaper published an article discussing the case and excluded reports. Id. at 427–428.

The manufacturer moved to disqualify Butler, Allis, and their firm. After evidentiary hearings and a show-cause process, the District Court found that Allis had attempted to obstruct investigation of a crucial witness and Butler had sought to prejudice the defense and circumvent evidentiary rulings. It revoked their pro hac vice admissions and their firm's appearance, noting that other competent counsel already represented Koller. These were trial-court findings, whose correctness the Supreme Court expressly did not decide. Id. at 428–429, 441.

## Procedural route and issue

Koller appealed immediately. The D.C. Circuit stayed the entire trial-court proceeding, accepted jurisdiction under § 1291, and held the disqualification invalid. Although counsel had suggested § 1292(b) certification in responding to the show-cause order, no post-disqualification certification motion apparently followed; the asserted appellate basis was § 1291 alone. The Supreme Court granted review of both jurisdiction and the merits of disqualification but ultimately decided only jurisdiction. Id. at 429 & n.1, 441.

The question was whether a civil-case order disqualifying counsel qualified for immediate appeal as a collateral order. The Court held that it did not. The order was not a final merits judgment, and the narrow collateral-order doctrine required conclusive determination, an important issue completely separate from the merits, and effective unreviewability after final judgment. Id. at 429–432.

## Why policy concerns did not create jurisdiction

The D.C. Circuit emphasized that civil cases lack the same urgency as criminal prosecutions, disqualification causes disruption, lawyers may need to vindicate their reputations, and tactical disqualification motions can harass opponents. The Supreme Court acknowledged those concerns but rejected treating them as an independent source of jurisdiction. Civil interlocutory appeals also cause delay; the stay here exemplified that cost. Substitution of counsel may require preparation time, but appeals of both proper and improper disqualifications would introduce additional delays. Id. at 432–434.

A lawyer's personal wish for vindication could diverge from the client's interest, whereas the ethical decision to appeal must serve the client. Conditional alternatives remained: § 1292(b) certification, exceptional mandamus, and review after an unsatisfactory final judgment. The Court also identified possible Judicial Council relief for an egregious reputational injury caused by judicial misconduct. Footnote 2 carefully distinguished such administrative relief from appellate review of an allegedly erroneous ruling. It did not promise a Judicial Council remedy for every disqualification. Id. at 434–435 & n.2.

Tactical abuse did not displace the district judge's primary responsibility for supervising prejudgment conduct. Mistakes and added expense accompany many pretrial rulings. If those possibilities alone justified immediate appeals, the exception would overwhelm finality. The statutory policy preferred deferring correction of some errors rather than repeatedly interrupting the trial process. Id. at 435–436.

## The collateral-order analysis and the prejudice reservation

The Court applied the reasoning of the criminal-disqualification decision *Flanagan*. If erroneous disqualification requires no showing of prejudice to justify reversal, its propriety can be reviewed after final judgment just as effectively as before trial. If prejudice must be demonstrated, the inquiry into its effect on the outcome makes the question inseparable from the merits. On either premise, the second or third collateral-order requirement fails. Id. at 436–439.

Importantly, the Court **did not decide whether prejudice was required** for reversal after erroneous disqualification in civil or criminal cases. The D.C. Circuit's concern that prejudice might be hard to show primarily implicated the eventual standard for reviewing a final judgment, not a reason to create immediate appellate jurisdiction. Nor could an adequate misconduct record in this particular case overcome the categorical analysis. Id. at 437–439.

Civil disqualification orders often overlap the merits even apart from that alternative analysis. A lawyer-witness issue requires evaluating the likely testimony and trial. A misconduct issue may require assessing whether conduct will affect future proceedings, the underlying claims, and the trial judge's ability to proceed impartially. Thus the category is not completely separate merely because some individual orders might appear so. Id. at 439–440.

## Separate opinions

Brennan joined the Court on the understanding that appellate courts would develop review standards effectively protecting a litigant's qualified right to chosen counsel. He emphasized that tactical disqualification can let an opponent dictate representation. Although the civil case differed from both a motion to disqualify the opponent's lawyer and criminal proceedings, later review could protect the right if courts were willing when necessary to set aside even verdicts reached after lengthy trials. He also thought that risk could discourage abusive disqualification motions. He did not endorse automatic immediate appeals. Id. at 441–442.

Stevens dissented. He viewed the loss of chosen counsel as an immediate prejudice to a fundamental interest. In civil litigation, however, he would hesitate to reverse a completed trial without some effect on the outcome, and proving that effect could be practically impossible amid the many variables affecting a verdict. He considered pretrial misconduct and the choice-of-counsel injury separate from the merits and therefore would allow collateral-order review. He also agreed with the D.C. Circuit that this disqualification was legally erroneous and would affirm its judgment. Id. at 442–444 & nn.1–3.

## Exact judgment and source scope

The Court **vacated the D.C. Circuit's judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss the appeal for want of jurisdiction**. Its preliminary description uses “reverse,” but its operative mandate at page 441 expressly says vacate and remand. The Court expressed no view on the merits of the District Court's disqualification and did not adjudicate the Bendectin claim. Id. at 426, 441.
