# Daimler AG v. Bauman

**571 U.S. 117 (2014) · Supreme Court of the United States · January 14, 2014**

## An Argentina-centered suit filed in California

Twenty-two residents of Argentina sued Daimler in the Northern District of California in 2004. All but one were Argentine citizens; the remaining plaintiff was a Chilean citizen residing in Argentina. They alleged that Mercedes-Benz Argentina, a subsidiary of Daimler’s predecessor, had collaborated with Argentine security forces during the 1976–1983 military dictatorship to kidnap, detain, torture, and kill workers, including plaintiffs or their relatives. The alleged conduct centered on the subsidiary’s Argentine plant. None occurred in California or elsewhere in the United States. The complaint sought to hold Daimler vicariously liable under the Alien Tort Statute, Torture Victim Protection Act, and California and Argentine law. These were allegations, not findings that Daimler or its subsidiary had committed the asserted abuses. 571 U.S. at 120–123 & nn.1–2.

Daimler was a German public stock company headquartered in Stuttgart, manufacturing Mercedes-Benz vehicles primarily in Germany. The operative complaint named Daimler alone; Mercedes-Benz USA was not a defendant. Plaintiffs sought to establish general jurisdiction over Daimler through MBUSA’s California business. General jurisdiction would permit claims unrelated to the forum activity, including this entirely Argentine controversy. Plaintiffs never attempted to establish specific jurisdiction. Id. at 121–123, 133–134 & n.12.

MBUSA was a Delaware entity with its principal place of business in New Jersey. As Daimler’s exclusive United States importer and distributor, it purchased vehicles in Germany and distributed them to independent dealerships nationwide. It maintained a regional office, vehicle preparation center, and classic center in California and was the largest supplier of luxury vehicles to that market. Its California sales represented 2.4 percent of Daimler’s worldwide sales. The distribution agreement, however, described MBUSA as an independent contractor buying and selling for its own account, denied that it was Daimler’s general or special agent, and denied authority to bind Daimler. Corporate affiliation, substantial sales, and contractual supervision therefore presented a jurisdictional question rather than automatically establishing the parent’s presence. Id. at 123–124 & n.3.

## Proceedings and the issues actually presented

After jurisdictional discovery, the district court dismissed. It found Daimler’s own California contacts insufficient for general jurisdiction and declined to attribute MBUSA’s contacts through agency. A Ninth Circuit panel initially affirmed, with Judge Reinhardt dissenting. On rehearing, the panel withdrew that opinion and adopted Reinhardt’s contrary approach, treating MBUSA as Daimler’s agent and exercising jurisdiction. The court later denied rehearing and rehearing en banc over eight judges’ dissent. Id. at 124–125.

Several limits framed Supreme Court review. Plaintiffs had not challenged the district court’s conclusion about Daimler’s direct contacts and had not claimed that MBUSA was Daimler’s alter ego. Daimler, conversely, had failed below to contest the assertion that MBUSA itself was subject to general jurisdiction in California. The Court consequently assumed MBUSA was at home there for this decision. That assumption was not an adjudication that the Delaware and New Jersey subsidiary’s California sales necessarily established its own general jurisdiction. Id. at 133–134.

Under Civil Rule 4(k)(1)(A), the federal district court ordinarily followed the forum State’s jurisdictional limits. California’s long-arm statute extended to the constitutional limit, so the question became whether due process permitted California courts to exercise all-purpose jurisdiction over Daimler on these facts. Id. at 125.

## Holding and exact judgment

California could not exercise general personal jurisdiction over Daimler in this case. The Ninth Circuit’s expansive agency rationale was unsustainable. More decisively, even assuming MBUSA was at home in California and its contacts could be attributed to Daimler, those contacts did not render Daimler itself essentially at home there. The Supreme Court reversed the Ninth Circuit’s judgment. It did not decide the underlying human-rights claims or find that the alleged abuses had or had not occurred. Id. at 134–142.

Justice Ginsburg wrote for eight Justices: Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Breyer, Alito, and Kagan joined. Justice Sotomayor concurred only in the judgment. All nine agreed that jurisdiction should be rejected, but Sotomayor disagreed with the Court’s rationale. There was no dissent from the result. The complete Court opinion includes twenty numbered judicial footnotes; Sotomayor’s opinion includes twelve, all considered here.

## Why the Ninth Circuit’s agency theory was too broad

The Ninth Circuit asked whether a subsidiary performed services so important to the foreign parent that the parent would undertake substantially similar services if the subsidiary did not exist. The Supreme Court explained why that hypothetical was predisposed to find agency: a corporation usually would arrange another way to perform activities it currently accomplishes through a contractor, distributor, or subsidiary. Importance alone would thus expose foreign corporations to all-purpose jurisdiction wherever they had affiliates. The circuit’s additional reliance on the parent’s contractual right to supervise MBUSA did not adequately narrow that sweeping approach. Id. at 134–136 & nn.14–15.

The Court did not adopt an absolute rule prohibiting attribution of a subsidiary’s contacts, nor did it resolve whether agency could ever support general jurisdiction. Its holding rejected this agency analysis. It separately recognized that agency or distribution arrangements can matter for specific jurisdiction: a corporation can purposefully avail itself of a forum by directing agents or distributors to act there. The relationship between those activities and the claim remains essential to distinguishing that route from jurisdiction over every possible claim. Id. at 135 & n.13.

## Being at home requires more than substantial business

The Court situated general jurisdiction within the history following *International Shoe*. Specific jurisdiction had expanded around the relationship among defendant, forum, and litigation. General jurisdiction retained a narrower role. *Perkins* supplied the exceptional example: the Philippine mining corporation’s president directed its wartime activities from Ohio, making Ohio its principal, temporary place of business. *Helicopteros* and *Goodyear* rejected treating purchases or dispersed product sales as sufficient for all-purpose jurisdiction. The Court read those cases as requiring affiliations that make the corporation essentially at home. Id. at 125–133 & nn.4–11.

A corporation’s place of incorporation and principal place of business are the paradigm forums. They ordinarily identify clear, readily ascertainable places where plaintiffs can sue on any claim. But those are paradigms rather than an exclusive two-place list. Footnote 19 expressly preserves the possibility that an exceptional corporation’s operations elsewhere might be so substantial and of such a nature as to make it at home there. Daimler’s California activities did not approach that level, so the Court did not explore the exception’s outer boundary. Id. at 137–139 & n.19.

The Court rejected the proposed test of a substantial, continuous, and systematic course of business in any State. *International Shoe* had used continuous and systematic activity when discussing claims connected to that activity; its formulation for unrelated claims demanded substantially more. The general-jurisdiction question concerns the nature of the corporation’s relationship to the forum, rather than the bare existence of sustained commerce. Id. at 138–139 & nn.17–18.

Footnote 20 further instructs courts to appraise corporate activities in their entirety, nationwide and worldwide. A corporation operating in many places cannot ordinarily be at home in all of them merely because its sales are sizable. Neither Daimler nor MBUSA was incorporated or headquartered in California. Attributing MBUSA’s contacts did not transform California into Daimler’s home; accepting the contrary argument would expose Daimler to suits about unrelated events worldwide in every State with substantial sales. Id. at 139–140 & n.20.

The majority also distinguished its approach from a separate, free-standing reasonableness inquiry. The familiar balancing of burdens, forum interests, and other interests had been applied after identifying a basis for specific jurisdiction. When a corporation is genuinely at home for general jurisdiction, the majority considered a second-step reasonableness inquiry superfluous. This point matters because Sotomayor would have used that alternative test to reach the result. Id. at 139–141 n.20.

## International context reinforced the conclusion

The Ninth Circuit had stressed the United States’ interest in redressing international human-rights abuses. The Supreme Court responded that intervening *Kiobel* and *Mohamad* decisions had undermined the asserted federal claims: the former addressed the Alien Tort Statute’s extraterritorial application and the latter limited Torture Victim Protection Act liability to natural persons. It did not use this discussion to enter a separate merits judgment on all claims. Id. at 140–141.

The Court also compared narrower jurisdictional practices abroad and considered the Solicitor General’s warning that expansive jurisdiction over foreign corporations through American subsidiaries created international friction, impeded negotiations over recognition of judgments, and could discourage foreign investment. Comity reinforced the constitutional conclusion reached through the at-home analysis. It was not an independently announced numerical limit on sales or an automatic exemption for all foreign defendants. Id. at 141–142.

## Sotomayor’s narrower agreement and broader objections

Sotomayor would have assumed the conceded contacts and rejected jurisdiction because exercising it was unreasonable in this particular dispute. Argentine plaintiffs sought to sue a German defendant over Argentine events, and they had not shown that California was more convenient than Germany, a sovereign with a greater interest. She would apply the reasonableness approach used in *Asahi* without deciding whether it belongs in every general-jurisdiction case. Id. at 142–146 & concurrence nn.1–2.

She objected both to the Court’s procedure and its substantive rule. In her view, the argument that Daimler remained outside general jurisdiction even with the subsidiary’s contacts attributed had not been argued or decided below and appeared only in a footnote in its Supreme Court brief. Because Daimler had conceded MBUSA’s general jurisdiction, the record did not fully develop the subsidiary’s management functions and other activities. The majority answered that its granted question encompassed this inquiry, that supporting amici had addressed it, and that deciding it after *Goodyear* was fair. These are competing accounts of decisional scope, not a unanimous conclusion that the issue was properly or improperly presented. Id. at 136–137 n.16, 146–149 & concurrence nn.3–5.

On substance, Sotomayor understood earlier decisions to focus on the quality and quantity of forum contacts, not their proportional size within a global enterprise. She disputed the majority’s reading of *Perkins*, arguing that its Ohio activities could support jurisdiction despite activities elsewhere. She feared that the comparison would favor multinational enterprises over smaller companies, enlarge discovery into worldwide operations, restrict States’ authority, and leave injured individuals without an American forum. She also identified the contrast with transient service on individuals and proposed venue transfer, forum non conveniens, reasonableness analysis, or legislative changes as less sweeping responses. Those criticisms belong to her concurrence, rather than the Court’s governing rule. Id. at 149–160 & concurrence nn.6–12.
