# Crabtree v. Elizabeth Arden Sales Corp.
305 N.Y. 48, 110 N.E.2d 551 (1953) | Contracts

## Negotiations and the three records of the employment arrangement
Nate Crabtree negotiated in September 1947 to become sales manager for Elizabeth Arden's cosmetics business. He would leave secure, well-paid work for an unfamiliar industry and wanted a definite period of employment. He initially sought three years at $25,000 annually. The company's president offered two years, with annual salary rates of $20,000 for the first six months, $25,000 for the next six months, and $30,000 for the second year, plus $5,000 annual expenses. Crabtree called that offer interesting. Arden then directed her personal secretary to record it on a telephone-order blank. The unsigned memorandum named the parties and participants, stated the compensation progression, and included the phrase “2 years to make good.” 305 N.Y. at 51–52.

A few days later, Crabtree called the executive vice-president and general manager, Robert Johns, and telegraphed Arden accepting the invitation to join. She replied with a welcome. When Crabtree reported, the company prepared a payroll-change card dated September 30 and effective October 22. Johns initialed it. It identified the parties, job classification, and the same sequence of annual salary rates, but did not expressly state a two-year term. Id. at 52.

Crabtree received the scheduled raise after six months, but not the increase due after one year. Johns and the comptroller, Carstens, told him they would try to resolve that problem with Arden. Carstens prepared and signed another payroll-change card specifying the raise from $25,000 to $30,000 “per contractual arrangements with Miss Arden.” Arden refused approval. After unsuccessful further discussions, Crabtree left and sued for breach. Id. at 52–53.

The evidentiary problem was precise. The two authenticated payroll records provided the employment relationship and compensation but lacked the disputed duration. The original office note supplied duration but had no signature. No single document alone recorded every essential term under an employer signature.

## Trial and the question on appeal
The corporation denied making a two-year agreement and alternatively asserted the statute of frauds. The trial court rejected both defenses and awarded approximately $14,000. The Appellate Division affirmed with two justices dissenting. At the Court of Appeals, the principal issue was whether the writings together satisfied Personal Property Law § 31 for an agreement that could not be performed within one year. Judge Fuld's opinion was joined by Chief Judge Loughran and Judges Lewis, Conway, Desmond, Dye, and Froessel; the Court of Appeals decision was unanimous, unlike the intermediate appeal. Id. at 53, 58.

The full opinion notes that the court considered the other argued points even though its written treatment centered on the memorandum question. Counsel disputed damages, mitigation, abandonment, and whether the employment was at will, but the judicial text does not develop a separate damages formula or detailed resolution of each contention. A brief should therefore report the affirmed award without inventing additional holdings from those arguments. Id. at 53 n.1.

## A memorandum need not have been created as a contract
The court first treated each payroll card as a signed memorandum. A document can satisfy the statute even if prepared after formation and even if its author did not intend to produce the formal evidence of a contract. What matters is a signature made to authenticate information that records contractual terms. Johns' initials and Carstens' signature served that purpose here. The court did not require an employer-signed, integrated contract form. Id. at 53–54.

That conclusion did not yet resolve duration. The question became whether the signed records could be combined with the secretary's earlier unsigned note. A statutory memorandum may consist of several writings, connected expressly or by their internal evidence of subject matter and occasion. Where all are signed, that principle presents little difficulty; where some are unsigned, courts had divided on how to establish the connection. Id. at 54–55.

## Connecting signed and unsigned writings
One approach required the signed writing itself to refer to the unsigned document, and would not permit oral testimony to establish the necessary connection. The more flexible approach permitted writings clearly relating to the same subject matter or transaction to be read together and allowed oral evidence to connect them and establish the charged party's assent to the unsigned record. After acknowledging apparently conflicting New York precedents, Fuld definitively adopted the latter approach. Id. at 54–55.

The safeguards matter as much as the flexibility. Every essential term must appear in the writings. At least one signed writing must establish a contractual relationship between the parties. The unsigned writing must refer on its face to the same transaction. Oral testimony may explain the circumstances connecting the papers and show assent to the unsigned contents; it cannot supply a missing contractual term from memory alone. If that testimony does not convincingly establish connection and assent, the judge may determine that the statute is unsatisfied as a matter of law. Id. at 55–56.

The court recognized a residual risk of false testimony but preferred that bounded risk to refusing enforcement solely because a signed paper did not specifically identify another document. A mechanical cross-reference requirement could itself facilitate repudiation of agreements the records clearly showed had been made. The rule preserved written evidence of terms while avoiding a formal condition the statute did not expressly impose.

## Why the documents qualified here
All three records described the same employment transaction through matching parties, position, and salary structure. That detailed correspondence made it highly implausible that they referred to different agreements. Carstens' signed reference to contractual arrangements with Arden also indicated a broader agreement that oral evidence could explain. The unsigned memorandum had been prepared by Arden's own personal secretary at Arden's direction, and the participants' conduct supported employer assent to its contents. The courts below were justified in treating the papers together as the memorandum. Id. at 56–57.

The holding does not mean that any unsigned note mentioning the same people becomes enforceable. The matching details, provenance, surrounding conduct, and proof of assent did the connecting work here. Nor did the Court of Appeals use the unsigned record to create an agreement the trial court had not found. The trial court had already found the two-year bargain; the issue was whether its written evidence met the statutory requirement.

## “Two years to make good” recorded a definite term
The corporation also disputed whether the office notation actually supplied duration. Fuld found that the phrase identified a two-year period, after which Crabtree could be discharged if he had not made good. Without that notation the employment would be at will, and the court would not treat its deliberate inclusion as purposeless. The staged salary increases further supported the trial court's reading that the parties contemplated a protected period rather than purely at-will employment. Id. at 57–58.

Even if the phrase were cryptic or ambiguous, oral evidence could explain its meaning. That use of testimony differs from supplying an omitted term: duration was already written down, and the negotiations, relationships, and Crabtree's insistence on security helped interpret it. The trial factfinder was entitled to conclude that the language granted the tenure he sought. Id. at 58.

## Judgment and practical significance
The Court of Appeals affirmed with costs, leaving the employee's roughly $14,000 trial recovery intact. It did not remand for a new attempt to prove a writing or declare the employment at will. 305 N.Y. at 58.

The case separates three questions often collapsed in a statute-of-frauds dispute: whether an agreement was made, whether its essential terms appear in a sufficient written memorandum, and how those written terms should be interpreted. A collection of records can answer the second question even though no single document is a formal signed contract, provided the connection and assent are established and the essential terms remain in the writings themselves.
