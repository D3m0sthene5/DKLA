# Tulsa Professional Collection Services, Inc. v. Pope

**485 U.S. 478 (1988) · Supreme Court of the United States · April 19, 1988 · Civil Procedure**

Justice O’Connor wrote for seven Justices; Brennan, White, Marshall, Stevens, Scalia, and Kennedy joined. Justice Blackmun concurred in the result without a separate opinion. Chief Justice Rehnquist dissented. The Court reversed the Oklahoma Supreme Court and remanded, producing an eight-to-one result but a seven-Justice majority opinion. The assigned account appears in *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 148, printed pages 246–247.

## Facts and procedural history

H. Everett Pope, Jr., entered St. John Medical Center in Tulsa in November 1978 and died there on April 2, 1979. His will designated his wife, Jo Anne Pope, as executrix. She petitioned the Tulsa County District Court to begin probate. After a hearing, the court admitted the will, appointed her, issued letters testamentary, and ordered her to give notice to creditors immediately. (482.)

Oklahoma required claims arising on contract generally to be presented within two months after the first publication of creditor notice. Following the statute and court order, the executrix published in the *Tulsa Daily Legal News* for two consecutive weeks beginning July 17, 1979. The notice told creditors to present their claims within two months of that first publication. The statute generally barred late claims permanently, although it contained exceptions for out-of-state creditors, mortgages, and debts not yet due. Oklahoma did not also have a longer nonclaim period running automatically from death; its provisions instead shortened the post-publication period in certain delayed or intestate proceedings. (479–482.)

Tulsa Professional Collection Services was a hospital subsidiary and assignee of the bill for Pope’s final illness. Neither it nor the hospital filed a timely claim. In October 1983, the collection company asked the court to compel payment, relying on a statutory command that an executrix pay expenses of the decedent’s last sickness. The probate court held that this obligation did not excuse compliance with the filing deadline and denied the application. The Court of Appeals affirmed; when the company raised due process on rehearing, the court rejected that challenge in a supplemental opinion. (482–483.)

The Oklahoma Supreme Court affirmed both rulings. It distinguished probate notice from notice making someone a party, emphasized that probate did not directly adjudicate the creditor’s claim, and treated the nonclaim provision as a self-executing limitation that cut off claims through the passage of time. The United States Supreme Court reviewed the federal notice issue and reversed. (483–484.)

## Issue and conditional holding

Did newspaper publication alone satisfy due process before Oklahoma’s court-triggered two-month nonclaim period extinguished a creditor’s protected claim?

The Court held that **known or reasonably ascertainable creditors must receive notice by mail or a comparably reliable means**. The probate process involved enough state action to invoke the Fourteenth Amendment, and an unsecured contractual claim was protected property. But the record did not establish whether this particular creditor was known or reasonably ascertainable. The Court therefore remanded for that factual inquiry; it did not itself order the hospital bill paid or find the collection company’s identity discoverable. (485–491.)

## Why the probate bar implicated due process

The majority first identified the affected interest. A cause of action for an unpaid bill was intangible property, and the statute extinguished that interest when no timely claim was filed. It did not matter that the notice invited the creditor to participate rather than announced an existing party’s merits hearing. A proceeding could adversely affect an interest without resolving its substantive validity. (485, 488.)

The harder question was state action. Mere legislative adoption of an ordinary limitation period did not generally create a constitutional duty to notify every potential claimant of an approaching deadline. The majority accepted that general distinction and the executrix’s reading of *Texaco v. Short*, but rejected her characterization of this statute as self-executing. Here the deadline did not run simply because time had elapsed after death. Probate had to begin, the court had to appoint an executor or executrix, and publication followed that appointment. The court ordered notice, and proof of publication had to be filed with it. The court’s involvement activated and permeated the process that terminated the claim. (485–487.)

Accordingly, the constitutional distinction rested on the way the particular period became operative, not merely on a comparison between Oklahoma’s short period and *Texaco*’s long one. The Court expressly left undecided whether two months was itself unconstitutionally brief. It also reserved the characterization of other states’ longer nonclaim periods running from death. (488.)

## Required efforts and their limits

Applying *Mullane* and *Mennonite*, O’Connor balanced the practical need for effective notice against prompt estate administration. Creditors might not know that their debtor had died or that probate had begun. An executor with a beneficial interest in the estate might also lack an incentive to call attention to claims that could reduce it. Newspaper publication was a poor way of reaching those creditors. These were general considerations about the process, rather than a finding that Jo Anne Pope deliberately concealed the bill. (489.)

The state nevertheless had a legitimate interest in swift settlement. The majority accommodated it through a bounded notice duty: reasonably diligent efforts to identify creditors, followed by inexpensive mailed notice to those known or reasonably discoverable. Mail already served other probate functions, including notice to heirs, legatees, and devisees. Neither impracticable extended searches nor notice to everyone with a merely conjectural claim was required. Publication could suffice for creditors who could not reasonably be identified. (489–491.)

The executrix knew that her husband had spent a long time in the hospital. But the Court said this awareness did not necessarily establish knowledge of the collection company’s claim. No lower court had decided the relevant inquiry. Remand was needed to determine whether reasonably diligent efforts would have identified the claimant and uncovered its claim. Only if its identity was known or reasonably ascertainable would extinguishing the claim without direct notice violate due process under the announced rule. (491.)

## Rehnquist’s dissent

Rehnquist considered the state-action distinction between this case and *Texaco* unpersuasive. In both, state law prescribed a time bar and state courts later applied it to defeat a claim. Probate’s routine administrative steps did not, in his view, justify treating the two statutes differently. For the affected claimant, the source of the trigger—an acquisition of a mineral interest or an executor’s published notice—made little difference. (492–493.)

He described ordinary probate as chiefly an uncontested transfer process in which debts had to be collected and proved before distribution. The court’s involvement was perfunctory administration, not an adjudication extinguishing this creditor’s rights. He read “self-executing” to distinguish a statutory lapse from a later judicial determination that a lapse occurred, rather than to require a complete absence of official participation in the trigger. He also worried that adding an official publication safeguard to an otherwise automatic statute could paradoxically generate a constitutional obligation that the statute previously lacked. Like the majority, he did not decide whether the two-month period was independently too short. (493–494.)

## Judgment and assigned-source distinction

The Court **reversed and remanded**. It did not invalidate all probate nonclaim statutes, require actual receipt of every notice, or resolve the bill’s substantive entitlement. Its directed method was mail or an equivalent means reasonably calculated to inform the relevant creditor, with identification limited by reasonable diligence. (490–491.)

The textbook account says the claimant’s name and address were reasonably ascertainable and describes due process as violated on that basis. The complete opinion is more qualified: the Court could not decide ascertainability on the record and remanded for that determination. The brief therefore retains the textbook’s teaching point about direct notice while correcting its description of the case-specific finding. The assigned conjectural-claims exception is supported by the majority’s discussion at 490.
