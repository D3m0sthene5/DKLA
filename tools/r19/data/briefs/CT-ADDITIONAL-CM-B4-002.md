# Delliponti v. DeAngelis
545 Pa. 434, 681 A.2d 1261 (1996) | Contracts

## Court, facts, and procedural history

The Pennsylvania Supreme Court, in Chief Justice Nix’s July 30, 1996 opinion, restored an order reinstating a municipal employee with back pay. Justices Zappala and Flaherty dissented. Although assigned for its mitigation analysis, the case first required deciding whether Norristown’s charter and personnel code gave the employee a protected expectation of continued employment. (545 Pa. 434, 436–437, 444.)

Anne Delliponti began as a borough secretary in September 1976 and became administrative assistant to the chief of police, a confidential position. She was the most senior of four nonunion administrative assistants. On January 22, 1991, the borough told her that her position would be eliminated three days later. In her Local Agency Law appeal, the trial court heard the dispute over whether economic necessity was the actual reason. She produced no evidence of another reason, and the court found the borough acted without malice. The remaining matter proceeded in the nature of a case stated. The Supreme Court therefore did not find a retaliatory or malicious discharge. (437 & n. 1.)

Common pleas held that the failure to exempt her position from the charter’s civil-service system protected her employment. Eliminating that position without the required process was an adjudication affecting a property right. Because the code contained no applicable layoff rules, the court concluded that a hearing would accomplish nothing and ordered reinstatement with back pay. The Commonwealth Court reversed, reasoning that the borough’s omission had not made her a civil-service employee or constrained an economic reduction in force. Delliponti appealed. (437–438.)

## Protected employment and the missing regulations

The majority explained that public employment is not itself a property right. The employee must have an expectation of continuation arising from statute, contract, or a quasi-contractual source; an at-will employee lacks the hearing entitlement at issue. Under Local Agency Law, an agency determination affecting protected rights is an adjudication requiring notice and a hearing. (438–439.)

Section 512D of the borough charter required civil-service rules governing appointment, discipline, removal, and layoffs, while permitting affirmative exemptions. Section 513 required a comprehensive merit-based personnel system with provisions addressing reductions in force. The code supplied just-cause disciplinary protections, set out in note 2, and separate economic-furlough provisions for police and fire employees. It omitted economic-layoff rules for Delliponti’s position but did not exempt it. Reading the charter provisions together, the majority held that the borough’s failure to exempt her afforded a legitimate expectation of continued employment. The council could not defeat the charter’s protection through its own failure to supply the required rules. (439–442 & n. 2.)

The borough argued that protection against misconduct-based dismissal did not prevent eliminating a position for financial reasons. The court distinguished Hill and Sergi: those cases involved limited statutory protections that did not cover the relevant employment action. Here the charter specifically required protection and rules addressing layoffs and reductions in force unless an exemption was adopted. Economic necessity therefore did not eliminate the protected interest. The three-day notification failed to provide adequate notice and a hearing. With no governing layoff regulations, the majority accepted reinstatement and back pay as the appropriate remedy. (441–443.)

## Mitigation: what the employer had to prove

The borough separately argued that Delliponti failed to mitigate because she had made telephone inquiries about only three advertised job openings and pursued no others. It asserted that comparable jobs were unquestionably available. The lower courts had not resolved that defense: common pleas did not mention it, and the Commonwealth Court’s rejection of the employment right made it unnecessary there. The Supreme Court rejected the defense on the record before it. (443–444.)

An injured employee must make reasonable efforts to reduce loss. Employment damages ordinarily equal promised wages less earnings actually received or reasonably obtainable through diligent efforts to secure similar employment. But the employer bears the burden of showing avoidable loss. That requires proof both that substantially equivalent positions were available and that the employee failed to exercise reasonable diligence in seeking them. An allegation of available comparable jobs does not establish actual vacancies available to this employee. Even assuming her efforts were inadequate, the borough had not proved the availability element. The decision thus does not approve every job search limited to three calls; it identifies a failure of the employer’s proof. (443–444.)

## Dissent and exact result

Justice Zappala, joined by Justice Flaherty, would have affirmed the Commonwealth Court. In their view, a legislative body’s failure to enact implementing rules did not confer the rights that those rules would have created. Delliponti remained at will, had not become a civil-service employee, and therefore had no protected property interest in her position. Their objection concerned the underlying employment entitlement; the dissent did not separately resolve mitigation. (444.)

The Supreme Court reversed the Commonwealth Court and reinstated the Montgomery County common-pleas order, restoring reinstatement with back pay. The seven-justice lineup and the two dissenters establish a 5–2 result. No numerical back-pay amount was determined in the reproduced opinion. (436, 444.)
