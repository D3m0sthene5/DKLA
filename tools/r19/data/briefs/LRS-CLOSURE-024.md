# Citizens to Preserve Overton Park, Inc. v. Brinegar
494 F.2d 1212 (6th Cir. 1974) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, consolidated appeals, and result

On April 3, 1974, Judge Celebrezze wrote for a unanimous Sixth Circuit panel with Judges Weick and Peck in Nos. 73-1668 and 73-1669. Citizens to Preserve Overton Park and the Secretary of Transportation separately appealed the district court's order requiring another administrative decision after Volpe rejected the park route. The state highway commissioner defended that order. The court held the Secretary had complied with his statutory duties and reversed the district court's remand order. It did not order construction through the park or choose another route. There were no separate opinions. (1213–1216.)

## The highway proposal and successive reconsideration

The original 1969 approval concerned a 3.7-mile Interstate 40 extension through Memphis, including 4,000 feet through publicly wooded Overton Park. The park-protection organization challenged approval under § 4(f), which prohibited approval of a project using public parkland unless no feasible and prudent alternative existed and all possible planning minimized harm. District and appellate judgments initially sustained approval; the Supreme Court reversed in 1971 for review of the full administrative record and consideration of the relevant factors without clear error of judgment. The prior appellate dissent described in the history belonged to the 1970 appeal, not this unanimous decision. (1213.)

After a full district hearing, the case returned to the Secretary to determine whether the park route was the sole feasible and prudent route. The district court left the design approval standing and enjoined federal and state activity advancing construction. Volpe then undertook a broader review, including an environmental impact statement and public hearing under intervening legislation and regulations. On January 18, 1973 he refused approval because he could not make the required no-alternative finding or find compliance with broader environmental and noise requirements. He suggested possible alternatives but endorsed none and did not reject no-build. (1213–1214.)

Tennessee's highway commissioner petitioned on February 1, 1973 to compel compliance with the remand or dissolve the injunction against the state. He argued that Volpe had failed to make a route determination and had improperly reconsidered design. The state's position effectively demanded either approval of the park route or identification of a feasible and prudent alternative, together with specific findings about other statutory shortcomings. It feared being left without an obvious proposal assured of federal funding. (1214.)

## The order under review

The district court's May 1, 1973 order required the successor Secretary, Brinegar, to find either that no feasible and prudent alternative existed or that at least one did exist and specify it. It required identification of any environmental or noise violations and allowed reconsideration of design. The court assigned the Secretary responsibility for obtaining any additional information, discharged the state from further production obligations, and imposed a 45-day deadline. The Secretary and park-protection plaintiffs challenged the resulting additional remand in their consolidated appeals. (1214–1215.)

The Secretary argued that § 4(f) foreclosed approval unless he was convinced no feasible and prudent alternative existed, but did not require him to affirmatively establish an alternative before rejecting the proposal. The state defended the district court's requirement as necessary for adequate review. The dispute therefore concerned what rejection required, not whether approval of a park route was protected by a presumption in favor of highway construction. (1214–1215.)

## A statutory prohibition on approval did not impose an alternative-planning duty

The Sixth Circuit agreed with the Secretary. Section 4(f) scrutinized highway proposals to protect parkland and imposed no affirmative obligation to specify a particular alternative. The sole judicial note emphasized the negative statutory formulation: approval was prohibited absent the necessary finding. Congress could have expressly required identification of another route but did not. The statute's primary purpose was protecting parklands and analogous sites, rather than guaranteeing a substitute federally funded project. (1215 & n. 1.)

The court also rejected the reviewability objection. Neither the transportation statute nor the highway statute required formal findings. Volpe had suggested alternatives, and a definite finding selecting one was unnecessary to make his explanation reviewable. His action's validity would stand or fall under the appropriate review standard applied to that explanation. The court did not hold that every unexplained rejection was adequate or that a refusal to approve escaped judicial review. (1215.)

The broader federal-highway scheme assigned route selection and proposals to the state, subject to federal approval. States submitted programs and then surveys, plans, specifications, and estimates under §§ 103, 105, and 106. The Secretary approved or disapproved what the state proposed; he did not undertake the state's planning task. After disapproval, the state had to submit another proposal, on which the Secretary would again have to act. This allocation answered the fear of being left without a federally guaranteed alternative. (1215–1216.)

## Exact judgment and assigned distinction

The court held the Secretary had, at this point, complied with his duties and reversed the district court's order remanding to him. That eliminated the challenged affirmative-alternative requirement and further-remand order; it did not resurrect approval of the park route or require selection of a particular alternative. The opinion contains one judicial note and no separate writing. (1216.)
