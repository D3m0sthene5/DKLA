Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly
Citation
550 U.S. 544, 127 S. Ct. 1955, 167 L. Ed. 2d 929 (2007). Supreme Court of the United States. Argued
November 27, 2006; decided May 21, 2007. Justice Souter wrote for seven Justices. Justice Stevens
dissented; Justice Ginsburg joined his dissent except Part IV.
Casebook pp. 617–627; source PDF pp. 326–331. Full judicial opinion.
Parties
William Twombly and Lawrence Marcus sued as representatives of a proposed class of local-telephone and
high-speed-internet subscribers. The defendants, petitioners in the Supreme Court, were incumbent regional
telephone carriers, including the Bell Atlantic successor Verizon, BellSouth, Qwest, and SBC. The
subscribers alleged that an agreement among the carriers kept prices above competitive levels. Opinion of
the Court, pt. I & n. 1; CB p. 617.
Material Facts
The 1984 breakup of AT&T produced regional local-service monopolies. The Telecommunications Act of
1996 sought to introduce competition by requiring incumbent carriers to share their networks with new
competitors and permitting competition in additional markets. These statutory obligations and the carriers’
historically separate territories supplied the setting for the alleged conspiracy. The full opinion supplies this
background more fully than the edited excerpt. Opinion of the Court, pt. I; CB pp. 617–618.
The complaint alleged two related forms of parallel behavior: each incumbent impeded new carriers trying
to use its network, and the incumbents refrained from entering one another’s territories. Plaintiffs also relied
on industry contacts and a carrier executive’s reported comments about entering another incumbent’s
territory. They inferred a concerted market-allocation arrangement. These were allegations tested on a
dismissal motion; the Court had no trial finding that the carriers had agreed. Opinion of the Court, pt. I & nn.
1–2; CB pp. 623–624.
Procedural History
The Southern District of New York dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6), concluding that parallel conduct was
consistent with independent efforts to preserve each carrier’s market position and that the complaint
supplied no additional facts suggesting concerted action. The Second Circuit reversed. It understood Conley
v. Gibson to permit dismissal only when no possible set of supporting facts could establish that the parallel
conduct resulted from agreement. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to determine the pleading standard.
Opinion of the Court, pt. I; CB pp. 617–619.
Issue
Does a Sherman Act § 1 complaint state a claim under Rule 8(a)(2) when it alleges parallel anticompetitive
behavior and a conclusory agreement, without factual context plausibly suggesting that the defendants acted
pursuant to an agreement?

Rule and Holding
A complaint must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to make entitlement to relief plausible.
Detailed evidentiary pleading is unnecessary, and plausibility is not a requirement to show that liability is
more likely than not. For a § 1 conspiracy claim, however, the pleaded circumstances must suggest
agreement and give a reasonable expectation that discovery will reveal evidence of it. Parallel conduct plus
an unsupported assertion of conspiracy does not satisfy that requirement. Opinion of the Court, pt. II.B; CB
pp. 619–620.
This complaint failed because the described conduct was naturally explained by each incumbent’s
independent interests, and its assertions of agreement supplied no further factual basis. The Court rejected
use of Conley’s no-set-of-facts formula as the minimum pleading threshold. Its holding concerns the
sufficiency of this complaint, not a factual finding that no conspiracy occurred. Opinion of the Court, pts.
II.B–III; CB pp. 621–623.
Judgment
The Court reversed the Second Circuit and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion. It held that
the complaint must be dismissed; it did not adjudicate the truth of the alleged agreement after discovery or
trial. Opinion of the Court, conclusion; CB p. 623.
Reasoning
First, the substantive cause of action requires concerted conduct. Independent conduct, even knowingly
parallel conduct by firms that understand their mutual interests, does not itself establish a § 1 agreement.
Because the pleaded behavior could arise from either independent decisions or agreement, the complaint
needed a factual setting supporting the latter inference. Opinion of the Court, pts. II.A–B; CB pp. 618–620.
Second, Rule 8 requires a showing of entitlement, not labels or a recitation of elements. A court accepts
factual allegations even when their truth is doubtful, but it need not treat an unsupported legal conclusion as
an independently pleaded fact. The majority read the allegations of agreement in context: the complaint
itself derived them from the previously described parallel behavior. Opinion of the Court, pts. II.B–III; CB
pp. 619, 622.
Third, each carrier already had an incentive to resist competitors and preserve its own regional dominance.
The historical monopoly arrangement also supplied an independent explanation for staying out of neighbors’
territories. These were the majority’s inferences from the pleading and market setting. The dissent contested
their use to dispose of the complaint before evidentiary development. Opinion of the Court, pt. III; CB pp.
622–623.
Fourth, the majority considered the unusually broad and costly discovery threatened by the proposed
nationwide, multiyear antitrust litigation. It reasoned that later summary judgment and ordinary case
management could not reliably prevent defendants from settling weak claims to avoid those costs. The
plausibility threshold therefore had an important screening function before discovery began. Opinion of the
Court, pt. II.B & n. 6; CB pp. 620–621.
Finally, the Court distinguished the pleading threshold from the breadth of proof available after a claim has
been adequately stated. Conley’s language, read literally, could allow a wholly conclusory complaint to

Separate Opinions
Justice Stevens, joined by Justice Ginsburg except Part IV, would have allowed the case to proceed. He
treated the complaint’s allegation of an agreement as an allegation of conduct that had to be accepted at this
stage. He argued that the majority revived unstable distinctions between facts and conclusions and imported
merits-based inferences into pleading. He accepted the need to control discovery, but proposed answers,
targeted discovery, case management, and summary judgment instead of dismissal. In Part IV, which
Ginsburg did not join, he further criticized the decision’s departure from the policies reflected in the statutes
and Rules. Stevens dissent, pts. I–IV; CB pp. 623–627.
Notes
Narrow reading: a § 1 complaint resting on parallel business behavior needs contextual facts suggesting
agreement. Broader reading: Rule 8 requires factual plausibility in addition to notice, which raises questions
about application outside complex antitrust litigation. The casebook expressly asks how far that broader
principle extends and how it relates to formal rule amendment. CB pp. 627–628, Notes 1–3.
Keep the procedural standards separate. Plausibility asks whether the alleged facts support the legally
necessary inference; it does not require a plaintiff to submit trial evidence, disprove every innocent
explanation, or establish a probability of victory. The disagreement over which assertions count as facts is
central to the majority–dissent divide. Opinion of the Court, pt. II.B; Stevens dissent, pts. II–III.
