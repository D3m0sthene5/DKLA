# Doutre v. Niec

**CP-125 · Civil Procedure · 2 Mich. App. 88, 138 N.W.2d 501 (1965) · December 20, 1965 · T.G. Kavanagh, joined by McGregor and Fitzgerald**

The court agreed that improperly excluded trade-practice evidence warranted a new trial, but refused to preserve the original damages award while retrying liability. With unliquidated damages and a trial affected by an admitted liability error, the jury deciding responsibility should also assess any resulting compensation.

## Facts and procedural setting

Pauline and Floyd Niec operated a beauty shop in Flint. On April 19, 1962, they administered a bleach-and-color treatment to Helen Doutre without a preliminary patch test. She suffered injuries to her head and face and sued for damages. The short opinion does not describe the particular chemical, injury treatment, or duration of disability; the relevant record concerned proof of care and the permissible scope of retrial. 2 Mich. App. at 89.

At trial, the defendants were prevented from testifying about the standard of care observed by local beauty shops in administering that treatment. The jury awarded Doutre $10,000. On defendants’ motion, the trial court granted a new trial but limited it to liability, intending to retain the damages determination. Both sides appealed. Doutre argued that the original exclusion was correct, so even the liability retrial should not occur. Defendants argued that liability and damages were too closely connected to permit the limited retrial. Id. at 89–90.

## Evidence ruling

Doutre maintained that defendants could know other Flint operators’ practices only through hearsay. The court found the premise unsupported by the record. One defendant had worked in the business for 24 years and the other for 14. They had attended beauticians’ conventions, observed others’ practices, and said they kept abreast of the practices of Genesee County operators. That experience supplied a basis for testimony, rather than the assumed exclusive reliance on hearsay. Id. at 90.

The appellate court agreed with the trial judge’s reconsidered ruling that excluding the witnesses was an error requiring a new trial. It stated, citing *Barton v. Myers*, that the testimony was admissible because no person was held to a higher standard than the industry average. That is the historical standard expressed in this 1965 opinion, not a verified claim that industry custom necessarily sets the ceiling of care in every present negligence action. The court did not resolve whether the Niecs ultimately acted negligently; it allowed the evidence to be heard at a proper new trial. Id.

## Scope of retrial

The more difficult question was whether the damages verdict could survive. The court described liability and damages as ordinarily intertwined. The exception then recognized by the Michigan Supreme Court permitted retrial of damages alone when liability was clear. Although the court rule authorized limited new trials, the practice was disfavored, and the court saw no compelling reason to expand that exception into a liability-only retrial here. Id. at 90.

The trial judge believed the exclusion might have materially influenced liability but could not have affected damages. The appellate court did not share that certainty. Damages were unliquidated, and liability had been decided in a trial containing a concededly erroneous evidentiary ruling. Under those circumstances, justice required the jury deciding liability or its absence to carry the responsibility for measuring damages as well. The decision did not hold that the $10,000 amount was independently excessive or inadequate; it refused to insulate it from a new, properly informed determination of responsibility. Id. at 90–91.

## Exact disposition and significance

The new-trial order was extended to all issues, with costs awarded to defendants. Kavanagh wrote and McGregor and Fitzgerald concurred; there was no separate judicial writing or footnote. The rehearing-denial date appears in the report’s history, without an additional rehearing opinion.

The case distinguishes authority to order a partial new trial from a justified finding that the issues are separable. Correcting an error affecting liability did not automatically permit retaining another part of the earlier verdict. The assigned comparison with Judge Freedman’s dissent in *Hutton v. Fisher* is an editorial study aid and a different case’s separate writing, not a dissent in Doutre.
