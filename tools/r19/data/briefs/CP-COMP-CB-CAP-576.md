# Baker v. General Motors Corp.

**522 U.S. 222 (1998) · Supreme Court of the United States · January 13, 1998**

Justice Ginsburg wrote for five Justices: Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Stevens, Souter, and Breyer joined. Justice Scalia concurred in the judgment separately. Justice Kennedy also concurred in the judgment, joined by Justices O’Connor and Thomas. All nine agreed to reverse the Eighth Circuit and remand. The assigned account is Note 4 on CivPro PDF 685, before the printed 1361 break and Thompson v. Thompson.

## Two lawsuits and two different agreements

Ronald Elwell worked for General Motors from 1959 until 1989. For fifteen years beginning in 1971, his work included studying vehicle performance, particularly fires, and helping GM defend product-liability suits. He also suggested fuel-line improvements. His relationship with GM deteriorated in 1987. An arrangement for two consulting years followed by retirement did not resolve the dispute. In May 1991, plaintiffs in a Georgia pickup-truck fuel-tank case deposed him. His testimony criticized the fuel system as inferior to competing designs, departing from his prior testimony as a GM expert. 522 U.S. at 226–227.

Elwell sued GM in Michigan the next month for wrongful discharge and related contract and tort claims. GM counterclaimed for alleged disclosure of confidential or privileged information and misappropriation of documents. A preliminary injunction restricted such disclosures. In August 1992, the parties settled for an undisclosed payment to Elwell and agreed to a permanent injunction. In addition to the confidentiality restriction, it prohibited testimony or consultation in litigation involving GM without GM’s written consent. A judge new to the case entered the parties’ submitted decree without another hearing. The decree expressly exempted the pending Georgia action so as not to interfere with that court’s jurisdiction. Id. at 227–228 & n.1.

The separate settlement contract had a broader qualification that the decree itself did not repeat: if another court or tribunal ordered Elwell to testify, his appearance and testimony would not support an action for violation of the injunction or agreement. This distinction between the injunction’s Georgia exception and the contract’s general protection for court-ordered testimony matters to the Court’s reasoning. Id. at 228–229.

The second lawsuit involved Kenneth and Steven Baker. Their mother, Beverly Garner, was a passenger in a Chevrolet Blazer involved in a February 1990 Missouri collision. The engine caught fire and both occupants died. In September 1991, the sons sued GM in Missouri state court, alleging that a defective fuel pump caused the fatal fire. GM removed on diversity grounds and denied both defect and causation, asserting that impact injuries caused Garner’s death. The Supreme Court did not independently resolve those contested merits. Id. at 229.

## Lower proceedings and question

The Bakers subpoenaed Elwell for deposition and trial testimony. GM argued that the Michigan injunction barred him. After reviewing both documents in camera, the district court permitted testimony. It reasoned alternatively that Missouri’s policy favoring disclosure of relevant nonprivileged information justified nonenforcement, and that an injunction modifiable in Michigan could be modified elsewhere. Elwell testified about the fuel-pump system and a 1973 GM memorandum concerning engine-fire risks. The jury awarded $11.3 million. Id. at 229–230.

The Eighth Circuit reversed on several grounds, including admission of Elwell’s testimony. Assuming a public-policy exception, it considered Missouri’s competing policy favoring full faith and credit decisive. It also found insufficient evidence that Michigan would modify the decree. The Supreme Court reviewed whether full faith and credit prevented the Bakers, who were strangers to the Michigan suit, from obtaining this testimony. Id. at 230–231.

The Court held that the Michigan decree did not bar the testimony through the national full-faith-and-credit command. It rejected the district court’s broad public-policy rationale, but also rejected GM’s effort to make the decree control evidence in this separate Missouri litigation. Id. at 231–241.

## Recognition, preclusion, and enforcement

Article IV’s command and §1738 require exacting recognition of a final judgment rendered with authority over its subject matter and governed persons. Choice-of-law treatment of another state’s statutes is different from recognition of its judgments. A forum may sometimes use its own law to decide a controversy; it cannot disregard a qualifying sister-state judgment merely because it dislikes the underlying policy. The majority expressly rejected a roving public-policy exception. Id. at 231–234 & nn.3–6.

Equity decrees are not categorically outside this command. Claim and issue preclusion should not depend merely on whether an adjudication granted legal or equitable relief. But recognition and preclusive effect differ from execution or enforcement mechanisms. A judgment’s effects may require respect while its original methods of enforcement do not automatically travel to another jurisdiction. The receiving forum retains evenhanded control over those mechanisms. Id. at 234–236 & nn.7–8.

The Court compared decrees concerning out-of-state land and injunctions directed at other litigation. A court may bind a person before it to an obligation without thereby accomplishing an official act belonging to another state or controlling that other court’s proceedings. The ninth note preserves the unsettled broader question of credit owed to interstate antisuit injunctions; it does not announce that the Court had already resolved every such situation. Id. at 235–236 & n.9.

## What Michigan actually concluded

The consent judgment settled Elwell’s employment claims and GM’s counterclaim. Those claims could not simply be brought again elsewhere for additional relief. But no privilege issue had been expressly joined, litigated, and decided in the settlement proceeding, and the Bakers had not been parties. Michigan could not require them to intervene in that unrelated employment controversy or use it to settle admissibility in their own case. Id. at 237–238 & n.11.

GM emphasized that the decree bound Elwell rather than the Bakers, and argued that the Bakers lacked a constitutionally protected entitlement to a particular witness. The majority therefore did not rely solely on the proposition that issue preclusion cannot bind strangers. It held more specifically that Michigan lacked authority to dictate which witnesses another court could hear in a separate controversy beyond Michigan’s governance. The decree could restrict Elwell’s voluntary testimony, but could not shield him from the other court’s subpoena power in this setting. Id. at 237 n.11, 238–240.

Nor did the result authorize relitigation of a genuine privilege adjudication. The majority’s twelfth note explains that if the Bakers had been parties in Michigan and actually litigated the privileged character of the testimony, they would be precluded from trying that issue again. The tenth note records that GM did not object to particular questions or answers in the Bakers’ trial on attorney-client, work-product, or trade-secret grounds. A broad agreed prohibition and an actual determination of specific privilege were not interchangeable. Id. at 237 n.10, 239 n.12.

The Georgia carveout acknowledged the potential interference with another court. The separate contractual promise not to sue over compelled testimony likewise showed GM’s recognition of the limits at issue. Injunctions are ordinarily enforced through their issuing court; GM had agreed not to pursue that route against Elwell for this category of testimony. The majority held that Michigan’s decree could not displace Missouri’s evidentiary determinations. Id. at 240–241.

## Separate concurrences

Scalia regarded the distinction between preclusive effects and enforcement as sufficient. Missouri was no more required to enforce the Michigan injunction by excluding testimony than by holding Elwell in contempt. Recognition of a judgment does not import its issuing jurisdiction’s machinery of execution. He concurred only in the judgment rather than adopting the full majority discussion. Id. at 241–242.

Kennedy, joined by O’Connor and Thomas, worried that the majority’s statements about another state’s official acts and interference with litigation might become poorly defined exceptions inconsistent with its rejection of public-policy avoidance. He noted that existing law sometimes recognizes obligations affecting out-of-state land, and regarded broader questions about equitable decrees as delicate and unnecessary here. His hypothetical privilege adjudication prompted the majority’s explicit response in note 12. Id. at 243–246.

His preferred route was state law first. Michigan’s rules required an opportunity to litigate and retained mutuality; the Bakers had never participated in the Michigan proceeding. GM had not shown that Michigan would bind them, and §1738 could give the decree no greater effect elsewhere. Michigan’s rule requiring modification applications before the issuing judge was procedural, not a rule imposing preclusion on out-of-state strangers. The decree’s intended breadth did not override the rendering state’s governing law. Kennedy also rejected the claim that Elwell was seeking to relitigate anything: here he was a witness, not a party. Id. at 246–251.

## Judgment and assigned significance

The Court reversed and remanded the Eighth Circuit judgment. It did not itself issue a new damages judgment or conclusively determine GM’s product liability. Its holding removes the full-faith-and-credit objection to testimony in this setting. The assignment accurately quotes both the authority limitation and the majority’s actual-privilege-adjudication qualification. Its later examples concerning limitations and exempt assets are editorial illustrations of enforcement, not additional findings about the Bakers’ recovery. PDF 685; 522 U.S. at 239 n.12, 240–241.
