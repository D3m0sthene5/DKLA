# Galloway v. United States

**319 U.S. 372 (1943), decided May 24, 1943.** Civil Procedure. Assigned account: *CivPro.pdf*, physical pages 564–566, printed pages 1112–1116, ending before the separate *Neely* account. Pinpoints refer to the complete United States Reports opinion.

The Court affirmed a directed verdict against a veteran seeking benefits under a lapsed war-risk insurance policy. The central evidentiary problem was whether the proof permitted a reasonable inference that total and permanent disability existed before the policy lapsed. The majority found the necessary continuity dependent on speculation across a long period for which the claimant had produced virtually no evidence. It also rejected the contention that directing a verdict for legally insufficient proof violated the jury-trial guarantee. Black, joined by Douglas and Murphy, dissented on both the evidence and the allocation of authority between judge and jury. **382–396, 396–411.**

## The insurance claim and its chronology

Galloway's yearly renewable insurance covered death or total permanent disability. His policy lapsed for nonpayment of premiums on May 31, 1919. To recover on the theory advanced, he needed to establish that his mental disability had become total and permanent no later than that date. The governing regulation treated disability as total when it prevented continuous pursuit of a substantially gainful occupation, and permanent when its underlying conditions made lifetime continuation reasonably certain. Later illness alone would not establish insured disability at the critical date. **372–373 & n.1.**

Galloway worked as a longshoreman before entering the Army in November 1917. He served in France, spent a period in hospital with influenza, resumed duty, and received an honorable discharge in April 1919. He subsequently served in the Navy during 1920 and again in the Army, deserting in May 1922. Veterans' Bureau examinations began in 1930 and supplied changing diagnoses. By the time suit was brought, total and permanent mental disability was conceded; the dispute concerned its earlier existence. His wife became his guardian in 1932, claimed insurance benefits in 1934, and received a final administrative denial in 1936. Suit followed June 15, 1938. **373–375.**

At the close of all evidence, the district court directed a verdict for the Government. The Ninth Circuit affirmed. Galloway argued that his proof was sufficient and that withholding it from the jury denied the constitutional right to jury trial. The majority initially emphasized that his own presentation did not generally deny judicial authority to remove insufficient cases from juries; it challenged the exercise of that authority here. **373.**

## What the proof showed—and what the majority found missing

Two former soldiers described abnormal incidents in France, including an episode when Galloway falsely shouted that Germans were coming and was gagged. His longtime acquaintance O'Neill described a marked change after the war, periods of withdrawal and crying, fears of persecution, and intervals of apparently normal behavior. The majority credited the testimony but considered O'Neill's later recollections indefinite about dates, frequency of contact, and particular events. **375–379.**

A chaplain described an apparently mentally ill soldier named Galloway at a California hospital in early 1920. The majority found the identification untenable in light of the claimant's Navy service during that period, the witness's eventual uncertainty, and the absence of corroborating hospital or Army records. Other military officers described disciplinary problems and alternating behavior in the ensuing service periods. The Court did not adopt their lay assessments as conclusive proof of sanity. **379–385 & n.11, 388.**

The claimant's expert, Dr. Wilder, examined him shortly before trial and used the earlier testimony and medical material to infer continuous disabling illness beginning during wartime service. But the expert knew practically nothing about Galloway's activities between 1925 and 1930 and had little more for the years immediately before that. He first regarded that missing information as unnecessary, acknowledged that continuous employment could matter greatly, and then returned to his original position. **381–383.**

The majority treated this gap as decisive. Apart from a marriage and vague references to a visit home, the record was blank for five years and nearly blank for eight. It viewed evidence about that period as available to Galloway and his wife rather than intrinsically impossible to obtain. Their failure to supply it could not generate favorable inferences bridging weak proof at each end. Nor did the Court find that Galloway actually held steady employment throughout the gap; possible employment illustrated why the missing facts mattered, rather than establishing a contrary employment history. **384–388; 385–387 nn.12–16.**

Expert retrospective reasoning could support a claim when factual evidence supplied continuity. Here, the majority concluded that the expert's inference substituted speculation for an essential evidentiary foundation. It expressly declined to decide whether the subsequent military enlistments themselves conclusively refuted disability, or to rest on the Government's competing evidence. Its stated ground remained the deficiency in the claimant's proof. **387–388.**

## Directed verdicts and the Seventh Amendment

The majority first explained that the Seventh Amendment did not apply of its own force to this monetary claim against the United States: a common-law right to a jury against the sovereign had not existed in 1791. Congress had supplied jury procedure for these insurance actions. Even treating the guarantee as incorporated through that legislation, the directed-verdict objection failed. **388–389; nn.17–18.**

The Court relied on the longstanding approval of directed verdicts and the procedural rules, but also examined the historical argument on its merits. The Amendment preserved the fundamental jury institution rather than freezing every procedural detail, evidence rule, and consequence of an eighteenth-century challenge to proof. Judges historically regulated admissibility and could examine sufficiency through demurrers to evidence and motions for new trials. Those devices had different consequences for renewed litigation, and the existence of both did not constitutionally require every later procedure to reproduce one particular combination of consequences. **389–394; nn.19–30.**

The majority resisted solving the issue by selecting a verbal formula such as “some” rather than “substantial” evidence. The substantive requirement was to distinguish probative facts and reasonably available favorable inferences from mere speculation. Errors in applying that standard were subject to appellate correction. Crediting witnesses did not cure inherent vagueness or supply essential missing facts. Accordingly, the Court **affirmed**; its mandate did not order a new trial. **395–396.**

## Black's dissent

Black's dissent, joined by Douglas and Murphy, described the decision as judicial encroachment on the jury's constitutional province. His historical account emphasized the founding-era significance of juries and the customary role of another jury, through a new trial, in correcting factual outcomes. A demurrer to evidence had forced the challenger to accept the opponent's evidence and reasonable inferences at the risk of judgment against the challenger. Modern directed-verdict practice removed that risk and, in his view, let judges displace juries through increasingly demanding sufficiency formulas. **396–405; dissent nn.1–18.**

His proposed boundary allowed a directed verdict, if at all, only where the evidence left no room for an honest difference of opinion without judicial weighing of credibility. He considered it particularly important for appellate courts to guard that boundary because relatively few such cases reached the Supreme Court. **405–407; dissent nn.19–21.**

On the facts, Black thought the wartime incidents, O'Neill's observations, later service problems, and expert evidence permitted a reasonable inference of continuous illness. Lack of direct evidence for five years was an argument for the Government to make to a jury, not a conclusive presumption excluding the claimant's case. Even intermittent work would not necessarily defeat disability. He rejected the lower court's reliance on reenlistment and thought that, at minimum, a new trial would permit the claimant to supply additional evidence once the perceived deficiency had been identified. These were dissenting conclusions, not the majority's factual or remedial holdings. **408–411.**
