# Piccoli A/S v. Calvin Klein Jeanswear Co.
19 F. Supp. 2d 157 (S.D.N.Y. 1998) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural setting
On September 8, 1998, Judge Kaplan dismissed a Scandinavian distributor's contract and several related claims against participants in an allegedly unlawful jeans-exporting scheme, but allowed specified unfair-competition, Lanham Act, and conspiracy theories to proceed. Piccoli was not an intended beneficiary entitled to enforce Calvin Klein Jeanswear's territorial restrictions: the agreement, read as a whole, limited its benefits to the parties and permitted successors and assigns. The ruling did not decide that no enforceable beneficiary right can coexist with an anti-assignment provision, nor did it absolve defendants of all potential liability. (157, 162–165, 172–174.)

Jeanswear and Azteca sought dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction and failure to state a claim; World Apparel sought judgment on the pleadings. The complaint's allegations supplied the facts, assumed true for these motions. The court also considered incorporated contractual documents. The decision resolved those motions, without a trial, damages award, or finding that the alleged export conspiracy actually occurred. (160; 162 n. 25.)

## The contracts, territorial restrictions, and alleged diversion
In 1991, Calvin Klein Inc. (CKI), beneficial owner of the trademarks, gave Piccoli exclusive distribution rights in Scandinavia. Piccoli had minimum purchase obligations and had to promote and sell through upscale retailers. It alleged that it developed that market by employing marketing personnel and investing thousands of hours and hundreds of thousands of dollars. In 1994, CKI separately licensed Jeanswear to manufacture, distribute, and wholesale in North America. That agreement barred sales outside North America and sales to third parties Jeanswear knew or should have known would export outside that territory. (160–161.)

In May 1995, CKI licensed CK Jeanswear Europe, S.P.A. (European) to manufacture and distribute in Europe. Piccoli's direct agreement with CKI expired in August. Piccoli then obtained exclusive Scandinavian rights from European under an agreement retaining essentially the same obligations. Note 9 explains the apparent sublicensing arrangement; it does not convert Piccoli and Jeanswear into parties to one contract. (161 & n. 9.)

Piccoli alleged that, beginning in summer 1995, defendants shipped Jeanswear's excess inventory to Denmark and sold through discount channels. It complained to European in February 1996; in March, upscale customers began complaining and canceling orders. CKI gave Jeanswear notice concerning Piccoli's complaints before June 10, 1996. In October 1997, European declined to participate in litigation against another Calvin Klein distributor. Piccoli terminated its European agreement on December 18, 1997 after learning that European also exported into Scandinavia. It alleged continuing discount sales and that approximately ninety percent of its customers had canceled orders and stopped future dealings. These were allegations of market loss, not adjudicated damage amounts. (161–162; 166.)

## Intended benefit and the right to enforce
Applying New York law, the court identified two routes to intended-beneficiary status: no one other than the third party can recover for the promisor's breach, or the agreement's language otherwise clearly demonstrates an intent to allow third-party enforcement. Piccoli failed the first route because CKI expressly retained rights to damages and injunctive relief. European's alleged opportunity to sue, which it declined, further undermined Piccoli's position. A contention that only Piccoli suffered unique loss, raised in motion papers rather than the complaint, could not amend the pleading; even considered, it would not displace CKI's right to enforce. Reduced Piccoli purchases could also harm European and, in turn, CKI. (162–163 & nn. 29–30.)

For the second route, Piccoli relied on the cooperation clause. Jeanswear promised to cooperate with CKI in preventing diversion into CKI's other licensees' and distributors' territories. Specific identification by name was not indispensable to beneficiary status, but the requisite protective intent had to appear on the agreement's face. Note 36 adds that Piccoli's previous listing as a direct CKI licensee did not settle whether the later sublicensee relationship enjoyed enforceable protection. (163 & n. 36.)

The court read the cooperation clause together with the contract's personal character, restriction on sublicensing or assignment without CKI's written consent, and provision that it bound and benefited the parties and permitted successors and assigns. The latter language had work to do: it limited the beneficiary category. The court expressly rejected the proposition that an anti-assignment clause alone necessarily defeats an intended beneficiary. Parties can confer enforceable rights on a third person while restricting assignments. Here, however, the combined provisions showed that the contracting parties retained enforcement for themselves. (163–164.)

Piccoli argued that CKI and Jeanswear knew about its Scandinavian franchise, that the worldwide distribution network depended on reciprocal respect for territories, and that Piccoli had invested in reliance on that structure. Even assuming extrinsic evidence could properly be considered, these circumstances did not overcome the agreement's language. Note 41 records conflicting authority about considering such evidence without resolving the dispute; note 44 emphasizes that Piccoli had not alleged actual knowledge of Jeanswear's promise when it signed with European, but only an assumption that the contract would contain similar provisions. The contract claim was dismissed. (164 & nn. 41, 44.)

## Good faith, unjust enrichment, and interference
The implied-covenant claim likewise failed. There was no Piccoli–Jeanswear agreement and no beneficiary right under the CKI–Jeanswear contract. The court declined to impose a duty running throughout a network of independent contracts. Piccoli's alternative tort argument invoked cases recognizing duties arising from contractual relationships, but supplied no authority for detaching those duties from a contract between the relevant parties merely because they occupied comparable distribution roles. (164–165.)

World Apparel proposed Danish law for the nonfederal claims, but no party identified an actual conflict requiring a choice between Danish and New York law. The court therefore addressed the claims under New York law. For unjust enrichment, it required more than a benefit received from another's activities: the asserted performance had to have been rendered for defendants. Piccoli developed demand for its own benefit and possibly that of customers, CKI, and European, rather than performing for these defendants. Their alleged exploitation of that demand therefore did not support this quasi-contract claim. Note 65 explains why the court declined to follow contrary reasoning in similar distributor cases. (166–167 & n. 65.)

Cancellation of orders was not alleged to breach customers' contracts, so the interference theory concerned prospective business relations. The asserted discount-store sales were not directed at Piccoli's upscale customers to persuade them against doing business with Piccoli. That indirect connection did not satisfy the court's direct-interference requirement. This claim was also dismissed. (167–168.)

## Claims that survived and limits of the ruling
Unfair competition required a colorable property or pecuniary interest, not necessarily ownership of the trademark. Piccoli's exclusive distributorship gave it a pecuniary interest when the alleged injury occurred, even though the agreement later ended. Its theory of bad-faith appropriation or passing off was not merely a disguised contract claim: Piccoli had no contract with defendants and could not enforce Jeanswear's license, while marketplace rules supplied a potentially distinct duty. The court accordingly denied dismissal of the common-law claim against Jeanswear. (168–169 & nn. 71–76.)

The court also declined to dismiss Piccoli's Lanham Act false-designation claim against Jeanswear at this stage. It examined the historical circuit framework for extraterritorial application, including a substantial effect on United States commerce. The parties disputed whether domestic promotional materials, showroom negotiations, purchases, and shipments sufficiently supported the alleged foreign deception. Circuit decisions pointed in different directions about the role of domestic activity. Given the alleged organized use of American commerce, the court could not exclude any possible basis for relief on the pleadings; defendants could raise the issue on a fuller record. This was a provisional pleading ruling under the standards then applied, not a definitive finding of the required effect or a certification of present trademark law. Piccoli's pecuniary interest also sufficed for the asserted Section 43 theory, although it lacked the ownership interest associated with Section 32 infringement. (169–172.)

The Paris Convention claim failed because controlling circuit authority treated the Convention, as implemented through Section 44, as providing no additional substantive rights beyond those already available domestically. Azteca's alleged manufacture and shipment did not establish direct wrongdoing: the complaint did not attribute obscuring the address label to Azteca or identify an applicable restriction it knew it was violating. World Apparel relied on conspiracy rather than direct liability. (172–173.)

The conspiracy allegations nevertheless survived. Under New York law, the court considered agreement, an overt act, intentional participation, and resulting damage, applying the then-prevailing Rule 8 notice standard rather than Rule 9(b). For the federal claim, mere knowledge would not suffice, but allegations of knowing participation in a common scheme adequately asserted the necessary concerted conduct and control. Note 110 discusses the historical pleading standard; the ruling did not find an actual agreement or involvement as a matter of fact. (173–174 & nn. 110–115.)

## Exact disposition and reading scope
Jeanswear's dismissal motion was granted except for the Lanham Act and common-law unfair-competition claims. World Apparel's judgment-on-the-pleadings motion and Azteca's dismissal motion were granted except with respect to the conspiracy allegations. No final monetary remedy was awarded. Judge Kaplan issued the district-court memorandum; there were no separate writings or appellate votes. (174.)
