# Title v. United States

**263 F.2d 28 (9th Cir. 1959) — United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, January 6, 1959, No. 16074.** Judge Barnes wrote for Judges Barnes, Hamley, and Jertberg; no separate opinion is reported. Civil Procedure: relief from judgment under Rule 60(b)(4) and (5), finality, and an unprosecuted appeal. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 598, Note 2, printed 1180.

## Facts and the two appellate stages

Sam Title, also known as Sam Teitelman, became a naturalized citizen on October 24, 1941. The government filed a denaturalization complaint on October 21, 1954 under §340 of the 1952 immigration statute, but filed no affidavit showing good cause with the complaint or later in the record. Title raised that omission in a motion to dismiss and his answer. The district court rejected his jurisdictional objection and entered judgment on July 12, 1955 revoking his admission to citizenship and canceling the certificate. (29 & nn.1–2.)

Title filed a notice of appeal on September 8, 1955. The Ninth Circuit dismissed that appeal for failure to prosecute; its mandate issued February 27, 1956 and was entered in the district court February 29. The present opinion does not decide the original appeal on the merits. (29.)

The Supreme Court subsequently decided *Zucca* on April 30, 1956 and the *Matles*, *Lucchese*, and *Costello* cases on April 7, 1958. Those decisions required the good-cause affidavit when proceedings began. On May 22, 1958, Title sought to vacate his judgment, arguing that it was void because the affidavit had never been filed and that continued prospective application was inequitable. On May 26 he also moved to dismiss the government’s complaint for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The district court denied both motions June 19, 1958. This timely appeal concerned that denial. (29–30 & nn.3–4.)

## The affidavit requirement did not make this judgment void

The court accepted that the affidavit was a prerequisite to maintaining the suit. It emphasized, however, that *Zucca* called it a **procedural** prerequisite, using language about a prescribed and safeguarded procedure. Title could not simply treat the word prerequisite as a holding that the omission was jurisdictional and rendered the final judgment void. The later companion decisions did not change that description. (30.)

The court supplied an additional, stronger ground. Even if the requirement were regarded as jurisdictional in the sense that proceedings could not be maintained without it, this defect did not make the judgment void for Rule 60(b)(4) purposes. The original court had adjudicated the jurisdictional objection; that determination could be reviewed on appeal, but was binding after final judgment in this setting. The opinion’s adopted argument distinguishes fundamental jurisdictional defects such as service of process and explains that most erroneous federal judgments remain valid rather than void. This reasoning does not declare that every possible jurisdictional defect is immune from attack. (30, including the embedded Moore quotation marked with an asterisk.)

The court expressly said that it **would reverse the denaturalization judgment if Title’s original appeal were presently before it**. The barrier was his failure to prosecute that appeal, not a conclusion that the affidavit omission was harmless or that the original result was substantively correct. It distinguished *Diamond* on that procedural basis and illustrated its finality reasoning with *Yanow*, where it had refused to recall an earlier mandate despite a later jurisdictional objection. (30–31.)

## Rule 60(b)(5) and finality

Title also asked for a special, more forgiving approach to denaturalization judgments under Rule 60(b)(5), relying on the dissent in *Ackermann*. The court held itself bound by the majority in that case. It treated Rule 60(b) as providing no substitute for the appeal Title had failed to pursue and held that the later change in judicial interpretation was insufficient to reopen this final judgment. It did not adopt the earlier dissent as its own holding. (31.)

The opinion concluded that the motion was addressed to the district court’s sound legal discretion and that appellate intervention required abuse of discretion. That is the historical opinion’s stated standard; the brief does not turn it into a current general statement about review of every modern void-judgment motion. (31.)

## Disposition, notes, and textbook connection

The Ninth Circuit **affirmed the final order denying each of Title’s motions**. It did not restore citizenship, remand for a new denaturalization trial, or reopen the dismissed original appeal. (31.)
