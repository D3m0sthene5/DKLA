# Schneberger v. Apache Corp.
890 P.2d 847 (Okla. 1994) | Contracts

## Certified questions and disposition
On October 25, 1994, the Oklahoma Supreme Court answered a federal district court’s certified questions about damages for breach of a water-pollution settlement. Vice Chief Justice Lavender wrote for five concurring justices, including himself. Alma Wilson, Kauger, and Summers concurred in part and dissented in part; Opala dissented. No separate reasoned writing accompanies those vote entries in the complete published report. Rehearing denial on January 18, 1995, is recorded in the caption. (847–848, 855.)

The court held that diminution in property value was the maximum recoverable measure in the circumstances presented and declined the conditional second question about evidence of a different remediation plan. Although the opinion uses “affirm,” this was an answer to certified legal questions, not a direct appellate disposition finally awarding the landowners $5,175 or entering judgment on whether Apache had breached. The federal action remained the setting for the parties’ disputed contractual claim. (848, 854–855.)

## Pollution, settlement, and renewed litigation
Fred and Zola Schneberger owned a 154.95-acre tract in Washita County. Apache began drilling the Schneberger 1-17 well on June 29, 1982. In March 1984, the owners sued in federal court, alleging contamination, property injury, and personal damages from that well and another upstream well, Rogers 1-18. A March 4, 1986 settlement resolved that action and related proceedings, followed by dismissal with prejudice. Apache paid $80,000 for damages and expenses covering, among other things, cattle, crops, land, water use, and location-related claims, and approximately $179,000 in attorney fees under a court order. These were prior settlement payments, not the disputed remediation award sought in this case. (848–849.)

The parties agreed that Apache would request a stay of Corporation Commission cleanup proceedings, but, if a stay was not obtained, would remain subject to the Commission’s regulatory requirements. The stay did not occur. The Commission ordered and monitored cleanup, and Apache remained subject to its approved plan and had spent substantial sums implementing it. In October 1989, the owners brought the present action, alleging that Apache had not achieved the reduced contaminant levels promised in the agreement. They requested cost-of-remediation damages and sought to introduce methods not contemplated by the Commission’s orders. Apache urged diminution in value. (849.)

The owners estimated approximately $1.3 million to remediate to the contractual standards; Apache estimated a $5,175 loss in market value. The court addressed the proper damages rule using those presented estimates, not an adjudicated dollar valuation or a final finding that cleanup had been abandoned. The district court’s first certified question concerned the measure for breach of this pollution-reduction settlement. Its second, expressly conditional on cost of remediation being the measure, asked whether a jury could consider a plan different from that approved by the Commission, which the agreement authorized to modify remediation. (848, 850, 854.)

## Governing damages principles and Peevyhouse
The general contract statute, 23 O.S. § 21, compensated detriment proximately caused by breach or ordinarily likely to result. Traditional property-damage rules distinguished permanent injury, measured by the before-and-after value difference, from reparable injury, initially measured by reasonable restoration costs. But Oklahoma decisions imposed a limit on restoration recovery. The court reaffirmed Peevyhouse’s treatment of remediation costs grossly disproportionate to the economic benefit, together with statutory constraints against greater recovery than full performance and against unreasonable, oppressive damages. (849–850 & n. 1.)

Peevyhouse had described the breached restoration provision as incidental to the main contractual purpose. Schneberger acknowledged that wording but emphasized that later cases had adhered to diminution principles without maintaining the same incidental-purpose distinction. It traced state decisions concerning both temporary and permanent property injuries and used construction and lease authorities to explain why an award of the full physical cure could exceed the loss properly compensated. It did not simply declare pollution noncompensable or deny that reasonable completion costs can be considered. (851–852 & nn. 3–9, 854.)

## Environmental policy and federal predictions
The owners argued that statutes protecting groundwater, regulating oil-and-gas pollution, coordinating pollution control, and requiring surface-damage negotiations reflected a policy change making Peevyhouse obsolete. They relied on federal decisions, including Rock Island and Davis, predicting that Oklahoma would no longer apply its diminution limitation. The Supreme Court rejected those predictions, noting that neither had been certified to it. Its opinion distinguishes those federal interpretations from the state court’s continuing understanding of state damages law. (850–851 & nn. 2–7.)

The Surface Damage Act changed liability and required negotiation with surface owners before drilling, but the court viewed it as balancing two valuable resources rather than making one industry categorically subordinate to another. Under that Act, its own precedents still used diminution in value for permanent injury and allowed restoration of temporary injury only when its cost was less than the relevant value loss. Factors such as location, land quality, inconvenience, access, changes in physical condition, and crops informed the overall diminution assessment; they were not eight separate damage items to add to it. (853–854 & n. 10.)

The statutory requirement of reasonable damages also applied to contract obligations. With the extraordinary disparity between $1.3 million and the presented value loss, the majority saw no basis for abandoning its existing rule. The parties were free to specify a contractual measure for breach, but had not done so. The court therefore supplied the remedy under governing law rather than treating the promised cleanup standard itself as a negotiated damages clause. (854 & nn. 11–12.)

## Regulatory duties and limits of the answer
The court distinguished private monetary compensation from public environmental enforcement. It expressly preserved the Commission’s authority to administer antipollution law and did not release Apache from regulatory cleanup. Its concern was that nothing in a private damages award itself compelled the recipient to spend the money restoring the property. That point helped justify an outer limit on the owners’ recovery, not immunity from compliance with the Commission’s plan. (855 & n. 13.)

Because remediation cost was not the proper measure under these facts, the court found no reason to answer whether a jury could consider an alternative plan. It did not decide a general jurisdictional contest between Commission proceedings and courts, authorize a different plan, or hold every item of alternative-plan evidence inadmissible. The actual result was the diminution ceiling and nonanswer to the dependent question. (848, 855.)
