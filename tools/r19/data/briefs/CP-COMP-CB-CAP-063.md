# Pennington v. Fourth National Bank

**Civil Procedure · CP-COMP-CB-CAP-063 · 243 U.S. 269 (1917) · March 6, 1917**

Pennington upheld the application of a nonresident husband's Ohio bank deposit to alimony when an injunction seized the deposit at the beginning of the divorce proceeding. The Court treated the decree as a charge against the seized property, rather than an enforceable personal judgment against the absent husband. It **affirmed the Ohio judgment for the bank** in the husband's later, independent action to recover the deposit. The admitted validity of the divorce itself was not the contested question. (243 U.S. at 270–272.)

Mrs. Pennington obtained a divorce in an Ohio state court and sought alimony in the same proceeding. She joined the Fourth National Bank of Cincinnati, which held her husband's deposit. When she filed suit, the court enjoined the bank from paying out any of the money. Later orders directed payments to the wife; ultimately the bank was permanently restrained from paying the husband and ordered to pay the remaining balance to the wife. It complied. (Id. at 270.)

The husband was not an Ohio resident. He had neither been personally served within Ohio nor appeared voluntarily, and notice to him had been by publication alone. After the court-directed payments, he presented a check for the full amount of his original deposit. He argued that the orders deprived him of property without the Fourteenth Amendment's due process and that the bank knew the jurisdictional circumstances. The bank refused payment. He then brought a separate Ohio action against it. Judgment went to the bank, and both the Hamilton County appellate court and Ohio Supreme Court affirmed. The U.S. Supreme Court reviewed that judgment. (Id. at 270–271.)

Justice Brandeis distinguished jurisdiction over a person from jurisdiction over property. The opinion's historical premise was that a state could exercise jurisdiction over property within its borders despite the owner's absence or nonresidence. That premise encompassed intangible property: the debt a resident bank owed its nonresident depositor counted as property within the state. But publication did not supply a basis for a judgment in personam. Garnishment and foreign attachment instead operated quasi in rem, seizing the defendant's property and applying it toward the obligation. (Id. at 271.)

The husband argued that alimony differed from ordinary garnishment because an ordinary debt already existed when suit began, while the obligation to pay alimony arose through the proceeding itself. The Court found that distinction immaterial. Jurisdiction over the seized property did not depend on whether the underlying claim was admitted or contested, liquidated or unliquidated, or already mature rather than inchoate and dependent on time or judicial action. The court could determine the obligation as part of the proceeding. (Id. at 271–272.)

The Court identified three essentials: the property must be within the state; it must be seized at the commencement of proceedings; and the owner must have an opportunity to be heard. When those conditions were satisfied, the alimony decree was valid to the same extent as a judgment on a debt against the seized property. This reasoning did not authorize collection against the absent husband's other assets as a personal obligation established by publication. (Id. at 272.)

The opening injunction satisfied the seizure requirement. It was as effective in immobilizing the bank deposit as customary garnishment or trustee process. Equity could reach property unavailable to attachment at law. The Court therefore rejected the contention that no legally sufficient seizure had occurred when the divorce suit began. The disposition was an affirmance, not a remand for the Ohio court to perform an additional attachment. (Id.)

Both judicial footnotes support those distinctions. Footnote 1 collects decisions enforcing alimony against property seized at the outset and decisions treating a wife's inchoate alimony entitlement as a creditor's interest for fraudulent-conveyance statutes. Footnote 2 collects authority treating an injunction against a resident debtor of a nonresident defendant as a sufficient seizure of the latter's property. The notes reinforce the reach of equitable process and the absence of a requirement that the claim already be fixed; they do not expand the judgment beyond the property seized. (Id. at 272 nn.1–2.)

Brandeis wrote the Court's opinion. The report contains no separate writing or numerical vote statement. The assigned account appears at CivPro PDF pages 128–129, printed pages 207–208, in the section on jurisdiction based on power over property. This brief describes the reasoning of the 1917 decision; the textbook's subsequent jurisdiction cases require their own treatment before that historical property rationale can be used as a statement of present law.
