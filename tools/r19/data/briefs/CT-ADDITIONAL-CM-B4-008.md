# Reynolds Metals Co. v. Westinghouse Electric Corp.
758 F.2d 1073 (5th Cir. 1985) | Contracts

## Court and the promised engineering service

Judge Higginbotham wrote the Fifth Circuit’s April 29, 1985 opinion for a panel with Judges Thornberry and Reavley. The court upheld a jury’s finding that Westinghouse failed to provide promised competent engineering services, but reversed the repair-cost damages and remanded for a new damages trial. No separate opinion appears. (758 F.2d 1073–1074, 1080.)

In 1970, Westinghouse proposed a large transformer for Reynolds’s Texas plant for approximately $250,000. The price included a competent service engineer for two full days during equipment check or startup. The proposal supplied a one-year shipment-based warranty, disclaimed other warranties, and excluded special, indirect, incidental, and consequential damages. Reynolds accepted by purchase order with some conflicting warranty terms, and Westinghouse acknowledged it. In January 1971, anticipating storage, Reynolds bought an additional year of warranty coverage for $2,637, conditioned on pre-startup inspection and Westinghouse startup supervision. Reynolds would pay supervision charges with credit for the original two days. (1074–1075 & n. 1.)

The unit shipped in July 1971 and remained stored until installation in early 1973. A local contractor did most installation under Reynolds engineer Younger. Westinghouse engineer Reindl arrived for final inspection and startup, worked with Younger over roughly two months, and the unit entered service in late April. Reindl had an electrical-engineering degree but limited experience with this type of transformer and regarded his role as technical assistance, not supervision. According to Reynolds’s experts, only one of two grounding cables passed through the alarm system, and its sensitivity setting compounded the problem. That configuration could fail to disclose internal burning. (1075.)

The transformer failed April 11, 1974. The experts attributed the initial burning to a compression-ring design defect, with the alarm failure allowing damage to continue undetected. Reynolds sent the unit to Westinghouse for repair and sought warranty coverage. Westinghouse denied coverage as expired; Reynolds paid $109,284.82 and sued in April 1976. The distinction between the design defect causing burning and the service failure aggravating damage remained important to causation. Note 6 rejects attributing the entire repair bill to the service breach alone. (1075–1077 & nn. 2, 6.)

## Trial rulings and the surviving claim

After removal on diversity grounds, negligence and strict-liability theories were barred by limitations. The district court directed a verdict on nearly all warranty matters. It treated the proposal as offer and the purchase order as acceptance, struck conflicting warranty terms under UCC § 2-207, and supplied Code terms. It concluded shipment started warranty coverage, which had expired by failure, while the nonconflicting disclaimer and damages limitation remained effective. Note 5 records disagreement over the proper treatment of conflicting forms; the appellate court expressly declined to approve or disapprove the district court’s approach because it did not affect the outcome. (1076–1077 & nn. 2–5.)

The surviving contract theory alleged total failure to provide the promised competent supervising engineer, rather than merely inadequate performance by an otherwise qualified engineer. Reindl’s experience, understanding of his role, and work allowed that distinction to go to the jury. The jury found breach and proximate causation, and the district court awarded the stipulated repair bill plus prejudgment interest. (1077.)

## Why breach survived the warranty defenses

The appellate court agreed that a warranty disclaimer did not necessarily excuse failure to supply the promised performance at all. If a competent supervising engineer had performed poorly, the claim would be for warranty breach under the district court’s analysis. But a person not meeting the promised qualification and not undertaking the promised supervisory role could amount to total nonperformance. The jury could reach that conclusion on this record. The ruling should not be generalized into an exception invalidating every warranty disclaimer whenever work is defective. (1077–1078.)

Westinghouse separately argued that the warranty-extension condition reserved its right to supervise for its own protection, without obligating it to supervise for Reynolds’s benefit. The court considered that reading more reasonable, but Westinghouse had not presented it below or objected to the instruction imposing the obligation. It therefore refused to entertain the new theory on appeal. The preserved breach finding does not establish an appellate endorsement of that contractual interpretation on its merits. Westinghouse’s notice objection also failed: Texas construed § 2-607 notice liberally, and the jury’s finding of reasonable communication of dissatisfaction had record support. (1078.)

## Direct value loss versus consequential property damage

The agreed exclusion remained enforceable. The district court had excluded transportation costs and lost profits, but the repair bill also represented consequential loss. Contract expectancy generally comprises the performance-value differential, incidental mitigation expense, and consequential losses such as foreseeable injury to property. A restriction on consequential recovery required isolating the direct value differential rather than awarding every loss that proximate causation connected to breach. (1078–1079.)

Because the transaction was predominantly a transformer sale, Texas’s UCC governed the incidental service promise, although the court considered the result the same under ordinary contract principles. Applying § 2-714 by analogy, direct damages meant the difference, when services were provided, between the value of competent, properly prepared engineering services promised and the market value of the services actually supplied. The court suggested the competent engineer’s fee less the value of Reindl’s work as a way to determine that difference; it did not award a particular fee amount or declare his services valueless. (1079–1080 & nn. 7–8.)

Failure to detect ground current and the resulting aggravated burning were foreseeable. Foreseeability nevertheless did not transform the repair losses into direct service-value losses. Those property losses were consequential and excluded by agreement. The jury’s proximate-cause question captured more than the contract permitted, requiring a new damages trial. Independently, the underlying design flaw meant even unrestricted service-related recovery could not simply encompass every repair cost. (1077 n. 6; 1080.)

## Exact result and reading scope

The jury’s breach finding was affirmed; the damages award was reversed and remanded for a new trial on damages. Note 9 also vacated prejudgment interest, declining to decide Westinghouse’s objections and leaving entitlement for initial decision on remand. Liability was not retried by the announced mandate, and no replacement damages or final interest amount was set. (1080 & n. 9.)
