# Réunion des musées nationaux v. Saint-Arromans
CA Versailles, chambres réunies, 7 January 1987, no. 298-85; Gaz. Pal. 1987 Jur. 34 | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
The combined chambers of the Versailles Court of Appeal decided this final appellate stage on January 7, 1987, following a December 13, 1983, Court of Cassation remand. The court affirmed the December 13, 1972, Paris trial judgment annulling the painting’s sale, extended its effects to the Minister of Culture, and ordered restitution of the painting to Madame Saint-Arroman, recording her undertaking to repay the 2,200-franc sale price. It did not adjudicate her alternative damages claim against the auctioneers and expert’s heirs. The complete reproduced judgment has no separate writing or numerical vote. Earlier decisions belong to the litigation history recited here, rather than additional independently briefed opinions. (French judgment, Faits et procédure; Par ces motifs.)

## Sale and evolving attribution
The Saint-Arromans needed money after the husband’s work transfer from the provinces to Paris and retained auctioneer Rheims to sell their painting. A family tradition attributed it to Nicolas Poussin, but Lebel, the auctioneer’s expert, classified it as an anonymous work of the Carracci school and estimated it at 1,500 francs. The catalogue offered it under that attribution. At the February 21, 1968, auction it fetched 2,200 francs; the administration exercised its statutory preemption right, and the painting went to the Louvre. (French judgment, first factual Considérant following Sur ce, LA COUR.)

The Louvre displayed it as a Poussin. A 1969 article by curator Rosenberg explained that attribution and reported supporting expert views; the owners brought their action in 1971. Other specialists later disputed the attribution. The Versailles court did not finally determine the artist. That unresolved question mattered because the asserted mistake was not simply that the sellers undervalued a demonstrably authentic Poussin. It was that they sold with a conviction excluding Poussin, whereas the possibility remained open. (Id.; main merits Considérant beginning with the continuing uncertainty of attribution.)

## Proceedings and parties’ contentions
The trial court annulled the sale under then-article 1110 of the Civil Code. Its reasoning was that the sellers thought they transferred a Carracci-school painting while the museum thought it acquired a Poussin and knowingly benefited from the sellers’ error. It put the auctioneers and expert out of the case. The museum appealed. By the time of the Versailles hearing the expert and Monsieur Saint-Arroman had died; their successors participated, and Madame Saint-Arroman acted for herself and as her husband’s heir. The Minister of Culture had intervened. (French judgment, Faits et procédure.)

The museum and Minister sought reversal and preservation of the sale. Their arguments included uncertainty about the true artist, lack of proof that the sellers had held a definite belief excluding Poussin, and the claim that the mistake concerned only experts’ opinions or monetary value. The Minister pointed to the painting’s deteriorated condition, enlargement and repainting, and divergent expert views as impediments to certainty. The museum argued that the family tradition contradicted the asserted belief and that failure to tell the professionals of it would be inexcusable fault. It also contested the trial court’s statement that the museum had perfectly known the sellers’ mistake. These were litigants’ positions, not findings that the painting was definitively authentic or that the sellers were culpably silent. (Id., three factual and argument Considérants preceding the merits heading.)

Madame Saint-Arroman requested affirmance, return of the painting, extension of the judgment to the State, and recognition of her offer to repay 2,200 francs. Alternatively, she sought damages against the auctioneers and expert’s heirs, a 250,000-franc provisional payment, and expert assessment of her loss. Those alternative amounts were requested relief, not awards. The professionals asked that the judgment releasing them be upheld. (French judgment, Faits et procédure.)

## Essential attribution and the sellers’ actual belief
For a public auction using an expert-certified catalogue, the attribution of an artwork was a substantial quality for both seller and buyer. The catalogue’s description evidenced the seller’s understanding of the thing offered. This description named the Carracci school and contained no suggestion of Poussin, his school, style, or manner. Since Poussin did not belong to that school, the description excluded that attribution rather than presenting it as a possibility the sellers knowingly put at risk. The court found their conviction at contracting was that this was not a Poussin. (French judgment, merits Considérant beginning “en matière de ventes publiques” and its continuation.)

The earlier family tradition did not change the decisive inquiry into belief at the time of sale. The owners were laypeople entitled to accept the categorical view of a reputable expert and auctioneer and to conclude that their family tradition was wrong. The professionals’ assertion that the owners had concealed that tradition lacked independent support, came from interested parties, and was implausible given the sellers’ interest in obtaining the best price and the professionals’ likely inquiries about provenance. The alleged inexcusable fault therefore lacked a factual basis. (Id., succeeding two merits Considérants.)

## Uncertainty did not defeat annulment
The court acknowledged that conflicting expert opinions prevented it from conclusively resolving whether Poussin painted the work. That did not reduce the mistake to an error about other people’s opinions. The inability to exclude an authentic Poussin demonstrated the error in the sellers’ absolute conviction that it could not be one. The museum, meanwhile, had at least believed the origin differed from the catalogue: its authority to preempt up to 40,000 francs, more than twenty-five times the expert’s estimate, supported that inference. A specialist’s favorable article fifteen days after the sale and the museum’s initial adoption of that view also informed the court’s assessment. The 40,000 francs was an authorized ceiling, not the price actually paid. (Id., merits Considérant addressing continuing uncertainty.)

A mistaken economic estimate based on accurate knowledge of an object’s qualities was distinguishable from a mistake about a substantial quality that consequently affected value. The latter was the operative error here. The owners’ erroneous conviction determined consent that they would not have given had they known the actual uncertainty. The court therefore confirmed nullity under article 1110 without needing to address every other argument. It did not award a price adjustment merely for an unequal exchange. (Id., final substantive Considérants.)

## Exact restitution and costs
The dispositive order affirmed the trial judgment in all its provisions, extended its effects to the Minister, ordered the painting returned, and recorded Madame Saint-Arroman’s repayment undertaking. Because the professionals’ damages claim was subsidiary, it required no ruling. The museum and Minister were charged with appellate costs incurred before Paris, Amiens, and Versailles, including costs of the quashed decisions. Costs attributable to the subsidiary action against the professionals remained with Madame Saint-Arroman; direct recovery by the participating avoués was authorized under procedural article 699. (French judgment, Par ces motifs.)
