# Noel Canning v. NLRB; NLRB v. Noel Canning
705 F.3d 490 (D.C. Cir. 2013), decided January 25, 2013; 573 U.S. 513 (2014), decided June 26, 2014 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## The dispute and the appointments that made it constitutional

Noel Canning, a Pepsi-Cola bottler and distributor, negotiated a replacement collective-bargaining agreement after its prior agreement expired in April 2010. By the final bargaining meeting on December 8, 2010, wages and pensions remained unresolved. The parties discussed different ways to allocate a forty-cent hourly increase between wages and pension contributions. The union's evidence supported an agreement allowing employees to choose the allocation without a ten-cent pension ceiling. After a handshake and an understanding that the proposal would go to a ratification vote, the company sent an email imposing that ceiling. Employees ratified a proposal assigning all forty cents to pensions. The company treated the vote as a counteroffer, declared impasse, and refused to execute the agreement. Following a two-day hearing, the administrative law judge credited the union's detailed contemporaneous notes over the company's account. The Board ordered the company to reduce the agreement to writing, execute it, and make employees whole for resulting losses. (D.C. Circuit slip opinion 3, 5–8; 573 U.S. at 520.)

The constitutional problem concerned the Board that issued the order on February 8, 2012. Of its five members, only Chairman Mark Pearce and Brian Hayes held valid Senate-confirmed appointments unaffected by the challenge. President Obama had appointed Sharon Block, Richard Griffin, and Terence Flynn on January 4, 2012, invoking the Recess Appointments Clause. Flynn's nomination had been pending approximately a year; Block's and Griffin's had been pending only a few weeks. The vacancies originated on August 27, 2010, August 27, 2011, and January 3, 2012. The last resulted from expiration of Craig Becker's earlier recess appointment at the end of the Senate's first session. Under the National Labor Relations Act and *New Process Steel*, the Board needed three lawfully appointed members to act. The panel deciding Noel Canning's dispute consisted of Hayes, Flynn, and Block. Invalidity of the challenged appointments therefore deprived the Board itself of a lawful quorum. (D.C. Circuit slip 13–15, 40–43; 573 U.S. at 520–522.)

On December 17, 2011, the Senate had unanimously arranged brief pro forma sessions every Tuesday and Friday through January 20, with ordinary business resuming January 23. Although the arrangement contemplated that no business would be transacted, the Senate actually passed a payroll-tax extension by unanimous consent on December 23. A January 3 pro forma meeting began the second session of the 112th Congress. The President's January 4 appointments thus occurred between January 3 and January 6 meetings. The administration regarded the pro forma meetings as ineffective interruptions of a longer period in which the Senate was unavailable to advise and consent. The company argued that the meetings instead divided the break into adjournments too short to support recess appointments. (D.C. Circuit slip 14–15; 573 U.S. at 520–522, 552–554.)

## The D.C. Circuit's review and judgment

The company petitioned for review, and the Board sought enforcement. Constitutional avoidance required the court first to address statutory objections that could independently dispose of the case. The court rejected the attack on the ALJ's credibility determination: the conflicting accounts were supported by substantial evidence, and the supposed contradiction in a union affidavit was an apparent typographical error, not an extraordinary basis to overturn credited testimony. The company's Washington-law argument was unpreserved because its exceptions had not alerted the Board to that theory. The court did not decide the agreement's enforceability under Washington law. The company nevertheless could raise its similarly unpreserved quorum challenge. Section 10(e)'s extraordinary-circumstances exception permitted consideration of the fundamental constitutional objection to the Board's authority, rather than a routine objection to the qualifications of a subordinate hearing examiner. The failure of the company's nonconstitutional objections made the appointment issue necessary. (D.C. Circuit slip 4–13.)

Chief Judge Sentelle, joined by Judges Henderson and Griffith on the first constitutional ground, construed the Recess Appointments Clause to permit appointments only during recesses **between formal Senate sessions**. The opinion read the definite article in “the Recess,” the singular noun, the contrasting references to “adjournment,” and commissions expiring at the end of the Senate's next “Session” as a coherent formal distinction. It emphasized early practice near ratification, including the absence of intrasession appointments for roughly eighty years, over more recent presidential practice. The recess mechanism was an auxiliary response to the Senate's unavailability, subordinate to the ordinary advice-and-consent process. Allowing the President to identify any sufficiently substantial break—or to decide for himself that the Senate was unavailable—would, in the court's view, leave uncertain boundaries and facilitate evasion of confirmation. It rejected the contrary reasoning of the Eleventh Circuit in *Evans v. Stephens*. Because the appointments occurred after the second session began, they were invalid on this ground. (D.C. Circuit slip 16–30.)

Sentelle and Henderson also construed vacancies that “may happen during the Recess” to mean vacancies that **first arise during that same intersession recess**, not vacancies that merely continue to exist then. Their reading gave independent effect to “that may happen,” used contemporaneous definitions and the parallel Senate-vacancy clause, and relied on early interpretations by Washington, Randolph, and Hamilton. Later salary statutes were not persuasive evidence that Congress had constitutionally authorized appointments to earlier vacancies; structural safeguards protected the public and could not be waived by interbranch agreement. Administrative inconvenience could be addressed through acting-officer statutes, holdover provisions, or changes to the Board's quorum statute. None of the three vacancies arose during an intersession recess. In particular, the Senate had not adjourned sine die before its January 3 transition, leaving no intervening recess; Becker's commission expired at the session's end, not during a recess. The opinion further concluded that a President could not save up a vacancy originating in one recess to fill in a later recess after the Senate had an intervening opportunity to act. (D.C. Circuit slip 30–44, including notes 1–2 at 40–42.)

Griffith concurred in the opinion except Part IV.B and in the judgment. The intrasession ground sufficed, so constitutional avoidance counseled against deciding which vacancies qualified. He noted that the administration's broader vacancy interpretation dated at least to the 1820s and deserved more caution than the newer practice of intrasession appointments. His concurrence did not endorse the majority's second ground. All three judges nevertheless agreed on the result: **petition granted, Board order vacated, cross-petition for enforcement denied**. The Chamber of Commerce and Coalition for a Democratic Workplace's intervention motion was dismissed as moot; the court did not decide their contested associational standing because the company's own standing allowed full resolution of the case. (D.C. Circuit slip 44–46; Griffith concurrence, unpaginated physical PDF page 47.)

## The Supreme Court's questions and controlling rules

On the Board's petition, the Supreme Court considered both appellate grounds and whether pro forma sessions prevented a qualifying recess. By the time of decision, the Senate had confirmed successors and the Board again had an unquestioned quorum. That did not moot the validity of its earlier order. Related challenges to Becker's appointment also made the scope of intrasession appointments and preexisting vacancies practically important. Justice Breyer's opinion, joined by Kennedy, Ginsburg, Sotomayor, and Kagan, rejected both of the D.C. Circuit's categorical interpretations. The Court nevertheless **affirmed** because the particular three-day recess was too short. Scalia, Roberts, Thomas, and Alito concurred only in the judgment. The result was unanimous; the controlling rationale had five votes. (573 U.S. at 519–526, 557, 569.)

The majority began with two commitments. The Clause supplies a subsidiary appointment mechanism, preserving continuity when the Senate is unavailable without routinely bypassing confirmation. And longstanding practice carries significant weight when resolving ambiguous allocations of power between elected branches. Historical practice was interpretive evidence, not permission for the President to disregard unambiguous constitutional restraints. (Id. at 522–526.)

First, “the Recess” includes **both intersession and intrasession recesses of sufficient length**. Text did not compel treating “the” as a reference to one formal intersession interval; a definite article may describe a class of recurring periods. The Senate's practical availability matters in either kind of break. Extended intrasession recesses were uncommon early in the Republic, limiting what silence in that period proved. The majority considered nineteenth-century appointments, increasingly extensive twentieth-century practice, executive interpretations, and the Senate's absence of formal opposition to intrasession appointments for many decades. It declined to treat an isolated contrary attorney-general opinion or the potential duration of an intrasession commission as controlling. The Senate's 1905 rejection of Roosevelt's purported appointments in an instantaneous recess also demonstrated that a merely formal label did not establish a useful appointment interval. (Id. at 526–536.)

The Court supplied temporal limits. A recess of **three days is too short**, informed by the Adjournments Clause's treatment of breaks that do not require the other House's consent. A recess of **more than three but fewer than ten days is presumptively too short**, whether intersession or intrasession. The historical record furnished no ordinary intrasession practice sufficient to justify appointments in those shorter intervals. The presumption accommodates highly unusual emergencies, such as a national catastrophe rendering the Senate unavailable while urgent appointments are needed. Political opposition in the Senate is not such an emergency. Thus the rule is not an absolute ten-day minimum without exceptions, and it does not exempt intersession recesses. (Id. at 536–538.)

Second, the power extends to **vacancies arising during a recess and vacancies arising earlier but remaining open during it**. The words “may happen” could describe a vacancy that happens to exist; the alternative reading was plausible but not textually mandatory. Restricting appointments to newly arising vacancies would frustrate the Clause's practical purpose when an office became vacant shortly before the Senate left or remained vacant despite an effort to obtain confirmation. Acting officials and special sessions were imperfect alternatives. Earlier appointments were disputed, but the broader interpretation became established through attorney-general opinions beginning with Wirt in 1823 and subsequent presidential practice. The majority analyzed the 1863 salary restriction, later congressional reactions, and the 1940 exceptions permitting payment for specified preexisting vacancies. It concluded that this history supported the broader reading rather than a continuing constitutional prohibition. The finite commission and the Senate's ability to remain in session limited circumvention. (Id. at 538–549.)

## Why the pro forma sessions defeated these appointments

For this purpose the Senate is in session when it says it is, **provided its own rules leave it capable of conducting business**. Respect for the Senate's authority to determine its proceedings required substantial deference, but a wholly disabling arrangement would present a different question. The January pro forma sessions qualified because the Senate retained the capacity to act through unanimous consent and presumed a quorum until a Senator raised its absence. Physical emptiness or a handful of attending Senators did not establish legal incapacity. The December 23 legislation was concrete evidence that the no-business arrangement could be overcome; the Senate also retained mechanisms for receiving messages and bringing absent members into attendance. (Id. at 549–555.)

The administration's functional inquiry into whether the Senate was realistically available would have required courts to second-guess its journal, quorum practices, and internal procedures. The majority instead distinguished practical inconvenience from a legal inability to conduct business. The Senate could choose to prevent recess appointments by conducting ordinary short sessions as well; the Court did not grant the President a constitutional remedy for the political impasse underlying these nominations. January 3 and January 6 were therefore real interruptions, leaving only a three-day recess when the appointments occurred. Block, Griffin, and Flynn were invalidly appointed; the Board lacked a quorum when it issued the company's order. The affirmed judgment left that order vacated and unenforceable. The Supreme Court did not reject the underlying labor-law findings on their merits. (Id. at 552–557.)

## Scalia's concurrence in the judgment

Scalia, joined by Roberts, Thomas, and Alito, would have adopted the two narrow constitutional readings substantially corresponding to the D.C. Circuit's reasoning. “Recess” and “Session” denoted formal legislative periods, and vacancies that “happen during” a recess were those that first arose then. A broader reading required judicially invented temporal lines, including the majority's three-day exclusion, ten-day presumption, and emergency qualification. In his view those lines lacked grounding in the constitutional text. A genuine intersession recess, unlike a merely constructive instantaneous interval, did not need a court-created minimum duration under his interpretation. (Id. at 569–585, particularly note 4 at 583.)

The concurrence disputed both the majority's historical inferences and its methodology. Rare early intrasession appointments, contested nineteenth-century practices, and a proliferation of appointments after World War II did not establish the original power. A unified Executive has institutional incentives to expand its authority; a divided Senate's failure to mount a collective objection does not reliably signify constitutional assent. Structural restraints safeguard citizens and cannot be acquired by adverse possession between branches. The concurrence likewise read early attorney-general opinions and the 1863 pay restriction as evidence against appointments to preexisting vacancies, questioned the majority's selected historical samples, and distinguished later statutory payment exceptions from wholesale approval. It expressly did not decide the constitutionality of those payment provisions. (Id. at 585–614, especially notes 7 and 9–17.)

For Scalia, efficiency could not justify changing the allocation of appointment authority. Congress had means to address vacancies, and the President could seek confirmation or a special session. All four concurring Justices agreed that these appointments were invalid, but their reading would have excluded substantially more appointments than the majority's availability-and-duration approach. Their position is a separate rationale, not the Court's rule. (Id. at 599–615.)
