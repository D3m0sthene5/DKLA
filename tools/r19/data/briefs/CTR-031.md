Drennan v. Star Paving Co.
Citation
Drennan v. Star Paving Co., 51 Cal. 2d 409, 333 P.2d 757 (1958). Supreme Court of California, decided
December 31, 1958. Justice Traynor wrote the opinion. The appeal concerned whether reliance made a
subcontractor's offer irrevocable before the general contractor communicated acceptance. Full judicial
opinion.
Parties
William A. Drennan, a licensed general contractor, was the plaintiff and respondent. He sought the
additional cost of obtaining paving work after the subcontractor refused to perform at its quoted price. Star
Paving Company was the defendant and appellant. It maintained that its bid was a revocable offer and that it
withdrew the offer before acceptance. The school district awarded the principal construction contract to
Drennan but was not a party to this damages action.
Material Facts
On July 28, 1955, Drennan prepared a bid for the Monte Vista school project in the Lancaster school district.
Bids were due before 8 p.m. Local practice was for general contractors to receive subcontractor quotations
by telephone on the bidding day and use them in calculating their own bids. Drennan's secretary received
approximately fifty to seventy-five quotations, recorded them on forms, and gave them to Drennan for his
master cost sheet.
Late that afternoon, Star's estimator telephoned a price of $7,131.60 for the paving work according to the
project's plans and specifications. He repeated the figure at the secretary's request. Drennan listened on an
extension and entered the quotation on his cost sheet. Star supplied the lowest paving bid. Drennan used it to
calculate his own total bid of $317,385 and identified Star as the paving subcontractor. The bidding
requirements called for identification of subcontractors responsible for at least one-half of one percent of the
work and a bond equal to ten percent of the general bid. Drennan's bid was the lowest, and he received the
principal contract.
The next morning, Drennan stopped at Star's office. Before he communicated acceptance, Star's construction
engineer said the bid contained a mistake and could not be honored. Star demanded at least $15,000.
Drennan responded that he had used the original figure, was obligated to perform his own contract, and
expected Star to perform at its quoted price. Star refused. After seeking replacement quotations for several
months, Drennan hired L & H Paving for $10,948.60, the lowest replacement bid he obtained. 51 Cal. 2d at
411-13.
Procedural History
Drennan sued Star for the additional paving cost caused by its refusal to perform. The trial court found that
Star made a definite offer to perform the specified work for $7,131.60 and that Drennan relied on the offer in
calculating his general bid and naming Star. It entered judgment for Drennan for $3,817, plus costs. The
damages represented the difference between Star's bid and the reasonable replacement cost.

Star appealed. Its principal argument presented a formation problem: no consideration supported an option,
no bilateral contract arose from Drennan's use of the bid, and Star communicated withdrawal before Drennan
communicated acceptance. It also relied on its mistake and challenged the sufficiency of Drennan's damages
allegations. The California Supreme Court reviewed the legal effect of reliance while accepting the trial
court's supported factual findings.
Issue
Did Drennan's foreseeable and reasonable reliance on Star's definite subcontract bid make the offer
temporarily irrevocable, even though he had neither paid consideration for an option nor accepted the
subcontract before Star attempted to withdraw? Could Star nevertheless escape liability because its bid
resulted from mistake, and did Drennan adequately establish mitigation and resulting damages?
Rule and Holding
Reasonable, foreseeable reliance producing a prejudicial change of position can support an implied
subsidiary promise not to revoke an offer, even without bargained-for consideration. Under the First
Restatement's § 90, Star's bid became irrevocable long enough to give Drennan a reasonable opportunity to
accept after the general contract was awarded. Drennan's use of the figure alone did not establish a bilateral
subcontract; reliance instead protected his later opportunity to accept.
Star's mistake did not defeat recovery because Drennan had no reason to know of it and had already
committed himself on the strength of the bid. The evidence also established reasonable mitigation. The result
was enforcement sufficient to recover the $3,817 additional cost. Id. at 413-17.
Judgment
The Supreme Court affirmed the trial court's judgment for Drennan for $3,817 plus costs. Drennan prevailed
on the reliance theory. The affirmance awarded damages for Star's refusal to honor the bid; it did not compel
Star to perform the paving work.
Reasoning
The court first rejected the easiest conventional contract theories. Star had not bargained for Drennan's use of
its quotation in exchange for a promise to keep the bid open. Nor did using the quotation manifest an
acceptance binding Drennan to award Star the subcontract if he obtained the principal contract. There was
therefore neither a consideration-supported option nor an already formed bilateral contract. Identifying this
gap was essential: the court's work was to explain why revocation could still be ineffective.
Section 90 supplied the answer. Star submitted the bid to obtain the paving subcontract. It could expect that a
low bid would be included in Drennan's calculation, help him obtain the principal award, and improve Star's
own prospects. Drennan's reliance was therefore commercially expected and beneficial to Star's objective.
Once Drennan committed himself to the owner, revocation would leave him bound to a price calculated from
a quotation that Star now refused to honor. Protecting a reasonable opportunity to accept allocated that risk
to the party that induced the commitment.

The court drew support from § 45's protection of an offeree who begins the performance invited by a
unilateral offer. In that setting an implied subsidiary promise prevents revocation while the offeree
completes the requested act. The court reasoned that foreseeable preacceptance reliance can justify
comparable protection for an offer contemplating a bilateral contract. The analogy did not convert Drennan's
bid calculation into the performance Star had requested. It explained why the absence of a completed
exchange did not necessarily preserve unrestricted revocation.
The protection had limits. A general contractor cannot delay acceptance after the principal award while
searching for a better price, or reopen negotiations with the subcontractor while continuing to insist on a
right to accept the original bid. Drennan promptly told Star that he had used its bid and expected
performance. His later search for replacement paving occurred after Star refused to honor the bid and served
to mitigate the resulting loss; it was not the forbidden attempt to keep Star's offer in reserve while bargaining
for a better original deal.
Mistake did not change the allocation. Drennan lacked notice of error, and paving quotations in the
Lancaster desert area commonly varied widely; the court referred to a 160-percent variance between the
highest and lowest bids. A low price therefore did not itself make reliance unreasonable. Star's cited
rescission cases involved circumstances in which the mistake was or should have been known and the other
party could be restored to its former position. Drennan had already committed to the principal contract. The
court placed the resulting loss on Star, which caused the mistake, rather than on the contractor who
reasonably relied on it.
Finally, the evidence showed that Drennan sought quotations for months and accepted the lowest
replacement bid. That conduct satisfied reasonable mitigation. His complaint alleged the amount he had to
pay for substitute paving. Any uncertainty in that allegation could have been challenged through a special
demurrer; Star's failure to raise the objection in that manner waived it. Id. at 413-17.
Separate Opinions
Chief Justice Gibson and Justices Shenk, Schauer, Spence, and McComb concurred in Justice Traynor's
opinion. The reported decision contains no separate concurrence or dissent.
Notes
Drennan distinguishes reliance from acceptance and reliance from consideration. The same act can
reasonably induce commercial expectations without having been bargained for as the price of a promise. The
court's solution was an implied commitment against revocation. Its discussion of a possible negligence duty
in preparing bids reinforced the fairness of allocating the loss to Star, but the court expressly left unresolved
whether negligence alone would support recovery on a case tried under that theory.
The opinion also stated that an offer expressly or clearly reserving revocation before acceptance would be
treated accordingly. Its holding should therefore be connected to the definite, unqualified bid and justifiable
reliance actually proved. The precise $3,817 recovery does not establish that every promissory-estoppel case
receives full expectation damages. The Second Restatement's § 90 expressly permits a remedy limited as
justice requires; § 87(2) more directly addresses protection of offers inducing preacceptance reliance.
