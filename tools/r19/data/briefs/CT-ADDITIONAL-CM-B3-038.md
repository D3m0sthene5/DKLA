# L-3 Communications Integrated Systems, L.P. v. United States
79 Fed. Cl. 453 (2007) | Contracts

## Court, procedural stage, and result

Judge Williams of the United States Court of Federal Claims issued this opinion under seal November 16, 2007, and reissued it with jointly proposed redactions and corrected errata November 26. Lockheed Martin Aeronautics Company intervened to defend its contract awards. The court denied the United States’ and Lockheed Martin’s Rule 12(b)(1) timeliness motions and Lockheed Martin’s Rule 12(b)(6) motion. It directed proposed redactions and a status conference for further proceedings. This was an interlocutory decision permitting the bid protest to continue, not a judgment finding corruption in the challenged procurement or awarding L-3 the $2,564,397 in bid-preparation and proposal costs it sought. No separate judicial writing appears. (79 Fed. Cl. at 453–455 & nn. 1–3, 466.)

## Procurement and later disclosure

The Air Force solicited proposals in August 1998 for engineering and manufacturing development and contractor-operated supply support in its C-5 Galaxy avionics modernization program. Raytheon E-Systems, L-3’s predecessor in interest, and Lockheed Martin were the only proposing contractors. Raytheon learned January 22, 1999 that Lockheed Martin had won the two contracts and received a debriefing January 28. That debriefing disclosed that senior Air Force acquisition official Darleen Druyun had acted as source selection authority and had changed evaluation ratings. It supplied her selection document, evaluation materials, and other information. Knowledge of those actions did not establish that Raytheon knew they were improperly motivated: a source selection authority ordinarily could exercise independent judgment and disagree with subordinate evaluators. (Id. at 456–457, 462 & n. 18.)

In 2002, before retirement, Druyun recused herself from matters involving Lockheed Martin and Raytheon, entered employment discussions with Lockheed Martin, and verbally agreed to accept a position there. She separately negotiated privately for Boeing employment while participating in Air Force negotiations with Boeing. She later retired, joined Boeing, and in April 2004 pleaded guilty to a conflict-of-interest conspiracy. Subsequent admissions described favors to Boeing influenced by its hiring of her daughter and future son-in-law and her own employment hopes. Her criminal conviction concerned Boeing dealings, not this C-5 procurement or her interactions with Lockheed Martin. Those transactions could provide context for investigation but did not themselves prove misconduct in the contract awards at issue. (Id. at 454 n. 3, 457–458 & nn. 9–12.)

A Defense Department study reviewed 407 acquisitions and selected eight anomalies, including the C-5 procurement, for further investigation. On February 28, 2006, the Inspector General issued a report identifying inadequately documented source-selection decisions, an unsupported reassignment of selection authority to Druyun, and unjustified ratings changes favoring Lockheed Martin’s higher-cost proposal. The report also identified inadequate oversight and management controls. L-3 filed this protest May 16, 2006 and amended its complaint in February 2007. The court drew the background from pleadings, appendices, the reports, and judicially noticed criminal proceedings; it did not adjudicate every report statement as a fact establishing liability. (Id. at 456 n. 6, 458–459; 463 n. 21.)

## Claims and the limitations dispute

L-3 alleged that Druyun improperly appointed herself source selection authority, manipulated evaluation ratings to favor Lockheed Martin, and concealed the resulting denial of fair consideration. It asserted breach of the implied obligation to consider proposals fairly, honestly, and in good faith, arbitrary and capricious action, and specified procurement-law violations. With the contracts largely performed, it sought its preparation costs rather than an injunction undoing the awards or requiring a new competition. Crucially, the pleading did not accuse Lockheed Martin of knowing about or participating in her alleged misconduct; the court expressly emphasized that distinction. (Id. at 454–455 & nn. 2–4, 461–462, 465 & n. 25.)

The United States and Lockheed Martin contended that the six-year limitation in 28 U.S.C. § 2501 ran from the January 1999 award, or at latest the debriefing. Under that theory a May 2006 complaint was late. L-3 argued that it could not know the injury underlying this particular unfair-consideration claim until the investigation disclosed the alleged irregularities. The court treated section 2501 as jurisdictional under then-binding Federal Circuit authority, notwithstanding L-3’s reliance on a different statute’s treatment in Kirkendall. L-3 carried the burden of establishing jurisdiction and jurisdictional timeliness, and the court could examine evidence beyond the pleadings when jurisdictional facts were disputed. (Id. at 459–461 & nn. 13–15.)

## Fair consideration and accrual reasoning

Before the Administrative Dispute Resolution Act, bid-protest jurisdiction rested on an implied contract to consider proposals fairly, equally, and consistently with the solicitation. Judge Williams reasoned that the Act supplied broader statutory protest jurisdiction and removed the need to use that implied contract as the jurisdictional predicate, while leaving unfair consideration actionable. The court acknowledged that the Federal Circuit had not resolved whether the implied-contract theory survived the Act and discussed contrary or differently phrased trial-court authorities. This opinion’s interpretation should therefore not be presented as a settled appellate holding disposing of that question. (Id. at 461–462 & nn. 16–17.)

The court distinguished knowing one had lost a procurement from knowing that the consideration process had allegedly been secretly manipulated. Raytheon knew Druyun served as selection authority and changed ratings, but neither action was unlawful on its face. Procurement officials exercised discretion under a presumption of good faith; bidders were not obliged to assume that an apparently authorized official had appointed herself without justification or secretly acted from personal bias. A speculative protective protest in 1999 could have disrupted the procurement without assuring discovery of the alleged conduct. The Inspector General report provided the relevant notice in February 2006. The court therefore held that this fair-consideration claim accrued then and was timely. (Id. at 462–463 & nn. 18–22.)

Alternatively, the narrowly applied accrual-suspension rule protected a claimant where government conduct was concealed or the injury inherently unknowable. The alleged concealment supplied that alternative basis. Accrual suspension concerned construction of when the claim accrued; it was distinct from equitable tolling. The court expressly did not resolve whether equitable tolling was available under section 2501. It also held that considering issuance of the Inspector General report to establish notice did not accept its assertions as true proof of wrongdoing; even an April 2004 accrual date would leave this suit timely. (Id. at 455 n. 5, 460–461 n. 15, 463–464 & nn. 21–22.)

## Why the pleading survived the merits motion

Lockheed Martin argued that L-3 could not overcome the presumption of officials’ good faith with clear and convincing evidence, particularly where the Boeing conviction postdated and did not concern this procurement. The court agreed that the heightened burden applied to allegations of fraud or comparable quasi-criminal misconduct. It did not apply that presumption indiscriminately to every exercise of contracting discretion. But the eventual evidentiary burden did not permit the court to weigh likely success at the pleading stage. (Id. at 464–465 & n. 24.)

The allegations tied Druyun’s earlier Lockheed Martin dealings, claimed employment ambitions, purported unauthorized assumption of selection authority, and asserted manipulation of evaluation ratings together. Although their connections were indirect and later proof might fail, they alleged more than an unsupported conclusion. Applying Twombly’s requirement for factual allegations supporting relief above speculation, the court held that the alleged denial of a fair opportunity could support bid-preparation and proposal costs if proved. Judicial notice of records from the criminal proceeding did not transform the pleading motion into summary judgment. Accordingly, the case could proceed to evidence; all challenged dismissal motions were denied. (Id. at 464 n. 23, 465–466 & nn. 25–26.)
