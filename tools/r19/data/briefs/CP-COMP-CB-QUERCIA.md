# Quercia v. United States

**289 U.S. 466 (1933).** Chief Justice Hughes wrote the Supreme Court’s opinion on May 29, 1933. The report contains no separate opinion or numerical vote announcement. The assigned own-caption account is CivPro physical page 574, printed page 1132, Note 1; the same physical page also reproduces Quercia’s limitation on judicial commentary in the preceding Nunley passage.

## Facts and procedural path

Quercia was convicted under the federal Narcotic Act after government agents testified against him and he took the stand to deny the charges generally. His testimony itself was not reproduced in the appellate record. The Supreme Court therefore did not reconstruct the underlying alleged narcotics transactions or decide the truth of his denial. The record supplied the challenged instructions and the question whether the judge’s comments exceeded permissible assistance to the jury. The trial court had denied a directed-verdict motion and related requested rulings; the First Circuit affirmed the conviction. (468.)

The judge instructed on innocence and reasonable doubt and generally told jurors that his opinions on evidence were not binding. He then pointed to the defendant’s hand-wiping while testifying and asserted that the mannerism almost always indicated lying. He described that proposition as fact despite being unable to explain its basis, and said everything Quercia had said—except agreement with the government’s witnesses—was a lie. He repeated that his opinion was not binding and that jurors who disagreed should acquit. Quercia excepted to this charge. (468–469.)

The Solicitor General did not oppose certiorari and acknowledged that part of the charge was improper. He submitted the matter for the Court’s judgment on unfairness and coercion without arguing that the conviction should either be affirmed or reversed. The separately printed summary at 467–468 states the government’s position, not the Court’s own reasoning. (467–468.)

## Issue and governing principle

Could the judge use his claimed experience with a defendant’s demeanor to denounce the defense testimony, with a general disclaimer preserving the jury’s factfinding role? The Supreme Court held that this charge improperly added to the evidence and was highly prejudicial. The disclaimer did not cure it because the unsupported factual assertion and forceful denunciation remained. (470–472.)

The Court did not adopt a rule of judicial silence. It recognized that a federal trial judge governs the proceeding, decides legal questions, and may help jurors understand evidence. Appropriate assistance can include discussing important evidence and expressing an opinion about facts, provided the judge makes clear that factual decisions belong to the jury. The opinion described that power as a common-law judicial prerogative maintained in federal courts under the Constitution. (469–470.)

The power carried corresponding restraints. A judge could analyze and dissect evidence but could not distort it, add to it, or assume a witness’s role. The judge’s institutional influence meant even a slight intimation could carry unusual persuasive force. Comments therefore had to avoid misleading or one-sided presentation, unwarranted deductions, and conjectural facts unsupported by evidence. In a criminal trial, hostile commentary must also not empty the defendant’s lawful opportunity to testify of practical value. (470.)

## Application and prejudice

The opinion reviewed examples showing why those limits mattered: treating concealment or flight as conclusive proof of guilt, dismissing a self-defense account through a general assumption about what any accused person would say, or attacking character where no character evidence existed. These illustrations came from other cases discussed by the Court. They were not additional behaviors or charges established against Quercia. (470–471.)

The judge’s treatment of hand-wiping crossed the line from evaluating the record into supplying a supposed fact drawn from personal experience. He placed that experience, backed by the authority of his office, against the accused. Instead of using the evidence to assist a fair credibility assessment, he swept aside everything the defendant said that conflicted with the government’s case. The combination of an unsupported generalization and blanket denunciation made the error highly prejudicial. The Court did not say that jurors could never consider demeanor; it condemned the judge’s added proposition and its use here. (471–472.)

The repeated reminder that jurors remained free to disagree did not withdraw the factual assertion. Its definiteness and the persuasive force of judicial utterance made it likely to remain in jurors’ minds and obstruct fair, dispassionate evaluation. A disclaimer was thus not an automatic cure for a concrete, prejudicial instruction. The reasoning focused on what this judge had said and left standing, rather than assuming every expression of a judicial opinion invalidated a verdict. (472.)

## Exact judgment and assigned comparison

The Supreme Court **reversed the judgment affirming Quercia’s conviction**. The original report’s final word is “Reversed”; its native OCR fails to capture that word reliably, so the original page 472 was inspected to confirm the mandate. The opinion does not announce an acquittal, find the defendant truthful, or supply a detailed remand instruction. There are no judicial footnotes, written separate opinions, or appendices. A reporter’s starred reference in the certiorari material is not a judicial footnote. (472.)
