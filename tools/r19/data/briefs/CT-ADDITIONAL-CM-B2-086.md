# North Gate Corp. v. National Food Stores, Inc.
30 Wis. 2d 317, 140 N.W.2d 744 (1966) | Contracts

## Court, issue, and disposition
The Wisconsin Supreme Court decided the appeal on March 17, 1966, after argument on February 1. Justice Fairchild wrote for the court; the published decision contains no separate writing or recorded dissent. The court affirmed the judgment for the corporate lessor because the restrictive lease provision did not extend to its shareholders merely by describing the lessor's beneficiaries. Questions about available remedies for breach could not prejudice the tenant when no breach existed under the proper construction. The report does not give a numerical voting lineup, and none is inferred here. (317, 321–324.)

## Lease and the tenant's proposed reach
North Gate was the lessor and National Food Stores the tenant. The assigned casebook describes a standard printed shopping-center lease drafted by National for its stores across the country. The actual opinion confirms National's role as draftsman and the form's design for varying factual situations. The litigated restriction concerned the use of nearby land for a retail food store. National sought to read paragraph Fifth as a warranty that North Gate's shareholders would not permit that use on land they owned individually within one mile. The issue was whether beneficiaries meant or included shareholders of this corporate lessor. It was not whether every conceivable entity listed in the clause was exempt from the restriction. (321–323; assigned C&M printed 567–568.)

The critical list extended beyond the lessor to its heirs or legal representatives, beneficiaries, subsidiaries, affiliates, successors, assigns, and entities in which the listed persons or entities had an interest. National's construction would therefore bring personally owned shareholder land within an obligation undertaken by North Gate. The opinion presents this as National's proposed interpretation; it does not establish an independent agreement signed by the shareholders or a basis to disregard corporate separateness. (321, 324.)

## Ordinary and technical meanings favored the lessor
The court began with context. Contractual terms generally receive their ordinary meaning, while technical terms ordinarily receive their meaning in the relevant profession or business unless context or applicable usage indicates otherwise. Neither the ordinary nor a technical meaning of beneficiary equated the word with shareholder. The cited dictionary included persons benefiting from trusts, estates, or insurance and a more general recipient of benefit or advantage, but that breadth did not turn the phrase into a reference to every owner economically benefiting from a corporation. (321 & nn. 1–2.)

Two interpretive considerations reinforced the result. A term admitting varying meanings is strictly construed against the draftsman, here National. The clause also restricted trade and land use, a kind of provision the court said should be strictly construed. That reasoning narrowed the contractual restriction; it did not invalidate the entire covenant or hold every retail exclusivity clause unenforceable. (322 & nn. 3–4.)

## Grammar and surplusage did not require beneficiaries to mean shareholders
National argued that the form contemplated only two lessor types: an individual or a corporation. On its reading, heirs and legal representatives referred to an individual, while the entire succeeding list beginning with beneficiaries had to apply to a corporation. It resisted treating the word as referring to a trust or estate, reasoning that the trustee or executor properly would be named lessor and that beneficiaries belong to the trust or estate rather than to that fiduciary personally. (322.)

The court recognized the grammatical point but found it inconclusive. There was no reason to prefer the assumption that the draftsman would never loosely name a trust as lessor over the competing assumption that a draftsman intending shareholders would have used that familiar word. The absence of an obvious term was especially telling when the drafter itself urged a strained extension to a situation that shareholders would have covered directly. Canons serve to identify the parties' intention, and both selected and omitted words can be informative. (322–323 & n. 5.)

National also invoked the rule against leaving contractual words useless. The court accepted the general rule but explained why it had reduced force here. A standard form designed for different lessor types necessarily contains terms inapplicable to some transactions. Beneficiaries could have work to do in a lease concerning trust property without having work to do in every lease by an ordinary corporation. Giving the word no application in this particular corporate transaction therefore did not justify stretching it to shareholders. This is a contextual limitation on the anti-surplusage canon, not a general permission to disregard language in individually negotiated contracts. (323 & n. 6.)

## Reserved exceptional relationships and the remedial point
The court qualified its conclusion: beneficiaries ordinarily had no meaning for a corporate lessor that was not a fiduciary. It noted that directors of a dissolved corporation might become trustees for creditors and shareholders by operation of law. Whether shareholders would qualify as beneficiaries in that exceptional setting, or another extraordinary setting absent here, was expressly left undecided. National had not claimed facts justifying departure from the usual separation between a corporation and its shareholders. Thus the ruling followed both the lease's words and the actual absence of an asserted exceptional corporate relationship. (323–324 & nn. 7–8.)

National further attacked trial-court observations about remedies for breach. The Supreme Court did not settle the accuracy of each observation; any error was nonprejudicial because the claim failed at the breach stage. It affirmed the judgment rather than remanding for remedial findings or awarding compensation. (324.)
