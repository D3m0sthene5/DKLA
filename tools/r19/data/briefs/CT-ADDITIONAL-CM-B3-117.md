# Hermanowski v. Acton Corp. — trial judgment and limited appellate remand
580 F. Supp. 140 (E.D.N.Y. 1983); 729 F.2d 921 (2d Cir. 1984) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Judge Glasser issued findings of fact and conclusions of law after a diversity bench trial on August 10, 1983. He found that Acton had granted Charles Hermanowski an irrevocable five-year stock option, that its attempted earlier cancellation did not terminate the option, and that its refusal of performance in November 1979 breached the agreement. This district-court judgment fixed damages at $415,030, with interest from November 12, 1979, and dismissed Acton’s unsupported counterclaims. The assigned problem changes the market prices and simplifies the transaction; those hypothetical figures are distinguished from the actual award below. (140–146.)

## Resignation agreement and purported cancellation
Hermanowski’s March 1973 employment contract made him Acton’s chairman, president, and chief executive at $65,000 a year through December 31, 1976. At an April 11, 1975 board meeting at his home, he said resignation would be in the company’s best interests. After discussion, the board accepted his resignation and resolved, in consideration of terminating his employment agreement, to pay $25,000 and grant a five-year nonqualified option for 50,000 common shares at $2 each. Company counsel prepared a handwritten agreement, signed that evening, saying the option would be under the terms of Acton’s 1972 nonqualified stock plan. (141–142.)

Acton had two plans. The qualified plan automatically ended an option on termination of employment or death. The nonqualified plan allowed company termination at the end of employment, within one year by notice, or thereafter on thirty days’ notice. In December 1975, after repeated requests, Acton sent option certificates bearing an April date and reciting the cancellation provision. Hermanowski signed with an express proviso that the option was noncancelable and returned a copy with a letter rejecting that provision. Acton did not respond until June 1976, when it asserted cancellability and then gave thirty days’ termination notice. Hermanowski objected in July and again in September; neither letter received a response. (142–144.)

On September 28, 1979, still within the five-year period, Hermanowski sent a $10,000 bank check requesting 5,000 shares in partial exercise. Acton returned it under a November 9 letter stating that its search showed no exercisable option. The court found November 12, when he learned of the rejection, to be the performance-breach date. The source’s dated letter and the court’s operative date are thus distinct, rather than interchangeable dates of dispatch. (142–144, 146.)

## Interpreting the option’s reference to the plan
Acton argued that the agreement incorporated all nonqualified-plan provisions, including cancellation. Hermanowski said the reference avoided the qualified plan’s automatic termination consequence and did not authorize cancellation of the consideration for his resignation. The court accepted his explanation in light of the surrounding circumstances. It considered implausible that an experienced executive would relinquish approximately $100,000 remaining under his employment agreement for $25,000 and an illusory option. (142–143.)

The court also treated the plan reference as ambiguous and admitted evidence of the negotiations. Directors Mamber and Koehl testified that the intended option was noncancelable for five years. Mamber explained that the cash-poor company could settle the employment contract inexpensively while allowing Hermanowski to participate in its future; Koehl had proposed the authorizing resolution. Neither director knew the plan provisions, and neither attached significance to their reference in the hastily prepared agreement. Upon the whole evidence, the court found an irrevocable option to buy 50,000 unregistered common shares at $2. (143.)

## Repudiation, election, and tender
Acton contended that any breach occurred when it sent the cancellation certificate in December 1975 or at the latest gave notice in June 1976. The court rejected that effort to force an early valuation date. An option is an irrevocable offer: Acton was bound to perform if Hermanowski elected to buy, but he remained free to exercise or decline. Its unilateral withdrawal did not extinguish that right. Good faith in believing cancellation permissible did not excuse repudiation. (143–144.)

Hermanowski could have treated the repudiation as a present breach, sought performance or retraction, or ignored it and awaited the time he chose to exercise. Although his 1976 letters demanded restoration or payment of the employment balance and threatened suit, he ultimately retained the option and exercised later. Acton’s later refusal constituted a breach as to all shares available under the option, not merely the 5,000 initially requested. Additional demand or tender for the remaining shares would have been futile. The court found readiness, willingness, and ability to perform implicit in its liability determination. Acton could have retracted by delivering shares but instead insisted that it owed none. Allowing Acton to choose the earlier date would take from Hermanowski the option’s contractual opportunity to speculate. (144.)

## Contract valuation and the actual calculation
Hermanowski sought the difference between the exercise price and the highest intermediate market price within a reasonable period after breach. The court distinguished cases applying that rule to conversion of fluctuating-value property. Those cases compensate owners whose stock or right of disposition was wrongfully taken, allowing time to replace it. Acton had not converted shares Hermanowski already owned or possessed. A broken promise to deliver stock therefore called for the ordinary contract measure: the loss or prevented gain at the time and place of breach. (144–146.)

Two ten-percent stock dividends had enlarged the option to 60,500 shares. On November 12, the stock’s high was $12.125 and low $11.50, averaging $11.8125. Because the option covered restricted stock, both experts accepted the need for a discount, disagreeing between 25 and 30 percent. The court chose 25 percent, fixing the share value at $8.86. It calculated $536,030 for the shares, subtracted what it described as the $121,000 option price, and awarded $415,030 plus statutory interest from November 12, 1979. These are the district court’s actual findings and arithmetic, rather than a claim that later appellate review necessarily left every component undisturbed. (146.)

## The separately reported 1984 appellate stage
On March 7, 1984, a Second Circuit panel of Judges Meskill, Pierce, and Pratt issued a per curiam decision affirming on Acton’s appeal but remanding on Hermanowski’s cross-appeal for reconsideration of the exercise-price calculation alone. The panel adopted the district court’s liability reasoning and approved the November 12 breach date and the market-value-minus-option-price measure. It questioned the multiplication of $2 by the dividend-adjusted 60,500 shares. Hermanowski argued that the initial aggregate exercise price remained $100,000, so the award should increase by $21,000. (729 F.2d at 921–922.)

The certificate authorized adjustment of both share numbers and prices for stock dividends. An apparently unanswered Rule 36 request and the district court’s finding of proportionate adjustments suggested that a price adjustment had occurred; Acton had not disputed the $100,000 figure in the trial submissions. Nevertheless, the appellate court could not be certain that the district judge lacked an evidentiary reason for using $121,000. It therefore did not itself award the proposed increase. It permitted reconsideration on the existing record or further evidence and entry of an appropriate judgment, affirming all other issues. The textbook’s unqualified “affirmed” label omits this limited remand. (729 F.2d at 923.)

## Assigned problem and reading scope
The problem at physical C&M PDF 348, printed 1005, substitutes $3 at attempted cancellation, a later $5 peak, and $4 at exercise, without the actual dividends or restricted-share discount. Applying this district opinion’s breach-date approach to those simplified facts gives 50,000 multiplied by the $2 spread, or $100,000, rather than using the earlier cancellation price or subsequent peak. Its separate questions about objection and tender are illuminated by the option’s continued irrevocability and the futility reasoning. That exercise result is not the actual $415,030 judgment.
