# King v. Trustees of Boston University
420 Mass. 52, 647 N.E.2d 1196 (1995) | Contracts

## Court and result
Justice Abrams wrote the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court's April 12, 1995 opinion. Chief Justice Liacos and Justices Nolan and Lynch also sat; the report contains no separate writings or recorded dissent. The court affirmed the judgment sustaining Boston University's ownership of papers deposited by Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr. A jury had found an enforceable charitable pledge supported by consideration or reliance, while answering negatively on the separate contract theory. The appellate decision upheld submission of the pledge questions to that jury and rejected challenges to the instructions; it did not substitute an appellate factual verdict. (52–54, 56–57, 64–66.)

## Deposit and the letter's two transfer provisions
In 1963 Boston University planned expanded library special collections. Its director, Howard Gotlieb, sought papers from Dr. King, a graduate-school alumnus. Other institutions, including Morehouse College, also approached him. Coretta Scott King testified that he believed Boston offered safety but worried that choosing it would be criticized as removing papers from a Black institution in the South. The volatile Southern circumstances helped explain his decision to deposit materials with BU. These facts were summarized favorably to BU as the nonmoving party on the challenged directed-verdict motion. (53–54.)

Dr. King's July 16, 1964 letter designated BU's library the repository of correspondence, manuscripts, other papers, awards, and research materials. It authorized immediate removal of most specified materials, including correspondence through 1961, and contemplated later annual shipments. Deposited materials would remain his legal property until otherwise indicated. BU would exercise scrupulous care, and he released it from responsibility for loss or damage despite that care. He then expressed an intention to identify portions each year as outright gifts until all were given, followed by a provision that at his death all materials deposited there would become BU's absolute property. The letter was signed. (54–55.)

The dispute therefore involved an initial transfer of custody and retention of legal title alongside provisions for later ownership. It was not a finding that every item became an immediately completed gift upon delivery. The court considered the whole letter and the relationship it established to determine whether the later-transfer language communicated a binding charitable commitment. (55, 57–61.)

## Conversion suit and jury findings
Mrs. King sued BU for conversion in December 1987 as estate administratrix and individually, asserting that the estate owned the papers. The jury considered ordinary contract, charitable pledge, limitations, and laches theories. Its special answers found a promise in the letter and consideration or reliance supporting the charitable pledge, but no contract under the separately submitted theory. It therefore did not reach limitations or laches. The trial judge denied judgment notwithstanding the verdict or a new trial, and the Supreme Judicial Court granted direct appellate review. (52–54; n.1.)

The second note records that the trial judge had removed BU's defenses of completed gift, gift of a future interest, and charitable trust. Because the judgment was affirmed on charitable pledge, the appellate court did not reach those issues or BU's other cross-appeal claims. The decision should not be read as sustaining all of BU's alternative ownership defenses. (53; n.2.)

## A charitable promise still required consideration or reliance
The court required a promise to transfer property for a charitable purpose and consideration or reliance supporting it. The third note explains that subscription and pledge are often used interchangeably, while the existing bailment technically made this a pledge. The fourth note discusses DeLeo, where a proposed synagogue library and an unperformed oral donation supported only hope or expectation, not proven consideration or reliance. Massachusetts had declined Restatement § 90(2)'s approach dispensing with proof of induced action or forbearance for charitable subscriptions. King did not change that choice or enforce every charitable promise merely because the recipient served public purposes. (55–57; nn.3–4.)

Donor intention remained central, assessed from the entire instrument and surrounding circumstances. The court sought to give effect to a sufficiently clear intention without abandoning specificity, consideration, or reasonable reliance. It recognized that charitable dealings differ from commercial exchanges, while preserving these requirements. (58–61.)

## Bailment supported submission of the promise issue
Read alone, the statement that Dr. King intended future gifts might convey only an expectation. But the letter and actual deposit established a consensual bailment: BU accepted custody and the specified standard of care while title remained initially with Dr. King. A factfinder could view the deposit as security for a promised future gift and thus as additional evidence of intent to be bound. In this context the letter and bailment supplied sufficient evidence to put the promise question to the jury. (59–61.)

The fifth note carefully limits the inference. Deposit with a charity creates no irrebuttable presumption of donative intent and does not override express conditions showing that the property was not promised or would be transferred only on specified terms. The sixth note identifies additional evidence: contemplated appraisal for tax purposes and later shipments consistent with the letter. Those circumstances reinforced the propriety of jury consideration, without compelling a universal finding from every charitable deposit. (60–61; nn.5–6.)

## Transfer at death and statutory formalities
Both parties agreed the letter could not itself operate as a will because it lacked the statutory witnesses. But the Statute of Wills did not prohibit a contractual arrangement for disposition at death. The court rejected the estate's distinct Statute of Frauds challenge to agreements to make testamentary dispositions. The letter was a promise of absolute title during life or, at the latest, at death, rather than a contract to make a will. (61–62.)

Even assuming that Statute of Frauds applied, the signed writing could state all terms of an enforceable agreement. Read together, the annual-gift provision and death provision permitted the jury to regard death as the latest date for completing the promised transfer of materials already in BU's custody. The alternative sufficiency holding did not make the letter a valid will or dispense with the requirements of consideration or reliance. (62–63.)

## Conduct beyond ordinary custody and the instructions
BU indexed the papers, made them available to researchers, employed trained staff to care for them and assist researchers, and held a convocation at which Dr. King spoke about selecting BU. The jury could find that activities such as indexing went beyond scrupulous custodial care and furnished consideration or reliance for the transfer promise. The appellate issue was evidentiary sufficiency for submission, not whether the appellate judges independently preferred the jury's outcome. (63–64.)

The instructions required BU to prove a sufficiently specific transfer promise and either consideration or reliance by a fair preponderance. The estate argued that the judge blurred intention and promise. Read as a whole, however, the charge distinguished a merely nonbinding present intention from a statement committing the speaker to specified conduct and permitting its recipient to expect performance. It correctly stated the pledge elements. A further objection that the judge should highlight and characterize the retained-title passage was unpreserved; the court additionally observed that a trial judge could not charge the jury on factual matters. Judgment was affirmed. (64–66.)
