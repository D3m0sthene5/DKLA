# Byrd v. Lamar
846 So.2d 334 (Ala. 2002) | Contracts

## Holding and limits of the student's success
The Alabama Supreme Court reversed summary judgment on a student's promissory-fraud claims against two university officials in their individual capacities, while affirming the remaining challenged judgments. Substantial circumstantial evidence created jury questions about promises to provide a music-media program allegedly known to be unavailable. The decision did not establish fraud conclusively or award damages. Lyons wrote the September 27, 2002 opinion; Chief Justice Moore and Houston, See, Brown, Johnstone, Harwood, Woodall, and Stuart concurred, making the reported decision unanimous. (334, 337, 343–349.)

The assigned account's abbreviated result for the student concerns survival of the promissory-fraud claims. It should not be read as judgment against the university itself, against every administrator, or on every fraud theory. The final disposition was affirmance in part, reversal in part, and remand. (340, 349; assigned printed 521.)

## Catalogue, assurances, and unavailable instruction
Bernard Byrd transferred from Miami-Dade Community College to Alabama State University in fall 1995 to pursue music media. The catalogue advertised specialized recording, music physics, digital-signal processing, film and video production, an internship, and an advanced recording laboratory. He chose ASU based on those representations. Music dean Horace Lamar was his academic adviser. Lamar repeatedly directed him to complete core requirements before entering specialized courses, although the catalogue allowed prospective music majors to begin music courses in their first semester. Byrd took general music courses but no music-media courses in his first three years. (337–338.)

According to Byrd, Lamar said in 1997 and 1998 that an instructor was being sought. In February 1999, instructor Carol Porter reported an admission by Lamar that he was deceiving Byrd and another student about the program. Lamar responded to Byrd's concern by assuring him that a changed course sequence still allowed completion and graduation in May 2000. An instructor appeared several weeks late for a spring recording course and offered grades without completed work. These accounts came from the summary-judgment record, not judicial findings after trial. (338, 344–345.)

After complaints to administrators, academic-affairs vice president Roosevelt Steptoe apologized and promised courses in the near future. A May 4, 1999 memorandum from Lamar scheduled courses across fall 1999, spring 2000, and summer 2000. Byrd enrolled in the two fall courses, but testified that an instructor appeared only after approximately five weeks, admitted lacking qualifications, and showed obsolete or nonworking equipment. The proposed alternative was minor computer work in exchange for passing grades; Byrd refused. Other professors reportedly said the program had been discontinued before he enrolled and qualified instructors were unavailable. (338–339, 344–345.)

By spring 2000 Byrd decided to withdraw without a degree. Student-affairs vice president Jacqueline Williams directed him back to Steptoe. According to Byrd, Steptoe then admitted the program did not exist and offered educational and living assistance, including tuition at another institution, if Byrd dropped his lawyer. Byrd rejected a deal and wanted the promised program taught and students no longer misled. That alleged exchange was part of the evidence about earlier knowledge; the court did not enforce it as a settlement. (339–340, 347.)

## Parties, claims, and summary-judgment posture
The original suit alleged breach of contract and fraud against ASU, named officials, and trustees in official and individual capacities. The trial court dismissed the university, contract claim, and official-capacity claims under Alabama constitutional immunity. The amended complaint asserted ordinary fraud against Lamar and president William Harris, promissory fraud against Lamar and Steptoe, and suppression against Harris and Williams. It named trustees without alleging a claim against them. Summary judgment subsequently disposed of the individual-capacity claims. (340.)

The appeal did not challenge the university's or official-capacity dismissals. Byrd also conceded the propriety of the trustees' dismissal or summary judgment. Review concerned the four named officials in their individual capacities. The evidence included deposition testimony, affidavits, correspondence, schedules, academic records, and catalogues; the defendants offered no testimony. That last point did not eliminate the plaintiff's burden or make all his assertions established facts. (337 n. 1, 340 & n. 2.)

Once a summary-judgment movant made the required prima facie showing, the nonmovant had to produce substantial evidence creating a genuine material dispute. The appellate court viewed evidence favorably to the nonmovant and resolved doubts against the movant. The substantial-evidence inquiry asked whether fair-minded persons could reasonably infer the asserted fact. (340–341.)

## Why ordinary fraud against Lamar and Harris failed
The substance of Lamar's separately labeled ordinary-fraud claim was nondisclosure: he allegedly knew the catalogue was inaccurate and should have informed Byrd. Byrd's opening appellate brief challenged misrepresentation, not suppression; he conceded the suppression characterization only in reply. The court would not consider a newly raised reply argument or reverse based on a version of the complaint not before the trial court. It affirmed this separately pleaded claim while later allowing promissory fraud against the same official to proceed. (341.)

Harris argued that the suit was effectively against the immune university that published the catalogue. The court addressed immunity as a threshold issue, recognized the exception for fraudulent conduct by an individual state agent, and proceeded to merits. Its analysis did not revive the dismissed university or official-capacity claims. (342.)

Existing-fact fraud required a material misrepresentation, reasonable reliance, and proximately resulting harm. Although the record supported an inference that catalogue descriptions were inaccurate, it did not substantially connect Harris personally to preparing the curriculum, knowing the advertised courses, approving the publication, or directly misleading Byrd. His status as president alone did not supply those missing facts. Byrd sent a complaint letter but received no demonstrated response from Harris. Summary judgment for Harris on fraud was affirmed on that evidentiary ground. (342–343.)

## Promissory fraud against Lamar survived
Promissory fraud required the ordinary fraud elements plus an intention, when the promise was made, not to perform and to deceive. Nonperformance alone was insufficient, but could combine with other circumstances to show prior intent. Later events could supply circumstantial evidence of that earlier intent. Recklessness alone did not suffice for a representation about future performance. (343, 346.)

Against Lamar the evidence went beyond courses simply failing to materialize. It included repeated assurances and delays, the reported admission to Porter, the scheduled courses that lacked adequate teachers and equipment, professors' accounts that the program had already ended and recruitment efforts were absent, and another student's corroborating affidavit about insufficient facilities and instruction. These circumstances created a jury question about knowledge and deceptive intent when Lamar promised future courses. (344–346.)

Lamar's core-requirements defense did not establish that the program actually existed but Byrd was ineligible. A referenced document about core-course completion was missing from the appellate record, and Lamar offered no showing that specialized courses were available to other students or that Byrd declined available courses. The claim concerned access to genuine specialized instruction, not merely obtaining a degree on time. Offered grades without instruction would not prepare him for the intended career. (344–345.)

The court also rejected a hearsay objection first raised on appeal. Defendants had submitted Byrd's deposition to support their motions without objecting to the relevant accounts. This was a preservation ruling, not a holding that every reported faculty conversation was inherently admissible for every purpose. (345.)

## Promissory fraud against Steptoe survived
Byrd's affidavit described Steptoe's early-1999 promises of timely completion and courses in the near future. Later testimony accused Steptoe of already knowing the program was absent, and described his eventual admission and attempted educational arrangement. Counsel had not challenged or sought elaboration of the testimony asserting prior knowledge, and Steptoe himself offered the deposition in support of summary judgment. (346–347.)

The court treated those assertions as factual testimony about knowledge, not simply speculation or an allegation of recklessness. Combined with Steptoe's involvement in academic affairs and the particular promises, they supplied substantial circumstantial evidence. A denial of earlier knowledge highlighted an issue for a jury rather than eliminating it. Summary judgment on his promissory-fraud claim was reversed, with ultimate credibility and liability unresolved. (347–348.)

## Suppression and exact remand
Suppression required a duty to disclose, actual knowledge and concealment of an existing material fact, induced action or inaction, and resulting harm. Byrd supplied no evidence establishing Harris's or Williams's knowledge of the unavailable courses, unqualified instructors, or inadequate equipment; knowledge could not simply be presumed. Even assuming knowledge, the record did not show concealment by either. His only conversation with Williams occurred after his decision to withdraw, and receipt of his letter by Harris was not established. Their suppression judgments were affirmed. (348–349.)

The court ultimately affirmed the judgments for Harris, Williams, and the trustees, and Lamar's separately labeled ordinary-fraud claim. It reversed only the individual-capacity promissory-fraud judgments for Lamar and Steptoe and remanded. No contract recovery, damages amount, or final fraud verdict was ordered. (349.)
