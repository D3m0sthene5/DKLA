# American Radio Relay League, Inc. v. FCC
524 F.3d 227 (D.C. Cir. 2008) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and regulatory setting

The FCC adopted technical rules for Access Broadband over Power Line, which transmits communications through electrical lines but also emits radio-frequency energy. Those emissions could interfere with licensed radio users, including amateur operators represented by the American Radio Relay League. The technology offered wider broadband access and competition, including in rural areas served by power lines. The agency had to accommodate those benefits while enforcing protections against harmful interference. Majority slip pp. 2–5, 24.

Sections 301 and 302 of the Communications Act supplied the statutory setting. The FCC's longstanding Part 15 rules permitted certain devices to operate without licenses if their emissions did not have significant potential for harmful interference. Harmful interference meant danger to safety services or serious degradation, obstruction, or repeated interruption of radio communication; mere interference was not enough. The existing scheme imposed both a condition against harmful interference and an obligation to stop operation when such interference occurred. The BPL rules added certification, emission limits, a nationwide operational database, frequency and geographical exclusions, and mechanisms to lower power, change frequencies, or stop system segments. Public-safety complaints received special treatment. Majority slip pp. 3–5, 8–11.

For mobile radio users, the FCC concluded that lowering BPL power by twenty decibels below the emission limit would leave only interference comparable to background noise at relevant distances. On reconsideration it clarified that operators complying with this reduction would not need further action to resolve mobile-interference complaints. It also retained an extrapolation factor of forty decibels per decade for frequencies below thirty megahertz. That factor estimates how quickly signal strength falls with distance; a twenty-decibel factor would produce a higher estimate of transmitted emissions at the regulatory measurement distance. Thus, the choice affected the practical strength of protection, rather than serving as an incidental arithmetic detail. Majority slip pp. 5–6 & nn.1–2, 20–22.

The FCC relied on its own field investigations when deciding that the interference risk was low and manageable. After one unsuccessful disclosure request and a later request following the final rule, it released five staff studies in partially redacted form and added them to the rulemaking record in December 2004, after promulgation. The studies covered particular companies and locations in New York, North Carolina, and Pennsylvania. The FCC characterized withheld passages as preliminary results or deliberative staff opinions and asserted that it had not relied on those portions. The League sought reconsideration, then petitioned for judicial review of the original and reconsideration orders. Majority slip pp. 5–7, 14–15.

## Challenges, procedure, and result

The League sought vacatur on four grounds: an unexplained departure from the FCC's longstanding shutdown protection; failure to disclose relied-on studies for meaningful comment; lack of reasoned support for the forty-decibel extrapolation factor; and inadequate consideration of restricting BPL to the thirty-to-fifty-megahertz band. The FCC opposed all four. The court reviewed agency action under the APA's arbitrary-and-capricious standard, recognizing considerable deference on technical matters while requiring an intelligible connection between facts and regulatory choices. The opinion also described Chevron-era statutory deference and deference to agency interpretations of their regulations as understood in 2008. Majority slip pp. 7–8.

Judge Rogers wrote for the court, with Judge Tatel joining and writing separately. Judge Kavanaugh joined the background, statutory-interference analysis, and alternative-frequency analysis; he concurred only in the judgment on disclosure and dissented on the extrapolation factor. Thus, the disclosure-remand result had all three judges' support, while the extrapolation remand rested on Rogers and Tatel. The court granted the petition in part and remanded the rule with specified instructions. The final order did not order vacatur. Majority slip pp. 24–25; Kavanaugh slip p. 1.

## Why two challenges failed

The court rejected the asserted abandonment of shutdown protection. The FCC did not redefine harmful interference or announce that actual harmful interference could persist indefinitely. Instead, it found that after the required power reduction the remaining emissions would not satisfy the existing definition for mobile communications, so the shutdown condition would not arise. The League acknowledged that longstanding policy did not require elimination of every interference at every place and time. Observing that mobile users could reposition their equipment reflected the character of mobile reception, rather than creating a new legal burden. The agency's shorthand reference to section 302 also did not show that it abandoned section 301: it used the public-interest authority to regulate devices remaining below the licensing trigger. Majority slip pp. 8–11 & nn.3–4.

The thirty-to-fifty-megahertz proposal was a significant alternative deserving a response, but the FCC supplied one. It explained that the restriction would reduce system capacity and design flexibility and raise public costs without corresponding need, given the adopted interference-remediation measures. Complete avoidance of amateur frequencies would apply even where no amateur users were nearby. The agency also explained why safety-critical services warranted protections beyond those appropriate for routine amateur and hobby communications. The court accepted that technical and policy explanation; it did not require the agency to select the League's preferred alternative. Majority slip pp. 23–24.

## Why the studies required disclosure

Section 553's notice-and-comment process must permit useful participation, including evaluation of technical material on which a rule rests. The court applied its Portland Cement and related precedents requiring disclosure of relied-on technical studies and staff reports. It examined the unredacted pages in camera and found summaries of test data, scientific recommendations, and analysis of methodology. Withholding pieces of studies on which the agency relied could conceal qualifications, adverse results, or explanations essential to assessing the published data. The agency remained free to credit particular parts after disclosure; it could not make a study central factual support while preventing meaningful scrutiny of intertwined portions. Majority slip pp. 12–19.

The disclosure error also met the prejudicial-error requirement. The League identified something useful it could say about omitted recommendations and methodology, including headings referring to new information warranting caution and spectrum tradeoffs. The withheld information could support a credible challenge to the FCC's manageable-risk conclusion. The League did not have to establish that comments would certainly change the final rule before obtaining access to the information needed to comment. Majority slip pp. 14–16.

The court distinguished EchoStar, where the pertinent study had become available before reconsideration and undisclosed staff material merely reflected thoughts about disclosed evidence, without a timely record objection. Here, the FCC's own statements made the five never-fully-disclosed studies central sources of data. Its invocation of FOIA's deliberative-process privilege did not justify blanket secrecy for factual scientific studies it had adopted or incorporated into public regulatory action. No separate confidentiality interest or merely supplemental character of the withheld material was asserted. Majority slip pp. 16–18.

The majority rejected the claim that disclosure imposed procedures forbidden by Vermont Yankee. It understood its order as enforcing the agency's chosen notice-and-comment process under established section 553 doctrine, rather than adding an extra statutory hearing device. On remand the FCC had to disclose the employed technical material, give a reasonable opportunity for comment, and include the studies in the record. The court did not decide the League's separate argument that the late release of the partially redacted studies also violated the APA. Majority slip pp. 18–20, 25.

## Why the extrapolation explanation failed

Technical deference did not cure the unexplained choice of forty decibels. Modeling evidence cited by the FCC did not satisfactorily address empirical results, and the NTIA study itself questioned whether the existing factor described BPL signal decay. Later modeling results were not in the record. The agency invoked inconclusive experimental data but did not explain why uncertainty justified applying a factor designed for technologies differing in scale, power, and frequencies. The existing regulation had itself characterized the factor as interim pending an appropriate measurement procedure. Majority slip pp. 20–22 & n.5.

The League submitted three 2005 British regulatory studies and its own analysis favoring twenty decibels. The FCC's conclusory statement that no new information convincingly justified modification did not demonstrate engagement with this evidence. The remand required either a reasoned justification for retaining forty decibels that grappled with the studies or another factor accompanied by a reasoned explanation. It did not judicially select twenty decibels. The court also left unresolved the alternative sliding-scale proposal and whether it had been properly presented. Majority slip pp. 22–23 & n.6.

## Separate opinions and assigned focus

Tatel emphasized section 706's whole-record requirement as an additional reason for complete disclosure. A reviewing court and challengers must be able to examine evidence detracting from the agency's cited support. Selective deletions from relied-on studies could otherwise allow the agency to evade its responsibility to confront contrary evidence. A mutually agreed limited record after both sides review the complete record differs from one party's unilateral withholding. Tatel slip pp. 1–5.

Kavanaugh accepted that circuit precedent compelled disclosure, but thought the Portland Cement doctrine lacked support in section 553's text and conflicted with Vermont Yankee's limits on judicially imposed procedure. That disagreement concerned the doctrinal foundation, not his agreement in the disclosure-remand judgment. He separately thought the FCC adequately explained its extrapolation choice: conflicting technical evidence, inconclusive new studies, and a willingness to revisit the factor supplied a discernible rationale within agency expertise. He warned that expanding procedural obligations together with demanding substantive review could make rulemaking protracted and unpredictable. Kavanaugh slip pp. 1–8.
