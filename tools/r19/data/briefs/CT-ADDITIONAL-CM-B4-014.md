# Mieske v. Bartell Drug Company
92 Wn.2d 40, 593 P.2d 1308 (1979) | Contracts

## Court and result
The Washington Supreme Court decided this en banc appeal on April 19, 1979. Justice Brachtenbach wrote; Chief Justice Utter and Justices Rosellini, Stafford, Wright, Horowitz, Dolliver, and Hicks concurred. No separate opinion appears. The court affirmed a $7,500 jury verdict against a retailer and film processor for losing irreplaceable developed family movie films. It upheld valuation by intrinsic value to the owners and rejected a receipt clause purporting to limit responsibility to the film’s retail cost. (40–42, 51.)

## Facts and litigation
Over many years the Mieskes recorded their wedding, honeymoon, vacations, holidays, their son’s activities, pets, construction of their home, and relatives who had since died. They had thirty-two fifty-foot reels of developed film and wanted them spliced onto four larger reels for easier viewing. Mrs. Mieske, a customer of Bartell’s camera department for at least ten years, was told that the service was available. The couple arranged and marked the reels in order and delivered them in four paper bags inside a larger bag. (42.)

Bartell’s manager attached a processing packet and handed Mrs. Mieske a receipt stating that responsibility did not extend beyond retail film cost unless otherwise agreed in writing. They did not discuss that clause. She specifically warned that the films represented her life and should not be lost. They also did not agree or discuss who would perform the splicing; she assumed Bartell would do it. Bartell instead sent the package to GAF, which intended to forward it to another lab. The films were present in GAF’s lab one day and missing the next. The court described disposal through a dumpster and landfill as the best explanation, not as an independently proven chain of custody. (42–43.)

The jury returned a $7,500 verdict against Bartell and GAF, but not the janitorial company that apparently removed the package. The janitorial company was not a party to the appeal. Bartell and GAF admitted negligence for purposes of appeal and challenged the damages instructions and exclusionary clause’s treatment. Liability for negligence was therefore not the contested appellate issue. (42–43.)

## Valuing irreplaceable developed film
Washington’s framework had three categories: market value for destroyed property having a market; replacement or reproduction cost when property without a market could be replaced or reproduced; and value to the owner when property had neither a market nor a replacement or reproduction. The last category did not authorize an owner’s unlimited sentimental or fanciful valuation. (43–44.)

Defendants wanted compensation confined to buying replacement blank film. That confused the physical medium with the property actually lost. The Mieskes had lost images of unrepeatable family events, not merely film capable of receiving new images. Blank reels would not replace those images. Consequently, the third category applied, often called actual or intrinsic value. Difficulty in fixing that value was not a reason to deny recovery for an actual, irreparable loss. (44–45.)

The court clarified its exclusion of sentimental value. Intrinsic value necessarily includes a subjective element; excluding all feeling would make the value-to-owner rule self-defeating. The prohibited sentiment was unusual, unwarranted emotional indulgence or a fanciful price placed on an object for peculiar reasons, rather than every ordinary human attachment. The trial instruction permitted fair compensation for actual or intrinsic value, including film and processing cost and other supported considerations, while excluding sentimental or fanciful valuation. Plaintiffs still bore the evidentiary burden, and the instruction prohibited speculation. The court upheld it. (45–46 & n. 1.)

Defendants’ proposed instruction was not a correct alternative. Although their theory favored blank-film replacement cost, the proposed language broadly allowed replacement or reproduction and did not exclude sentimental value. It was more liberal in that respect than the instruction given. The court therefore found no error in refusing it. (46.)

## Applying UCC principles to the service bailment
Defendants invoked the UCC rule allowing exclusion or limitation of consequential damages unless unconscionable. Plaintiffs answered that Article 2 concerned sales, whereas this transaction was a bailment for a service and transferred no title. The court rejected that narrow scope argument. It relied on Article 2’s stated reach to transactions in goods, authorities applying relevant sales principles beyond conventional sales, and its own precedent applying the Code’s disclaimer policy at least by analogy to a bailment. It saw no persuasive distinction, for this purpose, between a service bailment and a lease bailment. This was the court’s analysis of this particular film transaction and clause; the opinion did not examine every kind of service contract. (46–48.)

## Why trade usage and course of dealing did not validate the clause
Code definitions allowed the parties’ bargain to incorporate course of dealing and established trade usage, but those concepts required proof. Defendants proved similar limitation practices among film processors, not a usage understood by retail customers dealing with Bartell. They did not establish that Mrs. Mieske knew or should have known the asserted usage. An industry’s own widespread practice was insufficient by itself to prove a consumer understood or accepted it. (48–49.)

Her ten-year customer relationship likewise did not establish the necessary common understanding. She had never read the exclusion or discussed it with the manager, who was himself unclear about its language. The record did not show what language appeared on receipts in their earlier dealings. The court refused defendants’ request to establish a binding course of dealing as a matter of law from those facts. (49.)

## Unconscionability and the jury’s limited role
The controlling unconscionability analysis required examination of the transaction’s circumstances, including conspicuousness, prior dealings, negotiation, commercial setting, and trade usage. No single factor controlled. Unconscionability was for the court as a matter of law, and the parties had to receive a reasonable opportunity to present relevant evidence. Defendants conceded that this evidentiary opportunity had been provided. The judge heard testimony and reviewed documents, including another processor’s receipt admitted solely for the judge’s assessment rather than the jury’s use. (50.)

Defendants objected that the instruction made the limitation ineffective absent a specific agreement, implying that express agreement was the sole test. The Supreme Court agreed that a simple instruction announcing the judge’s legal conclusion would have been preferable. Nevertheless, any error was harmless: the judge had considered the required circumstances and had not delegated unconscionability to the jury. If anything, the instruction left defendants an additional favorable possibility if the jury found a specific agreement, which the evidence did not support. The court did not establish that every unread receipt clause is automatically unconscionable without considering its setting. (50–51 & n. 2.)

## Exact disposition and assigned significance
The court affirmed the judgment, leaving the $7,500 verdict against Bartell and GAF intact. There was no remand to revalue the films and no award against the nonappealing janitorial defendant. (42–43, 51.)
