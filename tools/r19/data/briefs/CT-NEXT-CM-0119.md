# Internatio-Rotterdam, Inc. v. River Brand Rice Mills, Inc.
259 F.2d 137 (2d Cir. 1958) | Decided September 12, 1958 | Contracts

## Transaction and the missed shipping call
In July 1952, exporter Internatio contracted to buy 95,600 pockets of rice from processor River Brand at $8.25 per pocket, free alongside ship at Lake Charles and/or Houston. The purchase memorandum required December shipment on a two-week call from the buyer. An immediately opened irrevocable letter of credit would pay against dock receipts and specified documents. Internatio had committed the rice to a Japanese customer but encountered unexpected United States export restrictions and difficulty obtaining a license. December was also a peak season for rice and cotton, leaving mills and docks congested. 259 F.2d at 139.

River Brand chose to ship 50,000 pockets through Lake Charles. It received instructions for that portion on December 10 and promptly shipped; deliveries continued through December 23, and the last car was unloaded December 31. The remaining 45,600 pockets were allocated to Houston. December 17 was the last date on which instructions would leave the seller its agreed two weeks entirely within December. Internatio, still unable to secure a ship or dock in Houston, had supplied no Houston instructions by then. River Brand canceled that portion on the morning of December 18 while continuing Lake Charles performance. Rice’s market price had risen to $9.75, and the court acknowledged that the higher price was one reason for prompt cancellation. Internatio sued over the Houston nondelivery. Id.

## Posture and the buyer’s interpretation
The Southern District of New York dismissed the complaint after Internatio presented its case. The Second Circuit reviewed that dismissal. The district court applied Texas law through New York’s choice-of-law rules; the appellate court agreed but did not elaborate because the parties conceded that Louisiana law would yield the same result. Id. at 139.

Internatio conceded both its duty to give instructions by December 17 and the seller’s entitlement to two weeks after receiving complete instructions. Its argument was more specific than a demand for immediate delivery: late instructions should start a fresh two-week period, requiring shipment in January if necessary. That interpretation would preserve the buyer’s opportunity to obtain the rice while allowing the seller its preparation time. River Brand answered that December delivery itself was essential, so the call had to occur early enough to make timely shipment possible. Id. at 139–40.

## Promissory condition and the delivery window
Giving shipping instructions served two functions. Internatio promised to give them, and receipt of them was a condition of River Brand’s duty to ship. A seller could not deliver free alongside ship without knowing the ship and its location. The court described this combination as a promissory condition. The decisive interpretive question was whether timely notice within the December arrangement was required, or whether any later notice could postpone shipment beyond the agreed month. Id. at 140.

December shipment was of the essence in this setting. The express delivery month protected the seller’s production and storage planning during its busiest period and the buyer’s own customer commitments. River Brand had other January obligations. Fluctuating prices also made a unilateral buyer option to postpone performance commercially implausible: a postponement could shift the time of an impending breach into a falling market, without giving the seller any reciprocal option. Most concretely, the letter of credit authorized payment only for deliveries during December. Compulsory January shipment would deprive River Brand of the agreed payment security. Id.

Because December mattered, instructions by December 17 were a condition of enforceable shipment, as well as a buyer duty. Failure of that condition entitled River Brand to rescind or treat the Houston obligations as discharged. Its December 18 cancellation ended those duties. Later success in securing a license, ship, and dock, an offer to accept January deliveries, and a liberalized credit arrangement could not revive them. Id. at 140–41. Footnote 1 adds that River Brand learned of the credit liberalization only after rescission and that even the revised credit did not authorize payment for January deliveries. Id. at 141 n.1. The report’s sentence attributing the December 18 exercise of rescission to “appellant” is an apparent party-label error: the narrative and resulting discharge concern seller-appellee River Brand, as the assigned editors also flag.

## Part performance, divisibility, and waiver
Internatio argued that its substantial preparatory activity prevented a finding of default. The court rejected that argument for the Houston portion. Seeking export and shipping arrangements and establishing a letter of credit prepared for later performance; they supplied no benefit to the seller from Houston deliveries that never occurred. This is a conclusion about these activities in this transaction, not a rule that opening credit can never fulfill a contractual undertaking. Id. at 141.

The buyer also claimed that a single indivisible contract made continued Lake Charles shipment an election to reaffirm the whole obligation. River Brand’s conceded option to split shipments, a price stated per pocket, and separately payable dock receipts supported treating the duties at each port as paired and reciprocal. The buyer benefited more from receiving the Lake Charles portion than from receiving nothing in a rising market. Those circumstances did not support an inference that the seller waived cancellation at Houston. The court rejected estoppel and waiver as lacking a basis in fact or law. Id.

## Judgment and limits
The Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal. Hincks wrote for a panel with Lumbard and Waterman; no separate opinion is reported. The complete judicial text includes one footnote, which has been read along with the assigned excerpt. The case illustrates how context can make a stated delivery period essential and how a promised act can also condition another party’s duty. It does not make every delivery date essential or erase the need to interpret the particular bargain. The source record distinguishes the historical court’s analysis from the casebook’s later editorial UCC discussion. No current citator review is represented.
