# Schwartzreich v. Bauman-Basch, Inc.
231 N.Y. 196, 131 N.E. 887 (1921) | Contracts

## Decision, vote, and procedural setting

The New York Court of Appeals affirmed the judgments reinstating an employee's jury verdict on May 10, 1921. Judge Crane's opinion was joined by Chief Judge Hiscock and Judges Hogan, Cardozo, McLaughlin, and Andrews. Judge Chase dissented without an accompanying explanation in the report. The six-to-one decision held that parties can expressly rescind an executory employment agreement and simultaneously make a replacement for the same services at higher compensation. The mutual promises then supply consideration. That holding preserved the requirement of new consideration for a mere modification of a continuing agreement, and made the parties' actual mutual cancellation the decisive fact. (197–198, 201–205.)

## Original employment and competing offer

On August 31, 1917, Louis Schwartzreich and Bauman-Basch entered a written agreement for him to design coats and wraps, beginning November 22 for twelve months. He would receive $90 weekly, devote his entire time and attention to the company's business, and use his best efforts. Before the work began, another concern offered him more money. The company's officer testified that he heard Schwartzreich intended to leave, reminded him of his existing contract, and learned of a $115 weekly offer. Needing a designer to prepare a sample line, Bauman offered $100 weekly rather than lose him, and Schwartzreich agreed to stay. (198–199.)

Schwartzreich's testimony differed in tone and amount. He said Scheer & Mayer offered $110, and he sought friendly advice because he recognized his existing commitment and thought he should not accept the competing offer. According to him, Bauman returned the next day offering $100, which he gratefully accepted. The court did not resolve the $110 versus $115 discrepancy into a single undisputed figure; the employer and employee supplied different accounts relevant to what they agreed. (199–200.)

A new contract dated October 17 used the same language and employment period, changing compensation to $100 weekly. Both parties executed it and kept duplicate originals. At that time the employee's copy of the old contract was returned or left with Bauman; Bauman testified that Schwartzreich tore off its signatures. The employee testified that Bauman said the old contract was no longer needed because the new one replaced it. He worked until the following December, when the employer discharged him, and sued for damages under the October agreement. (200.)

## The jury issue and lower-court rulings

The employer maintained that the increased salary lacked consideration because Schwartzreich already owed the same work for the same period at $90. The trial judge instructed the jury to find consideration if the parties had mutually cancelled the earlier agreement before or at execution of the replacement. Mutual intention could be shown through words or acts, but the old agreement had to become null from that point. Counsel specifically excepted to allowing cancellation simultaneously with the new agreement. The jury found for Schwartzreich; the trial judge then set aside the verdict and dismissed the complaint for insufficient cancellation evidence. (200–201.)

The Appellate Term reversed and reinstated the verdict and judgment rather than directing another trial. The Appellate Division affirmed. On the employer's permitted appeal, the Court of Appeals concluded that evidence viewed favorably to the employee supported mutual destruction, cancellation, or abrogation of the old contract. It therefore was a factual question for the jury, not a basis for dismissal as a matter of law. Because the verdict had been reinstated, the court also had to decide whether the jury instruction correctly allowed simultaneous cancellation and replacement. (197, 201–202.)

## Rescission distinguished from modification

The court accepted the settled rule that promising performance already owed under a contract ordinarily supplies no consideration for an increased payment. It also found established recognition that parties may cancel their obligations by mutual consent and then agree afresh. Vanderbilt required valid cancellation before a new compensation agreement could be sustained on that basis; Cosgray involved an ineffective increase where the original yearly employment had never been terminated. (202–203.)

The legally significant distinction was whether the old obligations continued with altered pay or ended by agreement. If they continued, new consideration was needed for the change. If both parties terminated the existing agreement and substituted another, the renewed mutual promises supplied consideration. The court acknowledged that the practical difference can look small when the work, period, and parties remain identical. But forbidding voluntary cancellation and replacement would prevent parties from restructuring obligations they both wished to end unless the replacement supplied some additional privilege. (203.)

There was no required pause between rescission and the new bargain. Once cancellation was mutually intended, expressed, and acted on, whether it occurred an instant before or simultaneously with the replacement was immaterial. The court surveyed other jurisdictions' approaches to threatened nonperformance, substitute bargains, unforeseen hardship, and mitigation, while distinguishing cases in which no complete rescission occurred. It did not presume rescission from every later pay promise. Here the evidence supported express termination rather than continuation with modifications, and the jury charge properly demanded that finding. (203–205.)

The judgments of the Appellate Division and Appellate Term were affirmed with costs, preserving the reinstated verdict and judgment for the employee. The report does not specify the verdict's dollar amount, so this brief does not manufacture one from the weekly rate or employment term. Judge Chase's recorded dissent supplies a voting distinction but no published reasoning to reconstruct. (205.)
