# Kryl v. Mechalson
259 Wis. 204, 47 N.W.2d 899 (1951) | Contracts

## Court and procedural outcome
The Wisconsin Supreme Court decided Kryl on May 8, 1951, with Justice Gehl writing. The reported opinion contains no separate writing or numerical voting lineup. The court affirmed an order granting a new trial on all issues because the original trial had wrongly excluded testimony about a condition to the writing’s becoming an effective contract. This was an evidentiary and new-trial ruling, not a final judgment establishing that the defendant proved the condition or owed nothing. (204–208.)

## Offered condition and opposing theories
The plaintiff sought to exclude offered proof under the parol-evidence rule. The defendant maintained that the writing was not to operate as a contract until both the union consented and Mr. Quaal approved. The defense therefore concerned the existence of a binding contract before those events, rather than a different price, performance term, or later right to cancel an already-effective agreement. The reported judicial text identifies these approval requirements but does not provide a detailed reconstruction of the underlying transaction, the monetary demand, or the jury’s original monetary result. Those facts are not supplied by borrowing the transactions described in the court’s precedent discussions. (205–206.)

The trial court ultimately concluded that the testimony should have been received and ordered a new trial. On appeal, the supreme court agreed. It described the offered testimony as proof of a condition precedent necessary to the instrument’s contractual effectiveness. It was not directed toward altering the writing’s contents. The court treated the asserted condition as compatible with the written terms. (206, 208.)

## Parol evidence directed to effectiveness rather than content
The court relied on decisions allowing extrinsic proof that a writing was not to take effect until a future event, provided the alleged condition did not contradict the written conditions. It distinguished such proof from a condition subsequent that would undo a contract after it had already become effective. Its explanation was that the evidence could establish that the instrument never became operative as a contract in the first place. (206.)

The court compared conditional stock subscriptions, where an obligation might depend on others’ subscribing or a specified total being obtained. It also discussed an earlier mining-stock note conditioned on the venture’s profitability and another note conditioned on obtaining additional signatures. Those examples supported the admissibility principle; they were separate precedents and not the factual subject of Kryl’s disputed writing. (206–208.)

## The merger clause did not expressly exclude conditions
The plaintiff relied on a clause describing the writing as complete in itself and denying that the first party was bound by other terms or agreements. The court did not find this language sufficient to exclude the offered effectiveness condition. It distinguished Edward T. Kelly Co. v. von Zakobiel, whose writing went further by declaring itself irrevocable and stating that no conditions affecting it existed outside the document. That latter formulation expressly excluded the very kind of condition sought to be proved. (206–207.)

Accordingly, Kryl did not hold that all integration clauses permit every alleged oral term. It preserved the requirement that the condition not contradict the writing and recognized that an express declaration excluding external conditions could bar the evidence. Its result turned on the actual clause’s failure expressly to exclude conditions of effectiveness, together with the nature of the offered proof. (206–207.)

## Application and scope of the remand
Excluding the condition testimony led the jury to answer inquiries that did not properly address the issues. The court therefore sustained a new trial on all issues. Admissibility allowed the defendant to present the asserted union-consent and Quaal-approval condition for evaluation; it did not itself prove that the parties had agreed to it or conclusively determine whether it occurred. The precise final order was affirmance of the trial court’s new-trial order. (208.)
