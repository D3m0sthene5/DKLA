## Case orientation

The court upheld OSHA's long-term exposure limit for ethylene oxide, but remanded its refusal to impose a short-term limit. The same opinion illustrates both deference to reasonable scientific judgments under uncertainty and meaningful judicial review of an unexplained gap in an agency's reasoning. Although the assigned chapter places the case in the debate over presidential supervision of administration, the court expressly avoided deciding whether the Office of Management and Budget's intervention was lawful.

## Facts and regulatory history

Ethylene oxide, or EtO, was widely used in manufacturing and to sterilize hospital instruments. Industrial systems were typically enclosed and automated, although shipping and maintenance could expose workers. Hospital sterilization consumed less EtO but created particularly serious exposure risks: workers entered areas where the gas had been used, and imperfectly purged sterilization chambers could release it. The danger was occupational exposure, rather than consumer use of the sterilized instruments.

OSHA adopted a consensus limit of 50 parts per million, averaged over an eight-hour working day, in 1971. Subsequent research suggested carcinogenic, genetic, and reproductive hazards. Scientific organizations recommended progressively lower limits. After litigation over delay, OSHA proposed a new rule in 1983 and held hearings producing about 1,600 transcript pages and more than 300 exhibits. The agency prepared an unpublished final rule in June 1984 providing a 1 ppm eight-hour permissible exposure limit, or PEL, and a 10 ppm short-term exposure limit, or STEL, averaged over fifteen minutes.

Under Executive Order 12,291, OSHA submitted the draft for OMB review. OMB challenged its predicted benefits, cost effectiveness, and scientific justification, particularly for the STEL. OSHA ultimately published the 1 ppm PEL, with monitoring, engineering controls, protective equipment, medical surveillance, warnings, and recordkeeping requirements. It reopened the record on the STEL and, in January 1985, declined to require one. Thus, the draft including a STEL was never itself a published, binding rule.

## Procedural posture and arguments

The court consolidated direct petitions for review of the exposure standard and the subsequent decision against a STEL. The Association of Ethylene Oxide Users challenged the evidentiary foundations for the PEL and associated warnings. Public Citizen challenged the refusal to require a STEL and argued that OSHA had unlawfully surrendered its decisional authority to OMB. Members of Congress supported the latter argument as amici. The Ethylene Oxide Industry Council supported OSHA's decision against a STEL.

## Questions presented

Were OSHA's findings about EtO's health hazards and the justification for its 1 ppm PEL supported by substantial evidence in the record as a whole? Did the agency adequately explain why that long-term limit made a feasible STEL unnecessary despite the significant risk remaining at 1 ppm? The court also faced, but did not decide, the legality of OMB's involvement.

## Governing law

The Occupational Safety and Health Act required a threshold finding of significant risk and a standard reasonably necessary and appropriate to remedy that risk. For toxic substances, § 6(b)(5) directed OSHA to select the standard that most adequately protected workers to the extent feasible, using the best available evidence. Section 6(f) required judicial review for substantial evidence on the record as a whole.

The court applied that substantial-evidence requirement to OSHA's hybrid rulemaking, which combines factual findings with legislative judgments and scientific projections. The agency had to identify relevant evidence, explain its reasoning and policies, disclose assumptions, and answer significant contrary evidence and arguments. A court did not choose its preferred scientific conclusion merely because reasonable scientists disagreed. *Industrial Union Department v. American Petroleum Institute*, the Benzene case, required an evidentiary foundation for significant risk, but allowed scientifically supported conservative assumptions and estimates at the frontiers of knowledge. Scientific certainty was not required. (Opinion, Parts II and III.)

## Analysis supporting the PEL

OSHA used epidemiological studies of exposed workers together with animal experiments. The worker studies had limitations: small samples, possible exposure to other chemicals, uncertainty about exposure levels, and differing cancer outcomes. OSHA acknowledged these weaknesses rather than treating any single study as definitive. The animal studies also prompted objections concerning infections, tumor types, and statistical methods. OSHA answered those criticisms with expert evidence and, where appropriate, reanalysis. Read together, the studies supported a reasonable inference of carcinogenicity. The court assessed that cumulative evidence rather than requiring one flawless, dispositive study.

Evidence of genetic changes and reproductive effects reinforced the hazard finding. The court distinguished established genetic effects from uncertain estimates of their health consequences. OSHA did not claim that genetic changes alone conclusively proved disease; it used those findings to support the broader evidence of carcinogenicity. Similarly, a Finnish study connecting exposure during pregnancy with spontaneous abortions had disputed methods and controls. OSHA considered the criticisms and relied on that study alongside corroborating evidence, including animal studies. Scientific disagreement, even disagreement by many commenters, did not automatically deprive the evidence of value. (Part III; notes 2–9.)

To estimate risk, OSHA extrapolated from the Bushy Run rat study using a linear, no-threshold model and an animal-to-human conversion. The association argued that Benzene prohibited the no-threshold assumption and that low-dose estimates were speculative. The court rejected that reading. Benzene had condemned an unsupported assertion of significant risk; here OSHA developed a dose-response analysis, evaluated competing views, and supported its assumptions with scientific evidence. The holding was specific to this record. Note 13 expressly reserved whether an unsupported no-threshold assumption would be valid.

The court also rejected the association's attack on the conversion method. OSHA relied on an expert study; the competing EPA method would actually produce higher risk estimates, contrary to the association's interpretation of the figures. At 50 ppm, OSHA estimated 634–1,093 excess deaths per 10,000 exposed workers; at 1 ppm it estimated 12–23. These were modeled estimates, not observed deaths in the litigated workplace population. OSHA selected 1 ppm because it could not demonstrate the feasibility of a lower long-term limit, rather than because that level eliminated all significant risk. Challenges to some estimates of actual exposure patterns did not change the result: even the challengers' lower projected benefits did not eliminate the significant-risk justification. The court therefore did not decide whether those disputed calculations were correct. (Risk-assessment discussion; notes 10–15.)

Note 16 separately upheld the warning-sign requirement. The Act contemplated hazard warnings, the signs were part of an appropriate response to the demonstrated risks, and the findings supported the wording about cancer and reproductive hazards. The court did not require a separate numerical significant-risk estimate for each ancillary protective measure.

## Why refusal to adopt a STEL failed

OSHA reasonably found that the record did not establish a dose-rate effect: exposure to a particular total dose over a short period had not been shown more harmful than receiving the same dose over a longer period. The court upheld that limited finding. New analyses in the supplemental proceeding identified weaknesses in the earlier dose-rate evidence. The agency was entitled to reassess those data despite the scientific staff's contrary recommendation. This did not amount to a judicial finding that short bursts were harmless. (STEL discussion; note 17.)

The problem was a different pathway to protection. Even without a special dose-rate effect, restricting short bursts could lower a worker's total dose. OSHA assumed that compliance with the eight-hour PEL would already deliver virtually all benefits of the STEL. That conclusion required evidence about actual exposure patterns. With low background exposure, a worker could experience relatively high brief exposures and still meet the eight-hour average. A STEL could reduce those bursts and thereby reduce cumulative exposure below the level that the PEL alone permitted. The record included comments and OSHA's own earlier analysis describing this possibility.

OSHA made no adequate findings establishing that workplace exposure patterns necessarily caused PEL compliance to subsume the proposed STEL. Its observation that background exposure must count toward the average did not fill that gap. Because significant risk remained under the 1 ppm PEL, and STEL feasibility was uncontested at this stage, the agency had to show why a STEL would not further reduce that risk or identify another equally protective avenue. It could not simply rest on the PEL's benefits. The court remanded for OSHA either to adopt a STEL or to explain, using empirical or expert evidence on exposure patterns, why one would not further control long-term average exposures. It did not itself promulgate the 10 ppm limit or categorically require it regardless of further evidence. (STEL discussion; notes 18–20.)

## Judgment and reserved question

The court affirmed the 1 ppm PEL and remanded the STEL issue for further proceedings. It also upheld the finding that the record did not establish dose-rate effects. No damages or individual enforcement liability were adjudicated. Because statutory review supplied the remand, the court declined to decide the constitutional questions concerning OMB's role. The case therefore cannot establish that White House review is inherently lawful or unlawful, or that OSHA actually surrendered its authority in a constitutionally impermissible manner.

## Exam use

Use this case to separate a permissible scientific assumption from an unsupported inference about regulatory design. A cumulative evidentiary record can justify protective regulation despite uncertainty and imperfect studies. The agency must nevertheless explain how its chosen mechanism addresses the remaining problem. Also distinguish the judicial opinion from the assigned chapter's framing and counsel recollection: the unresolved OMB issue is a teaching theme, not the holding. This brief describes the 1986 decision and does not certify the present EtO standard or current regulatory requirements.
