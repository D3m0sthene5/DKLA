# Cargill Incorporated v. Jorgenson Farms
719 N.W.2d 226 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006) | Contracts

## Decision and setting
The Minnesota Court of Appeals affirmed summary judgment for Jorgenson Farms because Cargill had not produced evidence from which a rational factfinder could find an agreement to sell the disputed 80,000 bushels of corn. It also affirmed denial of further discovery, refusal to compel arbitration, and a $5,000 attorney-fee sanction. Judge Dietzen wrote for a panel consisting of Presiding Judge Stoneburner, Chief Judge Toussaint, and Judge Dietzen. No separate opinion appears. This was an August 8, 2006 decision applying the statutes and procedural rules identified in the opinion. (228–229, 235.)

## Facts and competing accounts
Cargill supplied agricultural products and risk-management services; Jorgenson Farms raised corn and soybeans. Cargill asserted that the farm had agreed to deliver 80,000 bushels of corn in December 2003. The farm denied ever entering that transaction. Ben Presthus, its exclusive Cargill contact during the relevant period, had authority to make contracts. His usual practice was to record each agreement in his diary, notify accounting, and have Cargill send a confirmation for execution and return. He identified two July 17, 2003 contracts, for 100,000 and 135,000 bushels, that the farm performed. He denied negotiating the additional 80,000-bushel transaction, found no corresponding diary entry, and described the confirmation as a clerical error. (229.)

Presthus said he reported the problem to his supervisor, Brad Morrison, and that participants in a meeting agreed that contract number 27985 had never been made and should be deleted from Cargill’s system. He reiterated his denial at a January 2004 meeting. Co-owner James Jorgenson likewise denied an oral or written agreement for the 80,000 bushels. He acknowledged receiving an October 2003 information packet containing the document, but explained that harvest work delayed his attention to it. He said the participants in December and January meetings agreed there had been no contract, although Morrison later said his superiors would not permit deletion. These were affidavit accounts rather than trial findings resolving every disagreement about the meetings. (229–230.)

Cargill offered affidavits from manager Kurt Peterson and Morrison. Peterson said Jorgenson acknowledged receiving an October email concerning put options, including the disputed corn, without responding. Morrison disputed the account that he had agreed no contract existed or promised deletion; he also disputed that this was a routine clerical mistake. The notes accompanying Cargill’s affidavits identified meeting dates inconsistent with Presthus’s account. Cargill additionally relied on its July confirmation, an October put-option addendum, and internal communications among employees. None supplied evidence of mutual assent sufficient to survive summary judgment. (230, 233–234.)

## Procedure and discovery
Cargill requested National Grain and Feed Association arbitration in September 2004. The farm answered that it had made no contract containing an arbitration agreement. Cargill served a complaint on January 18, 2005 seeking arbitration or damages, delayed filing it, and eventually filed in July with a motion to compel arbitration. The farm sought summary judgment and sanctions after warning Cargill in March that the claim lacked a contractual basis. At the August 26 hearing Cargill orally requested additional discovery time. The district court denied that request, granted summary judgment, denied arbitration, and awarded $5,000 in sanctions. (230–231.)

A discovery continuance was reviewed for abuse of discretion. Although adequate discovery generally should precede summary judgment, Rule 56.06 required attention to diligence and a good-faith expectation of finding material evidence, rather than a fishing expedition. During approximately seven months after service Cargill had served no interrogatories, document requests, requests for admission, or deposition notices. Its explanation that it first wanted to resolve arbitration did not excuse that inactivity: whether arbitration could be compelled itself depended on whether a contract existed, and the farm had expressly disputed that premise. Denying further time was within the district court’s discretion. (231–232.)

## Formation and the limits of silence
The court reviewed the summary-judgment record favorably to Cargill, including reasonable circumstantial inferences. But unsupported conclusions, speculative future evidence, and mere doubt about an opponent’s witnesses did not create a material factual dispute. Although contract existence ordinarily presents a factual question, judgment was proper if a rational factfinder could not find for Cargill on the record as a whole. Minnesota’s UCC allowed agreement through language or conduct and recognized course of dealing, performance, and trade usage as relevant circumstances. Those flexible rules still required a bargain in fact. (232–233.)

The only witnesses the formation discussion identified as having direct knowledge of the alleged agreement—Presthus and Jorgenson—denied it. Cargill’s confirmation expressly directed the seller to sign and date the original and attached copy, return the original to the buyer, and retain the copy. Jorgenson neither signed nor returned it. Under those express terms the document had not formed a contract. Silence ordinarily does not constitute acceptance unless the parties’ relationship justifies expecting a reply or imposes a duty to respond. Cargill produced no course-of-dealing evidence making the farm’s silence assent. Nor did the October addendum receive written or oral acceptance. An email between Cargill employees listing put options could not demonstrate the farm’s agreement. (233.)

Cargill suggested Presthus might be related to the Jorgensons and motivated by employment with a competitor, Pro Pig. It supplied no evidence establishing the proposed family relationship, competitive relationship, business dealings, or effect on credibility. Speculation about bias therefore did not cure the absence of formation evidence. (233–234.)

## Statute of frauds, arbitration, and sanctions
The farm separately raised the statute of frauds. The court quoted Minnesota’s merchant-confirmation provision, section 336.2-201(2), under which a qualifying confirmation can satisfy the writing requirement against its recipient unless written objection is given within ten days. It expressly declined to decide that defense because no contract had been formed. Accordingly, this decision does not hold that the farm was a merchant, that the July or October packet satisfied the statutory confirmation requirements, or that silence under that provision independently created an agreement. The textbook uses the dispute to pose those confirmation questions; the actual judgment resolves the anterior formation issue. (234; assigned printed page 440.)

Review of the arbitration ruling was de novo and required a valid arbitration agreement and a dispute within its scope. Without the alleged corn contract, Cargill lacked the agreement necessary to compel arbitration. (234.)

The fee sanction was reviewed for abuse of discretion. Cargill contended that sanctions required an express bad-faith finding. The court distinguished the older, repealed statute from section 549.211, subdivision 2(1), which required reasonable investigation and barred presenting a pleading for an improper purpose. An express bad-faith finding was unnecessary under that provision. Cargill had continued the claim without conducting discovery or adequately addressing Presthus’s known denial despite advance notice of the dispute. The $5,000 award therefore remained within the district court’s discretion. (234–235.)

## Exact result and source scope
The court affirmed the judgment and attorney-fee award; its decision also sustained denial of the continuance and motion to compel arbitration. Cargill recovered no contract damages in this proceeding.
