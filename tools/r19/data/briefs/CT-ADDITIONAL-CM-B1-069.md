# Bonebrake v. Cox

499 F.2d 951 (8th Cir. July 2, 1974) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B1-069

The Eighth Circuit reversed a $27,000 judgment for the seller's estate and remanded. The district court, following a special master, had wrongly excluded one equipment-and-installation contract from Article 2, imposed rejection requirements on buyers who had accepted defective goods, and treated the buyers' effort to finish the work as their breach rather than a response to the estate's anticipatory repudiation. Judge Talbot Smith, sitting by designation from the Eastern District of Michigan, wrote for the panel with Heaney and Stephenson; the complete report contains no separate opinion. Iowa law governed by an unchallenged ruling. (499 F.2d at 951–953 & nn.1–3; 962.)

## Two contracts to reopen a burned bowling alley

Donald and Claude Cox had operated Tamarack Bowl in Missouri Valley, Iowa, for about twenty years when a February 5, 1968 fire destroyed its equipment. They decided to rebuild. On April 17 they agreed to pay Woodrow Simek $20,000 for used lane beds, ball returns, chairs and seating, a ball-cleaning machine, lockers, balls, racks, rental shoes, radarays, and foundation materials, delivered and installed. Simek would resurface the lanes and apply finish coats. His promises included freedom from defects in workmanship and materials and compliance with American Bowling Congress specifications. Payments were tied to execution, the start of foundation work, and completion of installation. (Id. at 953–954, 957–958.)

Simek ran a bowling alley and dealt in bowling equipment as a sole proprietor in Nebraska. He hired Ilert Avery, known as Blackie, to install most items. Lane work could not begin until August because the new building was unfinished. On August 23 the buyers separately agreed to pay $35,000 for ten used Brunswick Model A pinspotters delivered and installed, with a one-year guarantee for motors and gearboxes. Neither contract fixed a completion date. Nevertheless, the business depended on leagues normally beginning in late August or early September and playing four rounds over thirty-six weeks; delay could cost an entire round. Local league members volunteered to help get the alley open. (Id. at 953–954.)

By September 4 the pinspotters were placed at the lanes, but the master found them inoperative, in very poor condition, and not installed within the contractual meaning. Most machines needed attention to frozen bearings and replacement parts. The buyers and volunteers dismantled and repaired them with Blackie's advice. Blackie knew the buyers were dissatisfied, but Simek died on September 6 before they could speak directly with him. The court's narrative identifies $20,000 paid on the pinspotter contract and $7,000 on the April contract. Several April items remained undelivered, and the delivered ball returns and radarays had not been installed. (Id. at 954.)

The buyers tried to find the missing equipment through Simek's warehouses, suppliers, Blackie, and his sister, Frances Bonebrake. They found little usable equipment and no one able to organize completion. After those efforts failed, they ordered missing items themselves. Around September 12 an altercation led Blackie to leave, and specialists were hired to finish the repairs and installation. Their lawyer's September 17 letter explained the incomplete installation, repairs, anticipated lost income, and records being kept to substantiate a loss claim. The master found no reason to believe the letter had not been mailed, although Bonebrake denied receiving it. The alley opened October 14, allowing only three league rounds. (Id. at 954–955 & nn.4–5; 960–962.)

## The price judgment and the buyers' counterclaims

Bonebrake, as administratrix of Simek's estate, sought the unpaid contract price. The buyers counterclaimed for their losses and asserted that Simek or the estate had breached first. The special master rejected both positions and recommended the $28,000 unpaid balance less $1,000 representing the seller's value of unaccepted goods. The district court entered $27,000 for the estate. The master acknowledged the harshness of requiring payment for seriously defective pinspotters but believed the Code required that result. (Id. at 952–953 & n.1.)

The appellate court examined three errors. The first concerned notice and cure after acceptance. The second was the exclusion of the April contract as an indivisible mixture of goods and services. The third was the implied rejection of anticipatory repudiation: although the master made no direct ruling on that claim, his conclusion that the buyers breached by taking over the work necessarily rejected it. The court applied the Code's liberal, commercially practical construction and found clear error in the implied repudiation ruling after considering the entire record. (Id. at 953, 955–956, 960.)

## Acceptance did not extinguish the warranty claim

The master found that installing the pinspotters required putting them in operating condition and amounted to a warranty of fitness. He also found that the machines did not meet that standard. He nevertheless reasoned that the buyers failed to give adequate defect notice and wrongfully prevented the estate from curing within the performance period, thereby waiving warranty recovery. That combined two distinct remedial settings. (Id. at 956.)

Iowa § 554.2607(3)(a), corresponding to UCC § 2-607(3)(a), governs accepted goods: the buyer must notify the seller of breach within a reasonable time after discovery or lose remedies. Section 554.2605(1), corresponding to § 2-605(1), instead governs rejection and the effect of failing to specify a discoverable defect when a seller could have cured it, or in the stated merchant-to-merchant setting. The accepted-goods notice need not enumerate every defect, assert damages, or threaten litigation. It must inform the seller that the transaction is troublesome and claimed to involve a breach, opening the way to negotiation. The master had effectively demanded the more exacting rejection notice. (Id. at 956–957 & nn.7–8.)

The September 17 letter met the proper standard. It explained that the machines needed repair, the contractual guarantee was not satisfied, installation was incomplete, and the buyers were recording expenditures to substantiate losses. The letter supplied more than enough warning that the transaction remained troublesome. The court therefore rejected the estate's argument that the master's standard had never actually required particularized defect notice. (Id. at 957.)

The cure ruling likewise imported rejection rules into an acceptance case. Section 554.2508, corresponding to § 2-508 and reproduced fully in note 9, gave a seller opportunities to cure a rejected nonconforming tender. Here the buyers had accepted the goods and sought damages. The court held that they were not obliged to accept the seller's later cure proposal as a condition of preserving any recovery. Their refusal could raise mitigation questions affecting damages, but did not eliminate the warranty claim altogether. This distinction preserves acceptance as a choice compatible with damages; it does not guarantee recovery of every asserted expense. (Id. at 957 & nn.8–10.)

## The April contract's predominant purpose was a sale

The master excluded the April contract because it involved substantial labor, goods, a lump price, and no divisibility. Article 2, however, applies to transactions in goods; § 554.2105(1) defines goods through movability at identification to the contract. The purchased equipment was tangible, portable commercial property at that time. Installation services did not create an unstated exception to the definition. The contract's own language described equipment, workmanship and materials, and an overall purchase price. (Id. at 957–958 & n.11.)

Nor did eventual attachment to the alley make this a house-construction contract. Note 12 identifies the proper time for testing movability and independently explains that the lane beds arrived as three prefabricated sections, were joined and screwed to a wooden foundation built on the concrete floor, and were themselves used equipment previously installed elsewhere. Their former installation had not made them immovable. The estate's analogy to constructing a house therefore failed on both the governing definition and the actual equipment. (Id. at 958 n.12.)

The court surveyed decisions involving electrified flooring, poured concrete with labor, and an automobile dealership, where services furthered a transaction principally directed at goods. It contrasted a proposal expressly for data-processing services, where charges were for analysis and reporting rather than transferred goods. It declined to develop the parties' blood-transfusion and cosmetics analogies because of their different factual and policy settings. These comparisons supported examining the transaction's purpose rather than treating every mixed, indivisible arrangement as categorically outside Article 2. (Id. at 959–960 & nn.13–14.)

The resulting predominant-factor test asks whether the transaction, reasonably described, primarily supplies services with incidental goods, or primarily supplies goods with incidental labor. The court illustrated the first with an artist's painting contract and the second with installation of a bathroom water heater, citing the corresponding examples in notes 15–16. This contract replaced bowling equipment destroyed by fire. Its labor component did not alter that central purpose, so Article 2 governed the April agreement. (Id. at 960 & nn.15–16.)

## The buyers could act on the estate's anticipatory breach

The court did not hold that every seller's death automatically repudiates every contract. Its reasoning depended on this sole proprietor's operation, serious existing defects, missing equipment, urgent league schedule, failed searches, and the absence of anyone able to continue performance. The buyers' initial contacts showed efforts to carry out the agreement, not an immediate abandonment of it. Their later independent purchases followed the failure of those efforts. Whether Bonebrake saw them once or twice was immaterial because she could supply no productive course of action. (Id. at 960–961.)

Under § 554.2610 and the quoted § 2-610 comment, anticipatory repudiation did not require literal impossibility or proof of malevolence. Conduct reasonably indicating rejection of the continuing obligation sufficed. Taken together, the conditions facing these buyers supplied that indication while the performance period remained open. Their protective action was therefore justified rather than a prior breach by them. The court specifically attributed the repudiation to the circumstances rather than ill will. (Id. at 961 & nn.17–18.)

The administrators who arrived September 17 had no relevant business experience and did not know what had to be done. The court found no need to explore their later situation in detail: even if their completion offer were treated as retraction under § 554.2611, it came after the buyers materially changed position and was too late. The exact judgment was reversal and remand for proceedings consistent with these holdings. The court did not itself calculate the buyers' recovery or enter a substitute monetary award. (Id. at 961–962 & nn.19–20.)
