# Mims v. Arrow Financial Services, LLC

**565 U.S. 368 (2012) · Supreme Court of the United States · January 18, 2012**  
**Civil Procedure — federal-question jurisdiction and a statute permitting suit in state court**

Private claims under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act could be heard in federal court under §1331 even though the Act expressly permitted private suit in state court. That permissive provision did not eliminate general federal-question jurisdiction. Justice Ginsburg wrote for a unanimous Court. CivPro assigns the account in PDF page 199, printed page 353, Note 4, including the Court’s footnote 9 about federal issues embedded in state claims.

## The alleged calls and the jurisdictional dismissal

Marcus Mims alleged that Arrow, a debt-collection agency, repeatedly called his cellular telephone using an automatic dialing system or prerecorded or artificial voice without his consent. He sued in federal district court in Florida, asserting a willful or knowing violation of the TCPA and seeking declaratory relief, an injunction, and damages. His allegations concerned approximately twelve calls, with requested treble damages of $1,500 for each; that demand exceeded Florida’s then-$5,000 small-claims ceiling. The Supreme Court did not find that the calls occurred as alleged or decide liability. **375, 385 n.14.**

The district court dismissed for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, following Eleventh Circuit precedent that treated the TCPA’s private-action provision as making state courts the exclusive forum. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted review to resolve a circuit split: most circuits addressing the question had rejected federal-question jurisdiction, while the Sixth and Seventh Circuits had recognized it. **375–376.**

## The statutory design supplied a federal claim

Congress enacted the TCPA in response to intrusive telephone practices and concern that interstate operators could evade state restrictions. The Act imposed federal substantive restrictions and assigned the FCC a regulatory role. Its provisions addressed specified automated and prerecorded calls, unsolicited fax advertisements, and certain automatic dialing of business lines, subject to statutory exceptions. The case did not require a decision about every covered technology or exemption. **371–374 & nn.1–3.**

The Act furnished complementary enforcement mechanisms. State attorneys general could bring enforcement actions, over which federal district courts had expressly exclusive jurisdiction. The FCC had additional enforcement powers. Private persons could seek injunctions, actual loss or statutory damages, and potentially an increased award for willful or knowing violations. The private provision said a person could bring an action in an appropriate state court if state law or court rules otherwise permitted it. **374–375 & nn.4–5.**

The parties agreed that Mims’s claim arose under federal law. Federal law created the claim and supplied its substantive rules. Section 1331’s general grant therefore applied unless the TCPA displaced it expressly or by fair implication. The Court stressed that Congress had removed the federal-question amount-in-controversy requirement in 1980: small federal claims did not cease to arise under federal law merely because their value was modest. **376–379 & nn.6–9.**

## Permission to use one forum did not exclude another

Arrow argued that a later, more specific statute directed at state courts superseded the earlier general jurisdictional statute. Ginsburg rejected an inference of displacement merely from the TCPA’s state-oriented language. Giving one court jurisdiction did not, by itself, make that jurisdiction exclusive. The private-action clause did not say that suit could be brought only or exclusively in state court. Congress used express exclusivity language for state-official enforcement actions in the same Act, showing a distinction it did not use for private actions. **378–381.**

Arrow also argued that mentioning state courts served no purpose if ordinary concurrent jurisdiction already existed. The Court identified possible purposes that did not require federal exclusion. Congress could have wished to avoid an inference that the expressly exclusive federal forum for state enforcement also governed private litigation. It also arguably afforded States flexibility through the “if otherwise permitted” qualification. The Court described that flexibility as a possible explanation, not a comprehensive adjudication of every permissible state refusal to hear a TCPA claim. **381–382 & nn.11–12.**

The Act’s purpose likewise did not support exclusive state adjudication. Congress had not merely made out-of-state callers subject to receiving States’ laws. It enacted a uniform federal regulatory scheme, and liability depended on federal statutory or regulatory violations. Allowing federal-question jurisdiction advanced that federal interest without depending on state acceptance of claims or the happenstance of diverse citizenship. **383–384 & n.13.**

## Legislative expectations and practical objections

Arrow invoked Senator Hollings’s hope that consumers could conveniently proceed in state small-claims courts. The Court found no statement that federal jurisdiction should be excluded. A single legislator’s views were not controlling, and even a shared expectation about the likely forum would not determine §1331’s legal scope. Mims’s own demand, moreover, would not have fit within Florida’s small-claims limit. **384–385 & n.14.**

Arrow predicted a flood of small federal claims and removals that would pressure consumers to abandon inexpensive state litigation. The Court found those concerns unpersuasive. It pointed to the then-$350 federal filing fee and the experience after the Seventh Circuit’s jurisdictional decision: identified removals were class actions, and nearly all originally filed federal cases in that sample were too. Those were historical observations, not current filing-fee or caseload findings. Footnote 15 also emphasized that Congress expressly identifies federal claims it wishes to make nonremovable. **385–386 & n.15.**

## Judgment and doctrinal limits

The Court **reversed the Eleventh Circuit and remanded**, **387**. Federal and state courts had concurrent jurisdiction over private TCPA actions; the separate statutory treatment of state-official enforcement remained distinct. The judgment removed the jurisdictional dismissal without deciding whether Arrow violated the Act or what relief Mims should receive.

The assignment quotes footnote 9 recognizing that a state-created claim may arise under federal law when resolving significant federal issues is required. That was contextual explanation, not the ground on which Mims’s federally created action prevailed. Footnote 8 also preserved the rare *Shoshone Mining* exception to the usual federal-cause-of-action rule. **377–379 nn.8–9.**
