State Farm Fire & Casualty Co. v. Tashire
Citation
386 U.S. 523, 87 S. Ct. 1199, 18 L. Ed. 2d 270 (Supreme Court of the United States 1967). Argued February
14–15, 1967; decided April 10, 1967. Opinion by Justice Fortas; Justice Douglas dissented in part.
Casebook pp. 778–782; source PDF pp. 405–407. Full judicial opinion.
Parties
State Farm Fire & Casualty Company insured Ellis Clark, the pickup-truck driver, and initiated the federal
interpleader action. The defendants included Clark, the truck’s apparent owner Kenneth Glasgow,
Greyhound Lines, bus driver Theron Nauta, and persons asserting or potentially asserting accident claims.
Several claimants opposed interpleader; Greyhound eventually sought the protection of the injunction for
itself and its driver. 386 U.S. 525–528; CB 778–779.
Material Facts
A Greyhound bus and a pickup truck collided in California in September 1964. Two bus passengers died,
thirty-three others were injured, and the drivers and truck passenger also suffered injuries. Those affected
included Canadians and citizens of five states. Four injured passengers filed California state-court suits
seeking over $1 million from the bus company, drivers, and apparent truck owner. The individual alleged
tortfeasors were Oregon citizens; Greyhound was a California corporation. 386 U.S. 525; CB 778.
State Farm, an Illinois corporation, asserted that its policy covered Clark up to $10,000 per injured person
and $20,000 per occurrence. It deposited $20,000 in Oregon federal court and sought to require all claimants
to proceed there against Clark and the insurer. It also sought discharge from policy obligations, including
defense duties. Alternatively, it asserted a coverage exclusion and requested return of the deposit. The Court
was addressing the procedural use of that fund, not deciding fault or policy coverage. 386 U.S. 526–527; CB
778–779.
Procedural History
The district court issued an injunction and ultimately broadened it to require prosecution of claims against
State Farm, Clark, Greyhound, and Nauta within the interpleader action. On interlocutory appeal, the Ninth
Circuit ordered dissolution and dismissal. It reasoned that, absent a state direct-action right, tort victims did
not qualify as claimants against the insurer until they obtained judgments against the insured. The Supreme
Court granted certiorari. 386 U.S. 527–529; CB 779.
Issue
Does statutory interpleader permit an insurer to deposit a limited policy fund before tort claimants have
obtained judgments, when some adverse claimants share citizenship? If interpleader is available, may its
injunction compel all underlying accident litigation against the insured and other alleged tortfeasors into that
federal proceeding?

Rule and Holding
Section 1335 requires minimal diversity among adverse claimants: at least two adverse claimants must have
different citizenship, even though other rival claimants may be co-citizens. Article III permits that
jurisdictional arrangement. The statute also permits interpleader before the tort claims have been reduced to
judgment because its coverage includes persons who may claim benefits under the policy. 386 U.S.
530–533; CB 779–780.
The authorized injunction must remain tied to protection of the interpleaded fund. State Farm could protect
allocation of its $20,000 from competing enforcement efforts, but it could not force all tort suits against
Clark or other alleged tortfeasors into the interpleader action. Section 2361 did not authorize the district
court’s broader injunction. 386 U.S. 533–537; CB 780–782.
Judgment
The Court reversed the Ninth Circuit and remanded to the district court. The action could proceed under §
1335, but the injunction had to be substantially narrowed in accordance with the opinion. The decision did
not approve the district court’s original consolidation of all tort litigation or adjudicate the victims’ ultimate
entitlement to insurance proceeds. 386 U.S. 537–538; CB 782.
Reasoning
Jurisdiction: the Court raised the diversity issue itself. Statutory text, the purpose of providing one forum for
competing fund claims, and consistent interpretation supported minimal diversity. Strawbridge’s
complete-diversity rule construed a congressional statute, rather than setting the constitutional outer limit.
Thus shared citizenship among some rival claimants did not defeat statutory interpleader here. 386 U.S.
530–531; CB 779–780.
Unliquidated claims: the 1948 revision restored language extending the statute to persons who may claim
policy benefits. Requiring completed judgments would allow a first successful claimant to consume all or an
excessive share of a limited fund before others established their claims. Early interpleader addressed that
risk for both the insurer and competing claimants, even though the claimants’ substantive entitlement
remained contingent. 386 U.S. 531–533; CB 780.
Scope of relief: in a dispute where rival claims concern only a single fund, protecting that fund may
encompass the whole controversy. This collision produced broader personal-injury claims against several
defendants, many of which could not be satisfied from State Farm’s policy. The mere existence of the
insurance fund supplied no statutory basis for controlling every claimant’s choice of forum for those
separate claims. 386 U.S. 533–535; CB 780–781.
The insurer’s legitimate interest was protected by requiring enforcement against it of judgments against
Clark to occur through the interpleader proceeding. Enjoining adjudication of the underlying tort claims
went further than necessary. Congress had not designed interpleader as a general device for collecting all
mass-tort litigation in whichever forum a comparatively small stakeholder selected. 386 U.S. 535–537; CB
781–782.

and the reach or exclusivity of § 2361’s service provisions. It also declines to decide the Ninth Circuit’s
interpretation of Rule 22: State Farm properly invoked statutory interpleader, and Rule 22 presented
different procedural limits. Those unresolved questions should not be treated as holdings on nationwide or

Separate Opinions
Justice Douglas dissented in part. He agreed about minimal diversity and the need to limit any injunction,
but concluded that these victims were not yet claimants to the insurer’s fund. The policy and applicable state
law required a judgment or agreed settlement before an action against the insurer. He regarded the majority’s
reading of the restored statutory wording as unsupported and would have affirmed dismissal. The casebook
summarizes rather than reproduces his full dissent. 386 U.S. 538–541 (Douglas, J., dissenting in part); CB
782.
Notes
Separate three questions: jurisdiction over statutory interpleader, eligibility of contingent claimants, and
permissible injunctive relief. Success on the first two does not establish authority to centralize all related
litigation. Also distinguish the § 1335 holding from Rule 22 interpleader; the opinion expressly leaves the
latter interpretive question open. 386 U.S. 528 n.3, 530–537.
