# Sedmak v. Charlie's Chevrolet, Inc.
622 S.W.2d 694 (Mo. Ct. App. 1981) | Contracts | An oral car purchase and specific performance

## A collector's order became an invitation to bid

Dr. Joseph and Linda Sedmak were automobile enthusiasts who already owned six Corvettes. Chevrolet announced a limited edition commemorating the Corvette's selection as the Indianapolis 500 Pace Car. Approximately 6,000 would be produced. In November 1977, the Sedmaks asked Charlie's Chevrolet's sales manager, Tom Kells, about obtaining one. He said he would investigate and that, if the dealership received a car, they could purchase it. 622 S.W.2d at 695–696.

On January 9, 1978, Dr. Sedmak telephoned about placing an order. Kells required a deposit, and Mrs. Sedmak delivered a $500 check. Before paying, she asked whether they would definitely own the car; according to her testimony, Kells said yes. She then requested an L82 engine, a four-speed standard transmission, and an AM/FM radio with a tape deck. Kells arranged the changes, and the car ultimately arrived with those options. The agreed pricing method, as the trial court later found, was the manufacturer's suggested retail price at delivery, estimated at approximately $15,000. The precise amount remained uncertain because Kells did not know the costs of the options and appearance package. He promised to mail a retail order form, but none was sent. Id. at 696.

On January 25, the Sedmaks visited the dealership to collect another Corvette and asked about this car. Kells promised to notify them when it arrived and requested permission to retain it for showroom promotion until after the race. They agreed. On April 3, however, he told them that rising demand meant they could no longer purchase at the suggested retail price; instead, they could bid. They declined to bid and sued for specific performance. Id.

## The disputed conversations and the appellate posture

Kells gave a different account. He denied discussing a purchase price and characterized the requested options as advice from a knowledgeable Corvette enthusiast rather than a customer's order. His explanation of the $500 also shifted: first he said it secured the first opportunity to purchase, then the first opportunity to bid, and finally the last opportunity to submit a bid after acknowledging that other bidders had not paid for that privilege. The parties nonetheless agreed that the delivered car had the options the Sedmaks wanted. Id. at 696–697 & n.1.

The trial judge credited the Sedmaks, found an oral purchase contract excepted from the statute of frauds, and ordered Charlie's to make the car available for delivery. The dealership challenged the contract's evidentiary basis, its enforceability without a writing, and the propriety of specific performance. Under *Murphy v. Carron*, the appellate court would sustain a court-tried judgment unless unsupported by substantial evidence, against the weight of the evidence, or based on an erroneous declaration or application of law. Witness credibility belonged to the trial judge. Judge Satz's opinion rejected all three challenges. Id. at 695–697.

## A pricing formula could be sufficiently definite

The dealership argued that the Sedmaks' testimony was contradictory. The appellate court found the supposed conflicts reconcilable or not actually contradictory and accepted the trial judge's credibility determination. It also addressed the dealership's less clearly articulated claim that the parties had never agreed on a definite price. Id. at 697.

The relevant question was whether they agreed on an ascertainable price, not whether that figure accurately tracked market demand. The manufacturer's suggested retail price supplied a determinable figure at performance. A contractual price need not be expressed in dollars and cents at formation when the parties choose a method that will establish it. The later increase in market value therefore did not undo the earlier bargain. Id.

## Why partial payment validated the single-car agreement

The trial judge had relied on either transaction memoranda or the accepted deposit to overcome the statute of frauds. The appellate court relied only on partial payment and expressly did not decide whether the memoranda independently sufficed. Id. at 697–698.

Historically, partial payment could validate an entire oral goods contract. The UCC narrowed that effect to goods for which payment had been made and accepted, addressing the risk that a small payment for one item could be used to fabricate a much larger quantity agreement. For divisible goods, the payment consequently validates only the quantity to which it can fairly be apportioned. The complete opinion's second footnote reproduces the statutory language and the comment describing just apportionment. Id. at 698 & n.2.

Charlie's urged a literal approach under which partial payment could never validate a sale of one indivisible object unless the whole price had been paid. The court rejected that reading. The difficulty the UCC sought to address was an uncertain quantity. Here the dispute concerned whether there was a sale or merely an opportunity to bid, not how many cars the supposed agreement covered. An accepted deposit for one indivisible car retained its evidentiary force even though it could not purchase an apportioned fragment. Id. at 698–699.

The court compared that evidence with the Code's liberal requirements for a written memorandum: evidence of a goods contract, identification of the party charged, and a quantity. The deposit evidenced a purchase, identified Charlie's as recipient, and concerned one car. Denying enforcement because the deposit could not be apportioned would permit repudiation of a promise actually made rather than prevent fabrication. The holding was expressly limited to partial payment for a single indivisible commercial unit with no quantity dispute. Id. at 699.

## Replacement difficulty justified delivery of this car

Missouri's UCC permitted specific performance when goods were unique or in other proper circumstances. The car was not an heirloom or necessarily one of a kind. That did not defeat relief. Missouri had long used a practical inquiry into the feasibility of replacement; its pre-Code automobile-shortage decision, *Boeving v. Vandover*, exemplified circumstances in which obtaining a substitute would entail expense, trouble, and loss that could not be estimated in advance. Id. at 699–700.

The trial court found that a car with the same mileage, condition, ownership, and appearance could not readily be obtained on the open market. Each dealership received only one Pace Car, and only some cars had the particular requested options. Charlie's had received nothing comparable in the preceding two years. The sticker price was $14,284.21, but buyers in Hawaii and Florida offered $24,000 and $28,000. The location and size of those offers supported the inference of scarce supply and substantial demand. Those were outside offers, not the contractual price or a damages award. Id. at 700.

The court thus treated practical irreplaceability as an “other proper circumstance,” even without traditional uniqueness. It also described equitable discretion as narrow when the relevant principles were satisfied and the agreement was fair and plain. The holding did not make every limited-production automobile sale automatically enforceable by delivery; it rested on this record's replacement difficulty and inadequacy of money relief. Id. at 699–700.
