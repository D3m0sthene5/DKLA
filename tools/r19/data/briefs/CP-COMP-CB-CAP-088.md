# Rio Properties, Inc. v. Rio International Interlink

**284 F.3d 1007 (9th Cir. 2002) — March 20, 2002. Civil Procedure.** Circuit Judge Trott wrote for a panel comprising Judges Goodwin, Sneed, and Trott. No separate opinion is reported. Assigned account: CivPro, physical page 158, printed pages 267–268, Note 4. The complete appeal concerned alternative service, specific jurisdiction, discovery sanctions, and fees; the assigned account focuses on the first subject.

## Parties, controversy, and attempts at service

Rio Properties operated the Rio casino resort in Las Vegas, including a sports book, and owned registered RIO trademarks. It used a website to promote the hotel and accept reservations. Rio International Interlink, a Costa Rican Internet sports-gambling business, took wagers online and through a toll-free number. It advertised in publications including the Nevada edition of the Daily Racing Form and ran Las Vegas radio advertisements. After the casino sent a cease-and-desist demand concerning riosports.com, the foreign company disabled that site but opened an equivalent operation at a different domain. The casino sued for statutory and common-law trademark infringement and sought an injunction against continued use of the name. [Preserved full judicial reproduction, lines 65–69.]

The casino initially tried to locate the foreign company in the United States. The Florida address used in the domain registration belonged to IEC, its international courier, which lacked authorization to accept service. IEC nevertheless forwarded the complaint to the company’s Costa Rican courier. A Los Angeles attorney, John Carpenter, then called about the suit, said his client’s copy was partly illegible, and requested another. He declined to accept service voluntarily but asked to be informed when service was completed. An investigator could find no usable Costa Rican street address. The investigation instead showed that the company preferred its email address for communications and received mail and customer payments through IEC. [Lines 70–73.]

The casino sought an emergency alternative-service order. The district court authorized service under Rules 4(h)(2) and 4(f)(3) through three channels: mail to Carpenter, mail to IEC, and the company’s email address. After service by those methods, the company appeared and moved to dismiss for insufficient service and lack of personal jurisdiction. The court denied the motion on written submissions, and the company answered, asserting twenty-two affirmative defenses. Thus the eventual default was not simply the result of ignoring an initial email. [Lines 74–75.]

## Discovery and the appeal

The defendant later provided nearly useless discovery responses, commonly answering “N/A,” despite two informal extensions. After further unsuccessful efforts, the casino obtained an order compelling responses. The district court warned that monetary sanctions would be inadequate and that continued noncompliance would lead to preclusive sanctions. The defendant failed to comply, then supplied only partial, belated responses. The court entered default as a discovery sanction and ordered attorney’s fees of $88,761.50 and costs of $7,859.52. The defendant appealed service, jurisdiction, default, and the monetary awards. [Lines 76–79.]

## Alternative-service holding

The court reviewed the service decision for abuse of discretion. Rule 4(h)(2) made Rule 4(f)’s methods available to foreign business entities subject to the specified limitation concerning personal delivery. Rule 4(f)(3) authorized other methods directed by the court and not prohibited by an international agreement. In addition, due process required a method reasonably calculated to inform the defendant of the suit and allow objections. A judicial order did not dispense with either the international-agreement restriction or constitutional adequacy. [Lines 84–87, 107; notes 1–4.]

The defendant proposed a hierarchy: plaintiffs had to exhaust Rule 4(f)(2) procedures, including diplomatic channels or letters rogatory, before seeking court-directed service under subsection (3). The panel rejected that construction. The separately numbered alternatives, connected by “or,” gave no priority to the earlier subsections. Neither the text nor the relevant advisory notes imposed exhaustion. The notes contemplated court-directed special methods in urgent cases even when other efforts remained uncompleted or unattempted. The panel expressly disapproved Graval’s contrary treatment because it relied on commentary addressed to subsection (2), rather than subsection (3). [Lines 88–98; note 5.]

Consequently, court-directed service was neither an extraordinary remedy nor a last resort. But the plaintiff still needed to establish circumstances justifying the district court’s intervention. Here unsuccessful domestic inquiries, the undiscoverable Costa Rican address, and the defendant’s evasiveness supplied that justification. The court did not announce an entitlement to any requested alternative method merely because another subsection had no formal priority. [Lines 98, 102–105.]

The parties agreed that the Hague Convention did not apply because Costa Rica was not a signatory at the time. That historical proposition must not be carried forward as an unverified statement about current treaty membership. The opinion’s note 4 expressly recognizes that a Rule 4(f)(3) order cannot contravene an applicable international agreement. Its discussion of a method potentially inconsistent with foreign domestic law distinguishes such law from a prohibitory international agreement. [Lines 86–87; note 4.]

## Why each ordered channel met due process

IEC was a practical conduit: the defendant listed its address in the domain registration, directed customers to send payments there, and had received an earlier copy of the pleading through it. The court’s order made this channel permissible despite IEC’s earlier lack of authority to accept ordinary service voluntarily. Carpenter had been consulted specifically about this litigation, knew the defendant’s legal position, and was communicating with it. Those case-specific circumstances made delivery to him a reasonable notice method despite his earlier refusal to volunteer as a service agent. [Lines 109–111.]

Email was especially appropriate because the defendant operated an Internet business, designated email as its preferred contact, and had no readily discoverable physical business address. It was the only ordered channel directly and immediately aimed at the defendant rather than an intermediary. The court distinguished a cited case involving unilateral email service without judicial authorization. Here a Rule 4(f)(3) order authorized the method after the district court examined the circumstances. [Lines 112–123.]

The panel nevertheless recognized problems with confirming receipt, electronic signatures, compatible systems, and reliable transmission of exhibits or attachments. It left trial judges to balance those limitations against benefits in particular cases. Note 6 distinguishes the then-recent Rule 5 provisions for consented electronic service from initial process under Rule 4; note 7 reproduces the statutory electronic-signature definition. Note 8 adds that this defendant did not assert that notice was absent, incomplete, delayed, or prejudicial. That fact supported the court’s practical assessment but was not presented as a substitute for the rule’s authorization requirements. [Lines 124–126; notes 6–8.]

## Specific jurisdiction was a separate question

Valid service did not by itself establish Nevada contacts. The court reviewed jurisdiction de novo, applying Nevada’s then-coextensive long-arm statute and a three-part specific-jurisdiction test. Because the district court decided the motion on written materials, the casino needed a prima facie showing: uncontroverted allegations were accepted and factual conflicts were resolved in its favor. General jurisdiction was not at issue. [Lines 128–138.]

The panel found more than a website accessible everywhere. Nevada-directed radio and print advertisements, including the attached advertisement from the Nevada racing publication, supported intentional targeting and foreseeable local injury to the casino. It distinguished passive-site cases lacking additional forum-directed conduct. Applying its historical effects and but-for analyses, it connected the alleged trademark injury to the Nevada marketing and competition. Note 9 preserves an evidentiary limit: if litigation had proceeded, the casino would have had to prove jurisdictional facts, including the actual extent of Nevada business contacts. The prima facie ruling did not conclusively prove every advertising allegation at a trial. [Lines 139–149; note 9.]

The court also balanced seven reasonableness considerations, including purposeful interjection, defense burden, sovereignty, Nevada’s interest, efficient resolution, plaintiff convenience, and another available forum. The defendant identified no relevant sovereignty conflict or alternative forum. Nevada’s relation to the casino and gambling dispute, the available documents, and the directed marketing outweighed the foreign defendant’s burden. Jurisdiction was therefore reasonable on this record. This reports the panel’s 2002 analysis, without certifying that its formulations remain unchanged by later decisions. [Lines 150–155.]

## Default, fees, and exact judgment

The default rested on historical Rule 37(b)(2)(C), reproduced in note 10, which permitted a judgment against a party disobeying discovery orders. The panel assessed expeditious resolution, docket management, prejudice, disposition on the merits, and less drastic sanctions. Merits resolution favored the defendant, but the remaining considerations supported default, especially repeated noncompliance, explicit warnings, and the district court’s consideration of ineffective lesser sanctions. It found no abuse of discretion. [Lines 157–164; note 10.]

The court also affirmed fees and costs under the trademark statute’s historical exceptional-case standard. Default established the complaint’s allegations of knowing, malicious, oppressive conduct; those allegations supported the willfulness basis for fees. It rejected objections to nonattorney staff work, billing rates, hours, duplication, and travel items under abuse-of-discretion review. [Lines 167–171.]

The Ninth Circuit **affirmed the district court in all respects**, including service, jurisdiction, discovery default, and the specified fee and cost awards. The assigned passage accurately identifies the independence of Rule 4(f)(3) and the failed conventional efforts. The complete opinion adds the international-agreement restriction, separate constitutional analysis, prima facie jurisdiction limit, and discovery-based source of default.
