# Buchanan v. Warley

**245 U.S. 60 (1917) — November 5, 1917 — Civil Procedure**

Justice Day delivered the Supreme Court’s opinion invalidating Louisville’s racial residential zoning ordinance. The constitutional injury was concrete: the ordinance prevented a white seller from completing a sale to a Black purchaser who intended to live on the property. The report contains no separate writing or numerical voting tally. The Court reversed the Kentucky Court of Appeals and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion; it did not itself enter a decree of specific performance. (69, 82.)

## The sale, the ordinance, and the litigation

Buchanan agreed to sell Warley a Louisville lot at 37th Street and Pflanz Avenue. Warley’s written offer stated that he intended to build a home there and would not have to accept a deed or pay unless Kentucky and Louisville law permitted him to occupy the property as his residence. When they contracted, eight of the block’s ten residences were occupied by white people, and two, the nearest to the lot, by Black people. Buchanan sued in the Chancery Branch of the Jefferson Circuit Court for specific performance. Warley invoked the contractual condition and Louisville’s ordinance, which prevented him from residing there. (69–70.)

The ordinance, approved May 11, 1914, prohibited a Black person from moving into a residence or assembly place on a block with more white-occupied than Black-occupied properties. A parallel provision prohibited a white person from moving into a predominantly Black block. Existing occupancy and previously acquired occupancy rights were protected. Servants and employees could live in their employers’ residences, and existing racial uses could continue subject to restrictions on subsequent changes. Violation was an offense. Thus the ordinance regulated residential occupancy rather than expressly prohibiting the execution of a deed, but its practical effect controlled this sale. (70–72.)

Buchanan answered that the ordinance violated the Fourteenth Amendment and supplied no lawful defense to enforcement. The Kentucky trial and appellate courts treated the ordinance as constitutional and a complete defense. The Supreme Court reviewed the Kentucky Court of Appeals judgment, reported at 165 Kentucky 559, on writ of error. (70, 72.)

## Whose constitutional rights were at stake?

An initial objection sought dismissal because Buchanan was white, while the restriction supposedly implicated only Black residents’ rights. The Court accepted the general principle that a litigant must show injury to his own rights rather than rely merely on another person’s constitutional grievance. Buchanan met that requirement: the state courts had denied enforcement solely because the ordinance prevented his purchaser from occupying the lot. His own ability to dispose of property was necessarily impaired. Without the ordinance, the courts would have enforced the contract and required the willing purchaser to pay and take the conveyance. (72–73.)

This was therefore not a free-standing constitutional inquiry brought by an unaffected observer. The property transaction placed Buchanan’s own interest directly before the Court. Warley’s legal entitlement to acquire and occupy property remained essential to the merits, because the ordinance made his race the barrier to the transaction. (73–75, 78–79.)

## Police power and constitutional property rights

The ordinance’s defenders invoked public peace, prevention of racial conflict, racial separation, and protection of white-owned property values. They also argued that symmetric restrictions, prospective operation, and protection of existing uses made it a permissible exercise of police power. Their reliance on segregation precedents appeared in the reported arguments and was addressed in the opinion. (64–68, 73–74, 79–82.)

The Court recognized broad state power to regulate health, safety, and welfare. It expressly rejected the idea that ownership conferred an absolute right to every use: lawful regulation could restrict harmful activities and confine particular businesses to suitable locations. But police power remained subject to the Federal Constitution. Property included the rights to acquire, use, and dispose of a thing, and the Fourteenth Amendment protected those attributes against unconstitutional state interference. (74–75.)

The decisive question was whether occupancy, and necessarily purchase and sale, could be inhibited solely because of the intended occupant’s color. Although the law was phrased as an occupancy rule, a buyer forbidden to live there could not ordinarily make the contemplated purchase for residential use. Racial classification therefore burdened the white owner’s disposition and the Black purchaser’s acquisition of property directly. (73, 75.)

## The constitutional and statutory reasoning

Day examined the Reconstruction Amendments and their protection of citizenship, civil equality, and property. The Fourteenth Amendment’s language extended protection to all persons, even though protecting formerly enslaved people was a central historical purpose. The Court also invoked federal civil-rights enactments: the 1866 law, reproduced as Revised Statutes § 1978, secured equal rights to inherit, purchase, lease, sell, hold, and convey property; the 1870 enactment, § 1977, secured equal contractual and procedural rights and equal protection for persons and property. These constitutional and statutory provisions qualified a Black citizen to acquire property without a restriction imposed solely because of race. (75–79.)

The holding is expressed through these civil property rights and due process. It should not be rewritten as an application of a later strict-scrutiny formula. Nor did the opinion treat all regulation of land transactions as constitutionally forbidden. Its target was this ordinance’s racial bar to acquisition, occupancy, and disposition. (74–79, 82.)

## Why the asserted justifications failed

The Court distinguished rather than overruled *Plessy v. Ferguson*. It described the railway precedent as providing separate but equal accommodations without denying transportation altogether. *Berea College* had rested on Kentucky’s reserved authority over a corporate charter, not the property question presented here. The opinion also acknowledged historical school-segregation decisions. These distinctions show the limited contemporary reach of its reasoning; Buchanan did not announce the invalidity of every form of segregation. (79–81.)

Nevertheless, racial hostility could not justify sacrificing constitutional rights to preserve public peace. The asserted goal of racial purity did not fit a case about sale and residence rather than racial amalgamation, especially when the ordinance allowed Black servants in white homes and did not prohibit nearby Black residences outside the defined blocks. Protecting white property values was likewise insufficient: undesirable white neighbors or lawful disagreeable uses could produce similar effects. The ordinance’s racial restriction was not a legitimate exercise of police power and could not stand. (80–82.)
