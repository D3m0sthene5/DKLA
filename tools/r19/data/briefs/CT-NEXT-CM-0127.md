# Jacob & Youngs, Inc. v. Kent
230 N.Y. 239, 129 N.E. 889 (1921) | Contracts

## Residence, specified pipe, and an expensive correction
Jacob & Youngs built Kent’s country residence at a cost exceeding $77,000. Construction ceased in June 1914, and Kent occupied the house. A balance of $3,483.46 remained unpaid. The plumbing specifications required well-galvanized, lap-welded standard wrought-iron pipe of Reading manufacture. Kent complained in March 1915 after learning that some installed pipe came from other manufacturers. The architect directed replacement. Most plumbing was already enclosed in the walls, so complete compliance required substantial demolition of the finished structure, not simply exchanging loose goods. The builder declined the replacement demand, sought the final-payment certificate, and sued when it was refused. 230 N.Y. at 240–41.

The majority regarded the evidence as supporting innocent oversight by the plumbing subcontractor rather than fraud or willful substitution. The manufacturers’ names were stamped at six- or seven-foot intervals, and even the architect had missed the discrepancy. The builder offered to show that the other brands matched Reading pipe in quality, appearance, market value, and cost. The trial court excluded that evidence and directed a verdict for Kent. The Appellate Division reversed for a new trial; Kent appealed that order. Id. at 239–41.

## Question and the majority’s distinction between duty and condition
The dispute was not whether the builder had complied literally: it had not. The questions were whether the deviation necessarily defeated any right to the unpaid price and whether evidence of equivalence was legally relevant to substantial performance and the resulting allowance. Cardozo held that a trivial, innocent omission may be compensated in damages without producing forfeiture of the price. A party’s full contractual duty remains; the separate question is whether every minor departure breaches a condition of counterperformance. Id. at 241–42.

Some promises are clearly independent, some are necessarily dependent, and others can condition payment when the departure is substantive while remaining collateral when it is insignificant. The classification turns on presumed intention and justice in the particular transaction. Construction produces improvements inseparable from the land, making forfeiture especially severe compared with a returnable chattel. The law is reluctant to infer that parties meant an innocent minor default to produce a vastly disproportionate loss. Id. at 242–43.

## Degree, intention, and the limits of substantial performance
The rule is contextual, not permission for builders to install anything they consider equally good. A dominant or pervasive change frustrating the contract’s purpose cannot qualify. Art and personal preference can give substitution a different significance from mere utility. The court weighs the contract’s purpose, the desire to be satisfied, the explanation for deviation, and the severity of exact adherence. Doubtful factual inferences belong to the factfinder; certain inferences may be resolved by judges. Id. at 243.

Parties may use apt and certain language making every term a condition of recovery, although such an express allocation was not the question here. The majority also withheld its leniency from a willful transgressor. Its decision depended on a deviation viewed as both unintentional and trivial, and on compensation for the wrong rather than treating the promise as fulfilled perfectly. Id. at 243–44. The excluded equivalence evidence could support insignificance in relation to the whole project, making its exclusion and the directed verdict erroneous.

## The monetary allowance
Replacement cost is ordinarily the measure allowing the owner to obtain promised completion. But where that cost is grossly and unfairly disproportionate to the benefit obtained, diminution in value supplies the allowance. For this plumbing system, tearing apart the house would cost greatly while the value difference could be nominal or absent if equivalence was established. Although some exposed pipe might have been replaceable cheaply, Kent demanded replacement of the plumbing as a whole from cellar to roof. Id. at 244–45.

The builder had not reached the stage of proving the precise allowance: exclusion of the evidence supporting substantial performance foreclosed that progression. The court did not invent a numerical damage award from a completed valuation trial. It held that an offer addressing diminution in value would not necessarily have been defective and explained why the remedy should avoid the same disproportionate result the doctrine was designed to prevent. Id.

## McLaughlin’s dissent
McLaughlin, joined by Pound and Andrews, viewed the departure as intentional or grossly negligent to the equivalent effect. The initial delivery of approximately 1,000 feet was checked against the Reading specification. Later orders requested quantities without identifying the manufacturer, and later installations went unchecked. Of approximately 900 feet exposed and inspected after completion, only seventy feet bore the required manufacturer’s origin. In a system totaling roughly 2,000–2,500 feet, the dissent concluded that no more than 1,000 feet had been shown compliant and that the builder could have proved more compliance from its procurement records but did not. Id. at 245–47.

The dissent accepted the possibility of recovery for good-faith performance with minor inadvertent omissions, but denied that this record fit that category. Kent had a right to choose a manufacturer even for personal preference, and equivalence was no substitute for the bargain. The builder also failed to prove the cost of compliance where replacement was feasible. Relying on the owner’s right to specified materials and the policy against judicially replacing contracts, the dissent would reverse the Appellate Division and reinstate the trial judgment for Kent. Id. at 246–48. This differs from the majority both in characterizing the misconduct and in the weight assigned to literal specification and proportional remedies.

## Exact judgment and vote
The Court of Appeals affirmed the Appellate Division’s order and directed judgment absolute for the builder on the stipulation, with costs in all courts. The ultimate disposition therefore was not merely another trial: the appeal’s stipulation produced judgment absolute after affirmance. Cardozo’s opinion had four votes, joined by Hiscock, Hogan, and Crane; McLaughlin’s dissent had three, joined by Pound and Andrews. Id. at 245, 248.

## Source scope and historical limits
The distinct March 8, 1921 reargument order, reported at 230 N.Y. 656–57, was also read. The per curiam court rejected the contention that it had overlooked the specification requiring replacement of defective work. For trivial innocent defects, the replacement covenant, like the installation covenant, was treated as independent and collateral rather than a condition; the obligation was preserved while the remedy was restricted to damages. Reargument was denied. Hiscock, Cardozo, Pound, McLaughlin, Crane, and Andrews are listed as concurring in that order; its participation is distinct from the January vote. This is supplementary context, not a claim that the assigned principal is a second decision.
