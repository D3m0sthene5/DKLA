# United States v. Copar Pumice Co., Inc.

**714 F.3d 1197 (10th Cir. 2013) — May 6, 2013 — Civil Procedure**

Chief Judge Briscoe wrote for a panel with Judges Brorby and Murphy. No separate opinion is reported. The panel dismissed an interlocutory appeal from discovery orders, leaving the underlying mining litigation unresolved. Pinpoints below refer to the numbered pages of the complete court-issued opinion, rather than an inferred mapping to Federal Reporter pages.

## Facts and procedural history

Richard and Shirley Cook acquired twenty-three mining claims in New Mexico’s Santa Fe National Forest and leased them to Copar, a company owned by their three daughters. Federal mining law distinguished valuable, locatable mineral deposits from common-variety pumice. The Jemez National Recreation Area Act of 1993 prevented issuance of patents for claims in the area, and federal administrative proceedings eventually recognized claims nine through twelve as valid because their larger pumice could serve the laundry industry’s stonewashing market. Those claims comprised the El Cajete Mine. A 1997 operating plan permitted certain common-variety material to be used for reclamation. (Slip op. 3–5.)

The Cooks separately brought a takings action. Under a 2002 settlement they retained the four unpatented claims, agreed not to dispose of common-variety minerals, and received approximately $4 million from the United States. Copar crushed larger pumice for uses beyond the laundry industry. In 2003 the Forest Service alleged noncompliance and demanded sales information; ensuing administrative and judicial review upheld the Forest Service’s position in 2010. The United States filed this civil action in 2009 for trespass, conversion, and unjust enrichment, alleging unlawful removal of undersized pumice contrary to federal requirements and the settlement. These allegations were the litigation’s background, not merits findings newly made in this appeal. (Slip op. 5–7.)

The defendants asserted that their removal of pumice was lawful and had invoked good faith based on legal advice. In 2011 the government sought interrogatory answers and documents concerning that advice. Defendants asserted attorney-client privilege and work-product protection. The magistrate judge concluded that reliance on legal advice had waived attorney-client privilege and compelled discovery. When defendants did not comply, the government subpoenaed their current law firm and two former firms. Defendants moved to quash on scope and burden grounds, requested a protective order limiting disclosure to advice about restrictions on pumice’s end use, and withdrew their advice-of-counsel good-faith defense. The magistrate judge nevertheless denied relief, finding an at-issue waiver under federal doctrine and, alternatively, New Mexico law. (Slip op. 7–9.)

The district court overruled defendants’ Rule 72(a) objections but clarified important limits: advice concerning air-quality proceedings, environmental permit challenges, and civil-rights matters was excluded. The government had also disclaimed work-product documents and documents generated after pumice sales ceased in January 2010. Footnote 2 describes an administrative finding that falling stonewash-pumice prices had rendered the claims invalid as of a 2010 hearing; that later administrative conclusion did not decide appellate jurisdiction. One former firm produced documents that remained sealed in the district court pending this appeal. Defendants sought immediate review of the discovery rulings. (Slip op. 9–10 & n.2.)

## Issues and governing rules

Could the defendants immediately appeal the orders under the collateral-order doctrine, the Perlman exception for certain orders directed to third-party custodians, or pragmatic finality? Alternatively, would treating their notice of appeal as a mandamus petition justify relief?

Section 1291 ordinarily permits appeals from final decisions. Under Cohen, an otherwise interlocutory order must conclusively determine the disputed question, resolve an important question separate from the merits, and be effectively unreviewable after final judgment. Copar focused on the third requirement. Appealability is assessed categorically for the class of orders, rather than by how damaging disclosure might be to a particular litigant. Mohawk Industries had held that attorney-client privilege disclosure orders do not satisfy that requirement. (Slip op. 10–13.)

## Reasoning and holdings

**Collateral-order review remained unavailable despite third-party possession.** Defendants argued that Mohawk involved an order directed to a litigant, whereas their former counsel possessed the documents. A privilege holder cannot necessarily secure immediate review by having a third party incur contempt. But Mohawk identified several possible review routes: discretionary certification under § 1292(b), mandamus, and certain contempt proceedings. The possible absence of the contempt route did not eliminate certification or mandamus. More fundamentally, review after final judgment ordinarily sufficiently protects the privilege even if those other routes are unavailable. Footnote 3 distinguishes immediately appealable criminal contempt from civil contempt entered against a party during pending litigation. (Slip op. 12–14 & nn.3–4.)

The court accepted that disclosure cannot always be perfectly undone. That concern had already been considered in Mohawk and did not make the entire class of privilege-disclosure orders immediately appealable. A later court can vacate an adverse judgment and require a new trial excluding improperly disclosed material and its fruits. Defendants’ disagreement about whether federal or state privilege law governed concerned the correctness of the discovery ruling; it did not transform the order into an appealable collateral order. Copar therefore did not affirm the district court’s waiver analysis on its merits. (Slip op. 14–16.)

**Perlman did not provide an independent appeal here.** Perlman permits immediate review in some circumstances when a third-party custodian cannot realistically be expected to risk contempt to protect another person’s privilege. The Tenth Circuit had applied that principle narrowly in criminal grand-jury proceedings and had declined to extend it to civil discovery where the privilege holder could obtain later review. Decisions elsewhere permitting civil appeals before Mohawk did not establish jurisdiction contrary to controlling circuit precedent. The Ninth Circuit’s Krane case involved a nonlitigant privilege holder without the same postjudgment review opportunity; Copar’s defendants were parties to the civil action. (Slip op. 16–21.)

The court gave two related reasons: Mohawk’s explanation of adequate postjudgment relief undercut defendants’ position, and existing Tenth Circuit precedent independently foreclosed Perlman where other review remained available. Footnote 5 expressly reserved Mohawk’s effect on Perlman in criminal grand-jury proceedings, acknowledging differing circuit approaches. Copar consequently neither abolished Perlman universally nor decided the reserved criminal-context question. (Slip op. 20–21 & n.5.)

**Pragmatic finality also failed.** The circuit questioned whether this more subjective approach survived, particularly after Mohawk. Even assuming its continued existence, it was reserved for truly unusual situations in which waiting would create injustice outweighing piecemeal review and ordinary final-judgment review would be inadequate. Defendants did not explain why the unresolved federal-versus-state privilege question warranted immediate appellate intervention. Their principal interest was preventing disclosure of allegedly privileged information—the same interest Mohawk had found insufficient to justify an immediate appeal. The court rejected application here without definitively abolishing the doctrine. (Slip op. 21–23.)

**Mandamus would not change the result.** Defendants alternatively asked the court to construe their notice as a petition under § 1651(a). Mandamus requires no adequate alternative remedy, a clear and indisputable entitlement, and appropriateness of the writ. In privilege discovery disputes, the circuit additionally required that disclosure make meaningful appellate review impossible and raise questions substantially important to the administration of justice. Defendants established neither. Later review could remedy a discovery error, and the unsettled choice-of-law question alone did not establish substantial administrative importance, a usurpation of power, or gross abuse of discretion. Footnote 6 assumed procedural compliance with Rule 21 only for argument’s sake: even if the appeal were construed as a compliant petition, the court would deny the writ. (Slip op. 23–25 & n.6.)

## Judgment and significance

The exact mandate was dismissal of the interlocutory appeal for lack of jurisdiction. The court did not adjudicate mining liability, finally resolve privilege waiver or its governing law, or formally grant mandamus treatment. Its conditional mandamus discussion explained why that alternative route would fail. The case illustrates that directing discovery to counsel does not itself make a civil litigant’s privilege claim immediately appealable. (Slip op. 25.)
