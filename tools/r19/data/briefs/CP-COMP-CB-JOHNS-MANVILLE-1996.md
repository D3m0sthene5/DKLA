# In re Joint Eastern and Southern District Asbestos Litigation — Johns-Manville trust restructuring

**78 F.3d 764 (2d Cir. 1996) · February 21, 1996 · Civil Procedure**

Judge Kearse wrote for a unanimous panel with Judges Winter and Cabranes. The court vacated and remanded the unresolved Maryland setoff issue, dismissed an insurer’s appeal for lack of standing, and otherwise affirmed the trust-restructuring settlement. The assigned account, styled In re Johns-Manville Corp., is CivPro physical PDF 446, printed 866, Note 2. The actual appeal concerns restructuring an already existing trust, not initial confirmation of Manville’s reorganization.

## Why the trust needed another restructuring

The Manville Personal Injury Settlement Trust was created in 1988 under a bankruptcy-approved reorganization plan. It assumed health claims and other asbestos obligations, including contribution and indemnity claims by manufacturers and distributors. The bankruptcy court enjoined further litigation against Manville itself to protect the company’s equity, most of which had been contributed to the trust. Expected lifetime claims had initially been approximately 83,000–100,000; by December 1994, approximately 240,000 had been filed. The number and value of claims helped render the trust insolvent, prompting restructuring litigation in 1990. This concerns trust insolvency rather than a finding that every component of the reorganized company lacked value. (769.)

An earlier restructuring settlement had been vacated in Findley II. On remand, six subclasses were designated: present claimants, future claimants, claimants with pre-November 19, 1990 settlements or judgments, codefendant manufacturers, MacArthur distributors, and other Manville distributors. A trial began but stopped when the parties settled nearly all issues. Judge Weinstein, sitting in both federal districts, and Chief Bankruptcy Judge Lifland approved the revised settlement after fairness proceedings, culminating in the January 19, 1995 judgment now appealed. The distinct interests of injured claimants and entities seeking contribution explain why subclass representation mattered. (769–772.)

## The settlement’s payment and setoff mechanics

The Trust Distribution Process assigned values across seven disease categories and initially paid ten percent of a claim’s liquidated value. Assets and anticipated liabilities would be reevaluated at least every three years. Lower future percentages would not claw back past payments; an increase would require equalizing earlier recipients who had received less. Claimants could obtain individual valuation, arbitration, and eventually a tort suit against the trust, but payments remained constrained by the distribution process. The original injunction protecting Manville remained effective. A nominal claim value therefore did not guarantee immediate payment of that value. (770.)

Setoff rules determined how much an asbestos plaintiff’s recovery against another manufacturer would be reduced because of the trust’s responsibility. A pro tanto credit uses the amount paid or agreed to be paid; a pro rata credit uses an equal tortfeasor share; an apportionment system uses a factfinder’s allocation. The process generally referred to local law, but also modified particular contribution rights. In pro tanto and pro rata jurisdictions, defendants could elect the specified credit or relinquish it, pay the judgment, and retain a contribution claim. In apportionment jurisdictions, a plaintiff could forgo a trust claim and seek full compensation from codefendants, subject to their retained contribution rights after payment. (770–771.)

A separate $10 million MacArthur fund exchanged those distributors’ releases of claims against Manville and the trust for reimbursement of specified litigation expenses. It could fund insurance-coverage litigation and certain defense expenses, the latter only after available coverage was exhausted. If the subclass recovered at least $20 million from insurers, its rights to the fund would revert to the trust. USF&G, which denied covering MacArthur’s asbestos claims, objected that this provided a litigation fund to pursue it. (771–772.)

## Maryland: uncertainty did not justify abstention

Maryland claims were expressly excluded from the general setoff provisions pending a trial-court determination of the appropriate rule. The injured claimants favored pro tanto treatment: their small actual trust payment should not reduce recovery from others by an entire tortfeasor share. Codefendant manufacturers and distributors favored pro rata treatment, arguing that plaintiffs rather than codefendants should absorb the trust’s shortfall. A Maryland release could produce different credits depending on its terms and the settling party’s tortfeasor status. Which release arrangement applied could therefore determine who bore a substantial unpaid portion. (771–774.)

The trial courts acknowledged their power to resolve the question but deferred it to Maryland courts out of prudence. Announcing merely that Maryland law applied did not answer the parties’ actual question: which Maryland rule governed in this settlement. The Second Circuit treated the refusal as abstention and held it improper. Review was rigorous despite the abuse-of-discretion label; legal premises received plenary review, and discretion had to remain within the particular abstention doctrine’s limits. (774–775.)

Only Burford abstention was potentially relevant. That doctrine can protect important state regulatory or administrative arrangements from disruptive federal intervention. No comparable system or distinctive public policy had been identified here. Maryland’s ordinary interests in compensating injured persons and distributing tortfeasor liability did not transform difficult contribution law into a reason to decline jurisdiction. The federal courts had to predict how Maryland’s highest court would resolve necessary uncertain issues, especially where federal bankruptcy law also supported jurisdiction. Difficulty alone did not excuse decision. (775–776.)

The panel remanded without ultimately selecting the Maryland rule. It nevertheless rejected the distributors’ claim that Maryland clearly required a pro rata release whenever a settling tortfeasor could not pay its full share. The statute ordinarily reduced the claim by the consideration paid unless the release expressly provided a greater reduction. That statutory point constrained the argument but did not settle who would bear the shortfall in this trust arrangement. The final answer belonged to the trial courts on remand. (776–777.)

## District of Columbia claimants: no required opt-out

Approximately 150 District of Columbia claimants sought exclusion because a trust settlement could yield only ten percent while reducing recovery against another defendant by the trust’s entire pro rata share. They invoked Shutts and disputed adequate representation. The panel adhered to Findley II’s distinction: this was an equitable trust restructuring, rather than simply an action for money judgments against a defendant lacking jurisdictional contacts with absent plaintiffs. In rem and quasi in rem jurisdiction independently existed over beneficiaries of the New York bankruptcy-created trust. Shutts therefore did not automatically require opt-out rights in this setting. (777–778.)

That conclusion depended on fair and adequate Rule 23 representation. Counsel had to be qualified, and class interests could not be antagonistic. The record showed vigorous representation by experienced mass-tort lawyers, no demonstrated neglect, and no distinction between the objectors and many similarly situated claimants who accepted the settlement. Evidence that the trust’s liability share was likely twice as high in apportionment jurisdictions supported treating those jurisdictions differently; different terms did not themselves establish inadequate representation. The trial courts retained discretion to permit an opt-out, but found no special circumstances and reasonably concluded that exclusions would upset the settlement’s balance. Denial was not an abuse of discretion. (778–779.)

This is the panel’s 1996 resolution of this trust restructuring. It is not a statement that every mandatory mass-tort settlement satisfies Rule 23 or that later Supreme Court developments have been assessed.

## USF&G: intervention did not establish appellate standing

USF&G alleged that the MacArthur fund misallocated assets, financed litigation against it, and failed to represent its possible indemnification interests. To appeal, an intervenor still needed a concrete, actual or imminent injury traceable to the challenged action and redressable by the requested relief. A general concern about the settlement was insufficient. (779.)

The insurer had not paid MacArthur’s claims, so no present subrogation right had arisen. Having denied coverage, it also could not prevent its insured from settling with the trust and releasing claims against it. That permissible release extinguished the rights through which a later-paying insurer otherwise might seek reimbursement. Thus, neither a present claim nor a cognizable future indemnification interest gave USF&G standing here. The panel distinguished authorities involving insureds who had not executed such releases. It dismissed the appeal, rather than deciding the insurance-coverage dispute or simply affirming a merits rejection of it. (779–780.)

## Disposition and source scope

The Maryland deferral was vacated and remanded for resolution; USF&G’s appeal was dismissed for lack of standing; the district-court judgment was otherwise affirmed. The settlement’s denial of the D.C. opt-out request survived. (780.)

The textbook uses the case to illustrate bankruptcy-funded asbestos trusts, equity ownership, and an ongoing payment source. That broad account is distinguished from the actual 1996 holdings and from any promise of complete future compensation. This opinion documents the trust’s initial insolvency and the revised ten-percent distribution process; it does not independently establish every financing detail in the textbook or adjudicate the initial 1988 confirmation anew.
