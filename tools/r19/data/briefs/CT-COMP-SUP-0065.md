# Ng Giap Hon v. Westcomb Securities Pte Ltd and Others
[2009] SGCA 19 (Singapore Court of Appeal, Apr. 29, 2009) | Contracts

## Facts and commercial setting

Ng was a remisier, a self-employed securities trader affiliated with a stockbroking firm. Although remisiers cultivated customers and regarded them as their own clients, the legal agreement made Ng the agent of Westcomb Securities, rather than its employee. Westcomb Securities belonged to a group that also included its listed parent, Westcomb Financial Group, and a corporate-finance company, Westcomb Capital. The two individual defendants were senior group officers; Tan Kah Koon was also a director of Westcomb Securities. This structure matters because Ng sued the brokerage on the agency agreement and alleged that the other four defendants joined a conspiracy to deprive him of commissions. Original judgment ¶¶2–8.

The May 3, 2005 agency agreement paid Ng 40 percent of the commission that Westcomb Securities charged clients on transactions dealt by or through him during the first twelve months, and 50 percent thereafter. The percentage applied to the firm's commission, not to the shares' purchase price. Clause 12 expressly denied an employment relationship. Clause 18 said the document embodied the entire understanding and that there were no oral or written, express or implied obligations outside it, while preserving rights already accrued under earlier agreements. Those provisions did not promise Ng an exclusive channel to customers or require the firm to charge every customer commission. ¶8.

Ng alleged that he met Julian Sandt, CEO of the Australian company Orchid Capital, at a February 2006 networking event and generated interest in IPO placement shares for Sandt and connected companies. The defendants disputed the strength and originality of that connection: they said Sandt already had relationships with group officers and had discussed pre-IPO investments before dealing through Ng. The established account chronology was narrower than Ng's allegations. Sandt opened an individual trading account through Ng around March 6, and Orchid Emarb, an Orchid Capital subsidiary, opened a corporate account through him on March 15. On April 27, Sandt subscribed for 1.5 million Natural Cool placement shares at $0.20, totaling $300,000; Westcomb Securities charged no commission. That day Aktieninvestor, an Austrian investment company connected with Sandt, separately agreed to subscribe for 7.8 million shares in Westcomb's parent. Another broker arranged that transaction and received $85,000. ¶¶9–11.

Ng said he arranged a May meeting about further placements and sent Aktieninvestor account forms. His later emails revealed that Aktieninvestor's CEO had sent those forms to Tan at Tan's request, rather than returning them to Ng. The CEO could not recall the reason and suggested Ng might have been away. A group officer testified that Sandt and overseas secondary listings were too important or too large for Ng to handle. Whatever the reason for the redirection, Aktieninvestor opened its account on May 29 without going through Ng and received a firm dealer. In October Aktieninvestor subscribed for 1.5 million Swiber shares through the Australian broker BBY, while Sandt subscribed for 750,000 shares at $0.355, totaling $266,250. Neither transaction involved a commission charge. Orchid Emarb bought no placement shares; Ng received commission for its ordinary trades made through him. These distinctions defeat an account that treats every connected customer's purchase as Ng's completed commission transaction. ¶¶12–18, 93–96.

## Procedural history and competing positions

The High Court bifurcated liability and quantum and dismissed Ng's claim at the liability stage. It found no reliable evidence of the alleged customary one-percent commission on placement shares, no duty to charge commission when the firm allocated IPO shares as goodwill, and no proved coherent conspiracy. It rejected the requested implied good-faith obligation partly on the entire-agreement clause and also rejected the poorly developed quantum-meruit claim. ¶¶2–4, 19–23.

Ng appealed, claiming both a general obligation of good faith between principal and agent and a more specific obligation that Westcomb Securities would do nothing to deprive him of earning commission. His theory was that the firm had taken away his opportunity by dealing directly with customers he had cultivated. The Court of Appeal agreed with the findings against custom and conspiracy, and Ng did not pursue quantum meruit. The remaining questions were whether clause 18 barred implied terms at the threshold and, if it did not, whether either proposed term met the relevant implication test. ¶¶24, 41–43, 63–64.

## Rules and holdings

The three-judge court held that this entire-agreement clause did not bar implication of terms: its own reference to implied terms contemplated their existence. The court did not announce that every such clause permits implication. Clear and unambiguous language could exclude it, subject to applicable common-law controls and the Unfair Contract Terms Act if the clause substantively operated as an exclusion clause. A term also cannot be implied inconsistently with an express contractual term. ¶¶26–32.

The court distinguished terms implied in fact, which fill a necessary gap in a particular agreement by reference to the parties' presumed intention, from terms implied in law, which attach on broader policy grounds to a class of contracts. For implication in fact, business efficacy and the officious-bystander test are complementary: the hypothetical question whether both parties would unhesitatingly accept the term implements the inquiry into necessity. A desirable or fair addition is insufficient. A term implied in law has wider consequences because it becomes an incident of future contracts in that class. ¶¶34–40.

The court refused to imply Ng's broad good-faith term in law and refused the specific commission-protection term in fact. Andrew Phang delivered the judgment of Chao Hick Tin, Andrew Phang, and V. K. Rajah; no separate writing appears. The appeal was dismissed with costs and the usual consequential orders. ¶¶44–60, 95–98.

## Reasoning

The general good-faith claim failed because its theoretical content and operation were unsettled in the authorities examined in 2009. The court surveyed competing accounts of good faith, including honesty and commercial fair dealing, exclusion of forms of bad faith, and restrictions on recapturing opportunities surrendered in the bargain. It also considered disagreement over whether good faith should be an independent implied term or an element already present in interpretation and other doctrines. Conflicting Commonwealth decisions and academic accounts made a general obligation especially unsuitable for implication in law, with its consequences for an entire class of agreements. This was an argument about adopting the broad proposed duty in this case, not a conclusion that all dishonest conduct is lawful or that particular statutory good-faith duties do not exist. ¶¶44–60.

Rejecting that general duty did not dispose of the narrower commission claim. The court accepted that elements of good faith might appear in a fact-specific implied obligation, but the inquiry still had to satisfy necessity on the actual agreement and business setting. Its extended discussion of Luxor v. Cooper supplied guides rather than a mechanical rule for every commission contract. Courts must respect the parties' allocation of commercial risk, avoid rewriting agreements according to their own sense of fairness, and identify the precise event that earns commission. There can be exceptional protection where the agent has completed everything required, the principal then refuses to perform a binding transaction, or the principal wrongfully prevents performance of the agent's contractual part. Express exclusivity or other protective terms can also restrict the principal's freedom. ¶¶61–87.

Ng's position did not fall within those exceptional situations. Westcomb Securities retained discretion to allocate placement shares directly and to waive commission. Its general trading conditions expressly allowed commission waivers. It could pursue direct relationships with important customers; customers could likewise hold several accounts and use multiple dealers or remisiers. Sandt testified that he had not intended to obtain the disputed placement shares through Ng. Aktieninvestor's Swiber purchases passed through BBY, rather than through any Westcomb Securities trading account. Orchid Emarb's ordinary transactions through Ng were paid as the agreement contemplated. Thus, neither bringing a customer to the firm nor redirecting account forms established an exclusive right to every subsequent placement transaction. ¶¶88–96.

The requested promise would substantially surrender the brokerage's discretion in a setting where neither its contract nor the customers' intentions supported that surrender. It did not make the agreement commercially workable; the agreement already allocated commission by reference to transactions through Ng and commission actually charged. Nor would both parties necessarily have agreed to it if asked when contracting. The court therefore could not supply the restriction merely because the firm's competition with its own remisier seemed unattractive. It expressed sympathy, suggested a voluntary compromise might have been fairer, and explained that the court could not impose such a compromise as a legal remedy. ¶¶91–96.

The limits of the decision are essential. The court expressly reserved different facts, such as a deliberate and systematic campaign of sabotage against a remisier, that could require a protective term in fact and possibly in law. It also distinguished commercial reputation and other extralegal consequences from adjudicated liability. Its refusal here did not establish a universal privilege to sabotage agents or cancel vested commission rights. ¶¶97–99.

## Source reconciliation and historical scope

The assigned supplement, PDF65–75, renumbers and substantially abridges the judgment. Its editorial summary says the Austrian company opened an account in March and that its October purchase passed through the first respondent. The complete judgment instead places its account opening on May 29 and its Swiber purchase through BBY; the March accounts were Sandt's and Orchid Emarb's. This brief follows the complete judgment and uses its original paragraph numbers. All 99 paragraphs and 32 judicial record-reference notes were read. The assignment's later references to Wastech, Braganza, and Yam Seng are editorial developments, not holdings or authorities available to this 2009 panel. This brief explains the historical decision and does not certify its descriptions of foreign law as current law.
