# Barber v. Jacobs
58 Conn. App. 330, 753 A.2d 430 (2000) | Contracts

## Court, issue, and result

On June 20, 2000, the Connecticut Appellate Court affirmed a judgment requiring return of Thomas Barber's $327,000 home-purchase deposit. Judge Daly wrote, with Judges Schaller and Spear concurring. The mortgage contingency had not been fulfilled, and the buyer had nevertheless used reasonable efforts to obtain financing. His application to only one lender was adequate in light of the unresolved wetlands problem, the advice of experienced counsel and the family's need to move promptly. The court also upheld the finding of good faith and rejected the sellers' assertion that the trial judge had imposed a separate obligation to complete a wetlands application. (58 Conn. App. at 330–332, 335–339.)

## Purchase agreement and unsuccessful financing

Barber and his family planned to move from Toronto to the New York area before the school year. They first viewed Robert and Linda Jacobs's Greenwich property in April 1994. Barber's May offer requested a wetlands inspection and soil tests for a possible tennis court. He signed a June 17 contract for $3,275,000, with an August 8 closing; the sellers signed June 24. A mortgage contingency required a commitment for a first mortgage of no more than $1,300,000, a prompt application and diligent pursuit. The report gives the escrow deposit as $327,000. (Id. at 332–333.)

An application went to Putnam Trust on June 20. Barber disclosed substantial income and net worth, and the loan committee approved the loan on June 30, but set no interest rate and issued no formal commitment. Attorney Thomas Hartch represented both buyer and bank, a practice the trial court described as common locally. Reviewing plot plans and the wetlands agency file, he found discrepancies and unresolved violations dating to 1986. The agency had identified unauthorized clearing and ground disturbance; its final report indicated that the problem still needed resolution. (Id. at 333–334 & n. 3.)

Hartch told Barber on July 18 that he had to inform the bank, and did so the next day. The bank withdrew its approval and issued a written denial because the property did not satisfy agency standards. Hartch notified the sellers' attorney and escrow holder, Irwin Liu, and requested the deposit's return under the financing condition. The sellers refused. Meanwhile, Barber viewed other homes, including the Giffords' on July 17 and again July 21, offered $3 million on July 23, and obtained acceptance August 4 for an August 18 closing. That chronology supplied the setting for the sellers' argument that he was attempting to escape their agreement for another property. (Id. at 334.)

## Consolidated litigation and review

Barber's interpleader sought determination of rights to the deposit. The sellers separately sought damages beyond it for breach, breach of good faith, unfair trade practices and fraudulent misrepresentation. Their action against the bank was withdrawn before trial, so the bank was not an appellate party. After the consolidated bench trial, the judge ordered return of the deposit and treated the unfulfilled contingency as preventing the purchase agreement from coming into existence. The appeal concerned the reasonable-efforts finding, good faith and the claimed wetlands-application requirement. (Id. at 331–332 & nn. 1–2.)

Appellate review respected supported factual findings unless clearly erroneous; the panel could not retry credibility. It separately examined whether legal conclusions were correct and supported by the found facts. The affirmance therefore rested on the trial record, rather than establishing that every purchaser who receives one rejection has automatically satisfied a financing condition. (Id. at 334–335.)

## Reasonable efforts and futile applications

A mortgage contingency carried an implied promise to use reasonable efforts to secure a suitable loan. The sellers emphasized Barber's failure to apply elsewhere after Putnam's denial. The court analogized Luttinger, where experienced counsel knew that the one institution approached was the only available source of the needed financing; the law did not require futile applications. Here Hartch similarly drew on experience in judging that the wetlands obstacle would not be readily overcome. (Id. at 335–336.)

Barber had applied promptly and initially received a favorable response. The property's substantial noncompliance, rather than a lack of diligence, caused its withdrawal. Other lenders reasonably could be expected to hesitate as well. The family needed a home promptly for the children to start school, and Hartch correctly predicted that the problem could not quickly be resolved: the agency did not close its file until December 20, and then approved subject to six special conditions. The court accordingly upheld the reasonable-efforts finding. (Id. at 336–338.)

The sellers also asserted that they themselves offered a mortgage commitment. Assuming that assertion for argument, the court held Barber was not contractually required to accept it. Luttinger's buyers likewise could reject a proposed seller funding arrangement beyond the contemplated bank terms. Nor did the circumstances require Barber to postpone closing indefinitely so the sellers could correct their property. Those conclusions applied the parties' agreement and timetable, without imposing an obligation to accept any substitute financing. (Id. at 337–338.)

## Good faith and the alleged wetlands duty

The implied covenant protected reasonable contractual expectations, and bad faith required a dishonest purpose rather than mere negligence. The trial judge credited the family's genuine interest in the Jacobs home and the wife's compelling testimony about moving promptly, meeting neighborhood children and preparing for school. Their search for another home reflected that urgency and the unresolved property problem, rather than a dishonest effort to evade the agreement. The panel found sufficient support and declined to disturb the good-faith determination. (Id. at 338–339.)

Finally, the sellers misconstrued the judge's observation that timely correction and tender of a deed would have presented a different case. That was a hypothetical comment, identified as dicta, not an order requiring them to complete a wetlands application while excusing the buyer. It merely explained why resolution by the closing date could have removed the delay. The judgment was affirmed in full. (Id. at 339.)
