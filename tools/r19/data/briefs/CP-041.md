# Bates v. C & S Adjusters, Inc.

**Civil Procedure · CP-041 · 980 F.2d 865 (2d Cir. 1992).** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, PDF pp. 224–225, printed pp. 404–407. Judge Newman wrote for Judges Oakes, Newman, and Pierce; the reported decision contains no separate writing.

## A letter sent to one state and received in another

Phillip Bates incurred a debt while living in the Western District of Pennsylvania. His creditor, headquartered there, referred the debt to C & S Adjusters, a local collection agency conducting no regular business in New York. Bates had meanwhile moved to the Western District of New York. C & S mailed its demand to his former Pennsylvania address, and the Postal Service forwarded it to his new New York address. Those facts relevant to venue were undisputed. (¶ 2.)

After receiving the notice, Bates sued C & S in the Western District of New York under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, seeking statutory damages, costs, and attorney's fees. C & S asserted affirmative defenses and counterclaimed for costs on the theory that Bates had sued in bad faith to harass it. It later moved to dismiss for improper venue. District Judge Skretny granted the motion, and Bates appealed the May 21, 1992 dismissal. The appellate dispute concerned the permissible location of litigation, not whether the collection notice actually violated the Act or whether either side was entitled to its requested recovery. (¶¶ 1–3.)

## Issue and governing statute

Bates conceded that his plausible basis for venue was § 1391(b)(2), as amended in 1990, which allowed suit in a district where a substantial part of the events or omissions giving rise to the claim occurred. Was receipt of the forwarded collection demand a substantial event even though C & S had not deliberately addressed the demand to New York? The court answered yes. (¶¶ 5, 13–14.)

The distinction between statutory venue and personal jurisdiction was critical. A deliberate contact with the forum may matter to personal jurisdiction, but this venue provision directs attention to where claim-generating events happened. Moreover, C & S had waived its personal-jurisdiction defense by failing to raise it in its answer or dismissal motion. The court therefore did not decide whether involuntary forwarding by itself established constitutionally sufficient contacts. (¶ 13; n.1.)

## Why the 1990 revision mattered

The court first explained the statutory history. Before 1966, the ordinary federal-question venue rule could leave no single district available for defendants residing in different states. Congress addressed that gap by permitting venue where the claim arose. Courts then disagreed over whether a claim could arise in several districts and developed competing tests based on substantial contacts, their relative weight, injury, or performance. (¶¶ 6–8.)

Leroy v. Great Western United Corp. had emphasized protecting defendants from unfair litigation locations and generally discouraged recognizing several places where one claim arose. In that case, effects felt in Texas from Idaho officials' enforcement of Idaho law did not suffice for venue in Texas. Bates did not discard Leroy altogether. It identified which part of its approach the new statutory language displaced. (¶¶ 7, 11.)

The amended statute referred to substantial parts of the underlying events, with a legislative explanation specifically acknowledging events occurring in several districts. A court consequently did not have to pick the single best district from all plausible locations. Factors such as defendant convenience or the relative location of witnesses remained useful, but they were less decisive when their function was merely to rank otherwise permissible venues. The court characterized the amendment as at most a marginal expansion, not a license to sue wherever a plaintiff experiences some remote consequence. (¶¶ 9–11.)

## Applying the test to this collection notice

Prior decisions had permitted FDCPA actions where a collector intentionally sent a notice or made a telephone call to the debtor's district. The unusual feature here was the absence of an intentional New York destination. The court regarded that difference as inconsequential to the amended venue inquiry: the relevant question was the significance of receipt in New York, not the collector's purposeful contact with New York. (¶¶ 12–13.)

Congress enacted the FDCPA to address harms from abusive collection practices, including invasions of privacy and disruptions to consumers' lives. For a claim about this collection notice, the harm did not occur until the debtor received it. A notice lost in transit would be unlikely to produce the asserted violation. Forwarding was also an important part of the collection process: collectors mail demands to have them reach debtors, and a collector wishing to prevent forwarding can mark the envelope accordingly. Receipt was therefore a substantial event giving rise to the claim, rather than merely Bates's convenient place of residence. (¶ 14.)

The remaining Leroy considerations reinforced that conclusion. The notice itself was the most relevant evidence and was located in New York. Although a bona fide-error defense could matter, the alleged violations largely concerned the notice's contents rather than the agency's intent. Given the apparent absence of instructions forbidding forwarding and the waived personal-jurisdiction objection, trying the action in New York would not be unfair. The opinion did not find liability, reject every possible defense, or decide the counterclaim. (¶ 15.)

## Judgment and limits

**The court reversed the improper-venue dismissal and remanded for further proceedings.** Bates could pursue the action in the Western District of New York because receipt of the demand there satisfied § 1391(b)(2). He had not obtained an award of statutory damages. (¶ 16.)

Footnote 2 deliberately left another question open: when an FDCPA cause of action accrues for the limitations period. It identified decisions taking different approaches to mailing and receipt without resolving that disagreement. The opinion's venue reasoning must therefore not be converted into a holding that the limitations clock necessarily starts on receipt.
