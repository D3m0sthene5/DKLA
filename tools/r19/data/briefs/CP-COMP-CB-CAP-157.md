# Zahn v. International Paper Co.

**414 U.S. 291 (1973) · Supreme Court of the United States · December 17, 1973 · No. 72–888**

**Civil Procedure — individual jurisdictional amounts in diversity class actions and the historical limits of ancillary jurisdiction.** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, physical page 448, printed page 871, the Zahn account before the contrasting Allapattah discussion.

## Facts and litigation path

Owners of property fronting Lake Champlain in Orwell, Vermont, brought a proposed class action against International Paper, a New York corporation. They alleged that discharges from its New York pulp-and-paper plant flowed through Ticonderoga Creek into the lake, polluting the water and damaging the value and utility of nearby properties. The proposed class included the named owners and approximately 200 lakefront owners and lessees, as the majority described it. The Supreme Court did not decide whether the company had caused the alleged pollution or what damages particular owners were owed. (291–292.)

The owners invoked diversity jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a)(1). At that time, the statutory amount had to exceed $10,000, exclusive of interest and costs. Each named plaintiff satisfied that requirement. But the district court concluded to a legal certainty that not every individual owner had pollution damages exceeding $10,000. It also found that defining a class composed only of owners with qualifying claims was not feasible. Reading Snyder v. Harris as requiring each owner’s separate claim to satisfy the amount requirement, it refused class treatment. A divided Second Circuit affirmed, 469 F.2d 1033. The Supreme Court granted review and affirmed. (292; 293 n.1.)

The procedural distinction from Snyder was central: in Snyder no plaintiff had a claim large enough to create original diversity jurisdiction, whereas Zahn’s named plaintiffs did. The question was whether those qualifying named claims allowed absent owners with smaller, separate claims to participate in the same Rule 23(b)(3) action. (298–301.)

## Issue, holding, and exact judgment

Under the jurisdictional law then applied, could unnamed members with separate claims below the statutory minimum proceed in a diversity class action when the named plaintiffs’ claims exceeded the minimum?

**No.** The Court held that each plaintiff in a Rule 23(b)(3) action asserting a separate and distinct claim had to satisfy the jurisdictional amount. A qualifying plaintiff’s claim could not supply jurisdiction for another plaintiff’s independently deficient claim. It affirmed the Second Circuit’s judgment sustaining the refusal to permit this proposed class action. It did not dismiss the named owners’ independently sufficient claims or adjudicate the pollution merits. (300–302.)

## Majority reasoning: the statute, not the procedural label

Justice White located the rule in the statutory phrase “matter in controversy.” He distinguished separate and distinct demands joined for convenience from a single title or right in which multiple plaintiffs share a common and undivided interest. In the first situation, each plaintiff must meet the amount; in the second, the shared interest may be considered collectively. The alleged damage to individual lakefront properties belonged to the first category. A common source of alleged harm did not by itself convert each owner’s claim into a single undivided property right. (293–295.)

The Court traced the distinction through early jurisdictional cases and later joinder decisions. Clark v. Paul Gray had entertained the one qualifying plaintiff’s claim while directing dismissal of claims lacking the necessary amount. That demonstrated that the rule did more than prohibit adding small claims together when nobody qualified. It also barred independently deficient claims from accompanying a qualifying plaintiff. (294–296, including nn.3–4.)

Under the pre-1966 Rule 23 terminology, a “spurious” class action involving separate rights and common questions had operated as a permissive joinder device. The majority discussed Steele and Hackner as examples applying ordinary jurisdictional requirements to each claimant. The historical procedural categories were explanatory background; they were not treated as the source of Congress’s jurisdictional limit. (296–298 nn.5–7.)

Snyder had already rejected the contention that the 1966 revision of Rule 23 changed the statutory rule against aggregating separate claims. Giving revised class judgments a broader binding effect did not change “matter in controversy.” Congress had repeatedly adjusted the dollar threshold while leaving that operative language intact. Zahn’s majority considered Snyder’s reasoning controlling even though the named owners here qualified. It saw no reason to give unnamed class members an exemption unavailable to original plaintiffs asserting their own separate claims. (298–301 nn.8–10.)

The majority also declined to reconsider that construction. Congress’s longstanding acceptance and the absence of an expressed jurisdictional change in Rule 23 or its official commentary supported retaining the rule unless Congress acted. The exact statutory setting matters: the opinion addressed the then-$10,000 limit and stated in footnote 11 that a comparable limit then attached to general federal-question jurisdiction, while acknowledging numerous statutory exceptions. That historical footnote is not a statement of present § 1331 law. (301–302 n.11.)

## Brennan’s dissent: ancillary jurisdiction was a different theory

Justice Brennan, joined by Justices Douglas and Marshall, disagreed. He did not propose simply adding all the separate claims together to reach the threshold. He accepted that at least one qualifying plaintiff or joint interest was needed to establish original jurisdiction. Here the named plaintiffs had supplied it. The different question was whether the court could then exercise ancillary jurisdiction over related claims of nonappearing class members. In his view the majority used a nonaggregation principle to foreclose a theory that did not depend on aggregation. (302–305, especially 305.)

Brennan emphasized that the statute referred to civil actions without spelling out every requirement for each individual claimant. Judicial jurisdictional doctrines had accommodated fairness, efficiency, joinder structures, and the relationship among disputes. Ancillary jurisdiction had supported compulsory counterclaims, intervention as of right, and other closely related claims even without independent compliance with every ordinary jurisdictional requirement. A Rule 23(b)(3) class, with predominating common questions, could provide comparable safeguards against using a federal case to smuggle in unrelated controversies. (303–307 and nn.1–9.)

He pointed to Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur, which permitted a class action without requiring absent members individually to satisfy complete diversity, as evidence that representative status could matter. He distinguished Clark’s original appearing plaintiffs from absent class members. Likewise, he considered Snyder a decision about establishing jurisdiction over an action where no plaintiff qualified, rather than a decision rejecting ancillary jurisdiction after jurisdiction had properly attached. (309–310.)

Brennan also answered the procedural-rule objection. Rule 82 prevented construing the Federal Rules themselves to extend jurisdiction, but recognizing ancillary jurisdiction would exercise an existing judicial power, not obtain jurisdiction simply because Rule 23 had changed. Procedural innovations had often shaped how existing jurisdiction was exercised. He argued that revised Rule 23’s protections and binding effect made class treatment more appropriate, rather than supplying a reason to forbid it. (310–311 nn.11–14.)

The practical consequences reinforced his position. These pollution claims could require expensive expert proof of scientific issues. Separate litigation would repeat those costs and burden both court systems; small claims might become uneconomical to pursue at all. The district court’s inability to identify which proposed class members exceeded $10,000 could eliminate class treatment even for qualifying owners. The dissent described approximately 240 proposed claimants in developing this concern, a source description distinct from the majority’s account of the named owners and 200 others. He would have recognized the court’s power to decide class treatment under Rule 23, rather than requiring the particular proposed class to be certified regardless of that Rule. (307–312; particularly 310 and 312.)

## Votes and later development verified for the assignment

Justice White’s majority had six votes: Chief Justice Burger and Justices Stewart, Blackmun, Powell, and Rehnquist joined him. Brennan’s dissent had three, with Douglas and Marshall joining. The complete report contains the majority’s eleven numbered notes, Brennan’s fourteen numbered notes, and a separate amicus-counsel star on page 291; no additional separate writing or appendix is reported.

The assignment accurately describes Zahn’s historical holding and then contrasts Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc., 545 U.S. 546 (2005). The relevant later judicial passages were separately checked. Allapattah explains that an otherwise proper claim satisfying the amount requirement supplies original jurisdiction over a civil action; the court then considers supplemental jurisdiction over related claims under § 1367. Section 1367(b) does not exclude the additional Rule 23 class members at issue there. The Court expressly stated that § 1367’s text overruled Clark and Zahn and authorized the related diverse-party claims, subject to the statutory exceptions. Thus Zahn’s each-member amount rule should be studied as the historical rule, not presented as the governing result after that statutory interpretation. (Allapattah, 559–560, 566–567.)

This verified comparison is limited to those later passages, not a fresh full-source brief or current citator review of Allapattah. The crucial distinction is between aggregating deficient separate claims to create original jurisdiction and adding related claims through a statute after an otherwise qualifying claim establishes jurisdiction.
