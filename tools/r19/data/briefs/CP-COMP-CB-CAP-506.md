# Kerr v. United States District Court for the Northern District of California

**426 U.S. 394 (1976) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 14, 1976.** Justice Marshall wrote for all eight participating Justices; Justice Stevens did not participate. Civil Procedure: mandamus, discovery, governmental privilege, and available alternative relief. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 624, the own-caption Kerr account in Note 2, printed 1235–1236.

## Facts and the underlying action

Seven California prisoners brought a federal class action on behalf of present and future adult male felons in state custody, including those on parole. They alleged substantial constitutional violations in how the California Adult Authority determined the length and conditions of punishment. The defendants seeking Supreme Court relief were individual Authority members, its administrative officer, and the state Director of Corrections. The constitutional merits had not been tried when the Court considered the discovery dispute. (395–396, n.1.)

The plaintiffs sought two different groups of documents under Rule 34. The **Adult Authority files** included personnel files for Authority members and employees, reports and legislative statements, organizational and policy documents, and the chairman’s memoranda. Note 2 reproduces the detailed requests; this category was broader than individual personnel records. The **prisoners’ files** were correctional records for every twentieth inmate at each state institution, a sample meant to illuminate the system’s operation. Distinguishing the two categories matters because they produced separate discovery orders, protective arrangements, and mandamus petitions. (396–398, n.2.)

The state officials objected that the Authority documents were irrelevant, confidential, and privileged. They proposed that the district judge examine them privately, **in camera**, before ordering disclosure. The plaintiffs moved to compel under Rule 37. A magistrate recommended production without that preliminary inspection, and the district court adopted the recommendation. Its protective order limited access to personnel files to plaintiffs’ counsel and no more than two investigators, and only as necessary for this action. This was compelled discovery subject to restrictions, not an order making all records publicly accessible. (398.)

For the prisoner records, the magistrate recommended production of up to 200 files, with inspection restricted to plaintiffs’ attorneys for use in the lawsuit. The district court added a requirement that an inmate consent before that inmate’s file was turned over. Thus, the second order supplied a distinct privacy safeguard. (400–401.)

## Procedural route and arguments

The officials asked the Ninth Circuit for writs of mandamus under the All Writs Act, 28 U.S.C. §1651(a), directing the district court to vacate the discovery orders. Mandamus is an extraordinary command, rather than the ordinary appeal from a final judgment. The statute reproduced in note 3 authorizes writs in aid of a court’s jurisdiction, consistent with established legal principles. (399, n.3.)

The Ninth Circuit denied the Authority-file petition in a reasoned opinion. It considered relevance more broadly at discovery than at trial and found the documents part of a proper line of inquiry. It recognized a qualified governmental privilege, but the officials had not invoked it personally through appropriate high-level officials or specified which documents were protected and why. Counsel’s blanket objection to entire requests was inadequate. The appellate opinion nevertheless allowed particularized assertions and possible in-camera inspection in the district court. The prisoner-file petition was denied separately, without an explanatory opinion. The Supreme Court reviewed both denials. (399–401.)

The officials’ request before the Supreme Court was narrower than an absolute claim that no plaintiff could ever receive any document. They wanted production withheld until an informed district-court determination that the plaintiffs’ need outweighed confidentiality. In practical terms, they sought document-specific in-camera consideration. They argued that the Ninth Circuit’s denials left them without a remedy. (404–405.)

Two procedural developments did not change the issue. Note 4 recounts the temporary convening and later dissolution of a three-judge district court after amendments to the complaint; the underlying action was again before a single judge. Note 5 records that the plaintiffs initially suggested mootness because they expected trial before the Supreme Court could decide. They had not abandoned discovery in the district court, however, and at argument they renewed their desire for the documents because trial was delayed. The Court had no indication that the dispute was moot. Neither development constituted an adjudication of the prisoners’ constitutional claims. (401–402, nn.4–5.)

## Rule and holding

The Supreme Court **affirmed both denials of mandamus**. The officials had an adequate, less extreme route to request the relief they wanted: return to the district court, assert the qualified privilege specifically through responsible officials, and seek in-camera review. The availability of that route defeated the need for the extraordinary writ in these circumstances. (402–406.)

Mandamus is reserved for exceptional circumstances amounting to a judicial usurpation of power, although “jurisdiction” is not confined to its narrowest technical meaning. A petitioner must lack other adequate means of obtaining relief and show a clear and indisputable right to issuance; the writ also remains largely discretionary. These restrictions prevent mandamus from becoming a routine substitute for interlocutory appeal. Note 6 distinguishes the narrowly defined statutory circumstances for such appeals. Frequent extraordinary-writ review would undermine Congress’s general decision to postpone appellate review until final judgment and promote piecemeal litigation. It could also make the district judge personally defend the challenged action. (402–403, n.6.)

## Application and the privilege issue

The Court interpreted the Ninth Circuit’s Authority-file opinion as keeping renewed privilege assertions and in-camera inspection available. It did not read denial of mandamus as a final determination that every requested document must be disclosed regardless of its contents. Specificity mattered: a responsible official who had examined the documents could identify a particular record or class and explain the claimed protection, allowing a more informed district-court decision. (404–405.)

The claimed harms deserved serious consideration. Disclosure could chill candid communications within the prison and parole system, expose confidential sources, or unjustifiably compromise personnel and other sensitive files. Those were the officials’ asserted risks, not findings that all documents necessarily carried the same risk. Because the weight of confidentiality depended on each document’s nature, in-camera inspection offered a useful, relatively inexpensive way to balance the plaintiffs’ needs against privilege and relevance objections. Notes 7 and 8 identify supporting privilege authorities. The Court was confident that a proper application could obtain that inspection; it did not itself inspect the records or decide which particular documents were privileged. (405–406, nn.7–8.)

Although the second appellate order supplied no reasons, the Court saw no basis to conclude that it barred the same opportunity for renewed assertions and in-camera review of the prisoner files. The practical alternative therefore applied to both petitions. (406.)

Note 9 expressly reserves the officials’ separate Eleventh Amendment contention that discovery of property belonging to the state or its agencies was constitutionally limited. They had not raised it in the district discovery proceedings or their appellate mandamus petition, and the Court’s resolution made it unnecessary to reach. The case establishes neither an Eleventh Amendment immunity from discovery nor its rejection. (405–406, n.9.)

## Exact judgment and assigned comparison

The Court **affirmed the Ninth Circuit’s reasoned denial and its separate December 18, 1974 denial**. It did not grant a writ, categorically forbid discovery, adjudicate the substantive prisoner claims, or resolve every privilege objection. There was no separate concurrence or dissent. (395, 406.)

The assigned account accurately emphasizes a less drastic avenue for relief and resistance to piecemeal review. Its reference to “prisoner personnel files” compresses the actual two-category dispute: the opinion separately describes Authority personnel and policy files and sampled inmate correctional records. Its description of the Court’s “assumed” acceptance of a “suggestion” is editorial shorthand; the actual reasoning interprets the Ninth Circuit as leaving the route open and expresses confidence that proper applications could obtain in-camera review. (Assigned printed 1235–1236; actual 396–401, 404–406.)
