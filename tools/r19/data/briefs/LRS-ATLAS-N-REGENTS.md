# Department of Homeland Security v. Regents of the University of California
591 U.S. 1 (2020) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and the questions actually decided

The Supreme Court held that DHS’s September 2017 rescission of Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals was reviewable and arbitrary and capricious. Acting Secretary Elaine Duke had not considered keeping the program’s central protection against removal while eliminating associated benefits, and had not assessed recipients’ reliance interests. Secretary Kirstjen Nielsen’s later memorandum could not cure those omissions by introducing new reasons while expressly preserving Duke’s original action. The Court required DHS to consider the problem anew; it did not decide that DACA was lawful, that retaining it was wise, or that DHS lacked authority to rescind it through a properly reasoned decision. Principal opinion, slip 9, 13–26, 29. Citations here use the separately numbered pages of each archived judicial writing.

Chief Justice Roberts wrote for himself, Ginsburg, Breyer, Kagan, and Sotomayor on the APA issues. Sotomayor did not join Part IV, which rejected the pleaded equal-protection theory; that explanatory portion had four signatories. Thomas, joined by Alito and Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh separately concurred in the judgment rejecting that claim, producing an eight-Justice result over Sotomayor’s dissent. They disagreed with the APA result. Thomas, Alito, and Kavanaugh filed separate writings; Sotomayor also wrote separately. The Court reviewed three consolidated cases, including two before appellate judgment, and its orders differed by case. Principal slip 1–2, 8–9, 27–29; Thomas slip 3 n.1; Kavanaugh slip 8; Sotomayor slip 4.

## DACA, the related litigation, and rescission

DHS established DACA in June 2012 for specified individuals brought to the country as children. Eligibility included age, continuous residence, education or military service, criminal-record, and security criteria. The memorandum directed individualized decisions granting renewable two-year deferred action. Recipients could request work authorization upon a showing of economic necessity under regulations predating DACA. Other regulations treated them as lawfully present for specified Social Security and Medicare purposes. Approximately 700,000 people had obtained relief. Eligibility for these benefits was distinct from conferring permanent immigration status. Principal slip 2–3.

In 2014, DHS expanded DACA and established Deferred Action for Parents of Americans and Lawful Permanent Residents, potentially covering 4.3 million parents. Twenty-six states challenged DAPA and expanded DACA. The Fifth Circuit sustained a preliminary injunction on notice-and-comment and substantive INA grounds. It treated eligibility for benefits as more than unreviewable nonenforcement. The Supreme Court affirmed by an equally divided vote in 2016 without issuing a controlling explanatory opinion. That litigation and injunction concerned the 2014 changes, rather than adjudicating the original 2012 DACA program in the judgment reviewed here. Principal slip 3–5.

DHS rescinded the 2014 memorandum in June 2017. In September, Attorney General Sessions advised Duke that DACA shared the defects identified in the DAPA litigation and should be rescinded with an orderly wind-down. Duke acted the next day, September 5. Her memorandum relied on the court decisions and the Attorney General’s illegality determination. It stopped new applications, allowed two-year renewals for existing recipients whose benefits would expire within six months, and otherwise let existing grants expire on their terms without renewal. Principal slip 5–6; Thomas slip 6–7.

Recipients, states, universities, and other plaintiffs sued in California, New York, and the District of Columbia. The California and New York courts entered nationwide preliminary injunctions requiring acceptance of renewals but not new applications, finding likely success on APA claims. They also allowed equal-protection claims to proceed. The D.C. court instead granted partial summary judgment, vacated the rescission for inadequate explanation, and stayed that order for 90 days to let DHS provide a fuller explanation. Notice-and-comment and other due-process claims existed below but were not before the Supreme Court. Principal slip 6–8 & nn.1–2.

In June 2018, Nielsen declined to disturb Duke’s rescission and supplied what she regarded as three independently sufficient justifications: illegality, legal uncertainty and litigation concerns, and policy objections to class-based executive relief. Her policy grounds included preference for congressional action, individualized prosecutorial discretion, and a clear enforcement message. She acknowledged asserted reliance but concluded it did not outweigh those considerations. The D.C. court refused to revise its order because the memorandum did not adequately elaborate the original illegality rationale. The government sought certiorari before judgment in all three matters; the Ninth Circuit affirmed before the Supreme Court granted review, while the Second and D.C. Circuit appeals remained unresolved. Principal slip 7–9.

## Why the APA and immigration statutes permitted review

The government argued that adopting or ending a general nonenforcement policy fell within the unreviewable prosecutorial discretion recognized in *Heckler v. Chaney*. The Court did not decide whether that chain of reasoning applied to every general policy. DACA was more than passive nonenforcement: DHS solicited applications, created a standardized adjudicative process, and issued affirmative approvals identifying people who would receive forbearance. Benefits eligibility supplied an additional interest of the sort courts ordinarily protect. Creation and rescission of this affirmative program provided a focus for review, unlike simply declining a request for an enforcement action. Principal slip 9–12.

The government’s INA jurisdictional objections also failed. Section 1252(b)(9) concerned claims arising from actions or proceedings to remove a person; these plaintiffs were not challenging removal proceedings. Section 1252(g) targeted decisions to commence proceedings, adjudicate cases, or execute removal orders, rather than imposing a general bar on review of immigration policy. Revoking the deferred-action program and its associated benefits was none of those specified decisions. Principal slip 12–13.

## New reasons required new action

An agency may supplement a deficient explanation by elaborating the reasons it actually had when it acted. Alternatively, it may address the problem afresh through new action, with the procedures that action requires. Those routes permit different explanations. Once the original decision identifies its determinative rationale, a later elaboration cannot add new rationales while preserving the original action as though they had always supported it. Principal slip 13–14.

Nielsen chose elaboration: her memorandum expressly declined to disturb Duke’s decision, and the government asked the court to leave the September 2017 action in place. But Duke had relied on illegality alone. Legal uncertainty differs from illegality because uncertainty does not necessarily require remedial action. Nielsen’s policy preferences likewise did not appear in Duke’s explanation or its incorporated legal authorities. They therefore could not sustain the original rescission. The majority rejected the contention that only lawyers’ litigation explanations, rather than agency officials’ later explanations, were restricted: the problem concerned timing, not the speaker. Nor did it accept a categorical exception for rulemaking. Principal slip 14–17 & nn.3–4.

The distinction served public accountability and orderly review. A new decision lets affected parties respond to the authority actually exercised and the reasons actually chosen. Allowing successive justifications after courts identify flaws would invite convenient litigation positions and force courts and parties to chase an evolving rationale. The Court therefore considered Duke’s contemporaneous grounds rather than treating Nielsen’s policy analysis as a completed cure. Principal slip 15–17.

## Illegality did not answer the separate policy choices

The Court declined to adjudicate respondents’ attacks on the correctness or adequacy of the Attorney General’s legal determination. Section 1103(a)(1) made that determination controlling for Duke, and the respondents had not adequately addressed how that constraint affected the merits or whether these actions were appropriate vehicles to attack it. This reservation was not an endorsement of DACA’s legality or of every aspect of the Attorney General’s view. Principal slip 17–18.

What remained for DHS was how to respond. The majority understood the Fifth Circuit’s benefits-based reasoning, incorporated into Sessions’s advice, as leaving forbearance authority unimpaired. Designating a person lawfully present for certain benefits was not identical to deciding not to remove that person. Those concepts could be separated through the relevant regulations; deferred action did not inexorably produce every benefits designation. Yet Duke offered no explanation for terminating forbearance, the program’s centerpiece. Principal slip 19–23 & nn.5–6.

The Court analogized to *State Farm*: concerns about automatic seatbelts did not justify eliminating an entire restraint requirement without considering an airbags-only alternative. Similarly, a legal objection to benefits did not eliminate the need to consider forbearance alone. DHS could ultimately have sound reasons to keep the two together, but counsel’s assertion that the components were linked did not show that Duke had considered that choice. Agencies need not examine every imaginable alternative; this one was central to the policy already in place. Principal slip 21–23.

Duke separately failed to evaluate reliance. The program’s disclaimers of substantive rights and two-year renewal periods could reduce the weight of reliance, but did not automatically eliminate it. Plaintiffs and amici identified schooling, careers, businesses, home purchases, families, employer training, and government revenue effects. Those were asserted interests and estimates, not an adjudicated damages award or a judicial finding that every prediction was correct. The agency had to assess their existence and significance before weighing competing concerns. Principal slip 23–25.

Reliance did not require indefinite continuation. DHS could conclude that reliance on disputed benefits was weak, that other concerns outweighed even serious reliance, or that a particular accommodation was inappropriate. The existing wind-down demonstrated flexibility, but Duke had used it to address administrative complexity rather than recipients’ needs. Potential options included longer renewal windows, termination dates accommodating study, military service, or medical treatment, and individualized enforcement decisions accounting for reliance. The Court required consideration, not adoption, of accommodations. Principal slip 25–26.

## Equal protection and the separate opinions

Roberts’s four-Justice Part IV found the pleaded animus theory implausible, while assuming rather than resolving whether *Reno v. American-Arab Anti-Discrimination Committee* barred the claim. Disparate impact on Latino recipients, the decision’s sequence, and presidential statements did not suffice in that plurality’s assessment. The June DAPA rescission’s statement that DACA remained in effect was viewed as abstention rather than substantive reaffirmation; the later legal advice explained reevaluation. The cited presidential remarks were considered remote and unrelated, and no comparable remarks by Duke or Sessions were identified. This was a pleading-stage assessment, not a trial finding exonerating every possible motive. Principal slip 27–29.

Sotomayor argued that the complaints plausibly alleged discrimination when their facts were considered together. Presidential statements about Mexican immigrants, the policy’s disproportionate impact, and the abrupt change after DHS’s prior assurances warranted factual development. She did not find proven discrimination; she would have allowed the theory to proceed on remand. Sotomayor slip 1–4.

Thomas, joined by Alito and Gorsuch, regarded DACA as unlawful from inception. The detailed INA scheme, express legislative grants of classwide relief, and absence of delegated power foreclosed an additional program created by memorandum. He also viewed DACA as a legislative rule issued without required procedures. In his account, correct recognition of that illegality was sufficient to rescind it; reliance could not authorize continued exercise of power Congress had withheld. He disputed the majority’s dissection of benefits and forbearance and distinguished *State Farm*’s lawful prior policy. His footnote 14 warned that separating benefits after years of eligibility could itself require explanations and regulatory changes. He would reverse and dissolve the injunctions, while concurring in rejection of equal protection. Thomas slip 1–26, including all sixteen footnotes.

Alito joined Thomas and additionally argued that, insofar as DACA was lawful prosecutorial discretion, its rescission was equally unreviewable. Alternatively, he considered Nielsen’s reasoning sufficient, agreeing substantially with Kavanaugh. He criticized the litigation’s prevention of rescission without a holding that rescission was prohibited. Alito slip 1–2.

Kavanaugh treated Nielsen’s memorandum as reviewable agency action in its own right: an official statement of future policy comparable to reconsideration or a supplemental decision. He considered its legality, policy, and reliance discussion reasonable under deferential review. He distinguished later agency explanations from lawyers’ rationalizations and suggested different treatment for adjudications involving individual rights. The majority rejected those distinctions. Kavanaugh reserved the committed-to-discretion issue in his footnote because he would uphold the decision on the merits; he separately agreed to reject equal protection. Kavanaugh slip 1–8 & note at 7.

## Exact judgment and assigned scope

The Court affirmed the D.C. *NAACP* judgment vacating rescission. It vacated in part and reversed in part the Ninth Circuit judgment in *Regents*. In *Batalla Vidal*, it vacated the February 13, 2018 preliminary-injunction order, affirmed in part the November 9, 2017 order partially denying dismissal, and reversed in part the March 29, 2018 order partially denying the remaining dismissal motion. All three cases were remanded. Affirmance of the vacatur made it unnecessary to resolve the nationwide scope of the other injunctions. Principal slip 29 & n.7.
