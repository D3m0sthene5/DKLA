Citation
United Mine Workers of America v. Gibbs, 383 U.S. 715 (1966). Supreme Court of the United States. Argued
January 20, 1966; decided March 28, 1966. Justice Brennan delivered the Court’s opinion. Justice Harlan,
joined by Justice Clark, concurred in the result and joined the jurisdictional discussion in Part I. Chief Justice
Warren took no part. Assigned excerpt: casebook pp. 353–358. Full opinion: Full judicial opinion.
Parties
Paul Gibbs, the plaintiff and Supreme Court respondent, had been hired to supervise a new Tennessee mine and
haul its coal. He sued the international United Mine Workers of America, the defendant and petitioner, for
interference with those relationships. The international union must be distinguished from the local union and
individual miners whose conduct precipitated the dispute: their participation in violence did not itself establish
the international organization’s legal responsibility. Gibbs sought damages under both federal labor law and
Tennessee law. 383 U.S. at 717–721, 736–742.
Material Facts
Tennessee Consolidated Coal Company closed a mine in 1960, laying off approximately 100 miners belonging
to a UMW local. Its subsidiary, Grundy Company, planned to open a nearby mine with workers represented by
a rival union. Grundy hired Gibbs as superintendent and separately arranged for him to haul coal. Local UMW
members believed the new jobs should go to them. Armed members prevented the mine from opening,
threatened Gibbs, and assaulted an organizer associated with the rival union. Gibbs lost the superintendent’s
job, and the contemplated hauling work never materialized. These overlapping losses supplied the factual basis
for his federal and state theories. Id. at 717–720.
The international union’s representative, George Gilbert, was absent when the initial confrontation occurred.
On returning, he directed that violence cease and that a limited picket line be maintained. Picketing continued
for months without further violence at the mine. Whether the international authorized, participated in, or
knowingly ratified the local violence became a separate merits question. The same events could form one
jurisdictional controversy without every participant’s conduct being attributable to the international union. Id.
at 718–720, 739–742.
Procedural History
Gibbs sued in federal district court under § 303 of the Labor Management Relations Act, alleging prohibited
secondary pressure, and under Tennessee law, alleging an unlawful conspiracy to interfere with his
employment and hauling contracts. The federal claim supplied the asserted basis for original jurisdiction. The
Tennessee claim had no independent jurisdictional basis. The jury returned awards for lost employment, lost
hauling work, and punitive damages. Following post-trial motions, the district court set aside the hauling award
as unsupported and rejected federal liability for the employment loss because the pressure involved a primary
labor dispute rather than the secondary activity covered by § 303. It nevertheless sustained a reduced recovery
on the Tennessee claim. Id. at 720–721 & n.6.
The Sixth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court considered both the district court’s authority to decide the state
claim and the federal limits on imposing liability on the international union. It upheld the exercise of pendent
jurisdiction but reversed the judgment because the evidence did not meet the federal standard for the union’s

Issue
When a substantial federal claim and a state claim arise from the same labor confrontation, may the federal
court adjudicate the state claim despite its lack of an independent jurisdictional basis? If that power exists, must
the court retain the state claim, and does the later failure of the federal claim automatically invalidate its
exercise of jurisdiction? Id. at 721–729.
The full opinion also asks how federal labor preemption and § 6 of the Norris-LaGuardia Act constrain a
state-law damages judgment against a union. Those merits questions explain the ultimate reversal and extend
beyond the principal jurisdictional discussion reproduced in the assigned excerpt. Id. at 729–742.
Rule and Holding
A federal court has pendent judicial power when the federal claim is sufficiently substantial to support federal
jurisdiction, the federal and state claims arise from a common nucleus of operative fact, and their relationship
is such that a plaintiff ordinarily would be expected to try them together. Different legal theories or requested
remedies do not necessarily divide one constitutional case into unrelated controversies. Id. at 725.
Exercising that power remains discretionary. Judicial economy, convenience, fairness, the desirability of
allowing state courts to decide state law, predominance of state issues, and possible jury confusion bear on
whether the claims should remain together. The district court did not abuse that discretion here: the federal
claim was substantial, the theories arose from the same events, and the federal claim failed only after the case
had been tried. Id. at 726–729.
On the separate merits question, § 6 required clear proof of the international union’s actual participation in,
authorization of, or ratification of the unlawful acts after knowledge of them. The record did not satisfy that
requirement. The failure was evidentiary, not a conclusion that the district court lacked pendent power. Id. at
735–742.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Sixth Circuit’s judgment sustaining Gibbs’s recovery. It accepted the district
court’s exercise of pendent jurisdiction but held that the proof did not support the international union’s liability
under the governing federal standard. Id. at 742.
Reasoning
The Court rejected an excessively formal distinction between two grounds supporting a single cause of action
and two separate causes of action. Earlier formulations, associated with Hurn v. Oursler, made jurisdiction
depend on an uncertain conception of a cause of action. Modern federal procedure instead permits related
disputes to be resolved together. The constitutional question concerns the relationship among the facts and
claims forming the controversy, rather than whether the plaintiff has selected one legal label or several. The
procedural rules did not themselves enlarge jurisdiction; they helped explain why the older categorical
approach was unsatisfactory. Id. at 722–725.
The federal and Tennessee theories in Gibbs concerned the same confrontation at the mine and the resulting
loss of employment and hauling work. Proving one theory would substantially overlap with proving the other.
That factual relationship, combined with a substantial federal claim, supplied a single constitutional case. A
weak federal theory at the end of a trial is different from a purported federal claim so insubstantial that it

cannot support jurisdiction at the outset. The district court’s eventual rejection of the § 303 claim did not

The Court then separated power from its appropriate exercise. A plaintiff has no automatic right to keep every
related state claim in federal court. State-law questions may predominate in the necessary proof, the breadth of
the issues, or the remedy sought. They may also create confusion that makes a combined trial unfair. Avoiding
unnecessary decisions of state law respects state institutions and can obtain a more authoritative explanation of
that law. These considerations can justify declining a state claim even when Article III permits its adjudication.
Id. at 726–727.
The fate and timing of the federal claim are particularly relevant. When the federal claims disappear before
trial, the state claims ordinarily should be dismissed as well. When substantial litigation has already occurred,
economy and fairness may favor retaining the related claim. The judge must remain attentive throughout the
proceedings: a case that initially appears predominantly federal can develop into a substantially different
state-law controversy. The Court therefore described a continuing discretionary judgment rather than a
once-and-for-all permission to retain every claim originally pleaded. Id. at 726–727.
Here, the federal and state theories were tried together and addressed the same conduct. The federal theory
survived far enough to receive a jury verdict, which the judge later set aside on a legal distinction between
primary and secondary activity. Federal preemption also closely affected the state claim, making federal
expertise relevant. Those circumstances supported retention. The Supreme Court acknowledged that the district
court could have declined jurisdiction; it held that the choice actually made was permissible. Id. at 727–729.
The remainder of the opinion demonstrates why jurisdiction and liability require distinct analysis. State
authority over violence in a labor dispute did not permit damages for every consequence of peaceful activity
regulated by federal labor law. The state-law recovery had to be connected to the violence or threats that the
state could regulate. An abstract allegation of conspiracy could not eliminate that boundary. This discussion
qualifies the substantive theory; it does not withdraw the pendent power recognized in Part I. Id. at 729–735.

The decisive deficiency concerned the international union’s responsibility. Section 6’s clear-proof requirement
demanded more than ordinary inferences from membership or routine strike administration. The international
representative’s efforts to stop violence, his subsequent handling of the picket line, and ordinary assistance to
strikers did not establish knowing approval of the earlier unlawful acts. The Court found no adequate basis for
treating continuation of legitimate union activity as ratification of violence. Because the required proof was
missing, Gibbs could not retain the damages judgment against this defendant. Id. at 735–742. [Full-opinion
supplement.]
Separate Opinions
Justice Harlan, joined by Justice Clark, joined Part I’s pendent-jurisdiction analysis and agreed with reversal.
He considered the broader discussion of labor preemption unnecessary. He also resisted treating § 6 simply as a
general elevation of the quantity of proof required in civil litigation. His account emphasized the statute’s
restriction on inferring union authorization or ratification from ordinary organizational conduct. The
concurrence consequently supports the jurisdictional holding while providing a different explanation for the
merits result. Id. at 742–744. [Full-opinion supplement.]

Notes
The narrow jurisdictional holding concerns closely related claims against the same union after a substantial
federal claim reached trial. It does not establish that a federal court must retain all state claims or may add any
new party merely because the underlying events overlap. Its broader contribution is the separation of
constitutional judicial power from a reasoned decision whether to exercise that power. The distinction matters
when a problem asks both whether jurisdiction exists and whether a federal judge should decline it. Id. at
725–729.
The assigned excerpt emphasizes Part I. The discussions of labor preemption, the international union’s
responsibility, and Harlan’s merits analysis above are identified as full-opinion supplements because they
explain the actual disposition beyond that excerpt. The later statutory regime addressed in Allapattah must be
analyzed through its own text; Gibbs supplies the underlying common-fact conception of a constitutional case
rather than a substitute for the statutory restrictions. Id. at 725–729; see the separate Allapattah brief.
