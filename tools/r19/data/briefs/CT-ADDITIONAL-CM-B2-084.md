# Emerson Radio Corp. v. Orion Sales, Inc.
253 F.3d 159 (3d Cir. 2001) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
The Third Circuit decided this diversity appeal on June 5, 2001, applying New Jersey substantive law. Judge Sloviter wrote the court's opinion, joined by Judge Garth. Judge Ambro joined every resolution except rejection of an implied reasonable-efforts obligation. The court reversed summary judgment on Emerson's express-contract and implied-good-faith claims and remanded for further proceedings. It affirmed rejection of the separate implied-efforts theory and tortious-interference claim, and affirmed the judgment entered on an already-tried dispute about product returns. The result reopened two theories for trial; it did not establish a breach or award damages on them. (159, 161–162, 175–176.)

## Agreements, business relationship, and litigation
Emerson had ceased manufacturing its own consumer-electronics products by 1994 and licensed its trademark to other manufacturers. It regarded the brand as its principal business asset. On February 22, 1995, it granted Orion an exclusive, nontransferable three-year license concerning specified Emerson-brand video and television products sold to Wal-Mart, historically Emerson's largest customer. The operative language authorized Orion to utilize and exploit the marks in manufacturing, selling, marketing, distributing, and servicing the licensed goods. A simultaneous supply agreement required affiliated Otake companies to supply Emerson with video products for customers other than Wal-Mart. Both agreements ran from April 1, 1995, through March 31, 1998. The license guaranteed Emerson $4 million annually, regardless of the licensee's sales performance. (161–163, 166, 169.)

The affiliated companies included Orion in Illinois, Otake Trading in Japan, and Technos Development in Hong Kong. Emerson alleged that Shigemasa Otake owned or controlled them. Former Emerson executive John Bond, previously its principal Wal-Mart contact, had joined the Otake enterprise. Emerson sued the companies, Otake, and Bond on December 20, 1995, alleging breach, bad faith, unfair competition, interference, and conspiracy. Following discovery, the district court disposed of all but a product-returns issue through three summary-judgment opinions. A jury subsequently resolved that remaining dispute. Emerson appealed both the adverse summary-judgment rulings and the treatment of the jury's monetary findings. The appellate court reviewed summary judgment and the interpretation of state law plenarily, drawing reasonable factual inferences for Emerson as nonmovant. (161–163.)

## Express promise: ambiguity required a trial
Emerson's first theory was that the license's express language imposed reasonable efforts or diligence in marketing its brand, rather than merely giving Orion a permission it could leave unused. Its written presentations sometimes referred to best efforts, but counsel clarified the asserted express duty at oral argument as reasonable efforts or due diligence. Emerson offered evidence that Orion intended to displace the Emerson brand with its own brand, including divergent sales records and internal communications about concealing that strategy. These were allegations and evidence supporting an inference, not findings that Orion had committed the alleged misconduct. (163 & n. 1.)

The district court recognized two possible meanings of exploit: action advancing the licensed brand and action advancing Orion's own interests. It nevertheless treated the language's uncertainty as a reason to reject an express sales obligation as a matter of law. The Third Circuit found that approach inverted the proper analysis. Whether a contract is ambiguous is a legal question informed by the language, the parties' proposed meanings, and relevant extrinsic evidence. If only one interpretation is reasonable, summary judgment may be appropriate. If the language reasonably supports competing meanings, the factfinder ordinarily resolves the ambiguity. Recognizing an inconclusive term does not authorize the judge simply to select the interpretation denying an obligation. (163–164.)

The court did not declare every word with multiple dictionary definitions ambiguous. Here the alternatives were plausible in the actual licensing context, and other provisions invoked by the parties did not conclusively resolve the dispute. Decisions discussing exploitation of licensed property associated that concept with reasonable diligence or efforts. Although those decisions generally concerned implied obligations and did not control the construction of this particular express grant, they refuted Orion's contention that no reasonable jury could understand exploit to impose an affirmative duty. The court reversed summary judgment on this theory so that Emerson could try to prove the obligation and its breach. It did not itself finally construe the clause in Emerson's favor. (164–165.)

## Implied efforts: guaranteed royalties distinguished a royalty-only bargain
The second theory would supply reasonable efforts as an implied obligation even without an express promise. The majority examined Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon and New Jersey's Fenning decision. In a royalty-only exclusive arrangement, the owner's compensation may depend entirely on the licensee's activity; an implied undertaking can prevent the owner from being wholly at the other's mercy and give substance to the bargain. Emerson, however, had negotiated a substantial noncontingent return: $4 million annually for three years. Its chief executive acknowledged that the guarantee partly guarded against total nonuse. (165–169 & n. 2.)

The majority did not read Fenning's brief observation about minimum royalties as requiring an implied obligation whenever a guarantee exists. Nor did Bellows establish that every license lacking an express authorization for nonuse must imply diligence. More instructive authorities examined the parties' consideration and intentions and declined to add efforts obligations where substantial advance or minimum payments protected the owner against the licensee doing nothing. The majority also emphasized restraint in revising an elaborately negotiated bargain made with counsel. These considerations supported affirming summary judgment on Emerson's implied-efforts theory. The decision concerned this agreement and its substantial guaranteed compensation; it did not announce that any nominal minimum payment necessarily defeats any implied obligation. Emerson could still pursue its distinct express-language theory at trial. (167–169 & n. 3.)

Judge Ambro dissented only on this point. He read Fenning as recognizing that a minimum royalty can coexist with an implied reasonable-efforts obligation. He therefore would have permitted Emerson to try the implied theory as well as the express theory. His disagreement did not extend to the good-faith, interference, or returns rulings. (175–176.)

## Good faith: minimum compensation did not exhaust the bargain's benefits
Rejecting an implied efforts promise did not eliminate the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. New Jersey contracts carry that covenant, protecting the parties' justified expectations and their ability to receive the agreement's fruits. A party may breach it through bad-faith performance even while complying with express, unambiguous terms. The opinion referred to the UCC's honesty-in-fact definition by analogy but expressly recognized that this trademark license was not a sale-of-goods contract governed by the UCC. (169–170.)

The district court had confined Emerson's expected benefits to guaranteed royalties and added financial security. Emerson instead maintained that it had entrusted its important Wal-Mart relationship and brand goodwill to Orion and reasonably expected continuing exploitation beyond the minimum return. Its chief executive described hopes of approximately $6 million to $8 million annually; Bond characterized $4 million as the minimum necessary to obtain Emerson's signature. The defendants answered that Emerson was trying to salvage a financially troubled business and sought only the guarantee, assumption of returns obligations, and a $10.2 million supply-agreement settlement payment. These conflicting accounts could not be resolved against Emerson on summary judgment. (170–172.)

The record also supported disputed issues of injury, causation, and concealed intent. Emerson-brand Wal-Mart sales fell from $331 million to $74 million, a 78 percent decline, during the first two license years; Orion-brand sales rose from $92 million to $239 million in the first year. An internal projection anticipated Emerson sales falling to zero while Orion sales rose sharply. Communications suggested concealment of plans to eliminate Emerson's Wal-Mart business, and one memorandum reported persuading Wal-Mart to replace an Emerson nineteen-inch television with Orion's. The court treated this material as sufficient to support genuine factual disputes, rather than as conclusive proof of the projected strategy, resulting damages, or liability. (172–173.)

The district court also gave undue weight to a supposed absence of special economic vulnerability. Vulnerability was a relevant factor in the cited New Jersey cases, not an indispensable element of every good-faith claim; moreover, the record contained evidence of Emerson's losses and burdens from returns. The court reversed summary judgment on good faith. Footnote 4 left the district court to clarify defendants and contractual scope on remand: the complaint alleged misconduct by all the Otake companies, whereas the appellate briefs pressed Orion's covenant under a license to which the other companies were not parties. (173 & n. 4.)

## Tortious interference: no evidence of conduct outside the corporate relationship
Emerson also challenged judgment for Otake personally on interference with the license and supply contracts. Interference ordinarily requires a third party rather than a contracting party; the restriction extends to corporate agents, although acts outside agency or employment may supply the required separate relationship. Emerson's own allegations and appellate briefs described Otake as exercising systematic control and occupying a central role in the companies' business. At argument counsel sought to characterize him as retired and legally independent, offering suggestions that others need not follow, but conceded that all his suggestions were followed. The court found no evidence that the challenged conduct lay outside his alleged ownership and control relationship. It affirmed summary judgment on interference. The decision was a record-based failure to establish the relevant third-party relationship, not immunity for every act by a shareholder or former executive. (173–174.)

## Product returns: the tried monetary judgment remained intact
A separate dispute concerned returns of goods Emerson had sold before the license. Goods delivered and received in July 1995 were Emerson's responsibility, and those delivered and received in August were Orion's. The contested category was sent in July but received in August. The jury assigned that category to Orion, awarded Emerson $873,721, found the obligation to Wal-Mart satisfied in 1995 rather than 1998, and separately found Emerson owed Orion $215,283 for July returns incorrectly charged to Orion. The district court entered awards for both amounts with prejudgment interest and offset them. Other final-judgment amounts were not challenged on this appeal. (174 & n. 5.)

Emerson argued that $873,721 was already a net amount and that subtracting $215,283 again duplicated Orion's credit. The verdict form had not asked for a net figure, and the cited record did not support that characterization. Footnote 6 additionally identified a $10,435 arithmetic discrepancy in Emerson's proposed gross-to-net explanation. The appellate court upheld the judgment based on the jury's separate findings. It also upheld interest on Orion's $215,283 from July 15, 1995. The midpoint of the July period of incorrect charges was supported by the record, rather than selected randomly; the equitable award showed neither abuse of discretion nor a manifest denial of justice. (174–175 & n. 6.)

## Significance and reading scope
The opinion distinguishes three routes that an abbreviated exploitation problem can easily collapse: a disputed express promise, an efforts promise supplied by implication, and bad-faith performance defeating justified contractual benefits. The first and third survived summary judgment; the majority rejected the second. The actual appellate judgment also resolved interference, jury-award accounting, and interest, which are absent from the assigned interpretation exercise. No later proceeding or current-law treatment is certified here.
