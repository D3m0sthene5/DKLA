# Wm. R. Clarke Corporation v. Safeco Insurance Company of America
15 Cal. 4th 882, 938 P.2d 372 (1997) | Contracts

## Court, vote, and result
The California Supreme Court decided the consolidated construction-payment case on June 26, 1997. Justice Kennard wrote for Chief Justice George and Justices Werdegar and Brown. Justice Chin dissented, joined by Justices Mosk and Baxter, making the result 4–3. The court affirmed the Court of Appeal’s judgment for the subcontractors against payment-bond surety Safeco and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. Rehearing was denied September 3, 1997; Mosk, Baxter, and Chin would have granted it. (882, 885, 897, 908.)

## Project, contracts, and collection actions
In 1990 a Los Angeles commercial-building owner hired Keller Construction to rehabilitate the building. Keller subcontracted work to Wm. R. Clarke, Barsotti’s, Garvin Fire Protection, and Church and Larsen. Each subcontract expressly made receipt of owner funds a condition of Keller’s payment obligation, regardless of the reason for nonpayment, including fault of any participant. Three subcontracts also had an addendum reiterating the contractor’s lack of liability if unpaid and purporting to preserve mechanics’ lien rights as the subcontractor’s sole remedy. The dissent explains that Barsotti’s had not signed the addendum, but the parties treated all four subcontractors alike on appeal and Barsotti’s agreed with that treatment. (886–887 & n.3; 898–899 & dissent n.1.)

At the owner’s insistence and pursuant to the general contract, Keller obtained a $16.5 million labor-and-material payment bond from Safeco. Keller and Safeco were bound to persons furnishing work or materials to the identified project. The recorded bond invoked the statutory payment-bond scheme and required a claimant to record a lien or give timely notice before suing. After substantial work, the owner stopped paying, apparently because of insolvency; Keller then declined to pay the subcontractors. They recorded liens and brought separate bond actions against Safeco. Three were resolved by summary judgment and one by a bench trial. Each produced judgment for the subcontractor, and the Court of Appeal affirmed after consolidating the appeals. The stated bond amount was its penal sum, not a finding that the subcontractors recovered $16.5 million. (887–888.)

## The surety’s condition-precedent defense
Safeco argued that a surety’s obligation could not exceed its principal’s. Because owner payment never occurred, Keller’s contractual duty allegedly never arose, so there was no contractor default for Safeco to answer. This argument depended first on the condition’s validity. The majority distinguished clauses construed as merely identifying the usual payment time, which leave an unconditional duty after a reasonable period, from true conditions that can eliminate payment altogether. Here the court addressed enforceability of an actual pay-if-paid condition rather than solving an ambiguity by calling it pay-when-paid. (885–886, 888.)

## Mechanics’ lien policy: substance over form
California’s Constitution secures lien rights to those who improve property, and the statutory scheme restricts their waiver. Former Civil Code section 3262 prescribed permitted waiver-and-release forms and circumstances tied to payment or its promise, with conditional releases effective upon payment. The majority treated those restrictions as expressing a policy that private agreements could not circumvent. (888–889.)

Safeco initially argued that the condition did not waive a lien: it simply prevented any payment right from arising, leaving nothing due under the contract for a lien to secure. The majority acknowledged that formal distinction but rejected its practical consequence. Former section 3140 measured lien recovery by the amount due under the contract’s terms. An indefinitely unpaid owner, coupled with a valid condition, could therefore leave the subcontractor with no enforceable lien despite completed work. The condition accomplished indirectly what the anti-waiver rules forbade directly. The clause was unenforceable on public-policy grounds. (889–890.)

Freedom of contract did not defeat the legislatively expressed lien policy. Nor was this clause limited to allocating owner insolvency; its text covered nonpayment for any reason. The addendum’s apparent reservation of lien rights also exposed the practical inconsistency: if the condition left nothing due, the promised lien remedy could be illusory. The majority rejected Safeco’s later argument that section 3140 merely capped liens at the contract price. Its text incorporated the contract’s terms in determining what was due. The court distinguished Michel & Pfeffer’s specified sources for a final payment because that earlier decision did not decide indefinite postponement’s compatibility with the anti-waiver policy. (890–892.)

## The payment bond’s additional obligation
The majority added that Safeco would still lose under its alternative theory preserving valid liens despite the condition. Keller was a principal and co-obligor on the bond, whose language covered valid project lien claims independently of whether the claimant held an enforceable direct subcontract claim against Keller. Safeco could not look only to Keller’s liability under the subcontracts while ignoring this separate bond obligation. The bond referenced the general contract, not the subcontracts, and its protection could extend to lower-tier claimants lacking contractual privity with Keller. (893–896.)

Earlier decisions required a claimant to establish a legal and valid compensation claim independently of the bond. The majority explained that a valid project lien could satisfy that requirement; it did not necessarily require a separate direct payment obligation of the principal outside the bond. It accordingly disapproved Kalfountzos to the extent it implied that a principal incurred no bond liability absent another payment obligation. This was a limited disapproval of that proposition, not an abandonment of every defense or claim-validity requirement applicable to sureties. Because the pay-if-paid provisions themselves were invalid, Keller owed payment under the subcontracts and Safeco was likewise liable on the bond. (894–897.)

## Chin’s dissent
Chin accepted protection of mechanics’ liens but separated a lien against property from contractual claims between contractor and subcontractor. He would read section 3140 as fixing the value of the lien at the contract amount without allowing owner default to erase the lien. On that interpretation, the parties could share owner-nonpayment risk while preserving claims against the property. He emphasized the disclosed lack of construction financing, voluntary agreement, and the burden on a contractor that might otherwise remain unpaid while financing subcontractors’ payments. He did not rule out invalidation for particular defects such as misrepresentation or unconscionability, and expressly reserved a clause applied to nonpayment caused by the contractor’s fault. (897–903 & dissent nn.1–3.)

Chin also rejected the majority’s independent-bond reasoning. In his view, a surety guaranteed the principal’s underlying obligation and default; it did not create an obligation that the subcontract explicitly withheld. The bond protected against Keller’s default, not the owner’s. He regarded the majority’s treatment of the bond and prior surety cases as inconsistent with that distinction and would have reversed the Court of Appeal. This position did not prevail. (903–908 & dissent n.4.)
