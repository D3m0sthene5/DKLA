# Hikma Pharmaceuticals USA Inc. v. Amarin Pharma, Inc.

**608 U.S. 580 (2026) • Supreme Court of the United States • June 4, 2026**

**Assigned source:** Civil Procedure Supplement, printed pp. 520–521 (PDF 349). Justice Jackson delivered the unanimous opinion; there were no separate opinions. The official preliminary report supplies the page number left blank in the assigned caption and Justia's headline.

## Regulatory setting and facts

The Hatch-Waxman framework allows a generic manufacturer to rely on a brand-name drug's approved application rather than repeat the original safety and efficacy studies. A paragraph IV certification challenges a relevant patent's validity or applicability and permits patent litigation. A section viii statement instead seeks approval for unpatented uses, with a “skinny label” carving out protected methods of use. Approved generics may still be substituted for brand-name drugs under state law, including in uses covered by patents. Knowledge that substitution may occur does not by itself establish active inducement. 584–587.

Amarin developed Vascepa, whose active ingredient is icosapent ethyl. The FDA approved its treatment of severe hypertriglyceridemia, the SH indication, in 2012. Its label initially stated that cardiovascular effects in those patients had not been determined. Hikma filed a generic application with a paragraph IV certification in 2016, challenging the SH patents; a district court later invalidated them. Meanwhile, in 2019 the FDA approved Vascepa to reduce cardiovascular risk in hypertriglyceridemia patients taking statins, the CV indication. Amarin removed the earlier cardiovascular limitation and obtained two method-of-use patents covering that indication. 587.

Hikma supplemented its pending application with a section viii statement and a skinny label confined to the now-unpatented SH use. To mirror the brand label apart from the carved-out use, it also removed the old cardiovascular limitation. The FDA approved the application in 2020 and gave the generic an AB therapeutic-equivalence rating for use according to its labeling. 587–588.

## Litigation and allegations

Amarin sued in Delaware under 35 U.S.C. § 271(b), alleging inducement of infringement of the CV patents. It relied on the statements' cumulative effect across four sources. The label retained a study involving some statin users and omitted the old CV limitation. The patient leaflet warned of side effects for cardiovascular patients and noted that medicines can be prescribed for unlisted purposes. The website described the product as AB-rated and placed it in the broader hypertriglyceridemia category. Press releases called it generic Vascepa and cited sales figures including both SH and CV uses without emphasizing the limitation to SH. 588–589.

The district court dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6), finding no active encouragement. The Federal Circuit reversed: although the label alone was insufficient, a physician could plausibly understand the combined statements as instruction or encouragement to use the generic for patented indications. The Supreme Court reviewed the totality of the statements' sufficiency at the pleading stage. 589.

## Issue and holding

Did the complaint plausibly allege that Hikma actively encouraged infringement, rather than merely allege statements that someone might understand as encouragement?

**No.** The allegations, separately or together, did not plausibly show affirmative steps to encourage infringing use. The complaint therefore could not withstand the motion to dismiss. 592–596.

A § 271(b) claim requires direct third-party infringement, knowledge that the induced acts infringe, and active encouragement. The dispute before the Court concerned the third element; Hikma did not contest plausible pleading of the other two. 586–587, 590 n.2. This case is a pleading holding about the alleged conduct, not a trial finding that no doctor ever used the generic for the patented method.

## Reasoning

Twombly and Iqbal supplied the familiar distinction between possible misconduct and a reasonable inference of liability. The substantive element determined what needed to be plausible: affirmative, purposeful encouragement, not ordinary distribution or passive awareness of potential misuse. 589–590.

Amarin's proposed chain of possible influence focused on how medical providers might read the statements. But the relevant question was whether Hikma actively encouraged infringement. Vullo's standard for coercive government threats arose in a different substantive setting and could not replace § 271(b)'s active-inducement requirement. Grokster likewise concerned communications designed to stimulate unlawful conduct, a narrower category than statements that could conceivably have that effect. 590–591.

The Court also rejected Hikma's opposite overstatement: encouragement need not be express. An implicit message can qualify if it is clear to the relevant audience and affirmative. The opinion therefore establishes neither liability from any conceivable interpretation nor immunity for all indirect encouragement. 592.

**Compliance and normal practice:** Several statements had an obvious lawful explanation. Hikma generally had to copy the brand label apart from the carved-out use, accounting for the retained study and removed limitation. Truthfully describing a product as the generic equivalent was ordinary industry practice. The Court declined to turn those acts into an inducement claim on these allegations. The study itself concerned SH patients, the approved unpatented use, even though some participants took statins. 592–593 & judicial note at 593.

**Omissions:** The absence of an extra limitation or express press-release warning about the SH-only approval did not supply affirmative encouragement. The proposed inference rested on readers supplying meaning from inaction, far removed from purposeful assistance. 593–594.

**Vague statements:** A leaflet warning and disclaimer were implausibly indirect ways to promote infringement. A broad therapeutic category was not an instruction to use the drug for a protected method. AB equivalence concerned the generic's approved label conditions, and the website expressly indicated fewer approved uses than the reference drug. 594–595.

Finally, the sales figures required an attenuated sequence: a provider would need to read investor-directed releases, identify which uses contributed to the numbers, and infer encouragement to prescribe for the CV indication. The complaint supplied a possible scenario, not a plausible allegation of active inducement. 595.

## Exact disposition and course significance

The Court **reversed the Federal Circuit's judgment and remanded** for further proceedings consistent with the opinion. 596. It did not invalidate the remaining CV patents or announce blanket immunity for every generic with a skinny label.

The assigned passage connects substantive elements to pleading plausibility. Allegations must support the required active conduct; a conceivable response by a third party does not substitute for that element. At the same time, implicit affirmative encouragement remains legally possible.
