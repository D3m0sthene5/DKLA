# Lucas v. Hamm
56 Cal. 2d 583, 364 P.2d 685 (1961) | Contracts

## Court, judgment, and significance
The California Supreme Court decided this case on September 5, 1961. Chief Justice Gibson wrote for a unanimous court; Justices Traynor, Schauer, McComb, Peters, White, and Dooling concurred without separate writings. The court affirmed a dismissal after a general demurrer to the beneficiaries’ second amended complaint had been sustained without leave to amend. It recognized that intended will beneficiaries could pursue an attorney in negligence and as third-party beneficiaries of the attorney’s contract, but held that these plaintiffs had not pleaded actionable professional negligence or breach. Rehearing was denied October 4, 1961. (56 Cal. 2d at 583, 586, 595.)

## Facts and procedural posture
Some beneficiaries under Eugene H. Emmick’s will sued attorney L. S. Hamm, whom Emmick had retained for consideration to prepare a will and codicils. Their first two counts alleged that they were to receive 15 percent of the residue through a trust under paragraph Eighth. The trust would terminate at noon five years after the probate court entered its order distributing property to the trustee. They alleged that this language violated California’s then-applicable perpetuity and alienation restrictions. After the instruments were admitted to probate, Hamm allegedly advised the executors and beneficiaries that the trust was invalid and that they should settle with blood relatives. The beneficiaries alleged a $75,000 loss from the resulting settlement. These were allegations reviewed on demurrer, rather than damages established at trial. The former statutory provisions, reproduced in footnote 1, regulated suspension of alienation and vesting through distinct requirements. (Id. at 586–587 & n. 1.)

A third count concerned Hamm’s subsequent work for the executors in settling a will contest based on lack of testamentary capacity. The contestants, executors, and Hamm allegedly reached a tentative settlement, subject to court approval, under which the estate would pay $10,000 for appropriate releases. After the releases had been executed and transmitted to escrow, but before approval, other counsel allegedly warned Hamm about the trust’s perpetuity problem and requested releases of all claims against the estate. Hamm allegedly refused to modify the releases or bring those recommendations to the probate court’s attention. The court approved the compromise, the contestants received $10,000, and they later participated in an attack on the trust’s validity. The beneficiaries alleged that broader releases would have left them another $15,000. (Id. at 593–594.)

## Beneficiary standing in negligence
The attorney argued that the beneficiaries lacked contractual privity. The court rejected a categorical privity barrier and overruled Buckley v. Gray, which had denied will beneficiaries an action against the drafting attorney. Following Biakanja v. Irving, it evaluated whether liability to a nonclient was appropriate by considering the transaction’s intended effect, foreseeability of harm, certainty of injury, the connection between conduct and injury, and the policy of preventing future harm. The court also considered whether recognition of the duty would burden the legal profession unduly. (Id. at 587–589.)

A principal purpose of employing a lawyer to draft a will is to transfer the estate as the client directs. Failure to carry out that undertaking foreseeably injures the intended recipients. Ordinarily the estate suffers no corresponding loss from the intended beneficiaries’ failure to receive their gifts, so denying those recipients any remedy would impair the policy of preventing such harm. Recognizing their claim did not impose a greater standard of professional performance than the attorney already owed the client. The court therefore permitted liability despite the absence of privity; it did not make every disappointed recipient an automatic winner. (Id. at 588–589.)

## The attorney’s contract and Civil Code section 1559
The court separately recognized a contractual route. Section 1559 permits enforcement of a contract made expressly for a third person’s benefit. “Expressly” excludes merely incidental benefits; it does not require the contract to recite a formal declaration naming its beneficiary or require the promised services to be delivered directly to that person. Here the testator’s purpose in retaining the attorney was to benefit those designated in the will, and the attorney necessarily understood that undertaking. Preparing the instruments for the testator could therefore constitute performance of a contract for the intended recipients’ benefit. (Id. at 589–591.)

The court disapproved language in Smith v. Anglo-California Trust Co. and Fruitvale Canning Co. v. Cotton requiring the promisor personally to intend to confer the benefit insofar as that language conflicted with this conclusion. The relevant inquiry included the promisee’s beneficial purpose and the promisor’s understanding of the undertaking. The beneficiaries could invoke both contract and negligence principles, but neither theory relieved them of showing deficient performance. Footnote 2 also acknowledged the client’s usual ability to frame malpractice as either tort or contract. (Id. at 589–591 & n. 2.)

## Why the alleged drafting error did not state a breach
An attorney must exercise the skill, prudence, and diligence ordinarily possessed and exercised by lawyers. Without a special undertaking, an attorney is not an insurer of every opinion or result and is not liable for an error on a legal question that competent lawyers may reasonably regard as doubtful. The same performance standard applied to these contract and tort theories. (Id. at 591–592.)

The alleged defect arose in the exceptionally difficult interaction of perpetuity and alienation law, including California’s 1951 legislation. The trust’s five-year period ran from an order of distribution, so the claimed invalidity depended on the remote possibility that distribution would be delayed beyond a life in being plus sixteen years. Given that remote possibility, the state of the law, and the particular drafting error alleged, an ordinarily skilled lawyer could fail to recognize the danger without falling below the required standard. The court consequently held that these first two counts did not, and could not, state a cause of action on the alleged error. It upheld denial of leave to amend them. (Id. at 592–593.)

The court expressly did not decide that the trust was actually invalid. Nor did it resolve Hamm’s alternative contention that the complaint omitted facts needed to establish invalidity, including whether interests were contingent or whether beneficiaries acting together could terminate the trust within the statutory period. Footnote 3 preserves those questions. The holding concerns the pleaded professional-performance issue, rather than a definitive construction of the trust or a general immunity for mistakes about perpetuities. (Id. at 593 & n. 3.)

## Why the release count also failed
The releases admittedly settled the testamentary-capacity contest for which they were negotiated. The complaint did not allege that the contestants had agreed, or were willing, to relinquish other attacks on the will for the same $10,000. Without such allegations, the court treated that amount as consideration for settling the existing capacity contest. A more extensive waiver ordinarily would require additional payment, and the contestants’ own attorneys might violate their obligations by approving broader releases than their clients had agreed to provide. Hamm could not simply enlarge the negotiated settlement through release language. (Id. at 594–595.)

At most the pleaded circumstances supported a duty to request broader releases, but the plaintiffs did not allege that Hamm failed to ask for them. They also made no claim, despite the defendant’s identification of this defect below and on appeal, that they could amend to show he could have secured broader releases. The court thus found no abuse of discretion in denying further amendment and affirmed the dismissal of the third count as well. It awarded none of the alleged $75,000 or $15,000 losses. (Id. at 595.)
