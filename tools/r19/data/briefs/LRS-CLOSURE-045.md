# United States v. Richardson
418 U.S. 166 (1974) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and requested relief

Richardson wanted information about the Central Intelligence Agency’s expenditures. In 1967 he requested documents published under Article I, § 9, cl. 7, which requires a periodic statement and account of receipts and expenditures of public money. Treasury’s Bureau of Accounts supplied its Combined Statement and monthly and daily reports. Richardson then asked how the CIA Act’s special accounting provisions could be reconciled with the statement’s authenticity and how to obtain the missing CIA figures. The Bureau said it had no further information. Treasury also declined his request to seek the Attorney General’s opinion about the statute’s constitutionality. (167–169.)

Richardson sued as a taxpayer and alleged that the government was offering a fraudulent substitute for the required account. His complaint sought a permanent injunction preventing publication of the Combined Statement as fulfillment of the constitutional mandate until it actually complied. In practical terms, this attacked the CIA Act provision allowing expenditures to be accounted for solely on the Director’s certificate, 50 U.S.C. § 403j(b). He also described himself as a citizen and voter whose ability to assess government and select candidates depended on expenditure information. These were allegations of informational and political injury; the Court did not find misuse of CIA funds. (167–170, 175–177 & n. 1.)

## Proceedings, question, and judgment

The District Court dismissed for lack of standing under Flast v. Cohen and because the dispute presented political questions. It also declined initially to convene a three-judge court. The Third Circuit, sitting en banc with three judges dissenting, reversed and directed consideration by a three-judge court on remand. Its taxpayer theory was that the accounting provision was integrally connected to appropriations: Richardson could not challenge expenditures without knowing them. The Supreme Court noted that his complaint did not state an intention to bring the later appropriations challenge that this reasoning appeared to contemplate. (169–170 & nn. 2–3.)

The question presented on certiorari concerned standing. On June 25, 1974, Chief Justice Burger’s opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, Powell, and Rehnquist, held that Richardson lacked standing and **reversed the Third Circuit**. Powell separately concurred; Douglas and Brennan separately dissented; Stewart dissented with Marshall. The judgment was therefore 5–4. The Court did not decide the validity of the CIA accounting statute or exactly what the Statement and Account Clause requires. Note 3 also leaves unresolved a district judge’s participation in the en banc appellate court, a question outside the petition’s presentation. (166–167, 170 & n. 3, 174, 178 n. 11, 180.)

## The taxpayer nexus and the claimed voting injury

The majority read Flast as a narrow exception to Frothingham’s rejection of federal taxpayer litigation based on an indefinite interest in the public treasury. Flast requires both a connection between taxpayer status and a challenged congressional exercise of the taxing and spending power, and a connection between that status and a specific constitutional restriction on that power. It did not authorize taxpayers to litigate every objection to governmental conduct. The Article III requirement remained a personal stake sufficient for concrete adversity. (171–174 & nn. 4–5.)

Richardson failed that test because his challenge addressed the CIA’s accounting and reporting arrangements, rather than an exercise of taxing and spending power alleged to violate a specific limitation on that power. Wanting to compel information about expenditures did not itself establish the required nexus. The majority looked at the substantive claim only to perform that standing inquiry; it expressly declined to adjudicate the constitutional merits. Other agencies also had confidential reporting provisions, and Congress had combined wider disclosure through the Freedom of Information Act with continued protections for confidential material. Those observations supplied context, not a merits holding that any secrecy restriction was valid. (174–175 & nn. 6–8.)

The claimed inability to evaluate Congress, executive activity, and electoral candidates likewise did not supply a particular concrete injury. The majority regarded the asserted effect as common to the public and therefore a generalized grievance. Data Processing involved competitive economic harm within a specific statutory scheme; it did not establish standing merely from earnest interest in government. Levitt’s rejection of a citizen’s challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional judicial appointment illustrated that even an asserted violation of an express constitutional prohibition does not eliminate the plaintiff’s obligation to establish an appropriate injury. (176–178 & nn. 9–10.)

The possibility that nobody could bring this particular challenge did not change that result. For the majority, that possibility supported the place of congressional oversight and the electoral process in controlling the government’s accounting arrangements. A citizen could advocate political change even when Article III furnished no judicial remedy. This was the Court’s explanation for refusing standing, rather than an adjudication that the accounting practice complied with the Constitution. Note 11 discusses historical secrecy and Congress’s authority over reporting but expressly reserves the clause’s precise meaning; any congressional grant of citizen or taxpayer standing would remain subject to Article III. (178 n. 11, 179–180.)

## Powell’s concurrence

Powell agreed with the judgment and most of the majority’s reasoning but would abandon Flast’s two-part nexus analysis while retaining its settled result for Establishment Clause taxpayer cases. He thought the nexus formula neither measured concrete adversity nor clearly distinguished constitutional from prudential restrictions. This case exposed its weakness: Richardson’s commitment was evident, and the accounting clause could plausibly be understood as connected to spending, yet the formula excluded him. (180–185 & nn. 1–3.)

Powell rejected Stewart’s proposed distinction between governmental affirmative duties and prohibitions. Both could underpin an unlimited citizen action, and the grammatical form of a constitutional provision supplied no principled boundary. His broader concern was that unrestricted public actions would transfer supervision of the elected branches to an insulated judiciary, produce selective and uneven review based on who sued, and consume resources needed to protect individual and minority rights. His discussion of the rejected Council of Revision acknowledges limits to that historical analogy in note 9. He would preserve traditional prudential barriers and limit expansion to the results of Flast and Baker, while acknowledging the different situation presented by express statutory standing grants within Article III. These are Powell’s additional grounds, not an overruling of Flast by the Court. (185–197 & nn. 4–18.)

## The dissents

Douglas would affirm the appellate standing judgment. He considered access to expenditure information essential to the people’s sovereignty and electoral control, and read the history of the accounting clause as deliberately protecting the public against fiscal secrecy. A taxpayer’s attempt to obtain the account was, in his view, a concrete enforcement of that protection, not an abstract complaint about government. He contrasted the express constitutional allowance for secrecy in legislative journals with the accounting requirement. His position also invokes his separate dissent in the companion Schlesinger case. He did not treat political accountability as an adequate replacement for the constitutional disclosure protection. (197–202 & the substantive asterisk footnote at 200.)

Stewart, joined by Marshall, treated the claim as a conventional right-and-duty dispute: Richardson alleged a personal entitlement to information and a corresponding governmental affirmative obligation. Once the government denied that request, he should be able to litigate whether the obligation actually existed. Whether the duty ran to him, permitted secrecy exceptions, or presented a political question concerned later merits or justiciability issues. Flast’s nexus test, designed for challenges to taxing and spending legislation, did not answer this different claim. Stewart compared the asserted informational entitlement to a private obligation and to the FOIA request-and-denial framework; injury is not necessarily insufficient merely because many people share it. He also emphasized that political-question adjudication was outside the certiorari question and that the Solicitor General agreed the case should return to the District Court if standing existed. (202–207 & nn. 1–4.)

Brennan’s dissent, printed at 418 U.S. 235–238 in the companion Schlesinger report and expressly applicable to Richardson, separates injury in fact from reviewability and the merits. Richardson alleged denial of a personal informational right and impairment of his own ability to vote intelligently. Those good-faith allegations sufficed for standing even if he eventually lost on the existence or scope of the right. Brennan regarded expansion of Flast’s specialized formula to such claims as a source of doctrinal confusion. (235–238 & the applicability asterisk at 235.)

## Assigned scope and limits

The assigned November-b textbook PDF 111, printed 1168, contains an editorial account in note 2 about the CIA budget and informed voting. It accurately identifies the standing obstacle but compresses the judgment as dismissal; the Supreme Court’s actual decretal action was reversal of the appellate judgment. The adjoining textbook questions about statutory informational rights and generalized grievances are editorial analysis, not additional holdings of Richardson. This brief covers the complete 1974 Court decision and all separate writings, including Brennan’s separately located joint dissent. It does not certify the earlier Third Circuit judgment as a separately read decision or provide a current citator assessment.
