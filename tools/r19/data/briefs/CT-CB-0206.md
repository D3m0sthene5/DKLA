Parties
Edward B. Kincaid, the plaintiff, was a sixteen-year-old clerk employed by a banking firm. He
discovered a pocketbook and ultimately returned it to James Eaton, its owner and the
defendant. Kincaid sought the unpaid portion of an advertised reward. Eaton challenged the
plaintiff’s entitlement under the advertisement’s terms. The bank in which the pocketbook
was discovered was not a party; its position as the proper custodian of mislaid property
nevertheless controlled the analysis.
Material facts
On Wednesday, November 28, 1866, Eaton finished making a deposit at the Merchants’ Bank
in Boston and left as Kincaid entered to transact his employer’s banking business. After
completing his deposit, Kincaid saw a pocketbook on a desk outside the tellers’ counters,
near a doorway. The desk was supplied for bank customers’ use. Kincaid thought it belonged
to the man who had just left and tried unsuccessfully to catch him. The location mattered: the
court treated the object as something its owner had voluntarily placed on a convenient desk,
rather than something accidentally dropped and lost. Casebook pp. 206–207; Wells opinion.
Kincaid took the pocketbook to his employers’ office. It contained the owner’s name,
promissory notes totaling $1,000, a $100 United States bond, and lottery tickets representing
a $105 payment. He put it in his employers’ safe and repeatedly visited Eaton’s place of
business, but Eaton was out of town. Thursday was a holiday. On Friday, after further
unsuccessful visits, Kincaid left the pocketbook with a teller at the Merchants’ Bank at about
one o’clock, asking that it be delivered to whoever inquired. Eaton had inquired at the bank
approximately an hour earlier, although the report did not establish whom he had asked.
Casebook p. 206.
At noon that Friday, an advertisement first appeared offering $110 to the finder who
returned the lost pocketbook and its contents to the Transcript newspaper office. Kincaid
learned of it at about two o’clock. He visited the newspaper office, obtained Eaton’s home
address, and was advised to take the pocketbook there. He retrieved it from the teller and
personally returned it to Eaton. Thus Kincaid had acted both before and after learning of the

Eaton first produced a roll of bills and told Kincaid to help himself. Kincaid requested the

payment. Casebook p. 207.
Procedural history and posture
The action was tried in the Superior Court before Putnam, J. Eaton requested a ruling that
Kincaid could not recover on the evidence. The judge refused and instructed to the contrary;
the jury returned a verdict for Kincaid. Eaton alleged exceptions. The Supreme Judicial Court
reviewed whether the stated facts gave the plaintiff a legal entitlement to the reward. This
was review after a verdict, not a motion testing an unproved complaint. Casebook pp.
206–207.
Issue
Did a person who discovered an owner’s pocketbook on a customer-use desk inside a bank
qualify as the “finder” entitled to earn the advertised reward by returning it, when property
law treated the bank’s occupants as the proper custodians of an article voluntarily left there?
Rule and holding
No. The reward, as the court construed its terms, was payable to someone who had acquired
the special possessory rights belonging to the finder of a lost article. Discovering an article
voluntarily laid down by its owner within a bank, on a desk provided for its customers, did not
give Kincaid those rights. The bank’s occupants were the proper depositaries. Because
Kincaid did not satisfy the qualifying condition attached to the reward, he had no right to
retain his verdict. 98 Mass. 139, Wells opinion.
Actual judgment
The court sustained Eaton’s exceptions and held that Kincaid could not retain the verdict.
The reported disposition says “Exceptions sustained.” It does not award damages to Eaton,
announce a theft judgment against Kincaid, or expressly specify the detailed terms of a new
trial. Those additional consequences should not be inserted into the brief.
Reasoning
The promise identified a legally qualified class of recipients
The advertisement did more than promise money for the physical act of placing the
pocketbook in Eaton’s hands. It addressed the finder. Wells interpreted that category by
reference to the possessory rights attached to finding a lost article. Kincaid’s effort, good
intentions, and ultimate success in returning the property did not establish that he belonged
to the category the court understood the offer to designate. The decision therefore combines
interpretation of a reward offer with an antecedent property-law classification. Wells opinion.

The location supported custody by the premises’ occupants
The pocketbook lay on a desk provided for people doing business at the bank. The court
treated it as voluntarily placed and left there. On that classification, the occupants of the
banking house, rather than a subsequent visitor who noticed the object, were the proper
custodians. The opinion cited prior lost-property authorities rather than creating a new
consideration test for rewards. In modern study terminology, the distinction resembles the
division between lost and mislaid property; the court’s own operative description was an
article voluntarily laid down by its owner. Wells opinion.
Returning the object did not cure failure to satisfy the offer’s qualification
Kincaid’s physical delivery accomplished what Eaton wanted in an everyday sense, but the
court treated the reward’s eligibility condition as unsatisfied. That explains why the result
was unfavorable despite the unusually diligent efforts detailed in the report. A statement that
the boy “did not perform” would be misleading unless it explained that he returned the
pocketbook but did not return it in the legally qualifying capacity required by the court’s
reading of the offer.
Separate opinions
No separate opinion is reported. Wells’s short opinion states the court’s reasoning. The
substantially longer factual narrative in the assigned report should not be mistaken for an
additional judge’s opinion.
Limits and study use
The case does not hold that all reward claimants must know the offer when they first discover
an object. It does not decide that the newspaper-office delivery term was violated, that
Kincaid’s age prevented contracting, or that taking $10 necessarily settled the claim. Those
facts could provoke classroom questions, but the stated basis of decision is the absence of the
finder’s qualifying property interest.
For an offer-and-acceptance analysis, identify the promised reward, the requested act, and
the eligible offeree before deciding whether performance constitutes acceptance. The case is
especially useful for showing how background law can shape the meaning of an apparently
ordinary contractual word. Its fairness is contestable, but a criticism that the court construed
the recipient class too narrowly should remain a criticism of the holding rather than a
substitute for accurately stating it.
