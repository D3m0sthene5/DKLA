# Recker v. Gustafson
279 N.W.2d 744 (Iowa 1979) | Contracts

## Court, procedural stage, and review

The Iowa Supreme Court decided this merits appeal on May 30, 1979, in an opinion by Justice McGiverin. The case was considered by Presiding Justice LeGrand and Justices Rees, Uhlenhopp, McCormick, and McGiverin; no separate writing or dissent is reported. The district court ordered specific performance of an oral farm-sale agreement at a modified $300,000 price. The sellers appealed, denying an enforceable contract, and the buyers cross-appealed, seeking enforcement of the original $290,000 bargain. The Supreme Court’s review was de novo, giving weight to trial findings, particularly credibility findings, without being bound by them. An earlier appeal, reported at 271 N.W.2d 738, had been dismissed as premature because a posttrial motion to enlarge findings remained unresolved. That earlier procedural decision is a distinct stage, not the merits disposition briefed here. (744–748.)

## Original transaction, later price demand, and attempted withdrawal

Alvin and Irene Gustafson owned as tenants in common a 160-acre Fayette County farm, including a five-acre site containing their home and other buildings. The interested buyers were Loran and Mary Recker. At trial Alvin was seventy-two and Loran twenty-three; Alvin had been a licensed real-estate broker for twenty years, although inactive for ten. On August 30, 1976, the couples met in the office of the sellers’ lawyer, Hofmeyer. The buyers had no attorney in those negotiations. According to testimony credited by the court, they agreed to sell the acreage outside the retained building site for $290,000, with a $40,000 down payment upon possession on March 1, 1977. Annual $28,000 installments included principal and interest, with interest at seven-and-one-half percent and a final balloon payment by March 1, 1987. There would be no deficiency upon forfeiture. They also agreed on taxes, merchantable title, a warranty deed, settlement at a bank, and the buyers’ ownership as tenants in common. (746.)

The sellers would retain the five-acre site until giving notice of sale, after which the buyers would have forty-five days to buy it for $30,000 plus subsequent improvements. Alvin and Loran measured and agreed on its boundaries. Alvin supplied a diagram to his lawyer for preparation of a formal description. Loran tendered a $5,000 check marked as earnest money in Irene’s presence; Alvin accepted and deposited it in their joint account. Hofmeyer testified that the farm had been sold. The parties contemplated a later signed standard-form contract, but Irene instructed Hofmeyer not to finish it. No written contract was ever completed or signed. (746–747.)

A second meeting on September 20 produced two changes: the price for the land outside the building site increased by $10,000, and the fixed-price purchase right on the retained site became a right of first refusal at a price selected by the sellers. Before the buyers accepted those less favorable terms, Hofmeyer warned that the sellers would go to court to escape the first agreement and that litigation would be expensive. He said the sellers would perform if the changes were accepted. Other terms remained unchanged, and the $5,000 continued to count toward the down payment. On September 28, however, the lawyer advised that the sellers had decided not to sell and enclosed a $5,000 cashier’s check. The buyers sued on October 21 for specific performance of the August bargain. The report does not separately establish that they cashed the returned check. (747.)

## Statute of frauds and formation of the oral contract

Section 622.32 covered creation or transfer of land interests, but Iowa treated it as a rule restricting competent oral proof rather than a rule making every oral land contract intrinsically void. Section 622.33 excepted transactions in which the vendor received purchase money or any portion of it. A check accepted as part of the purchase price qualified under the cited precedents; the $5,000 tender, acceptance, deposit, and application to the price met that exception. (748–749.)

Irene argued that only Alvin had physically received and deposited the check. The court rejected that defense because she had actively participated in the negotiations and the funds went into an account jointly owned by her and Alvin. Physical endorsement alone did not determine ownership. Both vendors had received part of the price for purposes of the statutory exception. This was a decision on the joint account and participation shown here, not a categorical conclusion that every payment to one spouse binds the other. (749–750.)

The buyers still had to prove the agreement by a preponderance of clear, satisfactory, and convincing evidence. Definite terms can form an oral contract even when a writing is contemplated, unless the parties intend to be bound only upon execution. The court considered the transaction’s type, size, detail, and formality. Although land sales are usually written and the price was large, the conduct supported immediate obligation: Alvin accepted earnest money, measured the retained site, told Loran’s mother that the farm had been purchased, and took steps to terminate the existing tenant’s lease. The material duties were sufficiently certain. The court judged what the parties actually did, not whether prudent parties would have insisted on a signed document. It held that the August negotiations formed an oral contract. (750–751.)

## Homestead limitation and the diagram

The purchase-money exception did not eliminate the separate requirements of § 561.13 for a married owner’s homestead. That section required both spouses to execute the same joint instrument. Cases enforcing oral transfers of a homestead after possession and control were relinquished depended on abandonment of the homestead right. The Gustafsons had not surrendered possession of their home, so that exception did not apply. Both the original fixed-price right and the later first-refusal provision concerning the retained homestead were invalid without the required writing. (751–753.)

The remaining land could nevertheless be conveyed in part at the buyers’ election. Although the homestead statute ordinarily permitted a rural claim of up to forty acres, the sellers had represented and marked only the five-acre building site as their homestead. They were estopped, against these buyers, from expanding that claim to defeat enforcement of the remaining 155 acres. Footnote 2 quotes the statutory maximum and its low-value exception; the court did not find that exception enlarged this particular homestead. (752–753 & n.2.)

Exhibit E made the site’s boundaries ascertainable. Footnote 1 rejected attorney-client privilege despite the trial court’s contrary observation: the diagram depicted dimensions already shown to and approved by Loran, so the communication was not confidential. An otherwise unprivileged writing does not become privileged merely by delivery to counsel. The court could therefore use the diagram and record to identify the retained tract and subtract it from the farm. (746–747 & n.1; 753, 759.)

## Modification, rescission, and consideration

The September agreement recognized the original earnest-money payment and thus also escaped § 622.32’s evidentiary bar. But eliminating that bar did not supply consideration for the price increase. The court found the September transaction a modification, not an actual rescission followed by a new bargain. The sellers were already obliged to sell the nonhomestead land, and no new consideration was shown or claimed for the added $10,000. Consent to changed terms alone did not answer that problem. (753–755, 759.)

The court carefully distinguished mutual rescission from modification. Actual cancellation discharges existing reciprocal rights and duties, allowing parties to bargain anew; modification leaves the original obligation in force except as altered. Its historical survey explained that Iowa cases had blurred these categories. Lamb’s Estate, O’Dell, and Siebring contained language dispensing with consideration for modification based on reasoning associated with mutual release or fictional rescission. The court reaffirmed its earlier consideration requirement and declared contrary language in those cases no longer controlling. It did not invalidate all genuine rescissions or hold that a new service contract must include performance different from a truly terminated one. (754–759.)

The court also considered the draft Restatement’s fair-and-equitable modification principle for circumstances not anticipated when the contract was made. It declined to apply that principle because no unanticipated circumstance prompted this price increase; the demand appeared to reflect a desire for more money. It expressly left room for the Restatement approach in a future appropriate case. Thus the result is not a categorical rejection of that approach. Nor did the court decide the case by declaring the lawyer guilty of duress or professional misconduct: the operative conclusion was lack of consideration for this modification. (758–759.)

## Specific performance and the detailed disposition

Real estate presumptively possesses the uniqueness required for specific performance. The court enforced the August contract for the 155 acres outside the homestead at $290,000 rather than the modified $300,000. It adjusted performance to account for elapsed time. Possession would occur sixty days after issuance of procedendo, with the remaining $35,000 of the $40,000 down payment then payable and the original $5,000 credited. The March 1978 and March 1979 $28,000 installments were deferred until possession; subsequent annual installments commenced March 1, 1980, while the final balloon date remained March 1, 1987. Contract interest began upon actual delivery of possession. (759.)

The sellers had to account for rentals from March 1, 1977 until possession. They paid real-estate taxes due in 1977; the buyers paid or accounted for later taxes. Amounts owing on the accounting bore legal interest only from possession. The sellers were to supply an abstract showing merchantable title and execute a warranty deed upon completion of payments. The five-acre homestead remained excluded, and forfeiture would carry no deficiency judgment. (759–760.)

The decree was affirmed as modified on both appeal and cross-appeal, with the case returned to district court for a conforming decree and further proceedings. Appellate costs were taxed to the sellers. This was not an unqualified affirmance of the trial court’s $300,000 bargain, an order immediately conveying the five-acre home, or a denial of all relief because no document was signed. The reporter notes denial of rehearing on July 20, 1979 without reproducing a separate rehearing opinion. (744, 760.)
