# In re Certified Question (Bankey v. Storer Broadcasting Co.)
432 Mich. 438, 443 N.W.2d 112 (1989) | Contracts

## Court and certified posture
Justice Griffin wrote the Michigan Supreme Court's June 6, 1989 majority opinion, joined by Chief Justice Riley and Justices Brickley and Cavanagh. Justice Boyle concurred in the result in an opinion joined by Justice Archer. Justice Levin wrote separately, expressing substantial agreement with the majority's conclusion while questioning its protection of employee expectations and the court's constitutional authority to answer certified questions. The court answered the Sixth Circuit's question affirmatively: a generally applicable written discharge-for-cause policy based on legitimate expectations could be changed to at-will employment without an advance reservation of amendment power, provided reasonable notice of the change was uniformly given to affected employees. It did not affirm or reverse Bankey's federal judgment or decide whether Storer's actual notice was adequate. (441, 457–458, 461–462.)

## Employment and federal litigation
The Sixth Circuit's certification supplied the pertinent facts. Kenneth Bankey worked thirteen years as a salesman for Storer Broadcasting and was discharged on March 23, 1981 for asserted poor performance. In July 1982 he sued in Michigan state court, alleging a longstanding just-cause policy on which he had relied. Storer removed on diversity grounds. A 1980 Personnel Policy Digest said that an employee could be discharged for cause; the January 1981 replacement instead declared employment at the company's will. Following Storer's motion for a directed verdict at the close of Bankey's case, the district court ruled that the 1980 digest created a for-cause employment obligation under Toussaint that could not unilaterally be altered for existing employees. The jury awarded Bankey $55,000. Storer appealed, producing the certified question. These facts came from the certification, rather than a state-court examination of the full trial record; the second majority note expressly limits the record before it. (441–442; Part I & n.2.)

The question specifically assumed an obligation arising through employees' legitimate expectations grounded in written policy statements, not an individually negotiated express promise. It asked whether an employer could later adopt a generally applicable at-will policy without having announced its right to do so at the outset. The third majority note defines the handbook exception in terms of workforce-wide or classification-wide policies, rather than an individual employee's terms. (441–443; Part II & n.3.)

## Toussaint and competing formation theories
Toussaint recognized two paths to enforceable for-cause protection: express agreement, whether oral or written, and legitimate expectations generated by employer policies. Toussaint's facts also involved oral assurances and a manual, and no policy revocation preceded the termination. It therefore did not settle how long a policy-based expectation remained legitimate after an employer changed the policy. Its discussion contemplated unilateral amendment, while other language suggested employers could announce a reservation of amendment rights. Bankey addressed that unresolved policy-change issue. (443–445.)

Storer proposed a unilateral-contract explanation: continued work after a change accepted a new offer and supplied consideration. The majority found that reasoning inadequate for the question presented. Characterizing each day's work as completion of a new contract might support it, but would artificially portray ordinary ongoing employment. Bankey argued that modification of an existing obligation required mutual assent. That position also failed to explain an obligation which, under the legitimate-expectations branch of Toussaint, could arise without mutual agreement that the policy created contractual rights. The court did not reject unilateral-contract analysis for all employment arrangements; it declined to make it the foundation for this certified answer. (445–448, 453.)

The court reviewed other jurisdictions' conflicting approaches to handbook promises and amendments. Some relied on unilateral contracts or individual reliance; others allowed policy withdrawal or required consideration and acceptance. The discussion distinguished handbook enforcement from an immutable commitment. The eleventh majority note refused to determine the general application of Michigan's statutory contract-modification provision to employment contracts, holding only that it did not apply to discharge-for-cause obligations arising solely under Toussaint. The sixteenth note also acknowledged that Missouri's highest court had subsequently rejected the handbook exception discussed in an earlier Missouri appellate decision. These are limitations on the comparative account, not declarations of uniform national law. (448–453; Parts III–IV & nn.11, 16.)

## Benefit, uniformity, and reasonable notice
The majority grounded policy enforcement in the benefit an employer obtained by cultivating an orderly, loyal, cooperative workforce and a climate of fair treatment. That collective benefit could create an obligation without ordinary individualized assent or detrimental reliance. The obligation required consistent application of the official policy while in force, but did not make every policy permanent. Withdrawal of the for-cause policy also withdrew the corresponding benefit and rationale for its enforcement. A protection of limited duration still had value, as finite collective-bargaining protections illustrated. (453–455.)

Requiring renegotiation with every employee could produce multiple conflicting policy regimes depending on hiring date and handbook version, sacrificing uniformity. It could also tie employers indefinitely to outdated rules because they had not anticipated Toussaint and reserved amendment authority. The majority accordingly permitted changes applicable to the entire workforce or particular classifications. It expressly disclaimed approval of bad-faith manipulation, such as temporarily suspending a for-cause policy to fire one targeted employee. Reasonable notice had to be uniformly communicated to affected employees before revocation became effective. Prior notice reserving power to amend and reasonable notice of the actual amendment are distinct: only the former was unnecessary. (455–457.)

The seventeenth majority note reserves a different possible answer for employee benefits already vested or accrued, such as pensions or severance. The answer was confined to changing the prospective termination policy specified in the question, rather than permitting forfeiture of earned benefits or rewriting all express employment contracts. (Part V & n.17.)

## Separate writings
Boyle, joined by Archer, agreed with the affirmative answer and the reasonable-notice condition. She emphasized that the pure legitimate-expectations branch rested on the court's common-law authority to recognize enforceable noncontractual obligations. Traditional contract theory was therefore inappropriate for the assumed situation, and the court could require uniformly given reasonable notice. (458.)

Levin agreed that employers could change policies, but argued that employees who had served significant time under a for-cause policy might retain legitimate expectations deserving additional relief. His second note distinguishes an independently made express employment promise, which a later policy change could not alter merely by changing the manual. His third note questions whether uniform notice sufficiently protected a long-serving middle-aged employee compared with a recent entrant. The nature of additional relief had not been briefed and could not be decided abstractly. Those concerns and possible remedies belong to his separate opinion, rather than the majority's holding. (458–461; Levin Parts I–II & nn.2–5.)

Levin also questioned whether a response that could neither determine the controversy nor be enforced by the state court constituted judicial power under Michigan's Constitution. Federal courts were not subordinate state courts; state superintending control and writ powers did not supply the answer. He examined declaratory-judgment and advisory-opinion precedents, distinguishing binding adjudication from an abstract response whose effect remained for the federal courts. He acknowledged prior participation in certifications and the United States Supreme Court's encouragement of the practice, but urged further consideration of the state rule. The court nevertheless answered the question; it did not adopt his suggested jurisdictional objection as a holding or dismiss for lack of jurisdiction. (462–471; Levin Part III & nn.7–27.)

## Exact result and assigned scope
The disposition was an affirmative certified answer with a reasonable-notice requirement, leaving application to Bankey's federal proceedings. It was not a final ruling that Bankey lost his $55,000 award. The assigned “Administrative Law Model?” account spans C&M physical PDFs 141–142, printed 95–96; both complete scans and native passages were read. Its summary emphasizes consistent application but omits the separate requirement of reasonable notice of the actual change. The quoted collective-productivity rationale explains enforcement of desirable policies, rather than establishing that replacing them necessarily promoted productivity. The full majority, Boyle concurrence, Levin separate opinion, all seventeen majority notes and all twenty-seven Levin notes were read. This brief covers the 1989 decision and does not certify current Michigan law. Separate source review is tracked in the accompanying register.
