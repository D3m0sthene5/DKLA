# Arrington v. Burger King Worldwide, Inc.
47 F.4th 1247 (11th Cir. 2022) | Contracts

## Court and posture
Judge Rosenbaum wrote the Eleventh Circuit's August 31, 2022 opinion for a unanimous panel with Judges Wilson and Hull. The panel reversed a dismissal of employees' Sherman Act claim insofar as it rested on the absence of concerted action, and remanded for further proceedings. The employees plausibly alleged that independently operated Burger King restaurants joined otherwise independent hiring decisions through a no-hire agreement. The court did not decide whether that restraint was unreasonable, select the governing antitrust mode of analysis, certify a class, or award relief. References below use the complete official opinion's slip pages, including all four notes. (Slip 2, 18.)

## Franchise system and alleged restraint
The defendants were Restaurant Brands International, its subsidiary Burger King Worldwide, and Worldwide's subsidiary Burger King Corporation, collectively called Burger King in the opinion. More than 99 percent of Burger King restaurants worldwide were independently owned franchises; the corporation operated about fifty United States restaurants, all around Miami. A standard franchise generally lasted twenty years, carried a $50,000 fee, and required an initial investment of approximately $323,000 to $3.1 million. Franchisees also paid royalties of up to 4.5 percent of gross sales and an advertising contribution of four percent. These common financial and brand arrangements provided context, but did not answer whether particular hiring decisions came from independent economic actors. (Slip 5–6.)

From at least 2010 through at least September 13, 2018, the standard contract contained an “Interference with Employment Relations of Others” provision. Burger King Corporation and each franchisee agreed not to entice another's employees to leave, or employ them within six months after termination of that employment, unless the former employer supplied prior written consent. Thus the actual provision included a consent exception. A violator owed enforcement costs and attorney's fees, and the corporation could terminate a franchise for a default, including breach of this restriction. Burger King purportedly removed the language from new agreements beginning in September 2018, but the employees alleged that thousands of restaurants remained subject to older agreements. (Slip 6–7 & n.4.)

Jarvis Arrington had worked as an Illinois franchise's line cook until August 2017. Geneva Blanchard had worked for a New Orleans franchise since 2013. Sandra Munster had worked fifteen years for another Illinois franchise, advancing from supervisor to general manager. They alleged that the agreement prevented moves among Burger King restaurants and depressed wages, reduced benefits, and restricted mobility. These were pleading-stage allegations, not findings that the clause actually caused a particular loss. The first note expressly warns that the actual facts might differ from the allegations accepted on dismissal review. (Slip 5 n.1, 7–8.)

## Contractual independence and procedural history
The franchise agreement classified franchisees as independent contractors, rather than agents, partners, joint venturers, joint employers, or employees of the corporation, and disclaimed a fiduciary relationship. Franchisees had to identify their independent ownership in public records, business materials, and restaurant notices. More directly pertinent, each franchisee was solely responsible for its employees and could independently hire, discipline, promote, discharge, and set wages, hours, benefits, and employment policies without corporate approval. Burger King's website likewise said franchise employment terms varied and were determined solely by the franchisee. (Slip 8–9.)

A franchise covered one restaurant at a specified site and provided no protected territory or customer base. The disclosure document warned of competition from other franchisees, corporate restaurants, distribution channels, and other brands controlled by Restaurant Brands. The employees alleged that the restaurants otherwise competed for labor but collectively removed part of that competition through the no-hire agreement. Burger King moved to dismiss. The district court considered Burger King and each franchisee one economic enterprise incapable of conspiring under Sherman Act § 1. Having rejected that first element, it did not reach unreasonable restraint or restraint of interstate or foreign trade. It also denied leave to amend as futile. The court of appeals reviewed dismissal de novo under the plausibility standard, accepting nonconclusory allegations favorably to the employees. (Slip 9–11.)

## Governing inquiry and competing positions
Section 1 scrutinizes concerted action among separate actors; the Sherman Act treats unilateral conduct differently. Under Copperweld and American Needle, the relevant inquiry concerns economic substance rather than legal form. Separate incorporation alone does not establish concerted action. The question is whether an agreement joins independent decisionmakers pursuing distinct economic interests and thereby removes independent centers of decisionmaking from the marketplace. The inquiry focuses on the challenged decision, rather than classifying all activities of an enterprise together. (Slip 12–14.)

American Needle illustrated that distinction. NFL teams shared interests in promoting their league, yet retained separate economic interests when licensing their own intellectual property. Their common licensing arrangement could therefore involve concerted action. Common interests in some activities did not extinguish the independent interests affected by the specific arrangement. Likewise, Burger King invoked the unity of its franchise enterprise; the employees relied on separately owned restaurants' independent employment authority and competing recruitment interests. (Slip 14–16.)

## Application to hiring decisions
The panel found the employees' position plausible. The contract's independent-contractor language supported the analysis, but the express allocation of hiring and employment decisions was particularly significant. Franchisees could separately determine compensation and employment policies. The complaint and proposed amendment also described different recruitment approaches: one franchise advertised managerial bonuses including temporary use of a Jeep Wrangler or Chevrolet Camaro; another emphasized competitive wages, benefits, and scheduling; a third accepted applications through Burger King's website rather than its own. These allegations illustrated possible independent choices; the appeal did not establish that each recruitment representation was true. (Slip 16–17.)

Absent the agreement, independently operated restaurants could pursue their own economic interests by offering different terms or enticing employees away from competitors. The restriction joined those potential hiring choices and reduced independent decisionmaking. That was sufficient to plead concerted action. It did not establish every other element of an antitrust violation, nor hold that franchise businesses necessarily act in concert for every purpose. (Slip 17–18.)

## Exact result and assigned scope
Burger King asked the panel to affirm on the alternative ground that any restraint was reasonable. Doing so would have required selecting per se, quick-look, or rule-of-reason analysis and applying it. The panel expressly left those questions to the district court in the first instance. Its exact disposition reversed the dismissal to the extent based on the concerted-action element and remanded. (Slip 18.)
