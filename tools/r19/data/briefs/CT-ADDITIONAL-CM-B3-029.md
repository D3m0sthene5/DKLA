# Market Street Associates Limited Partnership v. Frey — judgment after remand
817 F. Supp. 784 (E.D. Wis. 1993) | Contracts

## Court, distinct stage, and result
Senior District Judge Reynolds issued findings of fact and conclusions of law after a bench trial on April 6, 1993, in the Eastern District of Wisconsin. He held that Market Street’s general partner, William Orenstein, knowingly exploited the pension trust’s misunderstanding during contractual performance, breaching the duty of good faith. He denied specific performance and ordered judgment for the trust and its five trustees, dismissing the action on the merits. This was the trial judgment after the Seventh Circuit’s 1991 reversal and remand, not that earlier appellate ruling or another summary judgment. (817 F. Supp. at 784–786, 788.)

## Sale-leaseback and purchase option
On January 15, 1968, J.C. Penney sold four properties to the General Electric Pension Trust and leased them back for twenty-five-year initial terms, with up to six five-year extensions. One property was a West Allis, Wisconsin retail store and warehouse. On October 30, 1987, Penney assigned the four leaseholds to Market Street, having advised the trust before the assignment became effective. Market Street borrowed to finance that acquisition. Orenstein, its general partner and manager, had worked in real estate since 1953 and had substantial financing and improvement experience. (Id. at 785 & n. 1.)

Paragraph 34 permitted the lessee to request financing for additional improvements costing at least $250,000, with reasonable detail of estimated costs. The lessor promised reasonable consideration and good-faith negotiations. If the parties could not agree on financing terms for qualifying improvements, the lessee could elect within sixty days to repurchase at the lessor’s unamortized cost plus specified accrued interest. Market Street sought conveyance for $216,336.13 plus interest and $658,590 in monetary damages. Those figures describe requested relief, not an award or finding that the property’s market value was that amount. (Id. at 785.)

The district court had entered summary judgment for the trust on October 3, 1990, as the original report prints. The Seventh Circuit reversed because the evidence had not been construed favorably enough for nonmovant Market Street and because Orenstein’s state of mind required live testimony. The reported 1993 findings followed that trial. The prior remand did not itself establish dishonest intent. (Id. at 786.)

## Financing correspondence and knowledge
In spring 1988, Market Street negotiated with drugstore chain Phar-Mor for a West Allis addition exceeding 50,000 square feet. Market Street wanted to buy the property because ownership would simplify financing and eventual sale. Orenstein expected obtaining financing from the trust to be difficult: the deal was relatively small and he was unsure how the trust would assess Phar-Mor’s credit. The trust directed him to investment manager David Erb. After five calls in May and early June, Orenstein’s June 8 letter sought discussion of purchasing the property and asked Erb to review his file. Eventually analyst Gregory Fletcher quoted a sale price of $3–$3.1 million on June 29. Orenstein considered that inflated and viewed the trust as unresponsive. (Id. at 786.)

Orenstein’s July 28 letter described the proposed Phar-Mor addition, estimated at $2 million, and requested discussion of financing. It did not mention the lease or paragraph 34, and he made no follow-up telephone calls about this financing inquiry. He anticipated that the trust would respond with reference to the paragraph. But the trust screened hundreds of proposals through general investment criteria, routinely rejecting deals below $10 million and avoiding construction loans. It treated this request accordingly and sent an August 10 form rejection, received August 17, saying that the proposal did not meet current investment criteria. After receiving it, Orenstein discussed with his son the possibility that the trust did not know he was proceeding under paragraph 34. (Id. at 786–787.)

On August 16, before receiving that rejection, Orenstein had sent a second letter asking whether the trust would provide financing under the lease, without identifying paragraph 34. He explained at trial that his office avoided specific provision numbers for fear of citing the wrong one or overlooking others. On August 22, he acknowledged the crossed letters, accepted that the trust was not interested, and said Market Street had opened negotiations with its original lenders. He still did not identify the option provision. He testified that this created a file record of seeking other financing because of the trust’s action and that he feared the trust might offer financing later. (Id. at 787.)

Only the September 27 letter, prepared by counsel to exercise the purchase option, identified paragraph 34. Erb testified that he had not known of the paragraph and the trust had not reviewed it until that letter arrived. Orenstein said he believed that the trust must have read the lease when offering a sale price, but the court’s ultimate finding distinguished his initial assumption from his subsequent realization that it had not evaluated financing under the paragraph. (Id. at 787–788.)

## Later negotiations and legal determination
Erb met Orenstein in Milwaukee on December 15 and offered to discuss financing or sale while declining to debate the lease’s legal merits. Possibilities included purchase of Market Street’s interests, purchase of the trust’s fee for $3.1 million, and financing. Orenstein refused financing discussions because Market Street had made other commitments. Shortly before the proposed closing, the trust refused to close, asserting that the purchase option was personal to Penney and could not be assigned. The reported conclusions did not decide the case on that asserted nontransferability ground. (Id. at 788.)

Exercising diversity jurisdiction under the then-applicable amount threshold and applying Wisconsin substantive law, the judge invoked the implied duty of good faith in every contract. The Seventh Circuit had framed the decisive question as whether Market Street tried to trick the trust and succeeded, exploiting its contracting partner’s mistake during performance. The trial findings answered that question against Market Street. Orenstein initially expected lease review, then recognized that the form rejection reflected general investment screening rather than paragraph 34 consideration. He nevertheless accepted the rejection in ambiguous correspondence without alerting the trust until exercising the discounted option. That knowing sequence, rather than silence or superior knowledge alone, constituted bad-faith performance. (Id. at 788.)

The court concluded that Market Street was not entitled to specific performance. Its operative order went further than an interlocutory denial of that remedy: it directed entry of judgment for the trust and all five named trustees and dismissal of the action on the merits. It awarded neither the discounted conveyance nor the monetary damages requested. There were no separate writings and no additional judicial notes beyond the lease-exhibit note. (Id. at 785 n. 1, 788.)
