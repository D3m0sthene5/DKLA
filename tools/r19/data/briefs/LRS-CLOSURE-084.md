# Dolan v. United States Postal Service — Third Circuit
377 F.3d 285 (3d Cir. 2004) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and litigation chronology

Barbara Dolan alleged that a Postal Service employee negligently left letters, packages, and periodicals on her porch. On August 25, 2001, she fell over that mail and suffered serious injuries. The case concerned an injury to a person caused by the placement of delivered mail, rather than a claim that the mail itself was lost, late, or damaged. After submitting the administrative claim required by 28 U.S.C. § 2675, Dolan received a denial on April 18, 2002. She filed her Federal Tort Claims Act complaint in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania on October 15, 2002, naming the United States and the Postal Service. Her husband, Michael, asserted loss of consortium. (286.)

The government moved under Rule 12(b)(1) on February 7, 2003. The Dolans conceded that the United States was the only proper defendant but opposed dismissal. On March 19, the district court held that the FTCA’s postal exception, § 2680(b), barred Barbara’s claim and dismissed Michael’s derivative claim. On appeal, the Dolans conceded that Michael had not timely exhausted administrative remedies. The question actually presented was therefore whether Barbara’s claim had properly been dismissed for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The Third Circuit reviewed that determination plenarily. (286–287.)

Chief Judge Anthony J. Scirica and Judges Jane R. Roth and Theodore A. McKee formed the panel. Roth wrote the unanimous August 2, 2004 opinion; there are no separate writings. This is the circuit judgment that the Supreme Court later reversed, a distinct decision from Dolan, 546 U.S. 481 (2006). Its reasoning must be understood as the position the Third Circuit took in 2004. (285–286; assigned printed 65.)

## Statutory issue and competing arguments

The FTCA generally waives sovereign immunity for negligence by federal employees acting within the scope of their duties. Section 2680(b) excepts claims arising from the loss, miscarriage, or negligent transmission of letters or postal matter. Dolan argued that negligent transmission meant postal loss, delay, or damage in transit, rather than negligently placing mail where it injured a recipient. Her interpretation would preserve immunity for mishandling postal contents while permitting an ordinary personal-injury claim arising from delivery. (287.)

Dolan invoked United States v. Yellow Cab Co. for the FTCA’s general waiver in favor of injured persons. She also relied on legislative history described in Suchomajcz v. United States: Congress sought to protect the government against the volume of claims associated with unavoidable mishaps in delivering immense quantities of mail. In her view, that concern addressed lost or delayed mail, not injuries caused by hazardous placement. She acknowledged that Bono v. United States had rejected a similar claim but argued that Bono and the district court had read transmission too broadly. Finally, she argued that broad immunity did not further the FTCA exception’s objectives identified in Kosak v. United States. (287.)

The Second Circuit’s recent Raila v. United States, 355 F.3d 118 (2004), supported Dolan’s narrower understanding. The Third Circuit expressly disagreed, creating the circuit split described in the assigned Katzmann account. This was disagreement over the same postal exception’s reach, not a finding that Dolan had failed to allege negligence or had failed to exhaust her own administrative claim. (287–288.)

## The court’s reasoning

The Third Circuit began with sovereign immunity. Consent to suit had to be unequivocally expressed, and the terms of the waiver defined the court’s jurisdiction. Any ambiguity in a purported waiver was to be construed favorably to the sovereign. Thus, even if negligent transmission admitted competing interpretations, the court treated that uncertainty as favoring the government. The statutory phrase addressing any claim arising out of the enumerated postal conduct further supported an expansive exception. Footnote 1 cited Robinson v. United States as another decision taking a similar approach. (287–288 & n. 1.)

The court read the same legislative history differently from Dolan. Congress’s concern with mishaps in ordinary postal operations necessarily included the ultimate act of delivery; it was not limited to mechanical sorting or movement of mail. Putting mail on a porch rather than in a mailbox was an ordinary delivery act. Dolan’s accident was incidental to that placement, and postal employees did not supervise the manner in which third parties later retrieved delivered items. The court regarded mishaps during retrieval as potentially unavoidable incidents of delivering millions of items. (288.)

It accordingly defined transmission, in the context of letters and postal matter, as the process of conveying an item from one person to another, beginning when the Postal Service received it and ending when it delivered it. Because delivery and placement fell within that process, the alleged negligence remained covered by § 2680(b). The interpretation also served the objectives of preventing disruption of a central governmental activity by damages litigation and limiting excessive or fraudulent claims. Those policy considerations reinforced the court’s statutory construction rather than establishing that Dolan’s particular claim was fraudulent. (288.)

## Holding, judgment, and limits

The panel held Barbara Dolan’s personal-injury claim barred by the postal exception and affirmed the district court’s judgment. The opinion did not adjudicate whether the carrier had actually been negligent or determine damages. Nor did it restore Michael’s consortium claim after the concession concerning his administrative exhaustion. The decisive ruling was that the government’s immunity encompassed the allegedly negligent placement of delivered mail. (286, 288.)

The assigned account reproduces the Third Circuit’s interpretive disagreement and then describes the Supreme Court’s later reversal and remand. Keeping those stages distinct is essential: the circuit’s immunity-favoring construction explains the split, but this judgment cannot be presented as the final Supreme Court resolution.
