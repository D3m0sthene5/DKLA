# Vanegas v. American Energy Services
302 S.W.3d 299 (Tex. 2009) | Contracts

## Court and posture
Justice Green delivered the Texas Supreme Court's December 18, 2009 opinion. The court reversed an appellate judgment affirming summary judgment for American Energy Services (AES) and remanded directly to the trial court for further proceedings. The alleged retention-bonus promise could become enforceable through the employees' completed performance despite their at-will status. The opinion did not finally determine that the disputed oral offer had been made or fix a recovery. The complete reproduced judicial opinion contains two notes and no separate writing; it identifies no numerical voting lineup. References below use its Parts I–III, rather than assigning unverified reporter-page boundaries. (Opening; Parts I, III.)

## Alleged retention promise
AES was formed in summer 1996 and hired the employees that year. In a June 1997 operational meeting, they voiced concern to vice president John Carnett about the company's continued viability and complained of long working hours and antiquated equipment. They alleged that, to induce them to remain, Carnett promised that the original eight employees still employed when AES was sold or merged would share five percent of the transaction's value. They were at-will employees, free to leave. AES Acquisition, Inc. acquired AES in 2001, and seven of the original eight remained. They demanded their share, the company refused, and they sued for breach of the oral agreement. The description of the promise rests on the employees' allegations, accepted favorably to them for review of summary judgment. (Part I.)

AES sought summary judgment on two grounds: the promise was illusory and the oral agreement violated the statute of frauds. It later expressly abandoned the statute-of-frauds defense, as the opinion's first note records. The employees argued that the promise offered a unilateral contract that they accepted by remaining until the sale. The trial court granted summary judgment, and the court of appeals affirmed on the view that the purported unilateral contract required at least one nonillusory promise. The Supreme Court reviewed that reasoning, rather than deciding a surviving writing requirement. (Part I & n.1.)

## Parties' competing contract theories
AES argued that at-will status made the promise unenforceable: the employer could fire the employees before the sale and avoid payment. The employees' freedom to leave likewise meant there was no binding mutual commitment to continued employment. AES and the court of appeals relied on Light v. Centel Cellular Co. of Texas and Alex Sheshunoff Management Services, L.P. v. Johnson, cases concerning employer-employee covenants not to compete. In Light, the court had stated that mutual promises dependent on continued at-will employment could be illusory and that an otherwise defective bilateral arrangement might yield a unilateral contract through performance. (Opening; Part II.)

The Supreme Court explained the context of those earlier statements. Light dealt with whether a noncompete covenant was ancillary to an otherwise enforceable agreement under the statutory requirement then discussed. Its agreement included identifiable nonillusory promises concerning initial specialized training, fourteen days' termination notice, and an inventory of employer property. The covenant failed the statutory ancillary-agreement requirement. Sheshunoff later rejected the reading of Light's sixth note that would permanently exclude a unilateral arrangement made enforceable by later performance merely because it was unenforceable when initially made. These prior decisions did not impose a universal requirement of an exchange of enforceable promises for every contract accepted by performance. (Part II.)

## Unilateral acceptance by completed service
The court distinguished the mechanisms of formation. A bilateral contract exchanges promises. A unilateral contract offers a benefit in return for actual performance or forbearance, and becomes enforceable upon that performance. The appellate court's formulation—one illusory promise and one nonillusory promise—addressed whether a failed bilateral exchange could be salvaged. It did not describe the entire category of unilateral contracts. The second note quotes Williston's terminology distinction: an undertaking without an enforceable obligation is a unilateral offer or promise, while an actual unilateral contract exists once an obligation is created. (Part II & n.2.)

Even assuming that AES's promise was illusory when made, the relevant question was whether it had become enforceable by the alleged breach. Assuming the offer alleged by the employees, they accepted it by staying through the acquisition. At that point they had done what the offer requested. AES's earlier ability to end the employment relationship could not erase the performance actually completed by those remaining employees. The court's house-painting illustration similarly contrasted a proposed payment before performance with a binding payment obligation after performance. The employees here had reached the specified sale event; the decision did not resolve protection against revocation during partial performance, a pre-sale dismissal, or a sale that never occurred. (Part II.)

The existing salary did not defeat consideration for the bonus. Because at-will employees were not previously obligated to remain, their requested continued service could support both salary and additional promised compensation. Requiring a separate binding promise to stay would also imperil ordinary compensation arrangements such as pensions, vacation leave, and bonuses earned through a term of service. The court treated the promised bonus as compensation for requested future service, rather than a purely gratuitous payment. (Part II.)

## Holding and exact result
The employees' seven completed periods of service through the sale could accept the alleged offer, rendering it enforceable regardless of initial illusoriness. The court expressly conditioned its application on the assumption that AES actually made that offer. Its statement that refusing payment breached the agreement is part of that assumed factual scenario, rather than a final trial finding that the oral promise existed and every element of recovery was proved. The exact result was reversal of the court of appeals' judgment and remand to the trial court for further proceedings consistent with the opinion under Texas Rule of Appellate Procedure 60.2(d). (Parts II–III.)
