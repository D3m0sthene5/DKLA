# Terminal Freight Handling Co. v. Solien
444 F.2d 699 (8th Cir. 1971) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and the distinct proceedings

Terminal Freight Handling and Terminal Freight Co-Operative Association charged Teamsters Local 610 on June 12, 1970, with a secondary boycott violating NLRA § 8(b)(4)(B). Regional Director Joseph Solien found reasonable cause on June 16 and concluded that a complaint should issue unless the unlawful picketing stopped. He initially sought neither a Board complaint nor a temporary injunction because the union gave written assurance on June 18 that it had altered its common-situs picketing to meet the Moore Dry Dock standards and tendered an informal settlement. At Terminal's request, he deferred consideration of that settlement and investigated further. (701–702 & nn. 1–4.)

On June 19, Terminal sued to compel an injunction petition. Its June 26 amendment additionally requested declarations that refusal to petition violated § 10(l)'s mandatory command and that Field Manual § 10230 improperly authorized discretion. After concluding the picketing remained secondary, Solien petitioned for a temporary injunction on June 29 and issued the unfair-labor-practice complaint July 2. Meanwhile, the primary dispute had ended June 27 and all picketing ceased June 29. Terminal voluntarily dismissed only its request to compel the petition, retaining its declaratory claims. The district court dismissed those claims as moot and found the General Counsel indispensable to review of the manual provision. (702–703.)

Related proceedings must remain separate. The court in the § 10(l) proceeding approved a July 9 settlement stipulation in lieu of temporary relief; an earlier appellate decision upheld refusal to give the charging parties full party status there. Separately, the Regional Director and union executed a July 24 formal settlement of the underlying unfair-labor-practice complaint providing for a Board order and appellate enforcement decree. The General Counsel approved that settlement March 19, 1971 and submitted it to the Board. Another challenge to exclusion from settlement discussions remained pending when this opinion issued. This appeal concerned the remaining declaratory claims, not resolution of all those related suits. (702–703 & nn. 5–6.)

## Jurisdiction, a live controversy, and proper parties

Judge Gibson wrote for the unanimous Matthes–Gibson–Henley panel on June 18, 1971; District Judge Henley sat by designation. The Regional Director relied on § 3(d)'s final General Counsel authority over prosecution to argue against judicial jurisdiction. The court agreed that the Declaratory Judgment Act created a remedy rather than jurisdiction, but held that 28 U.S.C. § 1337 supplied jurisdiction to determine whether an official exceeded statutory powers. Leedom v. Kyne's rationale applied to disregard of an affirmative statutory command as well as violation of an express prohibition. This was not ordinary review of a Board decision made within lawful discretion. The reasonable-cause finding also distinguished a prior case in which that threshold had not been satisfied. (699, 701, 703–704 & nn. 7–8.)

The dispute was not moot simply because Solien eventually petitioned or picketing stopped. The parties continued to disagree about his statutory duty; recurrence could again affect Terminal's rights. The question was inherently capable of escaping review, and a declaration would clarify future legal relations. Voluntary cessation of the challenged refusal did not terminate that controversy. The opinion referred to statistics offered by Terminal and undisputed by the Board to illustrate the practical recurrence issue, not to declare every decision against seeking relief unlawful. (704–705 & n. 9.)

The General Counsel was not indispensable. The manual question depended on the Regional Director's duty under § 10(l), and effective relief could operate on the Director already before the court. The General Counsel's power to override a Director did not require joining the superior official whenever relief could expend itself on the subordinate. (705–706.)

## The statute imposed a qualified mandatory duty

Section 10(l) directed expedited investigation and provided that, upon reasonable cause to believe the charge true and that a complaint should issue, the appropriate officer shall petition for interim relief. Solien read that language as two judgments: reasonable cause, followed by an independently discretionary decision whether to issue a complaint. Terminal argued that reasonable cause alone required immediate complaint and petition. The court rejected both an absolute discretion to do nothing and Terminal's wholly mechanical interpretation. (705–709.)

The contrast with § 10(j) mattered. Congress used permissive authority for other interim-relief proceedings but mandatory language in § 10(l), drafting and adopting both in the same 1947 legislation. Jurisdictional strikes were placed in the discretionary provision because other dispute-resolution machinery was expected to suffice. Legislative history also showed concern about secondary boycotts' immediate economic harm and a compromise rejecting private injunction petitions while requiring prompt agency action. The court acknowledged conflicting floor statements rather than depicting the history as unanimous or conclusive. Reports and the statutory contrast nevertheless supported a deliberately limited discretion. (706–708 & nn. 10–17.)

The Director must investigate forthwith and, after reasonable cause, proceed promptly to resolve the dispute. The additional language that a complaint should issue preserved a limited enforcement judgment. He could first demand cessation and undertake summary negotiations. If those efforts failed to secure cessation, he had to petition for temporary relief and issue a complaint, in either sequence, unless the matter was too insubstantial to warrant further action. The statute did not require a useless petition where the union had stopped the conduct and resumption was unlikely. Resource constraints and marginal facts explained why some judgment remained, but could not erase Congress's affirmative command. (708–709.)

## Settlement, the manual, and the decree

Even after filing an injunction petition, the Director could negotiate settlement in lieu of interim relief. Ordinarily a district court would approve such settlements, but it could override one failing adequately to protect the public interest and grant relief. Section 10(l) did not itself authorize the charging party to initiate the injunction proceeding or give it full party status. This opinion's qualified mandatory duty therefore did not contradict the earlier decision concerning those participation rights. (709.)

Field Manual § 10230 was valid under this construction. It recognized mandatory injunction proceedings but permitted holding commencement in abeyance where activity had voluntarily stopped and resumption was not threatened. The manual's introduction likewise described guidance for professional judgment rather than an exhaustive substitute for substantive law. The court found that provision consistent with Congress's command, despite rejecting the Board's broader all-or-nothing argument. (709–710 & n. 18.)

The judgment **vacated the district-court judgment and remanded with directions to file this appellate opinion as the declaration of the parties' rights**. Because Terminal had dismissed the demand for an injunction and only declaratory relief remained, no further trial-court action was required. The court assessed two-thirds of costs against the appellee and one-third against the appellants. It did not order a new temporary-relief petition in the ended dispute, invalidate the manual, or adjudicate all related settlement litigation. No separate opinion accompanied the decision. (710.)
