# United States v. Gray
642 F.3d 371 and 421 F. App'x 11 (2d Cir. 2011) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and procedural setting

Although the caption begins with Kirby Gray, the appellants were correctional officers Marvin Wells and Stephen Rhodes. They worked at the Queens Private Correctional Facility, a privately operated institution that housed federal prisoners under a contract with the United States Marshals Service. On April 17, 2007, Wells strip-searched inmate Rex Eguridu, struck his chest and throat, causing his head repeatedly to hit the concrete wall. Wells threatened Eguridu against reporting what happened. Rhodes and Gray witnessed the encounter. The ensuing concealment involved inaccurate accounts of the incident and efforts to get other officers to adhere to the false account. Officer Andrews eventually gave a truthful account in her fifth report; Officer Day did so in his second. The facility’s private ownership matters because the defendants later argued that its internal investigation fell outside federal obstruction statutes. (642 F.3d at 372–374 & nn. 2–3.)

Department of Justice Inspector General agents interviewed the officers in 2008. Wells and Rhodes made false statements in those interviews. A November 12, 2008 indictment led to a jury trial before District Judge Jack B. Weinstein. The April 1, 2010 judgments convicted Wells of conspiracy to obstruct justice under 18 U.S.C. § 371, obstruction under § 1519, two counts of attempted intimidation and corrupt persuasion under § 1512(b)(3), and a false statement under § 1001(a)(2). Rhodes was convicted of obstruction under § 1519 and a false statement under § 1001(a)(2). Wells received one year and one day of imprisonment and three years of supervised release; Rhodes received three years of probation. The jury acquitted Wells of the excessive-force charge and acquitted Rhodes and Gray of conspiracy. Those acquittals did not erase the other verdicts. (642 F.3d at 374–375; 421 F. App'x at 12, 14–15.)

The Second Circuit resolved the appeals on April 28, 2011 through two coordinated writings: Judge Robert A. Katzmann’s published opinion addressing § 1519 and an unsigned companion summary order addressing the remaining challenges. Judges Amalya L. Kearse, Robert D. Sack, and Katzmann constituted the unanimous panel; neither writing contains a separate concurrence or dissent. Reading both is necessary to reconstruct why the convictions were affirmed. (642 F.3d at 371–372 & n. 1; 421 F. App'x at 12–13 & n. 1.)

## Section 1519: arguments and governing text

Section 1519 punishes knowingly falsifying, concealing, or destroying specified records or information with the requisite intent to obstruct an investigation or the proper administration of a matter within a federal department’s or agency’s jurisdiction, including conduct in relation to or contemplation of such a matter. Wells and Rhodes argued that the government had to establish a nexus with a pending or imminent official proceeding. They relied on United States v. Aguilar’s interpretation of § 1503 and Arthur Andersen LLP v. United States’s interpretation of § 1512(b)(2). They also argued that an internal investigation at a private prison was not a matter within DOJ’s jurisdiction. Their position would have limited the statute to misconduct tied to an identifiable government investigation already underway or foreseeably impending. (642 F.3d at 375–377.)

The court rejected the proposed nexus requirement. Aguilar dealt with a provision directed at obstruction of the due administration of justice, and Arthur Andersen addressed a provision expressly referring to an official proceeding. Section 1519 instead speaks broadly about an investigation or administration of any matter within an agency’s jurisdiction and expressly reaches conduct undertaken in contemplation of such a matter. Importing an official-proceeding element from differently worded statutes would disregard Congress’s textual choices. No investigation or proceeding had to be pending or about to begin when the records were falsified. (642 F.3d at 376–377.)

The court also examined legislative history. The Senate report described a provision meant to avoid technical restrictions associated with existing obstruction offenses, including requirements that misconduct be tied to a pending or imminent proceeding. That history reinforced the distinction the court found in the statutory language; it did not supply a missing textual element. The court declined to follow contrary language in United States v. Russell insofar as that language suggested § 1519 required a connection to a particular anticipated federal investigation. (642 F.3d at 377–378 & nn. 4–5.)

## Application to the private prison and mens rea

The underlying excessive-force matter lay within DOJ’s jurisdiction. The prison performed the traditional federal function of housing federal prisoners, operated under a federal contract, and had obligations to report incidents involving force. Its initial internal investigation did not turn misconduct concerning federal prisoners into a purely private matter. DOJ’s eventual investigation confirmed the practical connection, but the statutory theory did not depend on an investigation having already started when the concealment occurred. The interval before the federal agents’ interviews therefore did not defeat liability. (642 F.3d at 378–379.)

The defendants also argued that they did not know their reports would be submitted to DOJ. The court expressly held that knowledge of a pending federal investigation or proceeding was not an element. Their broader objection concerning awareness of DOJ jurisdiction encountered a preservation problem: they had not objected to the instruction that such knowledge need not be proved. The panel further found ample evidence of awareness of DOJ’s policy from their training and the facility’s federal responsibilities. These distinct grounds should not be collapsed into a separately resolved universal rule about knowledge of federal jurisdiction. The statute’s knowing-falsification and obstructive-intent requirements remained applicable. (642 F.3d at 378–379.)

The holding was bounded by the actual institutional relationship. Footnote 6 expressly reserved how § 1519 would apply to other investigations at private entities. The opinion does not announce that every internal inquiry at every private institution automatically falls within a federal agency’s jurisdiction. Its reasoning connects the matter being concealed to this facility’s federal prisoners, contractual duties, and DOJ responsibilities. (642 F.3d at 379 n. 6.)

## Remaining convictions and evidentiary issues

The companion summary order rejected Wells’s challenge to the § 1512(b)(3) convictions. That provision protects communications about possible federal offenses to federal law-enforcement officers or judges. Section 1512(f)(1) does not require a proceeding to be pending or imminent, and § 1512(g) does not require proof that the defendant knew an officer or judge was federal. The statute also protects potential witnesses. Wells therefore could not defeat liability merely because the initial communications he tried to suppress were within the private facility. DOJ’s policy of investigating excessive force against federal prisoners and the trial record permitted a rational jury to find the required elements. The court applied ordinary sufficiency review, viewing the evidence and reasonable inferences favorably to the government. (421 F. App'x at 13–14.)

Wells’s false-statement argument confused actual reliance with materiality. He contended that Inspector General Agent Mary Chiu-Vaccariello did not rely on his statements. A statement is material when it has a natural tendency or capacity to influence the decisionmaking body; actual reliance is unnecessary. His denial that officers had covered up the assault addressed the central subject of the investigation and was supported as a material false statement. (421 F. App'x at 14.)

The acquittals produced no legal entitlement to overturn the remaining verdicts. A conspiracy conviction does not become invalid solely because all alleged coconspirators were acquitted. Likewise, inconsistency among counts or among codefendants’ verdicts does not itself establish insufficiency. Following United States v. Powell, the court declined to speculate about jury lenity or the reasons for the apparent inconsistency. Separately reviewing the evidence, it held that a rational jury could convict Rhodes of obstruction and a false statement. (421 F. App'x at 14–15.)

Finally, the defendants argued that admitting the facility’s use-of-force policies risked having the jury judge them by employer standards more stringent than the relevant Bureau of Prisons standards. Evidentiary review was for abuse of discretion affecting a substantial right. Wells’s acquittal on excessive force—the pertinent charge requiring a finding about force—undercut the claim that those policies had prejudicially confused the jury. The court found no abuse of discretion. (421 F. App'x at 15.)

## Judgment, significance, and source scope

The panel affirmed both district judgments. The published opinion explains why § 1519 reaches the proven concealment without an imported official-proceeding nexus; the companion order resolves the other conviction and evidentiary challenges. The assigned Katzmann example illustrates the textual distinction among obstruction provisions, but the complete judgment also depends on the facility’s federal relationship, the evidence, preservation rules, and the independent rejection of the other trial arguments. (642 F.3d at 379; 421 F. App'x at 15.)
