# Aguchak v. Montgomery Ward Co., Inc.

**520 P.2d 1352 (Alaska 1974) — Supreme Court of Alaska, April 1, 1974.** Justice Boochever wrote the opinion; Chief Justice Rabinowitz and Justices Connor, Erwin, and Fitzgerald also sat. No separate writing or division is reported. Assigned account: CivPro.pdf, physical page 150, printed page 251, Note 10.

## Facts and procedural path

Luther and Ida Aguchak lived in Scammon Bay, a remote Alaska village accessible only by air, approximately 500 miles from Anchorage. In late 1970 they bought a snowmobile and freezer from Montgomery Ward's Anchorage store using a credit account. Ward subsequently claimed that their payments were delinquent and sued in Anchorage's small-claims court. The couple lived in the Second Judicial District; Anchorage was in the Third. Their annual income was $5,470.50, they supported two children, and their listed assets were a small house and the purchased goods. Their affidavit established indigency prima facie, and Ward did not contest those facts. 520 P.2d at 1353, 1355 n.14.

The summonses arrived by mail in mid-November 1971. They ordered the Aguchaks to appear personally in Anchorage on December 27 at 8 a.m., ready for immediate trial, or face default. They did not tell the couple that a written pleading could present their defense or seek a venue transfer. A round-trip airfare cost $186 per person and required at least an overnight stop in Bethel because of poor connections. The couple did not appear. Ward's claim was $988.28; the entered default judgment totaled $1,024.15. Their December and January wages were then taken in partial satisfaction. That execution itself was outside the appeal. Id. at 1353, 1356 n.18.

The Aguchaks moved under Alaska Civil Rule 60(b)(4), arguing that the judgment was void because the court lacked personal jurisdiction and the procedure denied due process. The district court denied relief on March 28, 1972, and the superior court affirmed. The Alaska Supreme Court reviewed the denial of relief, a final appealable order. Id. at 1353–1354 & nn.2–7.

## Issue and holding

Did this summons give indigent rural defendants adequate information to defend a small-claims action in a distant court? The court held that it did not. Under **Article I, section 7 of the Alaska Constitution**, notice must communicate the information needed for a meaningful opportunity to respond in these circumstances. Omitting the option of a written response effectively deprived the couple of their opportunity to defend. The resulting assumption of personal jurisdiction and default judgment violated state due process. Id. at 1356–1358.

The holding expressly rests on Alaska's constitution and distinctive geographic setting. The court used federal notice precedents to interpret the state guarantee but reserved whether the federal Constitution compelled the same result. It also reserved the alternative territorial-jurisdiction and broader access-to-court arguments. Id. at 1354–1356 & nn.13, 20. These reservations prevent treating the case as a universal federal requirement to put every procedural defense into every summons.

## Why receiving a summons was insufficient

**Voidness was a legal question, rather than discretionary relief from neglect.** Rule 60(b)(4) differs from the provisions under which a reviewing court asks whether denying relief abused discretion. If the rendering court lacked jurisdiction or acted inconsistently with due process, validity is a question of law. A party challenging a void judgment need not first demonstrate a meritorious defense to the underlying debt. The Aguchaks still bore the burden of establishing the jurisdictional or constitutional defect. Id. at 1354 & nn.3–6.

**The available procedural route was hidden by the form.** Small-claims rules already permitted a written statement of the defense, and Alaska law required a venue change in specified circumstances involving unnecessary expense and inconvenience. A written application could have asked to move the case closer to the Aguchaks. Given the prohibitive travel burden, the court expected a transfer if there was a reasonable prospect that they would present a defense requiring evidence at a hearing. The court did not itself adjudicate a particular merits defense or grant a venue motion. It decided whether the summons adequately told them how to use existing procedures. Id. at 1355–1356 & nn.15–18.

Ordinarily, failing to request a venue change waives a venue objection. The couple's challenge, however, concerned a form that denied an adequate opportunity to respond by leaving them unaware of the written option. Resolving that constitutional question did not amount to entertaining an ordinary, untimely venue objection. Id. at 1356.

**Adequate notice depends on the recipient's circumstances.** Applying Mullane's requirement of notice reasonably calculated to inform and permit objections, the court examined the information rural indigent defendants needed. Collection defendants commonly lacked counsel and legal sophistication. Finding counsel in remote Alaska intensified those difficulties. A command to travel hundreds of miles at substantial cost, without identifying a practicable written alternative, could communicate that defense was unavailable even when the law actually allowed it. Id. at 1356–1357.

The court drew a limited analogy to notice directed to a known incompetent person: a statutorily authorized method can still fail constitutional scrutiny when it does not fit the recipient's capacities and circumstances. It expressly did not characterize the Aguchaks as incompetent or require guardians for rural defendants. The relevant analogy concerned tailoring information so that the opportunity to defend had practical meaning. Id. at 1357 & nn.25–30.

**Correcting the form imposed little countervailing burden.** The court acknowledged Alaska's interest in an efficient credit economy: excessive collection costs could raise credit prices or cause retailers to demand cash from rural customers. But explaining existing written-defense and transfer procedures neither enlarged substantive defenses nor added travel and proof burdens beyond those already imposed by statute. The State's direct interest in retaining the defective form was essentially avoiding reprinting costs. That interest did not justify the effective denial of a defense opportunity. Id. at 1357–1358 & nn.31–33.

## Remedy, limits, and assigned discrepancy

The court **reversed and remanded**, directing the superior court to declare the district-court default judgment null and void and entertain further proceedings consistent with the opinion. It did not forgive the purchase debt or decide that the Aguchaks would prevail if Ward pursued the claim with adequate process. Id. at 1358.

The court declined to constitutionalize an exhaustive list of required summons information. It used its separate rulemaking responsibility to order an interim form explaining that a distant defendant could write to seek a transfer, describe the practical burden, and state facts showing a defense. Footnote 34 supplied the model instructions while revised small-claims procedures were being prepared. Likewise, footnote 28 reserved whether due process required detailed information about the various possible pleadings and motions. The constitutional holding and the broader form prescribed through rulemaking should be distinguished. Id. at 1357 n.28, 1358 n.34.

The assigned account says the default was “$988.22 plus costs”; the actual opinion identifies the claim as **$988.28** and separately reports the entered judgment as **$1,024.15**. The account also compresses the decision into a requirement to include written-appearance and venue information. The complete opinion supplies the important distinction between the state constitutional defect and the interim form ordered through rulemaking. Assigned physical page 150; actual 1353, 1357–1358.
