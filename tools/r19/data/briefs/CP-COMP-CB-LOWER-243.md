# O’Regan v. Schermerhorn

**25 N.J. Misc. 1, 50 A.2d 10 (1946) — Civil Procedure**

Supreme Court Commissioner Ackerson decided these pleading motions in two substantially identical libel actions. The original report’s caption gives November 6, 1946; an Atlantic-derived reproduction gives November 7. This brief follows the original New Jersey Miscellaneous report rather than treating the dates as unanimously confirmed. No separate opinion or numerical vote is reported.

## Facts and procedural setting

Daniel O’Regan, former Hudson County prosecutor, and William George, former assistant prosecutor, served from February 4, 1934, through February 4, 1944. Each sued the twenty-three members of the county’s December 1944 grand jury for libel. The complaints alleged publication of a report accusing the prosecutors of suppressing the Hartkorn report and failing to call Hartkorn as a witness in criminal proceedings against John Longo, thereby withholding potentially exculpatory material. The quoted accusation invoked Canon 5’s obligation to seek justice rather than merely convictions. These were allegations about the publication, not a judicial finding that either prosecutor actually suppressed evidence. (5–6.)

The defendants’ joint answer in each action contained fourteen separate defenses. Plaintiffs sought to strike the answers or particular defenses. At oral argument, defendants obtained leave to supplement their answers with an objection that the suits were premature; the supplements also operated as cross-motions to strike the complaints. Plaintiffs elected to attack the supplemental matter as part of their existing motions. Because the pleadings were practically identical, Ackerson considered the motions together as though one joint action. The decision therefore concerns which allegations and defenses could remain, rather than a trial of the publication’s truth or a final damages judgment. (6–7.)

## The complaints and representation

Each complaint charged a joint tort in one count but demanded $100,000 from each defendant. The court found that demand technically defective and allowed amendment to one amount against the defendants jointly. It proceeded as if the amendment had been made, while warning that failure to amend within the specified period would cause the complaint to be stricken. (7.)

Defendants also argued that the publication was an official grand-jury report delivered to and accepted by the Court of Oyer and Terminer, making it absolutely privileged. But the complaints alleged only that defendants composed and published the defamatory material; they did not establish the official presentation defendants assumed. Nor would inserting conclusions such as clear absence of jurisdiction repair the problem: facts, rather than legal labels, determined sufficiency. Defendants had to plead the facts supporting privilege in their answer. Their claim that the report first had to be expunged likewise could not defeat a complaint that did not disclose that factual premise. (7–8.)

Plaintiffs unsuccessfully challenged the Attorney General’s authority to defend former grand jurors. His common-law power to protect public interests survived and was reaffirmed by the 1944 Department of Law statute. Litigation affecting the rights and liabilities of compelled grand-jury service implicated the statewide judicial system and the public’s interest in its proper operation. Authority was not confined to a narrow classification of defendants as formal state officers. (8–10.)

## Pleading facts, truth, and other defenses

Parts of the first defense were stricken for evasive or unresponsive denials, demands for proof of matters never charged, and unnecessary legal or evidentiary material. The court sometimes acted on its own motion and preserved explicit admissions or denials embedded in the defective paragraphs. The ruling thus did not eliminate the defendants’ general ability to contest the pleaded facts. (10–12.)

A particularly important paragraph alleged that jurors believed the report true and recited the evidence producing that belief. Belief was not a complete defense of truth. Honest belief might be pleaded in mitigation when punitive damages were claimed, but that purpose had to be clear and the pleading should not detail the underlying evidence. The paragraph instead appeared to assert belief as complete justification. It also failed as qualified privilege: belief in truth did not establish absence of malice, because a communication could be malicious on other grounds. The court struck the paragraph’s challenged substance while preserving its express denials. (11–12.)

The second defense alleged the statements were substantially true. The court treated that formulation as technically insufficient, requiring an averment that the words were true in substance and in fact. Importantly, the third defense independently alleged that the statements were true; plaintiffs did not attack it. The decision did not remove every truth defense from the case. (12.)

The fair-comment defense likewise needed allegations that the underlying facts were true, the subject was of public interest, and the comments were fair, made in good faith, and without malice. Alleging truth was an essential foundation for this defense, rather than impermissibly combining two defenses. Other defenses invoking public policy or a supposed exclusive remedy in the original court supplied legal conclusions without supporting facts. A defense about an absence of defamatory intention or understanding also contained irrelevant matter: an author’s intention was not the governing test of defamatory meaning. Those deficient defenses were stricken as specified in the opinion. (13–14.)

The separately reserved Rule 40 prematurity objection was different. Reserving a legal question for preliminary determination functioned like a demurrer rather than an affirmative factual defense. A motion to strike was not the appropriate way to obtain the Rule 40 determination. The court denied that attack without prejudice to a proper motion to decide the question before trial. (14–15.)

## Absolute privilege: sufficient facts, not a required label

The fourth, seventh, and fourteenth defenses required close examination. A party did not have to name absolute or qualified privilege if pleaded facts established it. But grand-juror status alone did not protect every publication. The fourth defense failed to show an official investigation and judicial act; the seventh failed to identify the circumstances of publication. Neither adequately incorporated other allegations. Publication outside judicial proceedings, potentially in a newspaper, could not acquire absolute privilege simply because its authors were jurors. The court struck those defenses. The complete report itself was not competently before the court merely because the parties discussed it. (15–18.)

The fourteenth defense supplied the missing facts: jurors were investigating allegations of suppressed evidence in a particular prosecution, the prosecutors appeared and testified under oath, and the publication resulted from an official presentation to the court. On a motion testing that defense, its facts were accepted for that limited purpose. Such an investigation could concern criminal misconduct by public prosecutors. It was neither merely an inquiry into private behavior nor a general fishing expedition into an official’s conduct. (18–19, 23–24.)

Grand juries exercised judicial functions as arms of the courts, with broad common-law powers to investigate possible offenses. Judicial immunity depended on the nature of the act and jurisdiction, not simply officeholding. The decisive distinction was between a clear absence of all jurisdiction and an erroneous or excessive exercise of actual or colorable jurisdiction. When facts presented a matter arguably within the officer’s judicial authority, deciding whether to proceed was itself judicial. Error, irregularity, malice, or corruption did not by themselves remove the protection for such an act. (19–24.)

Plaintiffs argued that an accusation unaccompanied by indictment was an unauthorized report. Ackerson examined the distinction between indictment and common-law presentment, including New Jersey’s continued constitutional and statutory recognition of presentments. He did not finally classify the document as a true presentment. Even assuming it was only a report without an intended indictment, composing and presenting it under the pleaded circumstances remained a judicial act protected by colorable jurisdiction. (25–27.)

Cases expunging grand-jury reports did not settle personal civil liability. Expunction might turn on actual authority or excess, while immunity additionally protected colorable authority. Indeed, conflicting precedents about reporting authority demonstrated the difficulty of the jurisdictional question. Jurors compelled to serve should not decide unsettled common-law questions at the risk of personal libel damages. Ackerson distinguished restrictive statutory-grand-jury cases and compared other libel decisions, emphasizing protection of judicial independence for the public’s benefit. The statements described in the fourteenth defense were pertinent to the investigation and absolutely privileged. Possible expunction remained distinct from civil damages. (27–32.)

## Exact result and assigned significance

The motion to strike the fourteenth defense was denied. The court directed preparation of an order implementing its mixed pleading rulings, allowed plaintiffs to amend their damages demand, and allowed defendants to amend paragraph eight of the first defense and the sixth, tenth, and twelfth defenses. Amendments had to be filed on or before November 25, 1946. It did not dismiss both suits outright or enter a final verdict establishing the accusation’s truth. (32.)
